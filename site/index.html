Political Philosophy

  • Leviathan Thomas Hobbes (1651) · ~16-hour read · epub Why we need government at all — the founding argument of the modern state, written amid civil war.
  • Two Treatises of Government John Locke (1689) · ~7-hour read · epub The demolition of the divine right of kings, and the case for government by consent.
  • The Social Contract Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1762) · ~3-hour read · epub The general will, the social contract, and the most famous opening line in political philosophy.
  • The Prince Niccolò Machiavelli (1532) · ~2-hour read · epub The infamous handbook of power politics.
  • Common Sense Thomas Paine (1776) · ~90-minute read · epub The pamphlet that talked America into independence.
  • Reflections on the Revolution in France Edmund Burke (1790) · ~7-hour read · epub The book that invented conservatism, by a Whig who had spent thirty years attacking the abuse of power in America, Ireland and India — and who then refused to cheer the Revolution. A society, he argues, is not a machine that can be taken apart and rebuilt to a drawing. Written in 1790, it predicted the confiscations, the collapse of the currency, the tribunals, and the general who would end up master of the whole republic.
  • Cato's Letters John Trenchard and Thomas Gordon (1720–1723) · ~3-hour read · epub Eighteen selected letters on liberty, power, and free speech — the columns that taught America to argue for revolution.
  • Utopia Thomas More (1516) · ~3-hour read · epub The island where everything works — and the deadpan joke is that you can't tell how much More means it.
  • The Federalist Papers Alexander Hamilton, James Madison, and John Jay (1787–1788) · ~12-hour read · epub The Constitution's authors explain — and sell — their design.
  • Democracy in America Alexis de Tocqueville (1835/1840) · ~18-hour read · epub A young Frenchman tours the United States and sees the democratic future — flaws and all.
  • Democracy in America (From the French) Alexis de Tocqueville (1835/1840) · ~19-hour read · epub A second pass, translated directly from Tocqueville's French.

Economics

  • The Autobiography of Benjamin Franklin Benjamin Franklin (1771–1790) · ~4-hour read · epub The original American life story — runaway apprentice to statesman, told with a wink.
  • The Way to Wealth Benjamin Franklin (1758) · ~15-minute read · epub Poor Richard's proverbs rolled into one sly speech — delivered to a crowd that applauds, then ignores it.
  • The Wealth of Nations Adam Smith (1776) · ~22-hour read · epub The book that invented economics: pins, markets, and the invisible hand.
  • An Essay on the Principle of Population Thomas Malthus (1798) · ~4-hour read · epub Malthus' unsettling arithmetic of people and food.
  • Progress and Poverty Henry George (1879) · ~12-hour read · epub Why progress and poverty rise together — and George's famous remedy, the land tax.

The Ancient World

  • The Art of War Sun Tzu (c. 5th century BC) · ~4-hour read · epub The oldest surviving book on war, and the one whose most famous sentence says that winning a hundred battles is not the height of skill — the height of skill is breaking the enemy's resistance without fighting. This edition keeps what most editions cut: two thousand years of commentary by eleven Chinese generals and scholars, disagreeing with Sun Tzu, disagreeing with each other, and illustrating him from the histories — a general who drew up his army with a river at its back so it could not run away, another who burned a camp at midnight with thirty-six men, and a herd of oxen sent uphill with blazing twigs tied to their horns.
  • Dialogues Plato (399–347 BC) · ~42-hour read · epub The complete dialogues — Socrates on trial, in the agora, and at the limits of knowledge.
  • The Memorabilia Xenophon (c. 371 BC) · ~4-hour read · epub Socrates remembered by his other student — practical, sociable, and useful, with the Choice of Heracles.
  • Nicomachean Ethics Aristotle (c. 340 BC) · ~6-hour read · epub The original inquiry into the good life — happiness, virtue as habit, the mean, and friendship.
  • Characters Theophrastus (c. 319 BC) · ~40-minute read · epub Thirty ruthless comic sketches of annoying Athenians — every one of them still alive and probably in your contacts.
  • Meditations Marcus Aurelius (c. 170–180 AD) · ~3-hour read · epub A Roman emperor's private notes to himself on how to live.
  • The Enchiridion Epictetus (c. 125 AD) · ~30-minute read · epub The Stoic pocket manual — fifty-one drills for keeping your head, from a philosopher born enslaved.
  • The Practice Instructions Dōgen (1227–1234) · ~40-minute read · epub A monk home from China writes a page and a half on how to sit down: which foot on which thigh, the ears in line with the shoulders, the eyes open. Then, in the same flat voice, think of the thing that does not think. With ten sections of advice for beginners, and a manual for the monastery cook — where an old man drying food on scorching paving, asked why he does not send an assistant, answers that other people are not him. All three complete, from the Chinese.
  • The Illustrated Life of Dōgen Kenzei (c. 1470; illustrated 1806) · ~90-minute read · epub The oldest life of the founder of Sōtō Zen, written by one of his successors so that ordinary people could read it. A boy runs away from court at thirteen; a young monk argues with the Chinese emperor about seating; a teacher says come and ask at any hour, in your everyday robe. With the travel permits, the letters, the farmers' sworn statement about a coloured cloud, the kitchen rules in his own hand, and the death verse. From the Japanese, complete.
  • The Discourses Epictetus (c. 108 AD) · ~8-hour read · epub The lectures the Enchiridion was cut down from — a former slave telling Rome's young gentlemen that they are the ones in chains.
  • On Duties Cicero (44 BC) · ~4-hour read · epub Cicero's last book, a letter to his son — translated from the Latin: nothing dishonorable is ever truly useful.
  • Tusculan Disputations Cicero (45 BC) · ~5-hour read · epub Five days on death, pain, grief, and the passions — from the Latin, with the Sword of Damocles.
  • Roman Lives Plutarch (c. 100 AD) · ~8-hour read · epub The fall of the Republic in five lives — Caesar, Cato, Cicero, Brutus, Antony — five angles on one catastrophe.
  • Moral Letters to Lucilius Seneca (c. 65 AD) · ~13-hour read · epub All 124 letters from a Stoic — a daily course in living, from saving time to facing the end.
  • The Histories Herodotus (c. 430 BC) · ~15-hour read · epub The first history book — Persia, Egypt, and the Greek wars, with magnificent detours.
  • History of the Peloponnesian War Thucydides (c. 431 BC) · ~14-hour read · epub Power, plague, and the ruin of Athens, by the first analytical historian.
  • Commentaries on the Gallic War Julius Caesar (c. 50 BC) · ~4-hour read · epub Caesar's own dispatches from the conquest of Gaul.
  • Confessions Augustine of Hippo (c. 400 AD) · ~13-hour read · epub The first autobiography — the stolen pears, the friend's death, the garden at Milan, and the night at Ostia; then memory, and time. Every sentence addressed to God, so that reading it is overhearing.
  • The Consolation of Philosophy Boethius (524 AD) · ~4-hour read · epub Condemned without a hearing and waiting to be executed, the last Roman philosopher is visited by Philosophy, who tells him nothing he has lost was ever his. Fortune's wheel, the five false goods, and the question of whether being foreseen leaves anything free. All five books and all thirty-nine poems.

Philosophy & Science

  • Selected Essays Michel de Montaigne (1580–1588) · ~9-hour read · epub The man who invented the essay, talking to you across four centuries about death, friendship, cannibals, and himself.
  • Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems Galileo Galilei (1632) · ~16-hour read · epub Four days of brilliant argument about whether the Earth moves — the vernacular bestseller that got its author hauled before the Inquisition, translated from Galileo's own Italian.
  • Philosophical Works René Descartes (1637–1644) · ~5-hour read · epub Descartes doubts everything and rebuilds from “I think, therefore I am.”
  • Ethics Baruch Spinoza (1677) · ~6-hour read · epub God and Nature are two names for the same thing, the mind is the idea of the body, and free will is what we call our ignorance of the causes acting on us. Spinoza sets it all out like a geometry textbook — definitions, axioms, propositions, proofs — and defines forty-eight emotions in order, with no more moralising than a geometer gives a triangle. Published the year he died, and banned almost everywhere for a century.
  • An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding David Hume (1748) · ~5-hour read · epub Why no amount of experience can prove the sun will rise tomorrow, why you never actually see one thing cause another, and why Hume thinks none of it need worry you: custom, not reason, is the great guide of human life.
  • A Vindication of the Rights of Woman Mary Wollstonecraft (1792) · ~6-hour read · epub Written in six weeks, three years after the Bastille fell, and dedicated to the French statesman whose education plan had just proposed that girls be taught nothing after the age of eight. Her claim is stranger than her reputation suggests: not simply that women are equal, but that nobody has any idea what women are like, because the character they have is manufactured by their upbringing. Taught from infancy that beauty is woman's sceptre, the mind shapes itself to the body, and, roaming round its gilt cage, only seeks to adorn its prison. Far funnier and angrier than expected, and fifty years ahead of everyone in asking why women have no representatives in Parliament.
  • On Liberty John Stuart Mill (1859) · ~5-hour read · epub One principle, pushed as far as it will go: the only thing that entitles anyone to interfere with your freedom is preventing harm to somebody else. The best case ever made for free speech, an argument that individuality is not a luxury but part of what it is to be well, and a warning about a tyranny with no tyrant in it.
  • The Subjection of Women John Stuart Mill (1869) · ~4-hour read · epub The companion to On Liberty, written in 1861 and kept in a drawer for eight years. What is called the nature of women is an eminently artificial thing, because nobody has ever seen it out of chains; marriage as the law then stood was literal bondage; and the real obstacle is not any doubt about what women can do, but that most men cannot yet tolerate the idea of living with an equal.
  • On the Origin of Species Charles Darwin (1859) · ~14-hour read · epub Darwin's patient, overwhelming case for evolution by natural selection.
  • On the Various Forces of Nature Michael Faraday (1860) · ~2-hour read · epub · original text (epub) Six Christmas lectures for children on gravitation, cohesion, chemical affinity, heat, magnetism and electricity — arriving at the discovery that they are not six forces but one, each convertible into the others. With the address on lighthouse illumination and all fifty-nine original figures.
  • The Chemical History of a Candle Michael Faraday (1861) · ~3-hour read · epub · original text (epub) The most famous science lectures ever given — six Christmas lectures for children drawing the whole of chemistry out of a single burning candle, and ending with the discovery that you are a slow candle yourself. With the Lecture on Platinum and all thirty-eight original woodcuts.
  • Sound John Tyndall (1867/1875) · ~8-hour read · epub · original text (epub) The lectures that founded the tradition all the others belong to — a push handed down a row of five boys is a sound wave, a glass tube is rubbed until it shivers itself to pieces, and a gas flame ducks and roars at the hiss of the letter S. Then, at sea off the South Foreland, Tyndall discovers the invisible acoustic clouds that stop a fog signal dead on the clearest of days, and overturns everything the century believed about fog, rain and snow deadening sound. With all 187 original plates.
  • Soap Bubbles and the Forces Which Mould Them C. V. Boys (1890) · ~2-hour read · epub · original text (epub) Three Christmas lectures for children that became a classic of popular science — why a bubble is round, why a falling jet of water breaks into beads, and how a fountain can be made to sing a tune — with all sixty-nine of the original plates.
  • Waves and Ripples in Water, Air, and Aether J. A. Fleming (1902) · ~5-hour read · epub · original text (epub) Six Christmas lectures arguing that a ripple on a pond, the wake of a ship, a sung note, the colour of a petal and the signal Marconi had just pushed across the Atlantic are all one thing happening in different media — the volcano heard round the world, a flame that hears what no ear can, a prism cut from wax that bends an invisible beam. With all eighty-seven of the original plates.
  • Star-land Robert S. Ball (1889/1899) · ~6-hour read · epub · original text (epub) The warmest and funniest of the Christmas lecturers takes you from the sun to the nebulae — setting off sunward with a candle, a bullet, a penny, a poker and a flint to watch each melt in turn, measuring the sky with two children and a pair of scissors, and ending on a library fifty miles long that would still be too small to describe the visible universe. With all ninety-four of the original plates.
  • Light Visible and Invisible Silvanus P. Thompson (1897) · ~5-hour read · epub · original text (epub) Six Christmas lectures on light, given thirteen months after Röntgen announced the X-ray — and the sixth is about nothing else, quoting the discoverer's own words: is it light? “No.” Is it electricity? “Not in any known form.” What is it? “I do not know.” Along the way a magic mirror throws the pattern from its back onto the wall, a lens of air magnifies when dropped into oil, and a swan turns black when you rotate a crystal. With all 127 surviving plates, recovered from the page scans.

Fiction & Ideas

  • The Life of Benvenuto Cellini Benvenuto Cellini (1558–1567) · ~13-hour read · epub The first autobiography by an artist, and still the most unembarrassed. A Florentine goldsmith dictates his own life to a boy of thirteen while he goes on working: he sets jewels for two popes, fires the guns of the Castel Sant'Angelo through the sack of Rome, kills several men, goes to France for Francis I, breaks his leg escaping over a papal prison wall, is fed powdered diamond by his enemies (they cheat him and use a cheap stone instead), and casts the bronze Perseus with his workshop roof on fire and a fever on him, throwing two hundred pewter plates into the melt to make the metal run. From the Italian, complete: boastful, litigious, devout, funny without meaning to be, and quite certain throughout that he has been wronged.
  • Grimm's Household Tales Jacob and Wilhelm Grimm (1812–1857) · ~20-hour read · epub All two hundred tales and ten children's legends, complete — the famous ones and the two hundred nobody prints. A hedgehog beats a hare in a footrace by cheating, a soldier traps the Devil in a boot with no sole, three army surgeons take out their own eyes and hearts overnight and get them back in the wrong bodies, and two roasted chickens fly past with their breasts turned to heaven. From Margaret Hunt's 1884 version, the first complete English Grimm and still the only one out of copyright — a text that misleads more than it puzzles, where “conversation” means conduct and “prevent” means go before, so that a reader trips on “hath” and knows it, and walks straight past the words that matter.
  • The Thousand Nights and a Night Anonymous (c. 800–1400) · ~16-hour read · epub A king marries a new wife every night and has her killed every morning, until the vizier's daughter volunteers — and stops her first story in the middle, so that he lets her live one more day to hear the end. Shahrazad's frame and eighteen of the great tales: a fisherman who hauls up a sealed jar and lets out what is inside it, a hunchback whose body is passed from hand to terrified hand across a whole city, Sindbad home rich from seven voyages that each nearly killed him, a walled city in the desert with nobody alive in it, Aladdin, and Ali Baba. From Burton's 1885 translation, with the antique costume he wrapped it in taken off — none of it was ever in the Arabic.
  • The Odyssey Homer (c. 700 BC) · ~9-hour read · epub A soldier has been trying to get home for ten years, and everybody has given him up except his wife, who is holding off a houseful of men eating her out of her own halls, and his son, who was a baby when he left. Half of it is monsters: a one-eyed giant blinded with a sharpened olive stake and then outwitted by a man who says his name is Nobody, so that when the other giants ask who is hurting him he shouts that nobody is; a witch who turns the crew into pigs and leaves their own minds inside the pigs' heads; the dead crowding up to drink blood so that they can speak, and Achilles — the greatest of the Greeks — saying he would rather be a hired labourer for some poor farmer on earth than king over all of them. The other half is quieter and crueller. He comes home disguised as a beggar and is kicked and pelted in his own hall by men eating his food, and has to take it; his old dog recognises him, lying on a dung-heap, wags his tail, and dies; and his wife, who is as careful as he is, will not believe he is her husband until she has set him a trap of her own and he has walked into it. From the Greek, all twenty-four books and nothing abridged, set as prose.
  • Paradiso Dante Alighieri (c. 1308–1321) · ~4-hour read · epub The third book of the Comedy, and the one Dante thought was his best. Having put the dead in Hell and changed them on the mountain, he now has to write about happiness, which is much harder — and his answer is the opposite of grand. Heaven here is a bird on the nest waiting for dawn, a baby flinging its face towards the milk, jackdaws warming their feathers, a tailor cutting the gown to the cloth he has. It is angrier than its reputation, too: Saint Peter goes red in the face and calls the papacy a sewer, and Dante's great-great-grandfather, a crusader killed on campaign, tells him he will be exiled and will find out how salt another man's bread tastes. At the top, after thirty-three cantos of insisting he cannot say it, he sees three circles of three colours, gives up, and ends the poem on a wheel turning evenly. From the Italian, all thirty-three cantos and all 4,758 lines, tercet for tercet, and without the rhyme.
  • Purgatorio Dante Alighieri (c. 1308–1320) · ~4-hour read · epub The second book of the Comedy, and the one people who have read only the Inferno are least prepared for. Everyone on this mountain is going to be saved, knows it, and is being changed on the way, so the dead here embrace, apologise, tease and ask after old friends. A musician sings a love song of Dante's own and the whole crowd stops to listen until an old man shouts at them to get moving; a Florentine sits behind a rock making fun of the climb; a Roman poet meets the poet who converted him without ever knowing it. It is also the only part of the poem with weather and a clock — three nights, three dawn dreams, and a sun whose position Dante keeps checking. From the Italian, all thirty-three cantos and all 4,755 lines, tercet for tercet, and without the rhyme.
  • Inferno Dante Alighieri (c. 1308–1320) · ~4-hour read · epub A man of thirty-five, lost in a dark wood, is led down through the whole of Hell by the ghost of a dead poet. What makes it unlike any other vision of the afterlife is that it is full of people the author knew: popes, neighbours, bankers, a former teacher, the man who sold his city — all named, still arguing about Florentine politics, still furious, still certain they were in the right. Francesca blames the book she and her lover were reading; Ulysses tells how he sailed past Gibraltar and drowned; Ugolino describes eight days locked in a tower with his sons and then stops, on the one line nobody has ever settled. From the Italian, all thirty-four cantos and all 4,720 lines, tercet for tercet — and without the rhyme, which cannot cross into English without the padding this poem exists to avoid.
  • Don Quixote Miguel de Cervantes (1605–1615) · ~31-hour read · epub The first modern novel, complete and unabridged — both volumes, all 126 chapters, retold fresh from Cervantes' Spanish. A dry country gentleman reads so many books of chivalry that his wits dry up with them, and rides out with a farm labourer who talks in proverbs to right the wrongs of the world: windmills, a barber's basin worn as a helmet, a puppet theatre cut to pieces, an island governorship that lasts ten days, and a knight in white armour on a Barcelona beach. The funniest book ever written, and the saddest ending in fiction, at full length and full speed — with all the interpolated tales that abridgements cut.
  • Pillow Problems Lewis Carroll (1893) · ~2-hour read · epub Seventy-two problems the author of Alice worked out in his head, in bed, in the dark — five beggars and a sack of pennies, a bag holding one counter that is either black or white, a wine company whose salesman is quietly robbing it. And an Introduction explaining that he does not recommend mathematics for insomnia, which he has never had, but against “the harassing thoughts that are apt to invade a wholly-unoccupied mind” — then saying, without flinching, exactly which thoughts he means.
  • Symbolic Logic Lewis Carroll (1896) · ~5-hour read · epub The author of Alice teaching formal logic to children of twelve and fourteen, as a game — a squared board, red counters for “something is here” and grey for “nothing is”, and 308 diagrams showing every move. Cats understand French, a lame puppy would not say thank you if you offered to lend it a skipping-rope, and a friend forming a club on strictly logical principles announces that some of the members are millionaires, though the club has no members at all.
  • Euclid and His Modern Rivals Lewis Carroll (1879/1885) · ~5-hour read · epub The author of Alice, in his day job as an Oxford mathematics lecturer, puts every geometry textbook of the age on trial — as a farce in four acts. Minos, a college examiner, falls asleep over his marking at midnight and is visited first by the ghost of Euclid and then by Herr Niemand, Mr. Nobody, a German professor who appears as counsel for each of thirteen modern manuals in turn and loses every case. The argument underneath is entirely serious: how do you know that two lines will ever meet? Niemand's evasions get funnier as his position gets worse, and somewhere in the middle he orders a hundred-and-seventy-letter Greek dish in an Egyptian restaurant.
  • A Tangled Tale Lewis Carroll (1885) · ~2-hour read · epub Ten comic stories with a mathematical puzzle hidden in each — and then, a month later, Carroll's own review of the answers his readers sent in, sorted into a Class List by pseudonym and taken apart with enormous and affectionate relish. The recreational-mathematics companion to the Royal Institution lectures, with all nine of the original plates.
  • The Pilgrim's Progress John Bunyan (1678/1684) · ~8-hour read · epub The most famous journey in English — the burden that falls off by itself at the cross, the Slough of Despond, Vanity Fair, and the key called Promise that was in his pocket the whole time. Both parts complete: Christian's road, walked alone and in terror, and then Christiana's, walked six years later with a guide, four sons and a great many meals, on which the weak all get across.
  • Metamorphoses Ovid (8 CE) · ~11-hour read · epub The great storybook of classical mythology — the making of the world, Apollo and Daphne, Phaethon, Narcissus, Pyramus and Thisbe, Daedalus and Icarus, Orpheus, Pygmalion, Midas, and hundreds more — all fifteen books complete, in vivid modern prose translated fresh from Ovid's Latin.
  • True History Lucian of Samosata (c. 175 AD) · ~1-hour read · epub The first science fiction story: a voyage to the moon, war between planets, and a narrator who swears that he lies.
  • The Decameron Giovanni Boccaccio (1353) · ~20-hour read · epub Ten friends flee the plague and tell a hundred stories — bawdy, sly, and humane.
  • Candide, or Optimism Voltaire (1759) · ~2-hour read · epub Voltaire's whirlwind satire of optimism in a broken world.
  • Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Sea Jules Verne (1870) · ~12-hour read · epub Captain Nemo's submarine Nautilus carries three captives on a voyage of wonder and captivity beneath every sea — the complete novel, translated fresh from Verne's French, not the abridged Victorian version.
  • Around the World in Eighty Days Jules Verne (1872) · ~6-hour read · epub The imperturbable Phileas Fogg wagers £20,000 that he can circle the globe in eighty days — Verne's funniest, fastest adventure, complete and fresh from the French, with a final trick of the calendar.
  • A Journey to the Center of the Earth Jules Verne (1864) · ~6-hour read · epub A coded parchment, a dead Icelandic volcano, and a plunge miles down into a lost world of underground oceans and prehistoric monsters — Verne's purest wonder-and-descent adventure, complete and fresh from the French, not the mangled Victorian version.
  • Flatland: A Romance of Many Dimensions Edwin A. Abbott (1884) · ~2-hour read · epub Geometry as social satire: a square discovers the third dimension.
