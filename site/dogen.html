Fukanzazengi: Universal Recommendation for Zazen (1227)

Now, when you look into it: the way is basically complete and reaches everywhere. What could it have to do with practising, or with getting anywhere? The vehicle carries itself freely. Why spend any effort on it? The whole of it is far beyond the dust of the world. Who could believe in a method for wiping the dust away? It is never apart from where you are standing right now. What is the use of going off somewhere to practise?

And yet. Let there be a hair's breadth of difference and it is the gap between heaven and earth. Let liking and disliking so much as start up, and you lose the mind of it in the confusion.

Suppose you are proud of your understanding and rich in realisation, so that you have got hold of a flash of insight; suppose you have found the way and cleared your mind, and your ambition goes up to strike the sky; suppose you are strolling about in the borderlands of the entrance to it. You will still be missing, more or less, the living road that leads out.

Look at the one in the Jeta Grove, who was born knowing. You can still see the marks of the six years he sat there. Look at the one at Shaolin, who passed the seal of the mind on. The report of the nine years he spent facing a wall is still going about. The old saints were like that. Why will people now not do the work?

So: stop chasing after words and running down phrases. Learn the step backwards, the one that turns your light inward and lets it shine on you. Body and mind will drop away of themselves, and your original face will be there in front of you. If you want to get that, get on with that.

For sitting, a quiet room is best. Eat and drink moderately. Put down all your involvements. Let the ten thousand things rest. Do not think good or evil. Do not concern yourself with right or wrong. Stop the wheels of mind, intellect and consciousness turning; stop the measurements of memory, imagination and contemplation. Have no design on becoming a buddha. This has nothing whatever to do with sitting or lying down.

At your usual sitting place, spread a thick mat and put a round cushion on top of it. Sit either in the full lotus or the half lotus. For the full lotus, first put your right foot on your left thigh, then your left foot on your right thigh. For the half lotus, simply press your left foot onto your right thigh. Loosen your robe and belt and get them straight and even.

Then put your right hand on your left foot, and your left palm on your right palm, with the tips of the two thumbs just touching. Sit upright in the correct posture. Do not lean to the left, or tilt to the right, or bend forward, or arch backwards. The ears should be in line with the shoulders, and the nose in line with the navel. Rest your tongue on the roof of your mouth, with the lips and teeth closed. The eyes should always stay open. Breathe faintly through the nose.

Once the body is settled, take one breath out, sway left and right, and then sit immovably, like a mountain.

Think of the thing that does not think. How do you think of the thing that does not think? Beyond thinking. This is the essential art of sitting.

The sitting I am talking about is not learning meditation. It is simply the dharma-gate of ease and joy. It is the practice-and-realisation of a fully accomplished awakening. The koan is realised in the present. Traps and cages have never reached it. If you get hold of this meaning, you are a dragon that has got to water, a tiger that has got to its mountain. Know that the true dharma is appearing of itself, and dullness and distraction have already been knocked away.

When you get up from sitting, move the body slowly and stand up calmly. Do not be abrupt about it.

I have seen it: going beyond the ordinary and beyond the holy, dying sitting or dying standing — these depend entirely on this power. And more than that: the pivotal moments that turn on a finger, a pole, a needle, a mallet; the confirmations that come with a whisk, a fist, a staff, a shout — none of these have ever been graspable by thinking and discrimination. How could they be known by supernormal powers, or by practice and realisation? They may be conduct beyond sound and form. Are they not the standard that comes before knowing and seeing?

That being so, it makes no difference whether you are clever or stupid, and there is no sorting people into the sharp and the dull. Single-minded effort is itself the practice of the way. Practice-and-realisation is by nature undefiled. Going towards it is simply an ordinary matter.

In general, in this world and in other quarters, in India and in the East alike, all hold the buddha-seal equally and each gives free rein to its own style; but they devote themselves solely to sitting, and are held fast by the immovable. Though we speak of ten thousand distinctions and a thousand differences, they simply take up Zen and practise the way.

Why abandon the sitting platform of your own house and go wandering uselessly in the dusty regions of other countries? Put one foot wrong and you go straight past what is right in front of you.

You have already got hold of the pivot of a human body. Do not spend your time for nothing. You are holding and maintaining the essential working of the buddha way. Who would idly enjoy a spark struck off a flint? On top of which: form and substance are like a drop of dew on the grass, and fate is like a flash of lightning. In a moment they are empty; in an instant they are lost.

I beg of you, distinguished friends in the study of this: do not, from long familiarity with feeling the elephant, be suspicious of the real dragon. Go forward on the road that points straight at the thing itself. Honour the person who is beyond study and beyond doing. Fall into step with the awakening of buddha after buddha. Take up in succession the samadhi of patriarch after patriarch. Behave like this for a long time and you will be like this. The treasure house will open of itself, and you will use it as you please.

Gakudo Yojinshu: Points to Watch in Studying the Way (1234)

One: You Must Raise the Thought of Awakening

The thought of awakening goes by many names, and one mind is one of them. The patriarch Nāgārjuna said: the mind that simply observes the world arising and passing away, the mind that observes impermanence, is also called the thought of awakening. So may we take that mind, provisionally, as the thought of awakening?

Truly, when you are observing impermanence, the mind of self does not come up, and thoughts of name and gain do not start. You are afraid of how fast the light goes, and so you practise the way as though you were putting out a fire in your hair. You look at how little your body and your life can be relied on, and so you make an effort, like the man who stood on tiptoe. Though you hear the praising voices of the kinnara and the kalavinka, they are the evening wind brushing your ear. Though you see the exquisite faces of Maoqiang and Xishi, they are the morning dew across your eyes. Once you are free of the bindings of sound and form, you naturally fall into agreement with what the mind of the way is about.

In the past and now, there have been men who had heard little and men who had seen little, and most of them have fallen into the pit of name and gain and lost the life of the buddha way for good. It is pitiable. It is a shame. You must know this. Though a man may read the fine scriptures of the provisional and the real teachings, though he may handle and pass on the texts of the exoteric and esoteric schools, if he has not thrown away name and gain, this is not to be called raising the thought of awakening.

Some say: the thought of awakening is the mind of unsurpassed perfect awakening, and must not be tied to reputation and profit. Some say: it is the contemplation of three thousand worlds in a single thought. Some say: it is the dharma-gate in which not a single thought arises. Some say: it is the mind that enters the realm of buddhas.

People like this do not know what the thought of awakening is. They slander it carelessly. They are far, far away from the buddha way.

Try turning and looking at your own self, at your own name and gain, at the mind you have now. Has it fused with the nature and the characteristics of three thousand worlds in one thought? Has it realised the dharma-gate in which not one thought arises? Or is there nothing there but the deluded craving for name and the love of gain, and no mind of the way to be had at all?

The saints of old who gained the way and gained the dharma had their skilful means for mixing with the dust of the world, but they had no crooked thoughts of name and gain. They did not even cling to the dharma — how much less to the world.

The thought of awakening I am speaking of is the one described above: the mind that observes impermanence. It is not at all what those madmen are pointing at. The thought in which nothing arises, the characteristics of the three thousand worlds — these are excellent practices that come after the thought of awakening has been raised. They are not to be treated carelessly. Only forget the self for a while, and practise quietly: that is close kin to the thought of awakening.

The sixty-two views all take the self as their root. So when the view of a self comes up, sit quietly and look into it. What is the root of everything inside and outside this body of mine, now? Body, hair and skin came from my parents, from two drops, red and white; from beginning to end they are empty, so they are not the self. Mind, intellect, consciousness and understanding are tied to the length of a life; one breath in and out, and in the end what are they? So they are not the self. There is nothing here or there that can be held on to.

The deluded hold on. The awakened let go. And then people work out a self that has no self, and hold on to an arising that does not arise; they do not do the practice of the buddha way, which can be done, and they do not cut off worldly feeling, which can be cut off; they are disgusted by the real dharma and go looking for a false one. Is that not a mistake?

Two: Having Seen and Heard the True Dharma, You Must Practise It

A loyal minister offers one word, and it can have the force of turning heaven round. The buddhas and patriarchs give out a single word, and there is nobody whose mind is not turned by it. But unless a ruler is enlightened, there is no room in him for loyal advice; and unless a man stands out from the crowd, there is no room in him for the words of the buddhas. If the mind is not turned, the flow of birth and death has not been cut off. If loyal advice finds no room, the virtuous government of the country has not been put into practice.

Three: The Buddha Way Must Be Entered Through Practice

In the world they say: study, and the salary is in it. The Buddha said: practise, and realisation is in it. Nobody has ever heard of a man who got a salary without studying, or realisation without practising. Whether your practice belongs to faith or to dharma, whether it is sudden or gradual, you have to wait on practice before you go beyond into realisation. Whether your study is shallow or deep, quick or slow, you have to pile study up before you take a salary. This is not a matter of whether the ruler favours you or not, or whether fortune is with you or not.

If a salary could be had without study, who would hand down the way in which the former kings ordered and settled the world? If realisation could be had without practice, who would understand the Tathāgata's dharma of delusion and awakening? You should know that you set up your practice in the middle of delusion and get realisation before awakening. Just then you first learn that the raft and the boat were last night's dream, and you cut off for good the old view that took a rope for a snake.

This is not something the Buddha forces on you. It is your own capacity, turning about.

And besides: what practice brings on is realisation. The treasure of your own house does not come from outside. What realisation puts to work is practice. How could the tracks across the ground of the mind be turned back?

And yet, if you turn the eye of realisation round and look back at the ground of practice, there is not a speck of dust in the eye, and what you will see is white clouds for ten thousand miles. If you lift the foot of practice and try it against the steps of realisation, there is not a mote of dust under it, and what you tread is the gap between heaven and earth. At this point you step backwards, and jump into the ground of the buddhas.

Written on the ninth day of the third month, the second year of Tenpuku.

Four: Do Not Practise the Buddha-Dharma With a Mind That Seeks Gain

In practising the buddha-dharma you must receive the true instruction of one who has gone before you, and not use your own private ideas. Besides, the buddha-dharma cannot be got with a mind, and it cannot be got without a mind. If the mind you are working with does not agree with the way, body and mind will never be at peace. When body and mind are not at peace, they are not at ease. When they are not at ease, thorns grow up over the way of realisation.

So how do you conduct a practice that agrees with the way? The mind does not pick things up and put them down; the mind has no name and gain in it.

The practice of the buddha-dharma is not something you do for other people. In the world as it is now, the minds of people who practise the buddha-dharma are far, far from the way. If something is admired, they practise it, even knowing it is not the way. If it is not respected and praised, they abandon it and will not practise it, even knowing it is the true way. How painful.

Try quieting the mind and looking into it. Is this conduct of the mind the buddha-dharma or not? It is shameful. It is shameful. The eye of the saints is on it.

A man who practises the buddha-dharma does not do it even for himself, let alone for reputation and profit. He does it only for the buddha-dharma's sake.

The compassion of the buddhas, their pity for living beings, is not for their own sake and not for the sake of others. It is simply what the buddha-dharma always is. Do you not see it in small insects and animals, rearing their young? Body and mind put to hardship, the whole management of it bitter labour, and in the end they raise them — and for the parents there is nothing in it at all. And still the compassion of a creature thinking of its young is like that, even in something so small. It resembles the buddhas thinking of living beings.

The wonderful dharma of the buddhas is not compassion alone. It appears everywhere, through every gate, and this is the root of all of it. Since we are already the children of buddhas, why not fall in with the way the buddhas do things?

A practitioner must not think of practising the buddha-dharma for his own sake. He must not practise it for name and gain, or to get a reward, or to get miraculous powers. He must practise the buddha-dharma only for the buddha-dharma. That is the way.

Five: In Taking Up Zen and Studying the Way, You Must Seek a True Teacher

A man of old said: if the raising of the thought is not correct, the ten thousand practices are all wasted. That is true. Whether your practice of the way is right or wrong depends on your guide.

The student is like good timber; the teacher is like a carpenter. However good the timber, without a good workman its beauty never appears. However crooked the wood, in skilful hands its excellence shows at once. You can tell from this that whether the awakening is true or false follows from whether the teacher is true or false.

But in our country there has never yet been a true teacher. How do I know? Look at what they say and examine it — as you would trace a stream to find its source. The teachers of our country from long ago have compiled books, instructed disciples and given teaching to gods and men. But their words are green; their language is not ripe. They have not reached the top of the ground of study, so how could they get anywhere near realisation? They have only handed down written words and made people recite names. Day and night they count another man's treasure, and have not half a coin of their own. The blame for this lies with the old teachers.

Some direct people to seek awakening outside the mind. Some teach people to pray for rebirth in another land. Confusion starts here, and crooked thinking has its office here.

Even if you are given good medicine, if you are not taught how to take it, it makes you iller than poison would. In our country from of old there has been, as it were, nobody to give out good medicine, and no teacher to take the poison out of it. That is why sickness in life is hard to get rid of, and why we cannot escape old age and death. It is all the fault of the teachers, and no fault of the students at all.

Why do I say so? Because those who set up as teachers make people abandon the root and chase the branches. Before they have got their own understanding settled, they attend only to their own opinions, and teach others carelessly, and drag them down into wrong states. It is pitiable that the teachers do not know they are in error themselves — so how are the disciples going to work out what is right and wrong? It is a sad thing.

In this remote little country the buddha-dharma has not spread, and a true teacher has not appeared. If you want to study the unsurpassed buddha way, you should go far off and consult those who know in the land of Song. You should look, far away, for the living road that is outside the mind. If you cannot get a true teacher, better not to study at all.

A true teacher is not a question of being old and long-established. He is one who has made the true dharma clear and received the seal of a true teacher's confirmation. Letters do not come first; understanding does not come first. He has a capacity outside the ordinary measure and a will that goes past the usual limits; he is not held by his own views; he does not get stuck in his feelings and his knowledge; his conduct and his understanding match. That is a true teacher.

Six: What You Should Know in Taking Up Zen

Taking up Zen and studying the way is the great matter of a lifetime. It must not be treated lightly, and certainly not casually. Men of old cut off an arm and cut off fingers; those are fine traces left in China. Long ago the Buddha left his house and gave up his country; those are the tracks left by the practice of the way.

People now say: practise the practice that is easy to do. That is badly said and thoroughly out of keeping with the buddha way. If you attend to one thing only in that spirit, then even lying down is laziness. Be lazy in one thing and you will be lazy in ten thousand. A man who likes what is easy is telling you himself that he is no vessel for the way.

Besides: the dharma that is current in the world today is the dharma that our great teacher Shakyamuni got only after immeasurable ages of difficult practice and bitter practice. If the source was like that, how could the streams running out of it be easy?

You who love the way — do not set your mind on easy practice. If you look for easy practice you will certainly not reach the real ground, and you will certainly not arrive at the place where the treasure is. Men of old had great capacity, and even they called it hard. You should understand from that how deep and how great the buddha way is. If the buddha way were easy to practise by its nature, the men of great capacity in the past would not have called it hard to practise and hard to understand.

Compare people now with the people of old: we do not come to one hair of nine oxen. With these shallow roots and this thin knowledge, even if we drove ourselves and tried to do the hard practice, we could not come up to the easy practice and easy understanding of the ancients.

So what is this dharma of easy understanding and easy practice that people now like so much? It is no longer a worldly dharma. Neither is it the buddha-dharma. It does not even reach the practice of Māra the Evil One. It does not even reach the practice of the outside ways or the two vehicles. It might be called the extremity of ordinary delusion. Though it makes a show of getting free, it is only endless turning in the wheel again.

Look at it: to break your bones and crush your marrow — is that so hard? Bringing the mind under control and handling it is harder. To keep the long fast and the pure practice — is that so hard? Bringing the body under control and conducting it is harder. If pulverising the bones were what counted, there have been many who could endure it from of old, and very few who got the dharma. If keeping the fast were what counted, there have been many from long ago, and very few who awoke to the way. This is because bringing the mind under control is so extremely difficult.

Cleverness does not come first. Learned understanding does not come first. Mind, intellect and consciousness do not come first. Memory, imagination and contemplation do not come first. Without using any of these at all, you bring body and mind into order and enter the buddha way. Old Shakyamuni said: Avalokiteśvara entered the stream and forgot what was known. That is the meaning of it. The two characteristics, moving and still, clearly do not arise. That is the order of it.

If the buddha way could be entered by cleverness and wide understanding, the senior monk Shenxiu would have been the man for it. If the buddha way could be refused to the plain-bodied and the lowly, how would the high patriarch of Caoxi have dared? It is clear from this that the transmission of the dharma of the buddha way lies outside cleverness and wide understanding.

Search it out, and look, and take part. It does not matter that you are old and failing; it does not matter that you are a child or in your prime. Zhaozhou began his practice past sixty, and he became the hero of the patriarchs' seat. The girl Zheng studied a long time from thirteen, and she stood out in the monastery. The power of the buddha-dharma appears according to whether you put yourself into it or not; it divides according to whether you practise or do not. Those who have long studied in the doctrinal schools, or who have old skill in worldly texts, may all come to the Zen gate. There are many examples. Huisi of Nanyue was a man of many talents, and still he went to study under Bodhidharma's line. Xuanjue of Yongjia was an outstanding man, and he studied under the great mirror Huineng, and made the dharma clear and got the way. This is the strength of studying with a teacher.

But when you put your questions to a master of the school, listen to what the teacher says and do not match it against your own views. If you match it against your own views you will not get the teacher's dharma. When you study with a teacher and hear the dharma, clean out body and mind, quiet the eyes and ears, and simply take in the teacher's dharma without mixing any other thought into it. Body and mind as one thing, like water poured from one vessel into another. If you can do that, then you get the teacher's dharma.

Now there are foolish, stupid people who either memorise texts or store up what they have heard before, and put that alongside the teacher's dharma. In that case what they have is only their own views and the old words, and the teacher's words never fit them. There is another sort who put their own views first and then open the scriptures, and hold on to a phrase or two and take that for the buddha-dharma. Afterwards, when they go to a clear-eyed teacher and a master of the school and hear the dharma, if it matches their own views they call it right, and if it does not agree with their old ideas they call it wrong. They do not know the method of throwing off what is false — so how are they going to climb the road back to what is true? They will still be deluded after ages as many as the sands. It is greatly to be pitied.

Students should know that the buddha way lies outside thinking, discrimination, guessing, contemplation, perception and intellectual understanding. If it lay within those, then since we have been in the middle of them from birth and have always played with them, why do we still not perceive the buddha way now? A person studying the way must not use thinking and discrimination and the rest. Take your own body and examine it while carrying thinking and the rest about with you, and this becomes clear as in a bright mirror.

The gate you go in by is fully known to a master of the school who has got the dharma. It is not something the teachers of the written word can reach.

Written on the Qingming day of the year of the horse, Tenpuku.

Seven: Those Who Want Release Must Take Up Zen

The buddha-dharma is superior to all the other ways, and that is why people seek it. When the Tathāgata was in the world there were not two teachings at all and not two teachers at all. The great teacher Shakyamuni drew living beings in by unsurpassed awakening and nothing else. Since Kāśyapa received the treasury of the true dharma eye, there have been twenty-eight generations in India and six in China, and on to the patriarchs of the five houses, each receiving it in direct succession, with no break in it. So from the Putong era of the Liang onwards, from the monks up to the kings and ministers, there is nobody of any standing who has not come over to it.

Truly, what you ought to love as superior is what is superior. You should not be like the Duke of She, who loved dragons.

In China and the countries east of it, the net of doctrinal writings is spread over the sea and lies all across the mountains. It lies across the mountains but has no mind of the clouds; it is spread over the sea but has a withered mind for the waves. Fools relish it — as though a man picked up a fish's eye and took it for a pearl. The deluded play with it — as though a man hid away a stone from Yan and honoured it as jade. Many fall into the pit of Māra and injure themselves repeatedly.

It is pitiable that in a remote and backward place a crooked wind blows easily and the true dharma goes about with difficulty. Yet China has already come over to the true dharma of the buddhas, while in our country and in Koryŏ the true dharma has not spread. Why is that? Why? In Koryŏ at least they hear the name of the true dharma. In our country it has not even been heard. This is because the teachers who went to China in the past all got stuck in the doctrinal net. Though they brought back buddhist books, it is as if they had forgotten the buddha-dharma. What is the good of it? In the end the work of it came to nothing. And this is because they did not know how the study of the way is actually done. It is pitiable — a whole human life spent in labour for nothing.

Now: when you study the buddha way and first come in at the gate, you hear the teaching of one who knows and practise as you are taught. At that time there is something you should understand, namely that the dharma turns me, and I turn the dharma. When I am able to turn the dharma, I am strong and the dharma is weak. When the dharma turns me in its turn, the dharma is strong and I am weak. The buddha-dharma has always had these two phases. Nobody who is not a true heir has ever known it. Nobody who is not a patch-robed monk has so much as heard the name of it. Without knowing how this actually works, the study of the way cannot be sorted out, and how is right to be distinguished from wrong?

People who take up Zen and study the way now have this handed to them directly, and so they do not go wrong. There is no other gate. A person who longs for the buddha way cannot come to know the true way except by taking up Zen.

Eight: The Conduct of a Zen Monk

From the buddhas and the patriarchs down, it has been directly pointed at and singly transmitted. Four and seven in the west, six generations in the east — not a thread or a hair added, and not one speck broken. The robe came down to Caoxi, and the dharma went round the worlds of sand. At that time the treasury of the true dharma eye of the Tathāgata flourished in the great Tang.

As for what this dharma is: you cannot grope for it and you cannot go looking for it. Where it is seen, knowing is lost; when it is got, it goes beyond mind. He lost his face at Huangmei. He cut off his arm at Shaoshi. He got the marrow, turned his mind over, and bought elegance. He made his bow, stepped back, and fell into the advantage of it. And yet in mind and in body there is no dwelling and no attachment, no staying and no getting stuck.

A monk asked Zhaozhou: does a dog have buddha-nature or not? Zhaozhou said: no.

Can you take a measurement on top of that word "no"? Can you get stuck on it? There is no handle to it anywhere. Please try letting go. Just let go and look.

What are body and mind? What is your conduct? What is birth and death? What is the buddha-dharma? What is the dharma of the world? Mountains and rivers and the great earth, people and animals and houses — in the end, what are they?

Look, and go on looking. Of themselves the two characteristics, moving and still, clearly do not arise. When they do not arise, this is not mere blankness. There is nobody to confirm it, and there are a great many who mistake it.

Those who take up Zen — you have got half the road. Do not refuse the whole road. I pray you. I pray you.

Nine: You Must Practise Facing the Way

A man of stature who studies the way must first know whether his facing of the way is correct or not. Shakyamuni, the tamer, sat under the tree of awakening, saw the morning star, and all at once was suddenly awakened to the unsurpassed way. What he was awakened to is not something the hearers and the solitary buddhas can reach. The Buddha awoke by himself, and buddha handed it on to buddha, and it has not been cut off down to now. Can one who has this awakening be anything but a buddha?

Facing the way means understanding the whole extent of the buddha way. It means making clear what the buddha way looks like. The buddha way is under everybody's heels. Be obstructed by the way, and it is clear where you are standing. Be obstructed by awakening, and the person is fully complete.

And so, even if you raise a complete understanding, you still come down in a half awakening — and that is the elegance of facing the way.

People studying the way now have not sorted out whether the way is open or blocked, and are very fond of having proofs of it. Who is it that does not go wrong? They leave their father and run off, they abandon the treasure and go wandering. Though he was the only son of a rich man, he spent a long time as a hired labourer, a low sort of person. There are good reasons for it.

A person studying the way seeks to be obstructed by the way. To be obstructed by the way is to lose the traces of awakening. One who practises the buddha way must first have faith in the buddha way. One who has faith in the buddha way must have faith in himself: that he is originally in the way, not confused, not upside down, without increase or decrease, and without error. Raise a faith like this, make a way like this clear, and practise accordingly — that is the ground of studying the way.

The rule for doing it is to sit and cut off the root of the intellect, so that it does not go down the road of intellectual understanding. That is a skilful means for drawing in beginners. After that, dropping away body and mind, and putting down both delusion and awakening, is the second stage.

Generally speaking, people who have faith that they themselves are in the buddha way are the hardest of all to find. If you have correct faith that you are in the way, then naturally you will understand whether the great way is open or blocked, and you will know how delusion and awakening come about.

Try sitting and cutting off the root of the intellect. Eight or nine out of ten will suddenly get to see the way.

Ten: Taking Hold of It Directly

In sorting out body and mind there are two ways: studying with a teacher and hearing the dharma, and the effort of sitting. Hearing the dharma moves about in mind and consciousness. Sitting is a matter of practice and realisation, right and left of you. So in entering the buddha way you must not throw either of them away when you take hold.

Everybody has a body and a mind. In what they do there is bound to be strength and weakness, boldness and dullness. It moves, and it takes a form. To realise buddhahood directly with this body and this mind is what taking hold means. Not to turn the body and mind you have always had into something else, but simply to follow it into realisation, is what is meant by directly. It is what is meant by taking hold. Because you simply follow it, this is not the old view. Because you simply take hold, this is not a new nest.

Tenzo Kyokun: Instructions for the Cook (1237)

From of old the house of the buddhas has had six officers. They are all children of the Buddha, and together they do the Buddha's work. Among them the cook is the one whose office is to look after the food for the assembly.

The Rules of the Zen Monastery say: he exists in order to make offering to the assembly. From ancient times the post has been filled by teachers who had the mind of the way and by men of high resolve who had raised the aspiration. It is one whole piece of practising the way. If a man has no mind for the way, he will labour and suffer over it and in the end get nothing from it.

The Rules say: put the mind of the way to work; change what you do as the occasion changes; and let the assembly be at ease and get the good of it. In the past Guishan and Dongshan held this office, and so did many other great ancestors. It is not the same thing as being a cook or a steward in the world.

While I was in China, I used my free days to ask former officers and old hands about it, and they told me a little of what they had seen and heard. What they said is the very marrow left to us by the buddhas and ancestors of old who had the way.

You should read the Rules of the Zen Monastery through until you know them, and then listen to the old hands on the details.

Here is a day and a night of the work.

After the midday meal, go to the prior and the steward and draw the materials for tomorrow's rice and gruel — the rice, the vegetables and the rest. Once you have them, guard them as you would your own eyes. Chan master Yong of Baoning said: guard the permanent property of the monastery as your own eyes. Handle them with the same respect you would show food set before the emperor. Raw or cooked, keep that attitude towards all of it.

Then the officers meet in the office and settle what tomorrow will be: what flavours, what vegetables, what kind of gruel. The Rules say that the materials drawn and the number of dishes for the rice and the gruel are to be settled beforehand with the officers of the storehouse — the officers being the prior, the steward, the assistant, the rector, the cook and the labour steward. When the dishes are settled, write them out and post them at the abbot's quarters and outside the monks' hall.

Then set about preparing the next morning's gruel. Wash the rice and prepare the vegetables with your own hands and your own eyes on them, working with diligence and a sincere mind. Do not for one instant be slack or careless. Do not attend to one thing and let another go. Do not let a single drop out of the ocean of merit; do not fail to add a single grain of dust to the mountain of good.

The Rules say: if the six tastes are not right and the three virtues are not there, the cook is not serving the assembly properly.

Watch the rice, and watch for the grit in it. Look carefully, again and again, and do not let your mind wander from it. Then the three virtues will be complete of themselves and the six tastes will all be there.

When Xuefeng was cook under Dongshan, he was washing rice one day. Dongshan asked him: do you strain out the sand and keep the rice, or strain out the rice and keep the sand?

Xuefeng said: I throw out sand and rice together.

Dongshan said: then what will the assembly eat?

Xuefeng turned the basin over.

Dongshan said: some day you will meet another man, and he will settle it for you.

That is how the men of high resolve of the old days who had the way did this work — with their own hands, and with exactness. How can those of us who come later be casual about it? The old ones used to say: for the cook, the mind of the way turns on rolling up his sleeves.

If any grit gets thrown out with the rice, pick it out yourself. The Rules say: when you are preparing food, look at it yourself, and it will be clean of itself.

Do not throw away the white water the rice was washed in. In the old days they used a cloth bag to strain it. Put the water for the gruel into the pot, and then look after it: do not let mice get at it or let idle passers-by handle it.

While the gruel is cooking, see to the vegetables, and then to the other dishes. Take the bowls and buckets and the utensils that will be used at the midday meal today, wash them with real care until they are clean, and put them all where they belong. What belongs high, put high; what belongs low, put low. High things settle at the high level, low things at the low. Tongs and ladles, everything of that kind — bring them all together, handle them with a true mind, and put them down gently.

Then attend to the materials for tomorrow's midday meal. Pick out any weevils, green beans, chaff, dust, grit or stones in the rice. While you are picking over the rice and the vegetables, the attendants chant a sutra and dedicate it to the god of the stove.

Then see to the vegetables and the rest of the material, according to what the storehouse has issued. Never mind whether it is much or little; never mind whether it is coarse or fine. Only prepare it with sincerity. Above all do not let your face show anything or your mouth say anything about how much or how little of it there is.

Through the whole day and the whole night, let things come to your mind and let your mind go back to them. Be one with the thing, and put the same effort into the way. Before midnight, look after what is wanted at dawn; after midnight, get on with making the gruel.

When the gruel is finished for the day, wash the pot, steam the rice and prepare the soup. While the rice for the midday meal is soaking, the cook is not to leave the sink. Watch it with a clear eye and do not waste a single grain. Wash it properly, put it in the pot, light the fire and steam it.

There is an old saying: treat the pot as your own head, and treat the water as your own life.

When the rice is steamed, put it in the basket and then into the tub and set it on the stand, and see to the vegetables and the soup. The cook is to be there in person while the rice is steaming and while the soup is being prepared. He may set the attendants, or the servants, or the fire-tender to handle the utensils. In the big monasteries lately there are rice-officers and soup-officers, but they work under the cook. In the old days there were no such officers; the cook managed the whole of it.

In handling any material at all, do not look at it with ordinary eyes and do not think about it with ordinary feelings. Take up a single blade of grass and build a sanctuary for the treasure-king; enter a single mote of dust and turn the great wheel of the dharma.

So when you make a soup out of the coarsest greens, do not let a mind of dislike or contempt or carelessness come up. When you make a soup rich with cream, do not let a mind of delight and pleasure come up. If you have no attachment, how can you have any aversion? So although you are handling something coarse, be in no way slack about it; although you come across something fine, be all the more diligent with it.

Above all, do not change your mind because the thing has changed, and do not change your words because of who is in front of you. That is not a man of the way.

Rouse your resolve and give it your whole mind, hoping to be cleaner than the men of old, more exact than those who went before. The way to do this is to make the working of the mind into the substance of the thing: they of old did it with three coins, and today we do the same with three coins and make a soup rich with cream.

That is hard to do. Why? Because now and then are utterly different, and heaven and earth lie between; how could we stand shoulder to shoulder with them? And yet, when you go about it closely and exactly, there is a way of seeing that looks down on the very principle of the ancients. That this is so is not yet clear to you only because your thoughts fly about like a wild horse and your feelings gallop off like a monkey in the trees. Let the monkey and the horse once take a step back and turn their light inward, and it all becomes a single piece. This is the means by which you, who are turned about by things, can turn things about instead.

Handle the food, then, and make it clean, and do not lose the one eye. Take up a single stalk of greens and make it into the sixteen-foot golden body; take the sixteen-foot golden body and make it into a single stalk of greens. This is the working of miracles and the work of transformation; it is the Buddha's work and it benefits living beings.

When the food is ready, close your eyes and count carefully. How many are on the platforms in the hall; how many are former officers and old hands; how many are living apart; how many are pensioners and old men retired; how many are on leave; how many wandering monks are passing through; how many leather bags there are inside. Go over it and go over it again. If there is the smallest doubt in it, ask the hall office, or the head monks, or the officers, and clear the doubt away.

Eat one grain of rice and add one grain of rice. Divide one grain and you get two half grains, three parts, four parts, one half, two halves. Add those two half grains together and they make one grain again. Add nine parts, and how many parts are over? Take in nine parts now, and see how many he has. Eat one grain of Luling rice and you see Guishan; add one grain of Luling rice and you see the water buffalo. The water buffalo eats Guishan; Guishan pastures the water buffalo.

Have I got the measure of it? Have you got the reckoning of it? Not until you have gone over it and checked it and it is perfectly clear. Then speak as the moment asks, and say it straight to the man in front of you.

Do this work as though the second and third day were the first, and do not forget it for a moment.

When a donor comes to the monastery and gives money for a feast, the officers are to consult together over that too, which is the old custom of the monasteries. Share out what has been given by common agreement. Do not encroach on another's authority or confuse the offices.

When the meal has been properly prepared and set out on the table, the cook puts on his robe, spreads his sitting-cloth, faces the monks' hall, offers incense and makes nine full bows. Only after the bows is the food sent in.

A whole day and night spent preparing the rice and the gruel, with no time idled away and everything really made ready — every movement of it becomes of itself the work of nurturing the sacred embryo. To step back and turn yourself around is exactly the way by which the whole assembly comes to be at ease.

It is a long time now since the name of the buddha-dharma was first heard in our country of Japan. But that the food of the monks should be made according to the dharma is something nobody before us wrote down; as for the nine bows before the monks' food, it has not been seen even in a dream. People in this country talk about the food of monks as though it were the way animals feed. It is genuinely pitiable. How has it come to this?

When I was at Tiantong, a monk of that district held the office of cook. One day after the midday meal I was going along the east corridor towards my quarters, and the cook was in front of the buddha hall drying things in the sun. He had a bamboo staff in his hand and no hat on his head. The sun was blazing and the paving was scorching, and the sweat ran off him as he went up and down working at it. He was clearly having a hard time of it. His backbone was bent like a bow and his eyebrows were as white as a crane's.

I went up to him and asked how old he was. He said: sixty-eight.

I said: why do you not get the attendants or a workman to do it?

He said: other people are not me.

I said: you are quite right, sir. But the sun is so hot — why do it now?

He said: when, if not now?

I said no more. As I walked on along the corridor I began to feel, deep down, how essential this office is.

Again, in the fifth month of the sixteenth year of Jiading, the year of the sheep, I was at Qingyuan, and I was talking with the ship's master aboard the vessel when an old monk came up. He was about sixty-one. He came straight aboard and asked the master to sell him some Japanese mushrooms.

I invited him to take tea and asked him where he was from. He said: I am a man of western Shu. I left my home country forty years ago, and I am sixty-one this year. I have been round a fair number of monasteries. Some years ago I was living at Guyun, and then I got a place at Ayuwang, and I have muddled along there. Last year, when the summer retreat ended, I was made cook of that monastery. Tomorrow is the fifth day, and I have nothing good at all to offer. I wanted to make a noodle soup, and I had no mushrooms, so I came specially to buy some to offer to the monks who have come from every quarter.

I asked him when he had left the monastery. He said: after the midday meal.

I said: how far is Ayuwang from here?

He said: thirty-four or thirty-five miles.

I said: and when will you go back?

He said: as soon as I have bought the mushrooms.

I said: we have met today without expecting it, and we are talking here on the ship. Is that not a good connection? Let me make an offering of a meal to you, reverend cook.

He said: I cannot. If I am not there to manage tomorrow's offering it will not be done properly.

I said: surely there is somebody else in the monastery who understands the rice and the gruel? What is missing if the cook alone is not there?

He said: I have held this office in my old age. It is the practice of the way of an old man. How could I hand it over to somebody else? Besides, when I came out I did not get leave to stay a night.

I asked him again: reverend sir, at your age, why do you not sit and practise the way, or study the sayings of the old masters, instead of troubling yourself with the office of cook and just doing chores? What is the good of it?

He laughed out loud and said: my good foreigner, you have not yet understood what practising the way is, and you do not yet know what words are.

When I heard him say that I was suddenly ashamed and startled. I asked him: what are words? What is practising the way?

He said: if you do not stumble over what you are asking, you will be the man for it.

I did not understand him then. He said: if you have not got it yet, come to Ayuwang some day and we will talk over the principle of words.

With that he got up and said: it is getting late and I must hurry. And he went off home.

In the seventh month of the same year, when I was staying at Tiantong, that cook came to see me. He said: when the retreat ended I gave up being cook and I am going home. I happened to hear the brothers say that you were here. How could I not come and see you?

I was delighted and moved, and while we were talking I brought up what he had said that day on the ship about words and practising the way.

He said: a man who studies words does it in order to know what words are; a man who works at practising the way does it in order to affirm what practice is.

I asked him: what are words?

He said: one, two, three, four, five.

I asked him: what is practising the way?

He said: nothing in the whole world is hidden.

We talked of a good many other things, which I will not set down here. That I know a little of what words are and what practice is, I owe to that old man.

I told my late master, Myozen, about all this, and he was extremely pleased.

Later I came across a verse Xuedou wrote for a monk:

	One word, seven words, three words, five words —
	you may search the ten thousand things to the bottom
	and none of them is the ground of it.
	At midnight, the moon white, down over the blue sea:
	hunting for the dragon's pearl, and how many there turn out to be.

What that cook said the year before about words and what Xuedou shows here agree exactly, and I know all the better that he was a true man of the way. So the words I had been looking at until then were one, two, three, four, five; and the words I look at today are six, seven, eight, nine, ten.

Brothers who come after, look at this side from that side and at that side from this side, and go about it in that way, and you will get the one flavour of Zen in words. If you do not, you will be poisoned by the five flavours of Zen that are current everywhere, and you will not be able to prepare the food of the monks with a good hand.

Whoever holds this office should hold to three minds: the joyful mind, the parental mind, and the magnanimous mind.

The joyful mind is the mind that is glad. Think: if I had been born in the heavens I would be stuck in pleasure without a break, I would not raise the aspiration, and practice would not be convenient — much less would I be making food as an offering to the three treasures. Of all things in the world the three treasures are the most honourable and the most excellent. The lord of the gods is no comparison, and a world-turning king is nothing beside them. The Rules say: honoured in the world, at leisure beyond things, pure and unconditioned — of all these the assembly of monks is first.

Now I have been born a human being, luckily, and I am making the food that the three treasures will use. Is that not a great connection? It is a thing to be very glad about.

Think again: if I had been born in hell, or as a hungry ghost, or as an animal, or as a fighting demon, or in any of the eight difficult conditions, then although I might have wanted the shelter of the monastic community, I could not with these hands have made pure food as an offering to the three treasures; my body and mind would be bound and suffering in that vessel of suffering. In this life I can already do it. This is a birth to be glad of; this is a body to be glad of. It is a good connection made across a vast stretch of ages; it is merit that will not rot. Take ten thousand lives and a thousand lives and gather them into this one moment, and do the work in it. That is how to bind a good connection for bodies through ten thousand lives.

A mind that sees this clearly is the joyful mind. Truly, even if you were born the body of a world-turning sage king, if you do not make food as an offering to the three treasures it comes to nothing in the end; it is only froth on water, a bubble, a flame.

The parental mind is the mind of a mother and father. It is to hold the three treasures in mind as parents hold an only child in mind. However poor and hard-pressed they are, they raise that one child with all their strength. What that feeling is like, no outsider knows; only a man who has become a father, or a woman who has become a mother, knows it. They do not care whether they themselves are rich or poor; they think only of the child growing up. They do not mind their own cold, and they cover the child. There is nothing in it but the closest and most urgent care.

Only a man who has raised such a mind knows it; only a man who is used to such a mind understands it. So look after the water, look after the grain, with all the tenderness of raising a child.

Our great teacher Shakyamuni gave up twenty years of the life due to him so as to shelter us in the last age. What did he mean by it? Nothing but the parental mind. He was not looking for any result from it, and he was not looking for wealth.

The magnanimous mind is a mind that is like a great mountain and like the great sea: it takes no side and it belongs to no party. It picks up an ounce and does not call it light; it lifts a pound and does not call it heavy. It does not go chasing after spring because of the sound of spring, and although it sees the colours of autumn it has no autumn mind about them. It puts the four seasons into one scene and looks at light and heavy with the same eye.

At this point you should write the character for "great." You should know the character for "great." You should study the character for "great." If the cook of Jiashan had not studied the character for "great," he would not have laughed the laugh that saved a man. If Chan master Guishan had not written the character for "great," he could not have blown three times on the dead ashes. If Dongshan had not known the character for "great," he could not have wrapped up the whole of it in three pounds of hemp for a monk who asked.

You should know that all the great teachers who came before us learned the character for "great" on the tips of a hundred blades of grass, and now, freely, they make the great sound and speak the great meaning and take up the great matter and bring great people to completion. All of it is that one great matter and its causes.

Abbots, officers, head monks, and monks of the assembly — who among you would ever forget these three minds?

Recorded in the spring of the third year of Katei, the year of the rooster, and shown to gentlemen who study the way hereafter.

Recorded by the sramana Dogen, abbot and transmitter of the dharma of Kannon-dori Kosho Horin Zen monastery.
