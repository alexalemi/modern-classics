Preface, Definitions, Axioms, Propositions 1 to 18

Preface

Human infirmity in moderating and checking the emotions I name bondage: for a man who is at the mercy of his emotions is not his own master but is at the mercy of fortune, so much so that he is often forced, while seeing the better course, to follow the worse. Why this is so, and what is good and what evil in the emotions, I propose to show in this part of my treatise. But before I begin it would be well to make a few prefatory remarks about perfection and imperfection, and about good and evil.

When a man has set out to make a given thing and has brought it to completion, his work will be pronounced perfect, not only by himself but by everyone who rightly knows, or thinks he knows, the intention and aim of its maker. Suppose someone sees a work—which I assume is not yet finished—and knows that its maker's aim is to build a house: he will call the work imperfect, and will call it perfect as soon as he sees it carried through to the end its maker intended. But if a man sees a work the like of which he has never seen before, and does not know the maker's intention, he plainly cannot know whether the work is perfect or imperfect. Such seems to be the original meaning of these terms.

But once men began to form general ideas, and to think out types of houses, buildings, towers and so on, and to prefer some types to others, it came about that each man called perfect whatever he saw agreeing with the general idea he had formed of the thing in question, and imperfect whatever he saw agreeing less with his own preconceived type—even when it had clearly been finished exactly as its maker intended. This seems to be the only reason for calling natural things, which are not made by human hands, perfect or imperfect. For men habitually form general ideas of natural things no less than of artificial ones, and hold those ideas as types, believing that Nature (who they think does nothing without a purpose) has them in view and has set them before herself as models. So when they see something in Nature which does not wholly conform to the type they have preconceived, they say that Nature has fallen short or blundered and has left her work incomplete. We see, then, that men are in the habit of calling natural things perfect or imperfect from their own prejudices rather than from any true knowledge of what they are pronouncing on.

Now we showed in the Appendix to Part One that Nature does not act with an end in view. For the eternal and infinite Being which we call God or Nature acts by the same necessity by which it exists; for we have shown that it acts by the same necessity of its nature by which it exists (Part 1, Proposition 16). The reason, or cause, why God or Nature exists and the reason why he acts are one and the same. Therefore, just as he does not exist for the sake of an end, so neither does he act for the sake of an end; his existence and his action have neither beginning nor end. So what is called a final cause is nothing but human desire, insofar as it is considered as the origin, or cause, of something. When we say that being lived in is the final cause of this or that house, we mean nothing more than that a man, conceiving the conveniences of household life, had a desire to build a house. So being lived in, insofar as it is regarded as a final cause, is nothing but this particular desire, which is really the efficient cause; and it is taken for the first cause because men are generally ignorant of the causes of their own desires. They are, as I have often said, conscious of their actions and appetites but unaware of the causes by which they are determined to any particular desire. So the common saying that Nature sometimes falls short, or blunders, and produces things that are imperfect, I set down among the fictions treated of in the Appendix to Part One. Perfection and imperfection, then, are in reality merely ways of thinking—notions we form by comparing individuals of the same species with one another. This is why I said above (Part 2, Definition 6) that by reality and perfection I mean the same thing. For we habitually refer all individual things in nature to one class, the highest of all, namely the category of Being, to which absolutely everything in nature belongs. So insofar as we refer individual things to this category and, comparing them with one another, find that some possess more being or reality than others, we say to that extent that some are more perfect than others. And insofar as we attribute to them anything implying negation—limit, end, weakness and so on—we call them to that extent imperfect, because they do not affect our mind as strongly as the things we call perfect, and not because they have any deficiency of their own, or because Nature has blundered. For nothing belongs to a thing's nature but what follows from the necessity of the nature of its efficient cause, and whatever follows from the necessity of the nature of its efficient cause necessarily comes to pass.

As for the terms good and bad, they indicate no positive quality in things considered in themselves, but are merely ways of thinking, notions we form by comparing things with one another. Thus one and the same thing can be at the same time good, bad, and indifferent. Music is good for a man who is melancholy, bad for a man in mourning, and neither good nor bad for a man who is deaf.

Nevertheless, we should keep the terms. For since we desire to form an idea of man as a type of human nature to hold before us, it will be useful to keep them in the sense I have indicated.

In what follows, then, by "good" I shall mean what we certainly know to be a means of coming nearer to the type of human nature we set before ourselves, and by "bad" what we certainly know hinders us from coming nearer to it. And we shall say that men are more perfect or less perfect in proportion as they come nearer to that type or fall further from it. It must be particularly noted that when I say a man passes from a lesser to a greater perfection, or the other way round, I do not mean that he is changed from one essence, or reality, into another; for a horse would be as completely destroyed by being changed into a man as by being changed into an insect. What I mean is that we conceive the thing's power of acting, insofar as it is understood through its own nature, to be increased or diminished. And last, by perfection in general I shall, as I have said, mean reality—that is, each thing's essence insofar as it exists and acts in a particular way, with no regard at all to its duration. For no thing can be said to be more perfect because it has spent longer in existence. The duration of things cannot be determined by their essence, since the essence of things involves no fixed and definite period of existing; but every thing, whether more perfect or less, will always be able to persist in existing with the same force by which it began to exist. In this respect, therefore, all things are equal.

Definitions

1. By good I mean what we certainly know to be useful to us.

2. By evil I mean what we certainly know hinders us from attaining some good.

(Concerning these terms see the Preface to this Part, towards the end.)

3. I call particular things contingent insofar as, in regarding their essence alone, we find nothing in it which necessarily asserts their existence or excludes it.

4. I call particular things possible insofar as, in regarding the causes by which they must be produced, we do not know whether those causes are determined to produce them.

(in Part 1, Proposition 33, Note 1)

5. By conflicting emotions I mean those which draw a man in different directions even though they are of the same kind—such as luxury and avarice, which are both kinds of love and are contraries not by nature but by accident.

6. What I mean by an emotion felt towards a thing future, present and past, I explained in Part 3, Proposition 18, Notes 1 and 2, which see.

(But I should also remark here that we can only distinctly conceive distance of space or time up to a certain fixed limit. All objects further from us than two hundred feet—or whose distance from where we stand is greater than we can distinctly conceive—seem to be equally far from us, and all in the same plane; and so too objects whose time of existing is conceived as further from the present than we can distinctly conceive seem to be all equally distant from the present, and are set down, as it were, to the same moment of time.)

7. By an end, for the sake of which we do something, I mean a desire.

8. By virtue (virtus) and power I mean the same thing; that is (Part 3, Proposition 7), virtue, insofar as it is referred to man, is a man's nature or essence insofar as it has the power of bringing about what can be understood through the laws of that nature alone.

Axioms

There is no individual thing in nature than which there is not another more powerful and strong. Whatever thing is given, there is something stronger by which it can be destroyed.

Proposition 1

No positive quality possessed by a false idea is removed by the presence of what is true, in virtue of its being true.

Proof. Falsity consists solely in the privation of knowledge which inadequate ideas involve (Part 2, Proposition 35), and inadequate ideas have no positive quality on account of which they are called false (Part 2, Proposition 33); on the contrary, insofar as they are referred to God they are true (Part 2, Proposition 32). So if the positive quality possessed by a false idea were removed by the presence of what is true, in virtue of its being true, a true idea would be removed by itself, which (Proposition 3 of this Part) is absurd. Therefore no positive quality possessed by a false idea, and so on. Q.E.D.

Note. This proposition is more clearly understood from Part 2, Proposition 16, Corollary 2. For imagination is an idea which indicates the present disposition of the human body rather than the nature of the external body, and does so not distinctly but confusedly; and this is why the mind is said to err. When we look at the sun, for instance, we conceive it to be about two hundred feet away from us; and in this judgment we err so long as we are ignorant of its true distance. When the true distance is known, the error is removed, but not the imagining—that is, not the idea of the sun, which explains the sun's nature only insofar as the body is affected by it. So although we know the real distance, we shall still imagine the sun to be near us. For, as we said in Part 2, Proposition 35, Note, we do not imagine the sun to be so near us because we are ignorant of its true distance, but because the mind conceives the size of the sun to the extent that the body is affected by it. So when the sun's rays, falling on the surface of water, are reflected into our eyes, we imagine the sun as though it were in the water, though we know where it really is. And other imaginings in which the mind is deceived are likewise not contrary to the truth and do not vanish at its presence, whether they indicate the natural disposition of the body or that its power of acting is increased or diminished. It does happen that when we fear an evil mistakenly, the fear vanishes when we hear the truth; but the contrary happens too, that we fear an evil which will certainly come, and our fear vanishes when we hear false news. So imaginings do not vanish at the presence of truth in virtue of its being true, but because other imaginings, stronger than the first, come in and exclude the present existence of what we imagined, as I showed in Part 2, Proposition 17.

Proposition 2

We are passive only insofar as we are a part of Nature which cannot be conceived by itself, without the other parts.

Proof. We are said to be passive when something arises in us of which we are only a partial cause (Part 3, Definition 2)—that is (Part 3, Definition 1), something which cannot be deduced from the laws of our own nature alone. We are therefore passive insofar as we are a part of Nature which cannot be conceived by itself without the other parts. Q.E.D.

Proposition 3

The force by which a man persists in existing is limited, and is infinitely surpassed by the power of external causes.

Proof. This is evident from the axiom of this Part. For given a man, there is something else—say A—more powerful; and given A, there is something else—say B—more powerful than A, and so on to infinity. So the power of man is limited by the power of some other thing, and is infinitely surpassed by the power of external causes. Q.E.D.

Proposition 4

It is impossible that a man should not be a part of Nature, or that he should undergo no changes except those which can be understood through his own nature alone as their adequate cause.

Proof. The power by which each particular thing, and consequently man, preserves its being is the power of God, or of Nature (Part 1, Proposition 24, Corollary)—not insofar as it is infinite, but insofar as it can be explained through the actual essence of a man (Part 3, Proposition 7). So a man's power, insofar as it is explained through his own actual essence, is a part of the infinite power of God or Nature, that is, of its essence (Part 1, Proposition 34). That was our first point. Again, if it were possible that a man should undergo no changes except those which can be understood through his own nature alone, it would follow that he could not die but would always necessarily exist. And this would have to follow from a cause whose power was either finite or infinite: either from the man's own power alone, in that he would be able to keep off from himself all the changes that could spring from external causes; or from the infinite power of Nature, by which all individual things would be so ordered that a man could undergo no changes except those tending to his own preservation. But the first alternative is absurd (by Proposition 3 of this Part, the proof of which is universal, and can be applied to all individual things). Therefore, if it were possible that a man should undergo no changes except those explicable through his own nature alone—and consequently, as we have shown, that he must always necessarily exist—it would have to follow from the infinite power of God, and consequently (Part 1, Proposition 16) from the necessity of the divine nature, insofar as it is regarded as affected by the idea of some given man, that the whole order of nature as conceived under the attributes of extension and of thought could be deduced. It would follow therefore (Part 1, Proposition 21) that a man is infinite, which (by the first part of this proof) is absurd. It is therefore impossible that a man should undergo no changes except those of which he is the adequate cause. Q.E.D.

Corollary. It follows that a man is necessarily always subject to his passions, that he follows and obeys the general order of nature, and that he accommodates himself to it as far as the nature of things requires.

Proposition 5

The force and increase of any passion, and its persistence in existing, are defined not by the power by which we ourselves endeavour to persist in existing, but by the power of an external cause compared with our own.

Proof. The essence of a passion cannot be explained through our own essence alone (Part 3, Definition 1; Part 3, Definition 2); that is (Part 3, Proposition 7), the power of a passion cannot be defined by the power by which we ourselves endeavour to persist in existing, but must (as is shown in Part 2, Proposition 16) be defined by the power of an external cause compared with our own. Q.E.D.

Proposition 6

The force of any passion, or emotion, can overcome the rest of a man's activity, or power, so that the emotion becomes obstinately fixed in him.

Proof. The force and increase of any passion, and its persistence in existing, are defined by the power of an external cause compared with our own (by Proposition 5 of this Part); therefore (Proposition 3 of this Part) it can overcome a man's power, and so on. Q.E.D.

Proposition 7

An emotion can only be checked or destroyed by another emotion contrary to it, and stronger than it.

Proof. An emotion, insofar as it is referred to the mind, is an idea by which the mind affirms of its body a greater or lesser force of existing than before (compare the general Definition of the Emotions at the end of Part 3). So when the mind is assailed by an emotion, the body is at the same time affected with a modification by which its power of acting is increased or diminished. Now this modification of the body (Proposition 5 of this Part) receives from its cause the force to persist in its being, and that force can only be checked or destroyed by a bodily cause (Part 2, Proposition 6), in virtue of the body being affected with a modification contrary to it (Part 3, Proposition 5) and stronger than it (the Axiom of this Part). Therefore (Part 2, Proposition 12) the mind is affected by the idea of a modification contrary to and stronger than the first—in other words (by the general Definition of the Emotions), the mind will be affected by an emotion contrary to and stronger than the first, which will exclude or destroy the existence of the first. So an emotion can neither be destroyed nor checked except by a contrary and stronger emotion. Q.E.D.

Corollary. An emotion, insofar as it is referred to the mind, can only be checked or destroyed by the idea of a bodily modification contrary to and stronger than the one we are undergoing. For the emotion we undergo can only be checked or destroyed by an emotion contrary to and stronger than itself—in other words (by the general Definition of the Emotions), only by the idea of a bodily modification contrary to and stronger than the one we undergo.

Proposition 8

The knowledge of good and evil is nothing but the emotion of pleasure or pain, insofar as we are conscious of it.

Proof. We call a thing good or evil when it serves or hinders the preservation of our being (Definition 1 of this Part; Definition 2 of this Part)—that is (Part 3, Proposition 7), when it increases or diminishes, helps or hinders our power of acting. So insofar as we perceive that a thing affects us with pleasure or pain, we call it good or evil; and therefore the knowledge of good and evil is nothing but the idea of the pleasure or pain which necessarily follows from that pleasurable or painful emotion (Part 2, Proposition 22). But this idea is united to the emotion in the same way as mind is united to body (Part 2, Proposition 21); that is, there is no real distinction between this idea and the emotion, or idea of the bodily modification, except in thought. Therefore the knowledge of good and evil is nothing but the emotion itself, insofar as we are conscious of it. Q.E.D.

Proposition 9

An emotion whose cause we conceive to be with us at the present time is stronger than if we did not conceive the cause to be with us.

Proof. Imagining, or conceiving, is the idea by which the mind regards a thing as present (Part 2, Proposition 17, Note), but which indicates the disposition of the body rather than the nature of the external thing (Part 2, Proposition 16, Corollary 2). An emotion is therefore a conceiving, insofar as it indicates the disposition of the body. But a conceiving (by Part 2, Proposition 17) is stronger so long as we conceive nothing which excludes the present existence of the external object; so an emotion is also stronger, or more intense, when we conceive the cause to be with us at the present time than when we do not. Q.E.D.

Note. When I said above, in Part 3, Proposition 18, that we are affected by the image of a thing past or future with the same emotion as if the thing conceived were present, I expressly stated that this is true only insofar as we look solely to the image of the thing itself; for the thing's nature is unchanged whether we have conceived it or not. I did not deny that the image becomes weaker when we regard as present to us other things which exclude the present existence of the future object; I simply did not call attention to it, because I intended to treat of the strength of the emotions in this part of my work.

Corollary. The image of something past or future—that is, of a thing we regard in relation to time past or time future, to the exclusion of the present—is, other things being equal, weaker than the image of something present. Consequently an emotion felt towards something past or future is, other things being equal, less intense than an emotion felt towards something present.

Proposition 10

Towards something future which we conceive as close at hand we are affected more intensely than if we conceive its time of existing to be separated from the present by a longer interval; and likewise we are affected more intensely by the memory of something we conceive to have passed away not long ago than of something we conceive to have passed away long since.

Proof. Insofar as we conceive a thing as close at hand, or as having passed away not long ago, we conceive less of what excludes the object's presence than if its time of future existence were more distant from the present, or if it had passed away long since—which is obvious. Therefore (by Proposition 9 of this Part) we are so far more intensely affected towards it. Q.E.D.

Corollary. From what was said in Definition 6 of this Part it follows that if objects are separated from the present by a longer period than we can define in thought, they all affect us equally faintly, however widely their dates may be separated from one another.

Proposition 11

An emotion towards something we conceive as necessary is, other things being equal, more intense than an emotion towards something possible, or contingent, or not necessary.

Proof. Insofar as we conceive a thing to be necessary, we to that extent affirm its existence; and on the other hand we deny a thing's existence insofar as we conceive it not to be necessary (Part 1, Proposition 33, Note 1). So (Proposition 9 of this Part) an emotion towards what is necessary is, other things being equal, more intense than an emotion towards what is not necessary. Q.E.D.

Proposition 12

An emotion towards a thing which we know not to exist at the present time, and which we conceive as possible, is more intense, other things being equal, than an emotion towards a thing contingent.

Proof. Insofar as we conceive a thing as contingent, we are affected by the conception of no further thing which would assert its existence (Definition 3 of this Part); on the contrary, we (by hypothesis) conceive certain things which exclude its present existence. But insofar as we conceive a thing to be possible in the future, we do conceive things which assert its existence (Proposition 4 of this Part)—that is (Part 3, Proposition 18), things which promote hope or fear. So an emotion towards something possible is the more vehement. Q.E.D.

Corollary. An emotion towards a thing which we know not to exist in the present, and which we conceive as contingent, is far fainter than if we conceived the thing to be present with us.

Proof. An emotion towards a thing we conceive to exist is more intense than it would be if we conceived the thing as future (Proposition 9 of this Part, Corollary), and much more vehement than if the future time were conceived as far distant from the present (Proposition 10 of this Part). So an emotion towards a thing whose time of existing we conceive to be far distant from the present is far fainter than if we conceived the thing as present; and it is nevertheless more intense than if we conceived the thing as contingent. Therefore an emotion towards a thing we regard as contingent will be far fainter than if we conceived the thing to be present with us. Q.E.D.

Proposition 13

An emotion towards a thing contingent, which we know not to exist in the present, is, other things being equal, fainter than an emotion towards a thing past.

Proof. Insofar as we conceive a thing as contingent, we are not affected by the image of any other thing which asserts its existence (Definition 3 of this Part); on the contrary (by hypothesis), we conceive certain things which exclude its present existence. But insofar as we conceive it in relation to time past, we are supposed to conceive something which recalls it to memory, or rouses its image (Part 2, Proposition 18, Note), which so far comes to the same thing as regarding it as present (Part 2, Proposition 17, Corollary). Therefore (Proposition 9 of this Part) an emotion towards a thing contingent which we know does not exist in the present is, other things being equal, fainter than an emotion towards a thing past. Q.E.D.

Proposition 14

A true knowledge of good and evil cannot check any emotion in virtue of being true, but only insofar as it is itself considered as an emotion.

Proof. An emotion is an idea by which the mind affirms of its body a greater or lesser force of existing than before (by the general Definition of the Emotions); so it has no positive quality which can be destroyed by the presence of what is true, and consequently the knowledge of good and evil cannot restrain any emotion in virtue of being true. But insofar as such knowledge is itself an emotion (Proposition 8 of this Part), if it has more strength for restraining emotion it will to that extent be able to restrain the emotion in question. Q.E.D.

Proposition 15

Desire arising from the knowledge of good and evil can be quenched or checked by many of the other desires arising from the emotions that assail us.

Proof. From the true knowledge of good and evil, insofar as it is an emotion, there necessarily arises desire (Definition 1 of the Emotions), whose strength is proportional to the strength of the emotion it arises from (Part 3, Proposition 37). But since this desire arises (by hypothesis) from our truly understanding something, it follows that it is present in us insofar as we are active (Part 3, Proposition 1), and must therefore be understood through our essence alone (Part 3, Definition 2); consequently (Part 3, Proposition 7) its force and increase can be defined solely by human power. And the desires arising from the emotions that assail us are stronger in proportion as those emotions are more vehement; so their force and increase must be defined solely by the power of external causes, which, compared with our own power, indefinitely surpasses it (Proposition 3 of this Part). So the desires arising from such emotions may be more vehement than the desire arising from a true knowledge of good and evil, and may consequently check or quench it. Q.E.D.

Proposition 16

Desire arising from the knowledge of good and evil, insofar as that knowledge concerns what is future, may be more easily checked or quenched than the desire for what is agreeable at the present moment.

Proof. An emotion towards a thing we conceive as future is fainter than an emotion towards a thing present (Proposition 9 of this Part, Corollary). But desire arising from the true knowledge of good and evil, even when it is concerned with things that are good at the moment, can be quenched or checked by any headstrong desire (by Proposition 16 of this Part, the proof of which is of universal application). Therefore desire arising from such knowledge, when it is concerned with the future, can be more easily checked or quenched, and so on. Q.E.D.

Proposition 17

Desire arising from the true knowledge of good and evil, insofar as that knowledge concerns what is contingent, can be checked far more easily still than desire for things that are present.

Proof. This proposition is proved in the same way as Proposition 16 of this Part is proved from Proposition 12 of this Part, Corollary.

Note. I think I have now shown why men are moved by opinion more readily than by true reason, and why the true knowledge of good and evil stirs up conflicts in the soul and often gives way to every kind of passion. This is what gave rise to the poet's cry: "The better path I gaze at and approve, The worse—I follow."

Footnote: Ov. Met. vii.20, "Video meliora proboque, Deteriora sequor."

Ecclesiastes seems to have had the same thought in mind when he says, "He who increaseth knowledge increaseth sorrow." I have not written all this in order to draw the conclusion that ignorance is better than knowledge, or that a wise man is no better than a fool at controlling his emotions, but because we need to know the power and the weakness of our nature before we can determine what reason can do in restraining the emotions and what is beyond her power. I said that in this part I should treat only of human weakness; the power of reason over the emotions I have decided to treat separately.

Proposition 18

Desire arising from pleasure is, other things being equal, stronger than desire arising from pain.

Proof. Desire is the essence of a man (Definition 1 of the Emotions); that is, it is the endeavour by which a man endeavours to persist in his own being. So desire arising from pleasure is increased or helped by the very fact of pleasure being felt, while desire arising from pain is diminished or hindered by the very fact of pain being felt. So the force of desire arising from pleasure must be defined by human power together with the power of an external cause, whereas the force of desire arising from pain must be defined by human power alone. The former is therefore the stronger of the two. Q.E.D.

Note. In these few remarks I have explained the causes of human weakness and inconstancy, and shown why men do not abide by the precepts of reason. It remains for me to show what course reason marks out for us, which of the emotions agree with the rules of human reason, and which are contrary to them. But before I begin proving my propositions in detailed geometrical fashion, it is worth sketching them briefly in advance, so that everyone may grasp my meaning more readily.

Since reason demands nothing contrary to nature, it demands that every man should love himself, should seek what is useful to him—I mean what is really useful to him—should desire everything that really brings a man to greater perfection, and should, each for himself, endeavour as far as he can to preserve his own being. This is as necessarily true as that a whole is greater than its part (compare Part 3, Proposition 4).

Again, since virtue is nothing but acting according to the laws of one's own nature (Definition 8 of this Part), and since no one endeavours to preserve his own being except according to the laws of his own nature, it follows: first, that the foundation of virtue is the endeavour to preserve one's own being, and that happiness consists in a man's power of preserving his own being; second, that virtue is to be desired for its own sake, and that there is nothing more excellent or more useful to us for whose sake we should desire it; and third and last, that suicides are weak-minded, and are overcome by external causes contrary to their nature. Further, it follows from Part 2, Postulate 4 that we can never do wholly without external things for the preservation of our being, or live so as to have no dealings with what is outside us. And if we consider our mind, we see that our intellect would be more imperfect if the mind were alone and could understand nothing but itself. There are, then, many things outside us which are useful to us and are therefore to be desired. Of these none can be found more excellent than those which agree entirely with our own nature; for if two individuals of entirely the same nature are joined together, they make up a combination twice as powerful as either alone.

To man, therefore, there is nothing more useful than man. Nothing, I repeat, more excellent for the preservation of their being can be wished for by men than that all should so agree in all points that the minds and bodies of all should form, as it were, one single mind and one single body; that all should, with one consent, endeavour as far as they can to preserve their being; and that all should, with one consent, seek what is useful to them all. From which it follows that men who are governed by reason—that is, who seek what is useful to them under the guidance of reason—desire nothing for themselves which they do not also desire for the rest of mankind, and are consequently just, faithful and honourable in their conduct.

Such are the dictates of reason, which I meant to indicate thus briefly before beginning to prove them in more detail. I have taken this course in order, if possible, to gain the attention of those who believe that the principle that every man is bound to seek what is useful to himself is the foundation of impiety rather than of piety and virtue.

Having briefly shown that the contrary is the case, I go on to prove it by the same method I have used so far.
