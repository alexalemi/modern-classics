Henry Watson Fowler (1858–1933) taught classics at Sedbergh School for seventeen years and left it in 1899, unwilling to prepare boys for confirmation in a faith he no longer held. He worked for a few years as a journalist in London, then joined his younger brother Francis on Guernsey, where the two grew tomatoes and wrote. Together they translated Lucian, wrote The King's English (1906), and compiled the Concise Oxford Dictionary (1911). In 1915, at fifty-seven, Henry enlisted in the army by giving his age as forty-four, and both brothers served in France. Francis died in 1918 of tuberculosis contracted in the war. The book is dedicated to him: they had planned it together, and Henry wrote it alone.

A Dictionary of Modern English Usage was published by the Clarendon Press in 1926. It is not a dictionary of meanings. Its entries answer the questions a writer actually gets stuck on: which of two spellings, which plural, how a word is pronounced, whether a phrase is idiomatic, which of two near-synonyms fits. Most entries are a line or two. Mixed among them are the "general articles", essays of a column to several pages on the ways writing goes wrong, under names Fowler coined or made famous: Elegant Variation, Genteelism, Love of the Long Word, Battered Ornaments, Wardour Street, the Fused Participle. His List of General Articles, reprinted here, is the way into them. The articles are argued from examples, most taken from the newspapers of the day, and he names the fault plainly.

Some conventions will look strange. Fowler writes "&" for "and" throughout. Pronunciations are respelled with the marks explained in his Key: ā for the vowel of "mate", ă for "rack", and an italic letter for a vowel said lightly. A reference to another entry is printed in small capitals; ")(" between two words means they are being contrasted; "OED" is the Oxford English Dictionary, which he cites constantly. His opinions are his own, and he knew it: the dedication wishes the book had been written with “its fads eliminated”.

This is a restored edition. The words are Fowler's, unchanged, including his few misprints. No usable transcription existed, so all 742 pages and the front matter were read from scans of the first printing and checked against a second copy. Every headword is an anchor and every cross-reference in small capitals is a link to it, as are the entries of the List of General Articles. The book was revised by Ernest Gowers in 1965; that version is a different book, and this is Fowler's own.
