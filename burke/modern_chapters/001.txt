Section Two: Dr Price's Sermon, and Whether We Choose Our Kings

On the morning of the fourth of November last, Dr Richard Price, an eminent Nonconformist minister, preached at the Dissenting meeting house of the Old Jewry, to his club or society, a very extraordinary miscellaneous sermon, in which some good moral and religious sentiments, not badly expressed, are mixed into a sort of porridge of assorted political opinions and reflections — but the Revolution in France is the chief ingredient in the cauldron. I regard the address the Revolution Society sent to the National Assembly through Earl Stanhope as originating in the principles of that sermon, and as a corollary of them. It was proposed by the preacher of the sermon. It was passed by men who came straight from the sermon still steaming, with no censure or qualification, stated or implied. If, however, any of the gentlemen concerned should wish to separate the sermon from the resolution, they know how to acknowledge the one and disavow the other. They may do it. I cannot.

For my part, I looked on that sermon as the public declaration of a man closely connected with literary intriguers and plotting philosophers, with political theologians and theological politicians, at home and abroad. I know they set him up as a kind of oracle, because, with the best intentions in the world, he naturally _philippizes_ — speaks as the interested party would have him speak — and chants his prophetic song in exact unison with their designs.

That sermon is in a style which I believe has not been heard in this kingdom, from any pulpit that is tolerated or encouraged in it, since 1648, when a predecessor of Dr Price, the Reverend Hugh Peters, made the vault of the king's own chapel at St James's ring with the honour and privilege of the saints, who, with the "high praises of God in their mouths, and a _two_-edged sword in their hands, were to execute judgment on the heathen, and punishments upon the _people_; to bind their _kings_ with chains, and their _nobles_ with fetters of iron." Few harangues from the pulpit, except in the days of your League in France or of our Solemn League and Covenant in England, have ever breathed less of the spirit of moderation than this lecture in the Old Jewry. But even supposing something like moderation were visible in this political sermon, politics and the pulpit are terms that have little in common. No sound ought to be heard in church but the healing voice of Christian charity. The cause of civil liberty and civil government gains as little as the cause of religion by this confusion of duties. Those who abandon their proper character to assume one that does not belong to them are for the most part ignorant both of the character they leave and of the one they take up. Wholly unacquainted with the world they are so fond of meddling in, and inexperienced in all the affairs they pronounce on with so much confidence, they have nothing of politics but the passions politics excites. Surely the church is a place where one day's truce ought to be allowed to the quarrels and animosities of mankind.

Footnote: Psalm 149.

This pulpit style, revived after being so long out of use, struck me as a novelty, and a novelty not entirely without danger. I do not charge every part of the discourse equally with that danger. The hint dropped to a noble and reverend lay divine, supposed to hold high office in one of our universities, and to other lay divines "of _rank_ and literature", may be proper and timely, if somewhat new. If these noble _Seekers_ should find nothing to satisfy their pious fancies in the old stock of the national Church, or in all the rich variety to be had in the well-stocked warehouses of the Dissenting congregations, Dr Price advises them to improve on Nonconformity and each set up a separate meeting house on his own particular principles. It is somewhat remarkable that this reverend divine should be so eager to have new churches set up and so perfectly indifferent about the doctrine that may be taught in them. His zeal is of a curious kind. It is not for spreading his own opinions but for spreading any opinions. It is not for the diffusion of truth but for the propagation of contradiction. Let the noble teachers only dissent; it does not matter from whom or from what. That great point once secured, it is taken for granted their religion will be rational and manly.

I doubt whether religion would reap all the benefits our calculating divine reckons up from this "great company of great preachers". It would certainly add a valuable set of unclassifiable specimens to the ample collection of known classes, genera and species that at present adorn the _hortus siccus_, the dried-flower album, of Dissent. A sermon from a noble duke, or a noble marquess, or a noble earl, or a bold baron would certainly increase and vary the amusements of this town, which is beginning to grow tired of the unchanging round of its insipid dissipations. I should only stipulate that these new _Mess-Johns_ in robes and coronets should keep some sort of bounds in the democratic and levelling principles expected from their titled pulpits. The new evangelists will, I dare say, disappoint the hopes formed of them. They will not become polemical divines in the literal as well as the figurative sense, or be inclined to drill their congregations so that they may, as in former blessed times, preach their doctrines to regiments of dragoons and to corps of infantry and artillery. Such arrangements, however favourable to the cause of compulsory freedom, civil and religious, may not be equally conducive to national tranquillity. These few restrictions are, I hope, no great stretches of intolerance, no very violent exercises of despotism.

Footnote: Discourse on the Love of our Country, Nov. 4, 1789, by Dr Richard Price, 3rd edition, pp. 17 and 18.

Footnote: "Those who dislike that mode of worship which is prescribed by public authority ought, if they can find _no_ worship _out_ of the Church which they approve, _to set up a separate worship for themselves_; and by doing this, and giving an example of a rational and manly worship, men of _weight_ from their _rank_ and literature may do the greatest service to society and the world." — P. 18, Dr Price's Sermon.

But of our preacher I may say, "_Utinam nugis tota illa dedisset et tempora sævitiæ_" — would that he had given all those hours to trifles instead of to cruelty. Not everything in this thundering bull of his is so harmless in its tendency. His doctrines strike at our constitution in its vital parts. He tells the Revolution Society, in this political sermon, that His Majesty "is almost the _only_ lawful king in the world, because the _only_ one who owes his crown to _the choice of his people_." As for the kings of _the world_, every one of whom except this one is swept by this arch-pontiff of the _rights of men_ into a single clause of ban and anathema — with all the fullness and more than the boldness of the papal deposing power at its twelfth-century height — and proclaimed a usurper by circles of longitude and latitude across the whole globe, it is for them to consider how they admit into their territories these apostolic missionaries who are to tell their subjects they are not lawful kings. That is their concern. Ours, as a domestic matter of some importance, is to consider seriously how solid the _only_ principle is on which these gentlemen acknowledge a king of Great Britain to be entitled to their allegiance.

This doctrine, as applied to the prince now on the British throne, is either nonsense, and therefore neither true nor false, or else it asserts a most unfounded, dangerous, illegal and unconstitutional position. According to this spiritual doctor of politics, if His Majesty does not owe his crown to the choice of his people, he is no _lawful_ king. Now nothing could be less true than that His Majesty holds the crown of this kingdom in that way. Therefore, if you follow their rule, the king of Great Britain, who most certainly does not owe his high office to any form of popular election, is in no respect better than the rest of the gang of usurpers who reign — or rather rob — all over the face of this miserable world, without any sort of right or title to their people's allegiance. The policy behind this general doctrine, so qualified, is plain enough. The propagators of this political gospel hope that their abstract principle — that a popular choice is necessary to the lawful existence of the sovereign power — will pass unnoticed while the king of Great Britain is exempted from it. Meanwhile their congregations' ears would gradually be habituated to it, as though it were a first principle admitted without dispute. For the present it would operate only as a theory, pickled in the preserving juices of pulpit eloquence and laid by for future use. _Condo et compono quæ mox depromere passim_: I store and stow away what I may soon bring out. By this policy, while our government is soothed with an exemption in its favour that it has no claim to, the security it has in common with all governments, so far as opinion is security, is taken away from it.

So these politicians proceed, while little notice is taken of their doctrines. But when they come to be examined on the plain meaning of their words and the direct tendency of what they teach, then the equivocations and the slippery constructions come into play. When they say the king owes his crown to the choice of his people, and is therefore the only lawful sovereign in the world, they will perhaps tell us they mean no more than that some of the king's predecessors were called to the throne by some sort of choice, and that he therefore owes his crown to the choice of his people. By this miserable subterfuge they hope to make their proposition safe by making it meaningless. They are welcome to the refuge they seek from their offence, since they take shelter in their own folly. For if you allow that interpretation, how does their idea of election differ from our idea of inheritance? And how does settling the crown on the Brunswick line, derived from James the First, make our monarchy any more legal than that of any of the neighbouring countries? At some time or other, to be sure, all the founders of dynasties were chosen by those who called them to govern. There is ground enough for the opinion that all the kingdoms of Europe were at some remote period elective, with more or fewer limits on who could be chosen. But whatever kings may have been here or elsewhere a thousand years ago, and in whatever way the ruling dynasties of England or France may have begun, the king of Great Britain is today king by a fixed rule of succession, according to the laws of his country. And while the legal conditions of the compact of sovereignty are performed by him — as they are — he holds his crown in contempt of the choice of the Revolution Society, who have not a single vote for a king among them, individually or collectively, though I have no doubt they would soon set themselves up as an electoral college if things were ripe enough to give effect to their claim. His Majesty's heirs and successors, each in his time and order, will come to the crown with the same contempt for their choice with which His Majesty succeeded to the one he wears.

Whatever success their evasion may have in explaining away the gross error of _fact_, which supposes that His Majesty owes his crown to the choice of his people — though he does hold it with their goodwill — nothing can evade their full and explicit declaration about the principle of a right in the people to choose, which is directly maintained and tenaciously held to. All the oblique hints about election rest on this proposition and refer back to it. In case the foundation of the king's exclusive legal title should be taken for a mere flourish of flattering freedom, the political divine goes on to assert dogmatically that by the principles of the Revolution the people of England have acquired three fundamental rights, all of which, for him, make up one system and lie together in one short sentence: that we have acquired a right

Footnote: P. 34, Discourse on the Love of our Country, by Dr Price.

1. "To choose our own governors."

2. "To cashier them for misconduct."

3. "To frame a government for ourselves."

This new and hitherto unheard-of bill of rights, though made in the name of the whole people, belongs to those gentlemen and their faction alone. The body of the people of England have no share in it. They utterly disown it. They will resist any practical assertion of it with their lives and fortunes. They are bound to do so by the laws of their country, made at the time of that very Revolution which is being appealed to in favour of the fictitious rights claimed by the society that abuses its name.

These gentlemen of the Old Jewry, in all their reasoning about the Revolution of 1688, have another revolution — the one that happened in England about forty years before it — and the recent French Revolution so much before their eyes and in their hearts that they are constantly confusing all three together. We must separate what they confuse. We must recall their wandering fancies to the _acts_ of the Revolution we revere, in order to discover its true _principles_. If the _principles_ of the Revolution of 1688 are to be found anywhere, they are in the statute called the _Declaration of Right_. In that most wise, sober and considered declaration, drawn up by great lawyers and great statesmen and not by warm and inexperienced enthusiasts, not one word is said, and not one hint given, of a general right "to choose our own _governors_, to cashier them for misconduct, and to _form_ a government for _ourselves_."

This Declaration of Right — the act of the 1st of William and Mary, session 2, chapter 2 — is the cornerstone of our constitution, reinforced, explained, improved, and settled for ever in its fundamental principles. It is called "An act for declaring the rights and liberties of the subject, and for _settling_ the _succession_ of the crown." You will notice that these rights and this succession are declared in one body and bound indissolubly together.

A few years later a second opportunity arose for asserting a right of election to the crown. With the prospect of a total failure of issue from King William and from the princess who afterwards became Queen Anne, the settlement of the crown, and a further security for the liberties of the people, came before the legislature again. Did they this second time make any provision for legalising the crown on the spurious Revolution principles of the Old Jewry? No. They followed the principles that prevailed in the Declaration of Right, indicating with more precision who was to inherit in the Protestant line. This act too, by the same policy, incorporated our liberties and a hereditary succession in one and the same act. Instead of a right to choose our own governors, they declared that the _succession_ in that line — the Protestant line drawn from James the First — was absolutely necessary "for the peace, quiet, and security of the realm", and that it was equally pressing on them "to maintain a _certainty in the succession_ thereof, to which the subjects may safely have recourse for their protection." Both these acts, in which we hear the unerring, unambiguous oracles of Revolution policy, instead of countenancing the delusive fortune-teller's prediction of a "right to choose our governors", prove conclusively how utterly opposed the wisdom of the nation was to turning a case of necessity into a rule of law.

Unquestionably there was at the Revolution, in the person of King William, a small and temporary deviation from the strict order of a regular hereditary succession. But it is against all genuine principles of jurisprudence to draw a principle from a law made in a special case and concerning an individual person. _Privilegium non transit in exemplum_: a privilege does not become a precedent. If ever there was a time favourable for establishing the principle that a king of popular choice was the only legal king, it was without doubt the Revolution. That it was not done then is proof that the nation held it ought never to be done at all. Nobody is so completely ignorant of our history as not to know that the majority in Parliament, of both parties, were so little inclined to anything resembling that principle that at first they were determined to place the vacant crown not on the head of the Prince of Orange but on that of his wife Mary, King James's daughter, the eldest of the children of that king, whom they acknowledged as undoubtedly his. It would be to repeat a very trite story to recall to your memory all the circumstances that showed their accepting King William was not properly a _choice_. For everyone who did not wish in effect to recall King James, or to drown their country in blood, and to bring their religion, laws and liberties back into the danger they had just escaped, it was an act of _necessity_, in the strictest moral sense the word can bear.

In the very act by which Parliament departed, for a time and in a single case, from the strict order of inheritance, in favour of a prince who, though not next, was very near in the line of succession, it is curious to see how Lord Somers, who drew the bill called the Declaration of Right, conducted himself on that delicate occasion. It is curious to see with what address this temporary break in continuity is kept out of sight, while everything in the act of necessity that could support the idea of a hereditary succession is brought forward, nursed, and made the most of by that great man and by the legislature that followed him. Leaving the dry, imperative style of an act of Parliament, he has the Lords and Commons break into a pious legislative ejaculation and declare that they consider it "as a marvellous providence, and merciful goodness of God to this nation, to preserve their said Majesties' _royal_ persons most happily to reign over us _on the throne of their ancestors_, for which, from the bottom of their hearts, they return their humblest thanks and praises." The legislature plainly had in view the Act of Recognition of the first year of Queen Elizabeth, chapter 3, and that of James the First, chapter 1, both strongly declaring the inheritable nature of the crown; and in many places they follow, with nearly literal precision, the words and even the form of thanksgiving found in those old declaratory statutes.

The two Houses, in King William's act, did not thank God that they had found a fair opportunity to assert a right to choose their own governors, much less to make election the _only lawful_ title to the crown. That they had been in a position to avoid even the appearance of it, as far as possible, was regarded by them as a providential escape. They threw a politic, well-worked veil over every circumstance tending to weaken the rights they meant to perpetuate in the improved order of succession, or that might provide a precedent for any future departure from what they had then settled for ever. Accordingly, so as not to slacken the sinews of their monarchy, and to keep close to the practice of their ancestors as it appeared in the declaratory statutes of Queen Mary and Queen Elizabeth, in the next clause they vest, by recognition, in their Majesties _all_ the legal prerogatives of the crown, declaring "that in them they are most _fully_, rightfully, and _entirely_ invested, incorporated, united, and annexed." In the clause that follows, to prevent questions arising from any pretended titles to the crown, they declare — observing here too the traditional language along with the traditional policy of the nation, and repeating as from a rubric the language of the preceding acts of Elizabeth and James — that on preserving "a _certainty_ in the SUCCESSION thereof the unity, peace, and tranquillity of this nation doth, under God, wholly depend."

Footnote: 1st Mary, session 3, chapter 1.

They knew that a doubtful title of succession would look far too much like an election, and that an election would be utterly destructive of the "unity, peace, and tranquillity of this nation", which they thought considerations of some weight. To provide for these things, and so to shut out for ever the Old Jewry doctrine of "a right to choose our own governors", they follow with a clause containing the most solemn pledge, taken from the earlier act of Queen Elizabeth — as solemn a pledge as ever was or can be given in favour of a hereditary succession, and as solemn a renunciation as could be made of the principles this society imputes to them: "The Lords Spiritual and Temporal, and Commons, do, in the name of all the people aforesaid, most humbly and faithfully submit _themselves, their heirs, and posterities forever_; and do faithfully promise that they will stand to, maintain, and defend their said Majesties, and also the _limitation of the crown_, herein specified and contained, to the utmost of their powers," and so on.

So far is it from being true that we acquired a right at the Revolution to elect our kings, that if we had possessed such a right before, the English nation at that moment most solemnly renounced and abdicated it, for themselves and for all their posterity for ever. These gentlemen may value themselves as much as they please on their Whig principles; but I have no wish to be thought a better Whig than Lord Somers, or to understand the principles of the Revolution better than the men who brought it about, or to read into the Declaration of Right mysteries unknown to those whose penetrating style engraved the words and the spirit of that immortal law on our statute book and on our hearts.

It is true that, aided by the powers that came from force and opportunity, the nation was at that time in some sense free to take whatever course it pleased for filling the throne — but only free to do so on the same grounds on which it might have abolished the monarchy altogether, and every other part of its constitution. They did not, however, think such bold changes within their commission. It is indeed difficult, perhaps impossible, to set limits to the mere _abstract_ competence of the supreme power, such as Parliament exercised at that time. But the limits of a _moral_ competence, which subjects even the most indisputably sovereign powers, and subjects momentary will to permanent reason and to the steady maxims of faith, justice and settled fundamental policy, are perfectly intelligible and perfectly binding on everyone who exercises any authority in the state, under any name or any title. The House of Lords, for instance, is not morally competent to dissolve the House of Commons — nor even to dissolve itself, nor to give up, if it wished to, its share in the legislature of the kingdom. A king may abdicate for his own person, but he cannot abdicate for the monarchy. By as strong a reason, or a stronger, the House of Commons cannot renounce its share of authority. The engagement and pact of society, which generally goes by the name of the constitution, forbids such an invasion and such a surrender. The constituent parts of a state are as much obliged to keep public faith with one another, and with everyone who has any serious interest depending on their engagements, as the whole state is bound to keep faith with separate communities. Otherwise competence and power would soon be confounded, and no law would be left but the will of whatever force prevailed. On this principle the succession of the crown has always been what it now is, a hereditary succession by law: in the old line a succession by common law, in the new by statute law operating on the principles of the common law, not changing the substance but regulating the manner and naming the persons. Both kinds of law have the same force and are derived from an equal authority, flowing from the common agreement and original compact of the state, _communi sponsione reipublicæ_, and as such are equally binding on king and people alike, as long as the terms are observed and they continue the same body politic.

It is far from impossible, if we do not let ourselves be tangled in the mazes of metaphysical sophistry, to reconcile the use of a fixed rule with an occasional deviation from it — the sacredness of a hereditary principle of succession in our government with a power of changing its application in cases of extreme emergency. Even in that extremity, if we take the measure of our rights from the way we exercised them at the Revolution, the change is to be confined to the diseased part only, to the part that made the deviation necessary; and even then it is to be done without decomposing the whole civil and political mass in order to originate a new civil order out of the first elements of society.

A state without the means of some change is without the means of its conservation. Without such means it might even risk losing the very part of the constitution it most religiously wished to preserve. The two principles of conservation and correction operated strongly at the two critical periods of the Restoration and the Revolution, when England found itself without a king. At both those moments the nation had lost the binding element in its ancient building; and yet it did not pull the whole fabric down. On the contrary, in both cases they regenerated the defective part of the old constitution through the parts that were not damaged. They kept the old parts exactly as they were, so that the recovered part could be fitted to them. They acted through the ancient organised estates in the shape of their old organisation, and not through the loose _moleculæ_ of a disbanded people. Perhaps at no time did the sovereign legislature show a more tender regard for that fundamental principle of British constitutional policy than at the Revolution, when it deviated from the direct line of hereditary succession. The crown was carried a little way out of the line it had moved in before; but the new line was drawn from the same stock. It was still a line of hereditary descent, still a hereditary descent in the same blood, though a hereditary descent qualified by Protestantism. In altering the direction while keeping the principle, the legislature showed that it held the principle inviolable.

On this principle the law of inheritance had been amended before, in the old days and long before the era of the Revolution. Some time after the Conquest great questions arose about the legal principles of hereditary descent. It became doubtful whether the heir _per capita_ or the heir _per stirpes_ was to succeed; but whether the heir _per capita_ gave way when heirship _per stirpes_ took effect, or the Catholic heir when the Protestant was preferred, the inheritable principle survived with a kind of immortality through all its transmigrations —

	Multosque per annos
	Stat fortuna domûs, et avi numerantur avorum.

This is the spirit of our constitution, not only in its settled course but in all its revolutions. Whoever came in, and however he came in, whether he got the crown by law or by force, the hereditary succession was either continued or adopted.

The gentlemen of the Society for Revolutions see nothing in the Revolution of 1688 but the deviation from the constitution, and they take the deviation from the principle for the principle itself. They pay little attention to the obvious consequences of their doctrine, though they might notice that it leaves positive authority in very few of the positive institutions of this country. Once such an indefensible maxim is established — that no throne is lawful but an elective one — not one act of the princes who reigned before this era of fictitious election can be valid. Do these theorists mean to imitate certain of their predecessors, who dragged the bodies of our ancient sovereigns out of the quiet of their tombs? Do they mean to attaint and disqualify, retrospectively, every king who reigned before the Revolution, and so to stain the throne of England with the blot of a continual usurpation? Do they mean to invalidate, annul or call into question, along with the titles of the whole line of our kings, that great body of statute law that was passed under the men they treat as usurpers? To annul laws of inestimable value to our liberties, of at least as great value as any that have been passed at or since the Revolution? If kings who did not owe their crown to the choice of their people had no title to make laws, what becomes of the statute _De tallagio non concedendo_? Of the _Petition of Right_? Of the _Habeas Corpus_ Act? Do these new doctors of the rights of men presume to assert that King James the Second, who came to the crown as next of blood, by the rules of a then unqualified succession, was not to all intents and purposes a lawful king of England before he had done any of the acts that were justly construed as an abdication of his crown? If he was not, a great deal of trouble in Parliament might have been saved at the very period these gentlemen commemorate. But King James was a bad king with a good title, not a usurper. The princes who succeeded under the act of Parliament that settled the crown on the Electress Sophia and her Protestant descendants came in as much by a title of inheritance as King James did. He came in according to the law as it stood at his accession, and the princes of the House of Brunswick came to the inheritance of the crown not by election but by the law as it stood at their several accessions, by Protestant descent and inheritance, as I hope I have sufficiently shown.

The law by which this royal family is specifically destined to the succession is the act of the 12th and 13th of King William. Its terms bind "us, and our _heirs_, and our _posterity_, to them, their _heirs_, and their _posterity_", being Protestants, to the end of time, in the same words in which the Declaration of Right bound us to the heirs of King William and Queen Mary. It therefore secures both a hereditary crown and a hereditary allegiance. On what ground, except the constitutional policy of building an establishment that would secure the kind of succession which shuts out a choice of the people for ever, could the legislature have fastidiously rejected the fair and abundant choice our own country offered them, and searched in foreign lands for a foreign princess, from whose womb the line of our future rulers was to derive its title to govern millions of men through a series of ages?

The Princess Sophia was named in the Act of Settlement of the 12th and 13th of King William as a _stock_ and root of _inheritance_ for our kings, and not for her merits as a temporary administrator of a power she might never exercise, and in fact never did. She was adopted for one reason and one only: because, says the act, "the most excellent Princess Sophia, Electress and Duchess Dowager of Hanover, is _daughter_ of the most excellent Princess Elizabeth, late Queen of Bohemia, _daughter_ of our late _sovereign lord_ King James the First, of happy memory, and is hereby declared to be the next in _succession_ in the Protestant line", and so on; "and the crown shall continue to the _heirs_ of her body, being Protestants." Parliament made this limitation so that through the Princess Sophia an inheritable line would not only be continued in future, but — what they thought very material — would be connected through her with the old stock of inheritance in King James the First, so that the monarchy might keep an unbroken unity through all ages, and might be preserved, with safety to our religion, in the old approved way of descent, in which, if our liberties had once been endangered, they had often been preserved through all the storms and struggles of prerogative and privilege. They did well. No experience has taught us that our liberties can be regularly perpetuated and kept as sacred as our _hereditary right_ by any course or method other than a _hereditary crown_. An irregular, convulsive movement may be necessary to throw off an irregular, convulsive disease. But the course of succession is the healthy habit of the British constitution. Was it that the legislature, in the act limiting the crown to the Hanoverian line drawn through the female descendants of James the First, lacked a due sense of the inconveniences of having two or three, or possibly more, foreigners in succession to the British throne? No. They had a due sense of the evils that might come from such foreign rule, and more than a due sense of them. But no more decisive proof could be given of the full conviction of the British nation that the principles of the Revolution did not authorise them to elect kings at their pleasure, without any attention to the ancient fundamental principles of our government, than that they went on adopting a plan of hereditary Protestant succession in the old line with all the dangers and inconveniences of a foreign line full in their view, and pressing on their minds with the utmost force.

A few years ago I should have been ashamed to burden a matter so well able to support itself with the then unnecessary support of any argument. But this seditious, unconstitutional doctrine is now publicly taught, avowed and printed. My dislike of revolutions, whose signals have so often been given from pulpits; the spirit of change that has gone abroad; the total contempt that prevails with you, and may come to prevail with us, for all ancient institutions when they are set against a present sense of convenience or the bent of a present inclination — all these considerations make it advisable, in my opinion, to call our attention back to the true principles of our own domestic laws, so that you, my French friend, may begin to know them, and that we may go on cherishing them. We ought not, on either side of the water, to let ourselves be imposed on by the counterfeit goods that certain people, by a double fraud, export to you in illicit bottoms as raw commodities of British growth, though they are wholly alien to our soil, in order afterwards to smuggle them back into this country manufactured in the newest Paris fashion of an improved liberty.

The people of England will not ape fashions they have never tried, or go back to ones they have found mischievous on trial. They look on the legal hereditary succession of their crown as one of their rights, not one of their wrongs — as a benefit, not a grievance; as a security for their liberty, not a badge of servitude. They look on the frame of their commonwealth, _such as it stands_, as of inestimable value, and they consider the undisturbed succession of the crown a pledge of the stability and permanence of every other member of our constitution.

Before I go any further, let me take notice of some paltry tricks that the champions of election as the only lawful title to the crown are ready to use, in order to make defending the just principles of our constitution a somewhat invidious task. These sophists substitute a fictitious cause and imaginary persons, whom they suppose you to be defending, whenever you defend the inheritable nature of the crown. It is common with them to argue as if they were in conflict with some of those exploded fanatics of slavery who used to maintain — what I believe no creature maintains now — "that the crown is held by divine, hereditary, and indefeasible right". Those old fanatics of single arbitrary power laid it down as dogma that hereditary royalty was the only lawful government in the world, just as our new fanatics of popular arbitrary power maintain that a popular election is the sole lawful source of authority. The old prerogative enthusiasts did indeed speculate foolishly, and perhaps impiously too, as if monarchy had more of a divine sanction than any other form of government, and as if a right to govern by inheritance were strictly _indefeasible_ in every person found in the succession to a throne, and in every circumstance — which no civil or political right can be. But an absurd opinion about the king's hereditary right to the crown does not damage a rational one, founded on solid principles of law and policy. If all the absurd theories of lawyers and divines were to spoil the subjects they deal with, we should have no law and no religion left in the world. But an absurd theory on one side of a question is no justification for alleging a false fact, or promulgating mischievous maxims, on the other.
