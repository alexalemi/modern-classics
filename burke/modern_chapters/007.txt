Section Eight: The Sixth of October, and the Age of Chivalry

(Part 1 of 2)

The kind of anniversary sermon that a great part of what I am writing refers to will, unless men are shamed out of their present course, cheat many of them out of the principles and deprive them of the benefits of the very Revolution they are commemorating. I confess to you, Sir, that I have never liked this continual talk of resistance and revolution, or the practice of making the extreme medicine of the constitution into its daily bread. It makes the habit of society dangerously sickly; it is like taking periodic doses of corrosive sublimate, and swallowing repeated stimulants to whip up our love of liberty.

This disease of the remedy, once it becomes habitual, relaxes and wears out, by common and prostituted use, the spring of the spirit that ought to be exerted on great occasions. It was in the most patient period of Roman servitude that themes of tyrannicide were the ordinary exercise of schoolboys — _cum perimit sævos classis numerosa tyrannos_, when a numerous class destroys its savage tyrants. In the ordinary state of things it produces the worst effects in a country like ours, even on the cause of the very liberty it abuses with the dissoluteness of an extravagant theory. Almost all the high-bred republicans of my time have, after a short interval, become the most decided and thoroughgoing courtiers. They soon left the business of a tedious, moderate but practical resistance to those of us whom, in the pride and intoxication of their theories, they had dismissed as little better than Tories. Hypocrisy naturally delights in the most sublime speculations, for since it never intends to go beyond speculation, it costs nothing to make them magnificent. But even where levity rather than fraud was to be suspected in these ranting theories, the outcome has been much the same. These professors, finding their extreme principles do not apply to cases that call only for a qualified — one might say a civil and legal — resistance, offer no resistance at all in such cases. With them it is a war, or a revolution, or it is nothing. Finding their political schemes unsuited to the state of the world they live in, they often come to think lightly of all public principle, and are ready for their part to abandon for a very trivial interest what they have decided is of very trivial value. Some, indeed, are of a steadier and more persevering nature; but these are eager politicians outside Parliament, who have little temptation to give up their favourite projects. They always have some change in the Church or the State, or both, in view. When that is the case they are always bad citizens and thoroughly unreliable connections. For, regarding their theoretical designs as of infinite value and the actual arrangement of the state as of none, they are at best indifferent to it. They see no merit in the good management of public affairs and no fault in the bad; they rather rejoice in the bad, as more favourable to a revolution. They see no merit or demerit in any man, or any action, or any political principle, except so far as it advances or retards their design of change. So they take up one day the most violent and strained ideas of prerogative and another the wildest democratic ideas of freedom, and pass from the one to the other without any regard to cause, or person, or party.

In France you are now in the crisis of a revolution, in transit from one form of government to another; you cannot see that kind of man in quite the same situation in which we see him in this country. With us he is militant, with you he is triumphant, and you know how he can act when his power matches his will. I would not be supposed to confine these observations to any one description of men, or to include all men of any description in them — far from it. I am as incapable of that injustice as I am of keeping terms with those who profess principles of extremes, and who under the name of religion teach little else than wild and dangerous politics. The worst of these politics of revolution is this: they temper and harden the breast in order to prepare it for the desperate strokes that are sometimes needed on extreme occasions. But since those occasions may never arrive, the mind takes a taint for nothing, and the moral feelings suffer not a little when no political purpose at all is served by the corruption. This sort of people are so taken up with their theories about the rights of man that they have entirely forgotten his nature. Without opening a single new avenue to the understanding, they have succeeded in blocking up the ones that lead to the heart. They have perverted, in themselves and in those who listen to them, all the well-placed sympathies of the human breast.

This famous sermon of the Old Jewry breathes nothing but this spirit through the whole of its political part. Plots, massacres, assassinations seem to some people a trivial price for obtaining a revolution. A cheap, bloodless reformation, a guiltless liberty, seems flat and insipid to their taste. There must be a great change of scene; there must be a magnificent stage effect; there must be a grand spectacle to rouse an imagination grown torpid with the lazy enjoyment of sixty years of security and the still less stimulating repose of public prosperity. The preacher found all of it in the French Revolution. It sends a youthful warmth through his whole frame. His enthusiasm kindles as he goes on, and by the time he reaches his peroration it is in a full blaze. Then, surveying from the Pisgah of his pulpit the free, moral, happy, flourishing and glorious state of France, as though it were a bird's-eye landscape of a promised land, he breaks out into this rapture:

"What an eventful period is this! I am _thankful_ that I have lived to it; I could almost say, _Lord, now lettest thou thy servant depart in peace, for mine eyes have seen thy salvation_. — I have lived to see a _diffusion_ of knowledge which has undermined superstition and error. — I have lived to see _the rights of men_ better understood than ever, and nations panting for liberty which seemed to have lost the idea of it. — I have lived to see _thirty millions of people_, indignant and resolute, spurning at slavery, and demanding liberty with an irresistible voice; _their king led in triumph, and an arbitrary monarch surrendering himself to his subjects_."

Footnote: Another of these reverend gentlemen, who witnessed some of the spectacles Paris has lately exhibited, expresses himself thus: "_A king dragged in submissive triumph by his conquering subjects_ is one of those appearances of grandeur which seldom rise in the prospect of human affairs, and which, during the remainder of my life, I shall think of with wonder and gratification." These gentlemen agree marvellously in their feelings.

Before I go further, I should remark that Dr Price seems rather to overrate the great acquisitions of light he has obtained and spread abroad in this age. The last century seems to me to have been quite as enlightened. It had, though in another place, a triumph as memorable as Dr Price's, and some of the great preachers of that period shared in it as eagerly as he has shared in the triumph of France. At the trial of the Reverend Hugh Peters for high treason, evidence was given that when King Charles was brought to London for trial, the Apostle of Liberty of that day conducted the _triumph_. "I saw," says the witness, "his Majesty in the coach with six horses, and Peters riding before the king _triumphing_." Dr Price, when he talks as if he had made a discovery, is only following a precedent. For after the king's trial began, this forerunner, the same Dr Peters, ended a long prayer in the royal chapel at Whitehall — he had chosen his place very triumphantly — by saying: "I have prayed and preached these twenty years; and now I may say with old Simeon, _Lord, now lettest thou thy servant depart in peace, for mine eyes have seen thy salvation_." Peters did not get the fruits of his prayer, for he neither departed as soon as he wished nor in peace. He became — and I heartily hope none of his followers in this country may become the same — a sacrifice himself to the triumph he had led as high priest. At the Restoration they dealt with this poor good man perhaps too hard. But we owe it to his memory and his sufferings to say that he had as much illumination and as much zeal, and had as effectively undermined all _the superstition and error_ that might impede the great business he was engaged in, as any who follow him and repeat after him in this age, which would claim for itself an exclusive title to the knowledge of the rights of men and all the glorious consequences of that knowledge.

Footnote: State Trials, Vol. II, pp. 360, 363.

After this sally by the preacher of the Old Jewry, which differs only in place and time from the rapture of 1648 and agrees perfectly with its spirit and its letter, the Revolution Society — the fabricators of governments, the heroic band of _cashierers_ of _monarchs_, electors of sovereigns and leaders of kings in triumph — strutting in the proud consciousness of that diffusion of knowledge of which every member had received so large a share in the handout, were in haste to make a generous diffusion of the knowledge they had received for nothing. To make this bountiful communication they adjourned from the church in the Old Jewry to the London Tavern, where the same Dr Price, in whom the fumes of his oracular tripod had not entirely evaporated, moved and carried the resolution, or address of congratulation, that Lord Stanhope forwarded to the National Assembly of France.

Here I find a preacher of the Gospel profaning the beautiful and prophetic ejaculation commonly called the _Nunc dimittis_, uttered at the first presentation of our Saviour in the temple, and applying it, with an inhuman and unnatural rapture, to perhaps the most horrid, atrocious and afflicting spectacle ever exhibited to the pity and indignation of mankind. This "_leading in triumph_", a thing unmanly and irreligious even in its best form, which fills our preacher with such unhallowed transports, must, I believe, shock the moral taste of every well-born mind. Several Englishmen were the stupefied and indignant spectators of that triumph. Unless we have been strangely deceived, it was a spectacle far more like a procession of American savages entering Onondaga after some of their murders — called victories — and leading their captives into huts hung round with scalps, overwhelmed by the jeers and blows of women as ferocious as themselves, than it was like the triumphal pomp of a civilised martial nation. If indeed a civilised nation, or any men with a sense of generosity, could be capable of a personal triumph over the fallen and the afflicted.

This, my dear Sir, was not the triumph of France. I must believe that as a nation it overwhelmed you with shame and horror. I must believe that the National Assembly find themselves in a state of the greatest humiliation at not being able to punish the authors of this triumph or the actors in it, and that they are in a situation in which any inquiry they may make into it must be destitute even of the appearance of liberty or impartiality. Their apology lies in their situation; but when we approve of what they _must_ bear, that is in us the degenerate choice of a corrupted mind.

With a forced appearance of deliberation, they vote under the dominion of a stern necessity. They sit, as it were, in the heart of a foreign republic. They reside in a city whose constitution has come neither from their king's charter nor from their own legislative power. There they are surrounded by an army raised neither by the authority of their crown nor by their own command, and which, if they ordered it to dissolve itself, would instantly dissolve them. There they sit, after a gang of assassins has driven away some hundreds of the members, while those who hold the same moderate principles, with more patience or better hope, remain exposed every day to outrageous insult and murderous threats. There a majority — sometimes real, sometimes pretended, and itself a captive — compels a captive king to issue as royal edicts, at third hand, the polluted nonsense of their most licentious and giddy coffee houses. It is notorious that all their measures are decided before they are debated. It is beyond doubt that under the terror of the bayonet, the lamp-post and the torch at their houses, they are obliged to adopt all the crude and desperate measures suggested by clubs made up of a monstrous medley of every condition, tongue and nation. Among them are men in comparison with whom Catiline would be thought scrupulous and Cethegus a man of sobriety and moderation. Nor is it only in these clubs that public measures are deformed into monsters. They undergo a preliminary distortion in academies set up as so many seminaries for the clubs in every place of public resort. In these meetings of every kind, every counsel is taken for the mark of superior genius in proportion as it is daring and violent and treacherous. Humanity and compassion are ridiculed as the fruits of superstition and ignorance. Tenderness towards individuals is regarded as treason against the public. Liberty is always to be reckoned perfect in proportion as property is made insecure. Amid assassination, massacre and confiscation, committed or intended, they are drawing up plans for the good order of future society. Embracing the carcasses of base criminals and promoting their relations on the strength of their crimes, they drive hundreds of virtuous people to the same end by forcing them to live either by beggary or by crime.

The Assembly, which is their instrument, plays the farce of deliberation before them with as little decency as liberty. They act like the comedians at a fair before a riotous audience. They act amid the tumultuous shouting of a mixed mob of ferocious men and of women lost to shame, who, as their insolent fancy takes them, direct, control, applaud and hiss them off, and sometimes mingle with them and take seats among them, domineering over them with a strange mixture of servile impudence and proud, presumptuous authority. Having inverted order in everything, they have put the gallery in the place of the House. This assembly, which overthrows kings and kingdoms, has not even the face and appearance of a grave legislative body — _nec color imperii, nec frons erat ulla senatûs_: no colour of empire, no countenance of a senate.

They have been given a power like that of the Evil Principle, to subvert and destroy, but none to construct, except such machines as may be fitted for further subversion and further destruction. Who is there that admires and is attached from the heart to national representative assemblies who must not turn with horror and disgust from such a profane burlesque and abominable perversion of that sacred institution? Lovers of monarchy and lovers of republics must alike abhor it. The members of your Assembly must themselves groan under a tyranny of which they have all the shame, none of the direction and little of the profit. I am sure many of the members who make up even the majority of that body must feel as I do, notwithstanding the applause of the Revolution Society.

Miserable king! Miserable assembly! How silently scandalised that assembly must be by the members among them who could call a day that seemed to blot the sun out of heaven "_un beau jour_"! How inwardly indignant at hearing others think fit to declare to them that "the vessel of the state would fly forward in her course towards regeneration with more speed than ever", driven by the stiff gale of treason and murder that preceded our preacher's triumph! What must they have felt when, with outward patience and inward indignation, they heard of the slaughter of innocent gentlemen in their own houses that "the blood spilled was not the most pure"! What must they have felt when, besieged by complaints of disorders that shook their country to its foundations, they were compelled to tell the complainants coolly that they were under the protection of the law, and that they would petition the king — the captive king — to have the laws enforced for their protection, at a time when that captive king's enslaved ministers had formally notified them that there was neither law nor authority nor power left to protect anybody! What must they have felt at being obliged, by way of new year's congratulation, to ask their captive king to forget the stormy period of the last year, on account of the great good _he_ was likely to produce for his people — the practical demonstrations of their loyalty being adjourned until that good should be completely attained, while they assured him of their obedience for a time when he would no longer have any authority to command!

This address was made with much good nature and affection, no doubt. But among the revolutions in France we must count a considerable revolution in their ideas of politeness. In England we are said to learn our manners at second hand from your side of the water, and to dress our behaviour in French frippery. If so, we are still in the old cut, and have not yet conformed so far to the new Parisian mode of good breeding as to think it quite the most refined strain of delicate compliment — whether of condolence or congratulation — to tell the most humiliated creature that crawls on the earth that great public benefits have been derived from the murder of his servants, the attempted assassination of himself and of his wife, and the mortification, disgrace and degradation he has personally suffered. It is a topic of consolation that our prison chaplain at Newgate would be too humane to use to a criminal at the foot of the gallows. I should have thought the hangman of Paris, now that he is liberalised by a vote of the National Assembly and allowed his rank and his coat of arms in the Herald's College of the rights of men, would be too generous a man, too gallant, too full of a sense of his new dignity, to employ that cutting consolation on any of the persons whom the crime of _lèze-nation_ might bring under the administration of his _executive powers_.

A man has fallen indeed when he is flattered like this. The soothing draught of oblivion, drugged in this way, is well calculated to preserve a galling wakefulness and to feed the living ulcer of a corroding memory. To administer the opiate of amnesty powdered with all the ingredients of scorn and contempt is to hold to his lips, instead of "the balm of hurt minds", the cup of human misery filled to the brim, and to force him to drink it to the dregs.

Yielding to reasons at least as forcible as those so delicately urged in the new year compliment, the king of France will probably try to forget these events, and that compliment along with them. But history, which keeps a lasting record of all our acts and exercises her awful censure over the proceedings of sovereigns of every sort, will forget neither those events nor the era of this liberal refinement in the dealings of mankind. History will record that on the morning of the sixth of October, 1789, the king and queen of France, after a day of confusion, alarm, dismay and slaughter, lay down under the pledged security of public faith to give nature a few hours of respite and troubled, melancholy sleep. From that sleep the queen was first startled by the voice of the sentinel at her door, crying out to her to save herself by flight — that this was the last proof of fidelity he could give — that they were upon him, and he was a dead man. He was cut down instantly. A band of cruel ruffians and assassins, reeking with his blood, rushed into the queen's chamber and pierced with a hundred strokes of bayonet and dagger the bed from which this persecuted woman had only just had time to fly, almost naked, and had escaped by ways unknown to the murderers, to seek refuge at the feet of a king and husband who was not himself secure of his life for a moment.
