Section Five: What France Might Have Done Instead

You might, if you had chosen, have profited by our example and given your recovered freedom a matching dignity. Your privileges, though they had lapsed, were not lost to memory. Your constitution, it is true, had suffered waste and dilapidation while you were out of possession of it; but you still had the walls of a noble and venerable castle in some parts, and its foundations everywhere. You might have repaired those walls; you might have built on those old foundations. Your constitution was suspended before it was perfected, but you had the elements of a constitution very nearly as good as anyone could wish. In your old estates you had that variety of parts corresponding to the various descriptions of people your community was happily composed of. You had all that combination and all that opposition of interests, that action and counteraction, which in the natural world and in the political world alike draws the harmony of the universe out of the reciprocal struggle of discordant powers. These opposed and conflicting interests, which you regarded as such a great blemish in your old constitution and in our present one, interpose a healthy check on all hasty resolutions. They make deliberation a matter not of choice but of necessity. They make every change a subject of _compromise_, which naturally breeds moderation. They produce _temperaments_ that prevent the grievous evil of harsh, crude, unqualified reformations and make all the headlong exertions of arbitrary power, whether by the few or by the many, for ever impracticable. Through that diversity of members and interests, general liberty had as many securities as there were separate points of view in the several orders; while by pressing the whole down with the weight of a real monarchy, the separate parts would have been kept from warping and springing out of their allotted places.

You had all these advantages in your ancient estates, and yet you chose to act as though you had never been formed into a civil society and had everything to begin over again. You began badly, because you began by despising everything that belonged to you. You set up in trade without any capital. If the last few generations of your countrymen seemed to you to have little lustre, you might have passed them over and drawn your claims from an earlier race of ancestors. Under a pious preference for those ancestors your imagination would have made them into a standard of virtue and wisdom above the common practice of the moment, and you would have risen towards the example you aspired to imitate. In respecting your forefathers you would have been taught to respect yourselves. You would not have chosen to regard the French as a people of yesterday, a nation of low-born, servile wretches until the emancipating year of 1789. You would not have been content, in order to furnish your apologists over here with an excuse for several of your enormities at the expense of your own honour, to be represented as a gang of Maroons suddenly broken loose from the house of bondage, and therefore to be forgiven for abusing a liberty you were unused to and ill fitted for.

Would it not have been wiser, my worthy friend, to be thought what I for one have always thought you: a generous and gallant nation, long misled to its own disadvantage by its high and romantic feelings of fidelity, honour and loyalty; that events had gone against you, but that you were not enslaved through any mean or servile disposition; that even in your most devoted submission you were moved by a principle of public spirit, and that it was your country you worshipped, in the person of your king? If you had given it to be understood that in the delusion of this amiable error you had gone further than your wise ancestors, and that you were resolved to resume your ancient privileges while keeping the spirit of your ancient and your recent loyalty and honour — or if, distrusting yourselves and not clearly making out the almost obliterated constitution of your ancestors, you had looked across to your neighbours in this country, who have kept alive the ancient principles and models of the old common law of Europe, improved and adapted to its present state — then by following wise examples you would have given new examples of wisdom to the world. You would have made the cause of liberty venerable in the eyes of every worthy mind in every nation. You would have shamed despotism off the earth by showing that freedom is not only reconcilable with law but, when it is well disciplined, an ally of it. You would have had a revenue that was productive without being oppressive. You would have had a flourishing commerce to feed it. You would have had a free constitution, a powerful monarchy, a disciplined army, a reformed and respected clergy; a nobility with its edges taken off but its spirit intact, to lead your virtue and not to smother it; a liberal order of commons to rival that nobility and to recruit it; and a protected, contented, industrious and obedient people, taught to look for and to recognise the happiness that virtue can find in every condition of life. That is where the true moral equality of mankind lies — not in that monstrous fiction which, by putting false ideas and vain expectations into the heads of men destined to travel the obscure path of laborious life, serves only to aggravate and embitter the real inequality it can never remove, and which the order of civil life establishes as much for the benefit of those it must leave in a humble state as of those it is able to raise to a more splendid condition, though not a happier one. You had a smooth and easy career of happiness and glory laid open before you, beyond anything recorded in the history of the world; but you have shown that difficulty is good for man.

Add up your gains. See what has been got by those extravagant and presumptuous theories that taught your leaders to despise all their predecessors and all their contemporaries, and even to despise themselves, until the moment when they became genuinely despicable. By following those false lights France has bought undisguised calamities at a higher price than any nation ever paid for the most unequivocal blessings. France has bought poverty with crime. France has not sacrificed her virtue to her interest; she has abandoned her interest in order to prostitute her virtue. Every other nation has begun the building of a new government, or the reform of an old one, by establishing some rite of religion or other, or by enforcing it more exactly. Every other people has laid the foundations of civil freedom in stricter manners and a more austere and masculine system of morality. France, when she slackened the reins of royal authority, doubled the licence of a ferocious dissoluteness in manners and an insolent irreligion in opinion and practice, and has spread through every rank of life, as if she were sharing out a privilege or opening up some benefit that had been kept back, all the unhappy corruptions that used to be the diseases of wealth and power. This is one of the new principles of equality in France.

France, through the treachery of her leaders, has utterly disgraced the tone of mild counsel in the cabinets of princes and stripped it of its most powerful arguments. She has sanctified the dark, suspicious maxims of tyrannical distrust, and taught kings to tremble at what will hereafter be called the delusive plausibilities of moral politicians. Sovereigns will now regard anyone who advises them to place unlimited confidence in their people as subverters of their thrones, as traitors aiming at their destruction by leading their easy good nature, under specious pretexts, into admitting combinations of bold and faithless men to a share of their power. This alone, if there were nothing else, is an irreparable calamity to you and to mankind. Remember that your Parliament of Paris told your king that in calling the estates together he had nothing to fear but the extravagant excess of their zeal in providing for the support of the throne. It is right that these men should hide their heads. It is right that they should bear their part in the ruin their counsel has brought on their sovereign and their country. Such sanguine assurances tend to lull authority to sleep, to encourage it to plunge rashly into perilous adventures of untried policy, to neglect the provisions, preparations and precautions that distinguish benevolence from imbecility, and without which no man can answer for the beneficial effect of any abstract plan of government or of freedom. For want of these, they have seen the medicine of the state corrupted into its poison. They have seen the French rebel against a mild and lawful monarch with more fury, outrage and insult than any people has ever been known to rise against the most illegal usurper or the most bloodthirsty tyrant. Their resistance was made to concession; their revolt was from protection; their blow was aimed at a hand held out with favours, graces and immunities in it.

This was unnatural. The rest follows in order. They have found their punishment in their success. Laws overturned; courts subverted; industry without vigour; commerce dying; the revenue unpaid and yet the people impoverished; a church plundered and a state no better off for it; civil and military anarchy made into the constitution of the kingdom; everything human and divine sacrificed to the idol of public credit, and national bankruptcy the consequence. And to crown it all, the paper securities of a new, precarious, tottering power — the discredited paper securities of impoverished fraud and beggared plunder — held out as a currency to support an empire, in place of the two great recognised metals that stand for the lasting conventional credit of mankind, and which vanished and hid themselves in the earth they came from as soon as the principle of property, whose creatures and representatives they are, was systematically subverted.

Were all these dreadful things necessary? Were they the inevitable results of a desperate struggle by determined patriots, forced to wade through blood and tumult to the quiet shore of a tranquil and prosperous liberty? No. Nothing of the kind. The fresh ruins of France, which shock our feelings wherever we turn our eyes, are not the devastation of a civil war. They are the sad but instructive monuments of rash and ignorant counsel in a time of profound peace. They are the display of an authority that is inconsiderate and presumptuous because it has been unresisted and irresistible. The men who have squandered away the precious treasure of their crimes in this manner, who have made this prodigal and wild waste of public evils — the last stake reserved for the ultimate ransom of a state — met on their way with little opposition, or rather with none at all. Their whole march was more like a triumphal procession than the progress of a war. Their pioneers went before them and demolished everything and laid it level at their feet. Not one drop of _their_ blood have they shed for the country they have ruined. They have sacrificed nothing to their projects of any more consequence than their shoe buckles, while they were imprisoning their king, murdering their fellow citizens, and plunging thousands of worthy men and worthy families into tears and poverty and distress. Their cruelty has not even been the base product of fear. It has been the effect of their sense of perfect safety, in authorising treasons, robberies, rapes, assassinations, massacres and burnings throughout their harassed land. But the cause of it all was plain from the beginning.
