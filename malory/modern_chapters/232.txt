Chapter 37: How Sir Tristram, Sir Palomides, and Sir Dinadan were captured and thrown into prison

Now we will speak of Sir Lucan the butler, who by chance came riding to the same place where Sir Tristram was, and he came in with no other purpose than to ask for lodging. Then the porter asked what his name was.

"Tell your lord that my name is Sir Lucan, the butler, a knight of the Round Table."

So the porter went to Sir Darras, the lord of the place, and told him who was there asking for lodging.

"No, no," said Sir Daname, who was nephew to Sir Darras. "Tell him that he shall not be lodged here. But let him know that I, Sir Daname, will meet him at once, and tell him to make himself ready."

So Sir Daname came out on horseback, and there they met with spears, and Sir Lucan struck Sir Daname down over his horse's crupper, and then he fled into the house, and Sir Lucan rode after him, and asked for him many times.

Then Sir Dinadan said to Sir Tristram, "It is a shame to see the cousin of the lord of this place trampled like that."

"Wait," said Sir Tristram, "and I will put it right."

And meanwhile Sir Dinadan got on horseback, and he jousted with Lucan the butler, and there Sir Lucan ran Dinadan through the thick of the thigh, and so he rode on his way. And Sir Tristram was angry that Sir Dinadan was hurt, and followed after him, meaning to avenge him; and within a while he overtook Sir Lucan, and told him to turn. And so they met, so that Sir Tristram wounded Sir Lucan exceedingly badly and gave him a fall. With that came Sir Uwain, a well-born knight, and when he saw Sir Lucan so hurt he called on Sir Tristram to joust with him.

"Fair knight," said Sir Tristram, "tell me your name, I beg you."

"Sir knight, you may be sure my name is Sir Uwain le Fise de Roy Ureine, that is, the son of King Uriens."

"Ah," said Sir Tristram, "if I had my way I would never fight you, at any time."

"You shall not have your way," said Sir Uwain, "for you shall fight me."

And then Sir Tristram saw no other remedy, but rode against him, and overthrew Sir Uwain and wounded him in the side, and so he went back to his lodging again. And when Sir Dinadan understood that Sir Tristram had wounded Sir Lucan, he would have ridden after Sir Lucan to kill him, but Sir Tristram would not allow it. Then Sir Uwain had a horse-litter made ready, and brought Sir Lucan to the abbey of Ganis; and the castle beside it was called the Castle of Ganis, and Sir Bleoberis was its lord. And at that castle Sir Lancelot had promised all his companions to meet him in the quest for Sir Tristram.

So when Sir Tristram had come to his lodging, a damsel came who told Sir Darras that three of his sons had been killed at that tournament, and two so grievously wounded that they were never likely to help themselves again. And all this was done by a noble knight who carried the black shield, and he was the one who won the prize. Then someone came and told Sir Darras that the same knight, the one who carried the black shield, was in the house. Then Sir Darras went to Sir Tristram's room, and there he found his shield and showed it to the damsel.

"Ah, sir," said the damsel, "that is the very man who killed your three sons."

Then without any delay Sir Darras put Sir Tristram, and Sir Palomides, and Sir Dinadan into a strong prison, and there Sir Tristram came near to dying of a great sickness. And every day Sir Palomides would reproach Sir Tristram over the old hatred between them. And Sir Tristram always spoke courteously and said little. But when Sir Palomides saw Sir Tristram falling sick, then he was downcast for him, and comforted him in the best way he could. And as the French book says, forty knights of Sir Darras's own kin came to him, and they would have killed Sir Tristram and his two companions, but Sir Darras would not allow that, but kept them in prison, and they had food and drink. So Sir Tristram endured great pain there, for sickness had taken hold of him, and that is the greatest pain a prisoner can have. For as long as a prisoner has his bodily health, he can endure, under the mercy of God and in hope of a good release; but when sickness touches a prisoner's body, then a prisoner may say that all his well-being is taken from him, and then he has cause to wail and to weep. And that is just what Sir Tristram did when sickness had taken hold of him, for then he fell into such sorrow that he almost killed himself.
