Chapter 26: How Sir Tristram fought with Sir Breunor and at last cut off his head

Then they took their horses and came together as if it had been thunder; and Sir Tristram struck Sir Breunor clean off his horse, and he quickly got up. And as Sir Tristram came past him again, he ran his horse through both the shoulders, so that the horse plunged here and there and fell dead to the ground. And all the while Sir Breunor ran after him to kill Sir Tristram, but Sir Tristram was quick and nimble, and got clear of his horse swiftly. And before Sir Tristram could set his shield and his sword, the other gave him three or four heavy blows. Then they rushed together like two boars, circling and sidestepping mightily and skilfully, like two noble knights. For this Sir Breunor was a proven knight, and before that had been the death of many good knights, so that it was a pity he had lasted so long.

So they fought, crashing here and there, for nearly two hours, and both were badly wounded. Then at last Sir Breunor rushed at Sir Tristram and took him in his arms, for he trusted greatly in his strength. At that time Sir Tristram was called the strongest and the tallest knight in the world; for he was said to be bigger than Sir Lancelot, but Sir Lancelot had the better wind. So at once Sir Tristram threw Sir Breunor down flat on his face, and then he unlaced his helm and struck off his head. And then all those who belonged to the castle came to him, and did him homage and swore him fealty, begging him to stay there a little while longer to undo that foul custom. Sir Tristram agreed to it. Meanwhile one of the knights of the castle rode to Sir Galahalt, the high prince, who was Sir Breunor's son, and who was a noble knight, and told him what misfortune had befallen his father and his mother.
