Sir Thomas Malory finished this book in the ninth year of the reign of Edward IV, which ran from March 1469 to March 1470, and he wrote it in prison. He says so himself: at the end of several sections he asks the reader to pray for "a knight prisoner." He was most likely the Sir Thomas Malory of Newbold Revel in Warwickshire, a landowner and former member of Parliament who spent much of the 1450s and 1460s in jail on charges that included robbery, cattle-raiding and assault. He died in 1471. England in those years was in the middle of the Wars of the Roses, and the book's last section, in which the Round Table destroys itself in a civil war, was written by a man who had lived through one.

Malory did not invent the stories. He took the long French prose romances about Arthur, Lancelot, Tristram and the Holy Grail, along with two English poems about Arthur's death, and cut, combined and retold them in English prose. When he says "as the French book says," he means a real source. Fourteen years after his death, in 1485, William Caxton printed the work at Westminster, divided it into twenty-one Books, gave every chapter a heading summarising what happens in it, added a preface, and called the whole thing Le Morte d'Arthur, the Death of Arthur, although only the last part is about Arthur's death. A manuscript that differs from Caxton in many places was found at Winchester College in 1934. This edition follows Caxton's text, because Caxton's is the book that English readers knew for four and a half centuries.

Some things will seem strange at first. Knights fight constantly, often for reasons that are hard to follow, and the jousts are told with the same moves again and again. That repetition is the form: Malory is keeping score of who is the best knight in the world, and the score matters to the plot. Many names change spelling in the original and several people share a name, including at least four women called Elaine; here each person keeps one spelling. Malory's word "worship" means honour, and his narrator often stops to say where the story is going next.

This edition retells the whole book in modern English, including the three long Books about Tristram that most selections leave out, because the ending depends on them. Caxton's preface and his chapter headings are kept, and so are Malory's own closing prayer and Caxton's colophon. The text is Alfred Pollard's 1900 edition of Caxton.
