Benjamin N. Cardozo (1870–1938) was a judge of the New York Court of Appeals, the state's highest court, when he gave these four lectures at the Yale Law School in 1921. He later became its chief judge, and in 1932 he succeeded Oliver Wendell Holmes on the Supreme Court of the United States. He was already known for opinions that changed American law, and he was known too as the rare judge who wrote well.

The lectures ask a question judges were not in the habit of answering in public: what does a judge actually do when he decides a case? "I take judge-made law as one of the existing realities of life," Cardozo says, and he sets out to describe it honestly. He names four methods a judge draws on: the method of philosophy, which follows the logic of existing rules by analogy; the method of history, which asks how a rule grew; the method of custom; and the method of sociology, which measures a rule by the welfare of society. "The final cause of law is the welfare of society." When the precedents run out, the judge must make law, but only within limits: "He legislates only between gaps." The last lecture turns to the forces a judge does not choose, the habits and sympathies below consciousness, and to the question of how far precedent should bind.

Two things help. First, "sociology" here does not mean the academic discipline. It is Cardozo's word for deciding by social welfare, the needs and standards of the community. Second, the notes are part of the argument. Cardozo read widely in the European legal philosophy of his day, Gény, Ehrlich, Duguit, Kohler and others, and cites them constantly. Where he quotes them in French or German, the quotations are left as he printed them.

This is a restored edition: Cardozo's words are unchanged. It is built from Wikisource's proofread transcription of the Yale University Press edition. That transcription was compared word by word with two scanned copies of the 1921 printing, and about forty words mistyped in it were corrected where both copies agree, "had" for "bad" and "given" for "giver" among them. It keeps the publisher's memorial leaf for Arthur P. McKinstry, in whose memory the volume was published, and sets every footnote after the paragraph that cites it. The edition also adds modern typesetting, a proper ebook, and this introduction.
