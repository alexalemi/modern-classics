The questions of the separation of Problems and Theorems, the use of super- position, and the omission of the diagonals in Book II, are general questions which I have considered by themselves. The only points, which you and I need consider, are the methods adopted in treating Right Lines, Angles, and Parallels, wherever those methods differ from Euclid's.

The first subject, then, is the Right Line. How do you define and test it ?

Niemand. As in Euclid. But we prove what Euclid has assumed as an Axiom, namely, that two right Lines cannot have a common segment.

Minos. I am glad to hear you assert that Euclid has assumed it ' as an Axiom/ for the interpolated and illogical corollary to Euc. I. ii has caused many to overlook the fact that he has assumed it as early as Prop. 4, if not in Prop. 1. What is your proof?

' For if two straight Lines ABC, ABH could have a common segment AB ; then the straight Line ^^C might be turned about its extremity A, towards the side on which BHisj so as to cutBH; and thus two straight Lines would enclose a space, which is impossible.'

Minos. You assume that, be/ore C crosses BH, the portions coinciding along AB will diverge. But, if ABH is a right Line, this will not happen till C has passed H.

Niemand. But you would then have one portion of the re- volving Line in motion, and another portion at rest.

Minos. Well, why not ?

Niemand. We may assume that to be impossible; and that, if a Line revolves about its extremity, it all moves at once.

Minos. Which, I take the liberty to think, is quite as great an assumption as Euclid's. I think the Axiom quite plain enough without any proof.

Your treatment of angles, and right angles, is the same as Euclid's, I think ?

Niemand. Yes, except that we prove that ' all right angles are equal.'

32.

Niemand. We prove as far as I. 28 as in Euclid. In order to prove I. 29, we first prove, as a Corollary to Euc. I. 20, that ' the shortest distance between two points is a straight Line.'

Minos. What is your next step ?

Niemand. A Problem (Pr. F. p. 52) in which we prove the Theorem that, of all right Lines drawn from a point to a Line, the perpendicular is the least.

Minos. We will take that as proved.

Niemand. We then deduce that the perpendicular is the shortest path from a point to a Line.

Next comes a Definition. ' By the distance of a point from a straight Line is meant the shortest path from the point to the Line.'

Minos. Have you anywhere defined the distance of one point from another?

Niemand. No.

Minos. We had better have that first.

Niemand. Very well. 'The distance of one point from another is the shortest path from one to the other.''

Minos. Might we not say ^ is the length of the right Line joining them ?'

Niemand. Yes, that is the same thing.

Minos. And similarly we may modify the Definition you gave just now.

Niemand. Certainly. ' The distance of a point from a right Line is the length of the perpendicular let fall upon it from the given point.'

Minos. No doubt. We see, then, that you propose^ as a substitute for Euclid's 12th Axiom, a new Definition, two new Axioms, and what virtually amounts to five new Theorems. In point of ' axiomaticity ' I do not think there is much to choose between the two methods. But in point of brevity, clearness, and suitability to a beginner, I give the preference altogether to Euclid's axiom.

The next subject to consider is your practical test, if any, for two given Lines meeting when produced.

Niemand. One test is that one of the Lines should meet a Line parallel to the other.

Minos. Certainly : and that will suffice in such a case as Euc. I. 44 (Pr. M. p. 60, in this book) though you omit to point out toliy the Lines may be assumed to meet. But what if the diagram does not contain ' a Line parallel to the other ' ? Look at Pr. (li) p. 69, where we are told to make, at the ends of a Line, two angles which are together less than two right angles, and where it is assumed that the Lines, so drawn, will meet. That is, you assume the truth of Euclid's liith Axiom. And you do the same thing at pp. 70, 123, 143, and 185.

Niemand. Euclid's 12th Axiom is easily proved from our Theorems.

Minos. No doubt: but you have not done it, and the omis- sion makes a very serious hiatus in your argument. It is not a thing that beginners are at all likely to be able to supply for themselves.

I have no adverse criticisms to make on the general style of the book, which seems clear and well written. Nor is it ^necessary to discuss the claims of the book to supersede Euclid, since the writer makes no such claim, but has been careful (as he states in his preface) to avoid any arrangement incompatible with Euclid's order. The chief novelty in the book is the introduction of the principle of ^equidistance/ which does not seem to me a desirable feature in a book meant for beginners : otherwise it is little else than a modified version of Euclid.
