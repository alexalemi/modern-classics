Fannie Merritt Farmer (1857–1915) grew up in Boston. As a girl she was left partly paralysed by an illness that kept her from finishing school. In her thirties she trained at the Boston Cooking School and stayed on to run it. The Boston Cooking-School Cook Book was first published in 1896. It was so successful that Farmer's name became attached to it, and in America it has been known ever since as "the Fannie Farmer cookbook." She left the school in 1902 to open one of her own, Miss Farmer's School of Cookery, and she revised the book several times. This edition gives her revision of 1910, which adds a hundred and twenty-five recipes and a hundred photographs.

The book is remembered for its measurements. Older recipes called for a handful of this, butter the size of an egg, a teacup of that. Farmer's recipes use standard cups and spoons, measured level, and the second chapter explains exactly how. That made a recipe something a beginner could repeat and get the same result. The first two chapters also set out the food science of the day, what each food is made of and what the body does with it. Then come the recipes, from bread and soups through meat, fish, vegetables and salads, puddings, cakes, confectionery and the chafing-dish. The book ends with hints for young housekeepers, suggested menus and a glossary of cooking terms.

A modern cook will notice a few things. Ovens are "hot," "moderate" or "slow," not set to a temperature, and the stove is a range burning coal, wood or gas. The food science uses the vocabulary of 1910: "proteid" is protein, and the tables of composition come from the Department of Agriculture and similar sources of the time. The quantities and methods are Farmer's, and they are left as she wrote them.

This is a restored edition, and Farmer's words and recipes are unchanged. The text is the Project Gutenberg transcription of the 1910 edition. A scan of a 1911 printing was also compared, but that printing differs from 1910 in its own small ways, so the transcription is followed. The ingredient lists and tables are set one item to a line, as they were printed. Every illustration is kept, with the caption printed beneath it, and each now has a description of what it shows, which also serves as its alt text. The index and the pages advertising Miss Farmer's school are omitted. The edition also adds modern typesetting, an ebook, and this introduction.
