First Supplement: On the Guarantee of Perpetual Peace

What provides this *guarantee* is nothing less than the great artist *Nature* herself (*natura daedala rerum*, "nature, the maker of things," as Lucretius calls her). In her mechanical course a purpose plainly shines through: to let harmony grow out of human discord, even against human will. Seen as the compulsion of a cause whose laws of operation we do not know, this is called *fate*. Seen in light of the purposiveness it shows in the course of the world, as the deep wisdom of a higher cause directed at the objective final end of the human race and determining the course of the world in advance, it is called *Providence*. We do not, strictly speaking, *recognize* Providence in these artful arrangements of nature, or even *infer* it from them. We can only, and must, *add it in thought* (as with every relation of the form of things to purposes in general), in order to form some concept of how such arrangements are possible, on the analogy of human works of art. To picture how they relate and agree with the purpose that reason prescribes to us directly (the moral one) is an idea that goes beyond all bounds for *theoretical* purposes. For practical purposes, though — for instance, with regard to the duty of *perpetual peace*, and of using that mechanism of nature to achieve it — it is dogmatic, and its reality is well founded. — When, as here, the subject is only theory (and not religion), the word *nature* is also more fitting for the limits of human reason (which must keep within the bounds of possible experience when it comes to the relation of effects to their causes). It is also *more modest* than talking of a *Providence* we can know, which means presumptuously strapping on wings like Icarus to get closer to the secret of its unfathomable purpose.

Footnote: In the mechanism of nature, to which human beings (as creatures of the senses) also belong, there appears a form that already underlies its existence, and that we can make intelligible to ourselves only by attributing to it the purpose of an author of the world who determined it in advance. We call his determining in advance (divine) *Providence* in general. Insofar as it is placed at the *beginning* of the world, we call it *founding* Providence (*providentia conditrix*: *semel iussit, semper parent*, "he commanded once, and they obey forever," as Augustine says). In the *course* of nature, maintaining it according to universal laws of purposiveness, we call it *governing Providence* (*providentia gubernatrix*). Directed further at particular purposes, which human beings cannot foresee but only guess at from the outcome, we call it *guiding* Providence (*providentia directrix*). And finally, with regard to single events taken as divine purposes, we call it no longer Providence but *dispensation* (*directio extraordinaria*). But to want to recognize such dispensations as such (since they point in fact to miracles, though the events are not called that) is a foolish presumption on the part of human beings. To infer from a single event a special principle in its efficient cause (that this event is a purpose, and not merely a side effect of nature's mechanism following from some other purpose wholly unknown to us) is absurd and full of conceit, however pious and humble the language in which it is put. — In the same way, dividing Providence (considered *materially*, according to the *objects* in the world it concerns) into *general* and *particular* is false and contradicts itself (as when people say, for example, that it takes care to preserve the species of creatures but leaves the individuals to chance). For it is called general precisely so that no single thing will be thought of as excepted from it. — What people probably meant here is a division of Providence (considered *formally*) according to the way it carries out its purpose: into *ordinary* Providence (for example, the yearly dying and reviving of nature with the change of the seasons) and *extraordinary* Providence (for example, the way ocean currents bring wood to the icy coasts, where it cannot grow, for the people who live there and could not live without it). Even though we can explain the physical and mechanical cause of these appearances perfectly well (for instance, by the wooded riverbanks of the temperate lands, into whose rivers those trees fall, to be carried farther by something like the Gulf Stream), we must not overlook the teleological cause either — the explanation by purpose, which points to the foresight of a wisdom that rules over nature. — Only the concept, common in the schools, of a divine *assistance* or cooperation (*concursus*) in producing an effect in the world of the senses must be dropped. For to try to pair things that do not belong together (*gryphes iungere equis*, "to yoke griffins with horses," as Virgil puts it), and to have the one who is himself the complete cause of the changes in the world *supplement* his own predetermining Providence during the course of the world (which would mean it had been deficient) — to say, for example, that *after God* it was the doctor who cured the patient, as though the doctor had been there as an assistant — is, *first*, self-contradictory in itself. For *causa solitaria non iuvat*: a cause that acts alone does not need help. God is the author of the doctor together with all his remedies, and so the effect must be ascribed *entirely* to God, if we insist on climbing up to the highest ultimate ground, which theoretically we cannot grasp. Or it can be ascribed *entirely* to the doctor, insofar as we follow this event through the chain of causes in the world as something explicable by the order of nature. *Second*, such a way of thinking takes away all definite principles for judging an effect. But for *moral and practical* purposes (which are directed entirely at the supersensible), the concept of the divine *concursus* is quite fitting, and even necessary — for instance, in the faith that God will make up for the lack of our own righteousness, so long as our intention was genuine, even by means we cannot understand, so that we should never slacken in our striving for the good. It goes without saying, though, that no one should try to *explain* a good action (as an event in the world) in this way. That would be a supposed theoretical knowledge of the supersensible, and so absurd.

Before we define this guarantee more closely, we will first need to look into the situation that nature has arranged for the actors on her great stage, the situation that in the end makes her guarantee of peace necessary. — Only then can we look at the way she provides it.

Her provisional arrangement consists in this: (1) she has taken care that human beings can live in every region of the earth; (2) she has driven them everywhere by means of *war*, even into the most inhospitable regions, in order to populate them; (3) by that same means she has forced them to enter into relations that are more or less lawful. — It is wonderful enough that in the cold wastes by the Arctic Ocean the moss still grows that the *reindeer* scrapes out from under the snow, so that the reindeer can itself become food, or pull the sled of the Ostyak or the Samoyed; or that even the salty sandy deserts still hold the *camel*, which seems to have been created, as it were, for traveling through them, so that they will not go unused. But the purpose shines out still more clearly when we notice how, besides the fur-bearing animals on the shores of the Arctic Ocean, seals, walruses, and whales give the people living there food from their flesh and fuel from their oil. Most wonderful of all, though, is nature's care in bringing driftwood (no one quite knows where from) to these treeless regions. Without this material the inhabitants could make neither their boats and weapons nor the huts they live in. There they have enough to do with their war against the animals that they live peacefully among themselves. — But what *drove them there* was probably nothing other than war. The first *instrument of war*, among all the animals that human beings learned to tame and domesticate during the time when the earth was being populated, was the *horse* (for the elephant belongs to a later time, the time of the luxury of states already established). In the same way, the art of growing certain kinds of grass, called *grain*, whose original nature we can no longer even recognize, and the multiplication and refinement of *fruits* by transplanting and grafting (in Europe perhaps from only two species, the crab apple and the wild pear), could arise only in the condition of states already established, where landed property was secure. This came after human beings had previously made their way, in lawless freedom, from the *hunting*, fishing, and herding life to the *farming* life; and after *salt* and *iron* were discovered. These were perhaps the first articles to be sought far and wide in trade between different peoples, and through them peoples were first brought into a *peaceful relation* with one another, and so into understanding, community, and peaceful relations even with peoples far away.

Footnote: Of all ways of life, the *hunting life* is without doubt the most opposed to a civilized constitution. For the families, which must live apart, soon become *strangers* to one another and, scattered through vast forests, soon *hostile* as well, since each needs a great deal of room to get its food and clothing. — The *prohibition of blood given to Noah* (Genesis 9:4–6) — which was often repeated, and which the Jewish Christians later even made a condition for newly converted Christians from among the pagans, though for a different reason (Acts 15:20, 21:25) — seems originally to have been nothing other than a prohibition of the *hunting life*. For in the hunting life one must often eat meat raw, and so forbidding the second meant forbidding the first along with it.

Having taken care that human beings *could* live everywhere on earth, nature at the same time despotically willed that they *should* live everywhere, even against their inclination — and without this "should" presupposing a concept of duty that would bind them to it by means of a moral law. Instead, to reach this purpose of hers, she chose war. — We see peoples whose shared language shows their shared descent, like the *Samoyeds* on the Arctic Ocean on one side and, on the other, a people with a similar language some nine hundred miles away in the *Altai* Mountains. Between them another people, a Mongol people on horseback and therefore warlike, pushed in, and so scattered that part of the stock far away from the rest, into the most inhospitable icy regions, where they would certainly not have spread of their own accord. It is the same with the *Finns* in the northernmost part of Europe, called *Lapps*, who are separated from the *Hungarians*, now just as far away but related to them in language, by Gothic and Sarmatian peoples who pushed in between. And what else could have driven the *Eskimos* in the north of America (perhaps very ancient European adventurers, a race quite different from all other Americans) and the *Pesherais* in the south, all the way to Tierra del Fuego, but war — which nature uses as a means to populate the earth everywhere? War itself, though, needs no special motive. It seems to be grafted onto human nature, and even to count as something noble, which human beings are inspired to by the love of honor, without selfish motives. So *courage in war* is judged (by American savages as much as by Europeans in the age of chivalry) to be of great value in itself — not only *when* there is war (as is fair), but also *that* there should be war. And war is often started merely to show that courage, so that an inner *dignity* is placed in war itself. Even philosophers sometimes deliver eulogies on war as a kind of ennobling of humanity, forgetting the saying of that Greek: "War is bad in that it makes more wicked people than it takes away." — So much for what nature does *for her own purpose* with regard to the human race as a class of animals.

Footnote: One might ask: if nature willed that these icy coasts should not remain uninhabited, what will become of their inhabitants if one day (as is to be expected) she no longer brings them driftwood? For it is likely that, as civilization advances, the people of the temperate zones will make better use of the wood that grows along the banks of their rivers, and will not let it fall into the rivers and be washed out to sea. I answer: the people living along the *Ob*, the Yenisei, the Lena, and so on will bring it to them by trade, and get in return the animal products in which the sea along the icy coasts is so rich — once nature has first forced peace among them.

Now comes the question that concerns what is essential in our aim of perpetual peace: "What does nature do toward this aim, with respect to the purpose that a human being's own reason makes his duty, and so to further his *moral purpose*? And how does she guarantee that what human beings *ought* to do under the laws of freedom, but do not do, they *will* do — secured, without harm to that freedom, by a compulsion of nature as well — and this in all three relations of public right: *civil right*, *the right of nations*, and *cosmopolitan right*?" — When I say of nature that *she wills* that this or that should happen, that does not mean she imposes on us a duty to do it (only practical reason, free of compulsion, can do that). It means that she *does* it herself, whether we want it or not (*fata volentem ducunt, nolentem trahunt*: "the fates lead the willing and drag the unwilling," as Seneca put it).

1. Even if a people were not forced by internal discord to submit to the constraint of public laws, war from outside would force it to. For, by the arrangement of nature just described, every people finds itself next to another people pressing on it, against which it must form itself internally into a *state*, so as to be armed as a *power* against it. Now the *republican* constitution is the only one perfectly suited to the right of human beings. But it is also the hardest to establish, and harder still to keep — so hard that many people maintain it would have to be a state of *angels*, since human beings, with their selfish inclinations, are incapable of a constitution of so sublime a form. But now nature comes to the aid of the general will grounded in reason — revered, but powerless in practice — and she does it precisely through those selfish inclinations. All that is needed is a good organization of the state (which certainly is within human power), directing those forces against one another so that one holds back the destructive effects of the others or cancels them out. The result, for reason, is then the same as if they were not there at all, and so a human being is forced to be a good citizen, even if not a morally good person. The problem of setting up a state, hard as it may sound, can be solved even for a nation of devils (if only they have understanding). It goes like this: "Given a multitude of rational beings who all want general laws for their own preservation, but each of whom is secretly inclined to exempt himself from them, so order them and so arrange their constitution that, although their private attitudes work against one another, these attitudes hold one another in check, so that in their public conduct the result is just the same as if they had no such evil attitudes." Such a problem must be *solvable*. For it does not demand the moral improvement of human beings. It demands only to know how the mechanism of nature can be used on human beings, so as to direct the conflict of their unpeaceful attitudes within a people in such a way that they force one another to submit to coercive laws, and so must bring about the state of peace in which laws have force. We can see this in the states that actually exist, still very imperfectly organized: in their outward conduct they already come very close to what the idea of right prescribes, even though inner morality is certainly not the cause of it. (Indeed, we should not expect a good constitution to come from morality; rather the reverse: we should first expect a people's good moral formation to come from a good constitution.) So the mechanism of nature, working through selfish inclinations that naturally oppose one another outwardly as well, can be used by reason as a means of making room for its own purpose, the precept of right, and so, as far as the state itself can, of promoting and securing both internal and external peace. — Here, then, it holds true that nature *wills* irresistibly that right should win supreme power in the end. What we neglect to do here will in the end come about by itself, though with a great deal of discomfort. — As the poet Bouterwek puts it: "Bend the reed too hard and it will break; and he who wants too much wants nothing."

2. The idea of the right of nations presupposes the *separate existence* of many neighboring states independent of one another. And although such a condition is in itself already a state of war (unless a federal union among them prevents hostilities from breaking out), even this condition is better, according to the idea of reason, than their being melted together by one power that outgrows the rest and turns into a universal monarchy. For laws lose more and more of their force as the reach of government grows larger, and a soulless despotism, after it has rooted out the seeds of good, finally falls into anarchy anyway. Nevertheless it is the desire of every state (or of its head) to put itself in a lasting state of peace in this way — by ruling the whole world, if possible. But *nature wills* otherwise. — She uses two means to keep peoples from mixing and to keep them apart: the difference of *languages* and of *religions*. These do bring with them a tendency toward mutual hatred and pretexts for war. But as civilization grows and human beings gradually come closer to one another in their principles, they lead to agreement in a peace that is produced and secured not, like that despotism (in the graveyard of freedom), by weakening all forces, but by their balance, in the liveliest competition among them.

Footnote: *Difference of religions*: an odd expression! Just as if one were to speak of different *moralities*. There may well be different kinds of *historical faith* — belonging not to religion but to the history of the means used to promote it, which falls in the field of scholarship — and in the same way different *religious books* (the Zend-Avesta, the Vedas, the Koran, and so on). But there is only one *religion*, valid for all human beings and at all times. Those others, then, can contain nothing but the vehicle of religion, which is accidental and can differ with differences of time and place.

3. Just as nature wisely separates the peoples that the will of every state, even on grounds of the right of nations, would like to unite under itself by cunning or force, so on the other hand she unites, through their mutual self-interest, peoples whom the concept of cosmopolitan right would not have protected against violence and war. It is the *spirit of commerce*, which cannot coexist with war, and which sooner or later takes hold of every people. For of all the powers (means) at the disposal of a state, the *power of money* is probably the most reliable, and so states find themselves driven (certainly not by moral motives) to promote the noble cause of peace, and, wherever in the world war threatens to break out, to head it off by mediation, just as if they were in a permanent alliance for that purpose. For great alliances for war, by the nature of the case, can come about only very rarely, and succeed still more rarely. — In this way nature guarantees perpetual peace through the mechanism of human inclinations themselves. The certainty is not, of course, enough to *predict* its future (theoretically). But it is enough for practical purposes, and it makes it a duty to work toward this goal, which is not merely a fantasy.
