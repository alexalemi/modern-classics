Section Two: The Definitive Articles for Perpetual Peace Between States

(Part 2 of 2)

Second Definitive Article for Perpetual Peace

*"The right of nations shall be founded on a federation of free states."*

Peoples, as states, can be judged like individual human beings. In their state of nature (that is, when they are independent of external laws) they injure one another simply by existing side by side. Each of them, for the sake of its own security, can and ought to demand that the other enter with it into a constitution like a civil constitution, under which each can be guaranteed its right. This would be a *league of nations*, but it would not have to be a state of nations. That, indeed, would be a contradiction. Every state involves the relation of a *superior* (the one who makes the laws) to an *inferior* (the one who obeys, namely the people). But many peoples in one state would make up only one people. And that contradicts our assumption, since we are considering here the right of *peoples* against one another, insofar as they make up so many different states and are not to be melted together into one.

Now, we look with deep contempt on the attachment of savages to their lawless freedom — their preference for brawling with one another endlessly rather than submitting to a lawful constraint they would set up themselves, and so their preference for mad freedom over rational freedom. We regard it as crudeness, boorishness, and a brutish degradation of humanity. So, one would think, civilized peoples (each united into a state of its own) would hurry to get out of so degraded a condition, the sooner the better. Instead, every *state* places its majesty (for "the majesty of the people" is an absurd expression) precisely in not being subject to any external lawful constraint at all. And the glory of its ruler consists in having many thousands at his command, ready to let themselves be sacrificed for a cause that is no concern of theirs, while he himself need not run any risk. The difference between the European savages and the American ones lies chiefly in this: many tribes of the Americans have been eaten outright by their enemies, while the Europeans know a better use for the people they conquer than eating them. They prefer to add them to the number of their subjects, and so to the supply of tools for still more far-reaching wars.

Given the wickedness of human nature, which shows itself openly in the free relations between peoples (while in the civil, lawful condition it is well veiled by the constraint of government), it is surprising that the word *right* has not yet been banished from the politics of war altogether as pedantic, and that no state has yet been bold enough to declare publicly that it holds this view. For *Hugo Grotius*, *Pufendorf*, *Vattel*, and the rest (sorry comforters, every one of them) are still faithfully cited to *justify* an attack. This is so although their code, whether drawn up philosophically or diplomatically, does not have, and cannot have, the slightest *legal* force (because states as such are not under any common external constraint). And there is not a single example of a state ever being moved to give up its plan by arguments armed with the testimony of such important men. — Still, this homage that every state pays to the concept of right (at least in words) proves that there is a still greater moral capacity in human beings, though it is asleep for now, to master the evil principle in themselves at last (which they cannot deny is there), and to hope that others will do the same. Otherwise the word *right* would never come into the mouths of states that want to go to war with one another, unless only to mock it, as that Gallic prince did when he declared: "It is the privilege nature has given the stronger over the weaker, that the weaker shall obey him."

The way states pursue their right can never be a lawsuit, as before an external court, but only war. Yet war, and its favorable outcome, *victory*, does not decide what is right. A *treaty of peace* does put an end to this particular war, but not to the state of war, in which a new pretext can always be found. (Nor can this state of war simply be declared unjust, since in it each party is the judge in its own case.) At the same time, what natural right says of human beings in a lawless condition — that they "ought to leave this condition" — cannot hold for states under the right of nations. As states, they already have a rightful constitution internally, and so they have outgrown the compulsion of others to bring them, according to those others' ideas of right, under a wider lawful constitution. Meanwhile reason, from the throne of the highest morally lawgiving power, condemns war as a way of settling rights absolutely, and makes the state of peace a direct duty; yet a state of peace cannot be established or secured without a treaty among peoples. — So there must be a *league* of a special kind, which can be called a *league of peace* (*foedus pacificum*). It would differ from a *peace treaty* (*pactum pacis*) in this: a peace treaty tries to end merely *one* war, while a league of peace tries to end *all* wars forever. This league does not aim at acquiring any of the power of a state. It aims only at preserving and securing the *freedom* of a state, for itself and at the same time for the other states in the league, without their having to submit, as human beings in the state of nature must, to public laws and to coercion under those laws. — It can be shown that this idea of *federation*, which is meant to extend gradually over all states and so lead to perpetual peace, can actually be carried out (that it has objective reality). For if fortune so arranges it that a powerful and enlightened people can form itself into a republic (which by its nature must be inclined toward perpetual peace), then this republic provides a center for federal union. Other states can join it, and so secure the condition of freedom among states in accordance with the idea of the right of nations, and the union can spread further and further, little by little, through more alliances of this kind.

That a people should say, "There shall be no war among us; for we mean to form ourselves into a state, that is, to set over ourselves a supreme power to make laws, to govern, and to judge, which will settle our disputes peacefully" — that is understandable. — But when this state says, "There shall be no war between me and other states, although I recognize no supreme lawgiving power that guarantees my right to me and to which I guarantee its right," then it is not at all clear what I am going to base my confidence in my right on — unless it is the substitute for the union of civil society, namely free federation. Reason must necessarily connect free federation with the concept of the right of nations, if there is to be anything left to think in that concept at all.

The right of nations understood as a right *to* go to war really cannot be thought at all. It would be a right to decide what is right not by universally valid external laws that limit the freedom of each individual, but by one-sided maxims, through force. Unless what is meant is this: that it serves people of that mind exactly right if they destroy one another, and so find perpetual peace in the wide grave that covers all the horrors of violence together with those who committed them. — For states in their relations with one another, reason allows no other way out of the lawless condition, which is nothing but war, than this: just like individual human beings, they must give up their savage (lawless) freedom, accept public coercive laws, and so form a *state of nations* (*civitas gentium*), which would of course keep growing until at last it took in all the peoples of the earth. But this is exactly what they do not want, given their idea of the right of nations; so they reject *in practice* what is right *in principle* (they reject *in hypothesi* what is correct *in thesi*). Therefore, if all is not to be lost, in place of the positive idea of *a world republic* there can only be the *negative* substitute: a *league* that wards off war, endures, and keeps expanding. Such a league can hold back the current of the hostile inclination that shies away from right, though with a constant danger that it will break out. As Virgil says of the god of war in chains: *Furor impius intus — fremit horridus ore cruento* — "Within, unholy Rage roars horribly with bloody mouth."

On it they would call on heaven, in the name of the state, for mercy for the great sin the human race still keeps committing: refusing to submit to any lawful constitution in its relations with other peoples, and preferring instead, proud of its independence, to use the barbaric means of war (by which, after all, the thing sought — the right of each state — is not settled). — The thanksgiving services held during a war for a *victory* won, the hymns sung (in good ancient Israelite style) to the *Lord of Hosts*, stand in no less glaring contrast to the moral idea of the Father of humankind. Besides showing indifference to the way peoples seek their rights against one another (which is sad enough), they add rejoicing at having destroyed a great many people, or their happiness.

Third Definitive Article for Perpetual Peace

*"Cosmopolitan right shall be limited to the conditions of universal hospitality."*

Here, as in the preceding articles, we are talking not about philanthropy but about *right*. And here *hospitality* means the right of a stranger not to be treated as an enemy by another people because he has arrived on their land. They can turn him away, if this can be done without causing his death; but so long as he behaves peacefully where he is, they may not treat him with hostility. What the stranger can claim is not a *right to be a guest* (for that, a special charitable agreement would be needed, making him a member of the household for a certain time). It is a *right to visit*, a right that belongs to all human beings: the right to present themselves to the society of others. It belongs to them by virtue of their right to the common possession of the surface of the earth. Since the earth is a sphere, people cannot scatter across it without end, but must in the end put up with living next to one another; and originally no one had more right than anyone else to be in any particular place on earth. — Uninhabitable parts of this surface, the sea and the sandy deserts, divide this community, but in such a way that the *ship*, or the *camel* (the *ship* of the desert), makes it possible for people to approach one another across these regions that belong to no one, and to use the right to the earth's surface, which belongs to the human race in common, for possible contact. The inhospitality of sea coasts (for instance, the Barbary Coast), where ships in nearby waters are robbed or stranded sailors are made slaves, or of sandy deserts (the Arab Bedouins), where coming near the nomadic tribes is treated as giving them a right to plunder, is therefore contrary to natural right. But this right of hospitality — that is, the right of foreign arrivals — extends no further than the conditions that make it possible to *attempt* contact with the native inhabitants. — In this way distant parts of the world can enter into peaceful relations with one another, relations that may eventually become publicly lawful, and so the human race can be brought ever closer at last to a cosmopolitan constitution.

Compare with this the *inhospitable* conduct of the civilized states of our part of the world, especially the trading states, and the injustice they show in *visiting* foreign lands and peoples (which to them means the same as *conquering* them) goes to terrifying lengths. America, the lands of Black Africa, the Spice Islands, the Cape, and so on were, when they were discovered, lands that belonged to no one, as far as the discoverers were concerned; for they counted the inhabitants as nothing. In the East Indies (Hindustan), under the pretext of merely intending to set up trading posts, they brought in foreign troops, and with them the oppression of the natives, the stirring up of the various states there into far-reaching wars, famine, rebellion, treachery, and the whole litany of evils that weigh on the human race, however it goes on.

China and Japan (*Nippon*), having tried such guests, have therefore wisely restricted them. China has allowed them to come to its shores but not to enter the country. Japan has allowed even the first only to a single European people, the Dutch, and even they are kept like prisoners, shut off from any contact with the natives. The worst of it (or, from the standpoint of a moral judge, the best) is that the trading states do not even get any joy out of this violence. All these trading companies are on the verge of collapse. The sugar islands, that seat of the cruelest and most calculated slavery, yield no real profit. They serve only indirectly, and for a not very praiseworthy purpose: they train sailors for navies, and so serve once more to carry on wars in Europe. And this is done by powers that make a great show of piety and, while they drink injustice like water, want to be thought of as the elect in true belief.

Footnote: To write the name of this great empire as it names itself (that is, *China*, not Sina or some similar sound), one need only consult Georgi's *Alphabetum Tibetanum*, pages 651 to 654, especially note b at the bottom. — Strictly speaking, according to the observation of Professor *Fischer* of St. Petersburg, it has no fixed name for itself. The most common is still the word *Kin*, meaning gold (which the Tibetans express as *Ser*), and so the emperor is called King of *Gold* (of the most splendid land in the world). In the empire itself the word may well sound like *Chin*, but the Italian missionaries may have pronounced it *Kin* (because of the guttural letter). — From this we can see that the land the Romans called the land of the *Seres* was China, and that silk was brought to Europe across *Greater Tibet* (probably through *Lesser Tibet* and Bukhara, by way of Persia, and so on). This leads to a number of reflections on the antiquity of this astonishing state, compared with that of Hindustan, through its connection with *Tibet* and, through Tibet, with Japan. The name Sina or Tschina, on the other hand, which the neighboring peoples are said to give the country, leads nowhere. — Perhaps the very ancient, though never well understood, contact between Europe and Tibet can also be explained by something *Hesychius*, the old Greek lexicographer, preserved for us: the cry *Konx Ompax* that the high priest called out in the Eleusinian Mysteries (see *The Travels of the Younger Anacharsis*, part 5, page 447 and following). — For according to Georgi's *Alphabetum Tibetanum*, the word *Concioa* means *God*, which is strikingly like *Konx*. *Pah-cio* (ibid., page 520), which the Greeks could easily have pronounced as *pax*, means *promulgator legis*, the one who proclaims the law: the deity spread throughout the whole of nature (also called *Cenresi*, page 177). — *Om*, which *La Croze* translates as *benedictus*, blessed, can hardly mean anything, when applied to the deity, but *the one called blessed* (page 507). Now Father *Francesco Orazio* often asked the Tibetan lamas what they meant by God (*Concioa*), and always got the answer: "*It is the assembly of all the saints*" — that is, of the blessed souls who, after many wanderings through all kinds of bodies by Lamaist rebirth, have at last returned into the deity and been transformed into *Burkhans*, beings worthy of worship (page 223). So that mysterious phrase *Konx Ompax* probably means the *holy* (*Konx*), *blessed* (*Om*), and *wise* (*Pax*) supreme being spread everywhere through the world (nature personified). Used in the Greek *mysteries*, it probably signified *monotheism* for the initiates, as opposed to the *polytheism* of the common people — although Father *Orazio* (in the place cited) scented *atheism* in it. — How that mysterious phrase came to the Greeks by way of Tibet can be explained in the way just described; and in turn this makes it probable that Europe had early contact with China by way of Tibet (perhaps even earlier than with Hindustan).

The community among the peoples of the earth, narrower or wider, has now gone so far everywhere that a violation of right in *one* place on earth is felt in *all* of them. So the idea of a cosmopolitan right is not a fantastic or exaggerated way of thinking about right. It is a necessary completion of the unwritten code of both civil right and the right of nations, turning them into the public right of human beings in general, and so a completion necessary for perpetual peace. Only under this condition may we flatter ourselves that we are continually drawing closer to it.
