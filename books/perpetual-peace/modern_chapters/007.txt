Appendix Two: On the Agreement of Politics with Morality According to the Transcendental Concept of Public Right

Suppose I set aside all the *matter* of public right, as legal scholars usually think of it (the various relations, given by experience, of human beings within a state or of states with one another). What I still have left is the *form* of *publicity*. Every claim of right contains the possibility of being made public, because without publicity there could be no justice (which can only be thought of as something that can be *publicly announced*), and so no right either, since right is conferred only by justice.

Every claim of right must be capable of publicity. And since it is quite easy to judge whether this is so in a given case — that is, whether publicity can be squared with the principles of the person acting — it can provide a criterion that is easy to use and is found *a priori* in reason. Where publicity cannot be squared with those principles, we can recognize at once, as if by an experiment of pure reason, that the claim in question (*praetensio iuris*, the claim to a right) is false, that is, contrary to right.

Once we have set aside in this way everything empirical that the concept of civil right and the right of nations contains (such as the wickedness of human nature, which makes coercion necessary), we can call the following proposition the *transcendental formula* of public right — "transcendental" because it holds prior to all experience:

*"All actions relating to the rights of other human beings are wrong if their maxim is incompatible with publicity."*

This principle should be regarded not only as *ethical* (belonging to the doctrine of virtue) but also as *juridical* (concerning the rights of human beings). For take a maxim that I cannot *let be heard* without thereby defeating my own purpose, a maxim that must be kept entirely *secret* if it is to succeed, and that I cannot *publicly acknowledge* without inevitably provoking everyone's resistance to my plan. This necessary and universal resistance of everyone against me, which can be seen *a priori*, can come from nowhere else than the injustice with which the maxim threatens everyone. — Further, the principle is merely *negative*: it serves only to recognize, by means of it, what is *not right* toward others. — Like an axiom, it is certain without proof, and it is also easy to apply, as can be seen from the following examples from public right.

1. *As regards civil right* (*ius civitatis*), that is, right within a state: here the question arises — one that many think hard to answer, and that the transcendental principle of publicity answers quite easily — "Is rebellion a legitimate means for a people to throw off the oppressive power of a so-called tyrant (*non titulo sed exercitio talis*: a tyrant not by title but by the way he uses his power)?" The people's rights have been violated, and the tyrant suffers no wrong by being dethroned; there is no doubt about that. Nevertheless, it is wrong in the highest degree for the subjects to seek their right in this way, and they can just as little complain of injustice if they were defeated in this struggle and afterward had to suffer the harshest punishment for it.

A great deal can be argued for and against here, if we try to settle the matter by a dogmatic deduction of the grounds of right. But the transcendental principle of the publicity of public right can spare itself all this long-windedness. By this principle, the people asks itself, before the civil contract is made, whether it would dare to make public the maxim of intending to rise up when occasion offers. It is easy to see that if, in founding a constitution, the people wanted to make it a condition that force might be used against the head of state in certain cases, the people would have to claim a lawful power over him. But then he would not be the head of state; or, if both were made conditions of setting up the state, no state at all would be possible — and setting one up was the people's purpose. The wrongness of rebellion is therefore clear from this: its maxim, if *openly acknowledged*, would make its own purpose impossible. So it would necessarily have to be kept secret. — But keeping it secret would not be necessary on the part of the head of state. He can say quite openly that he will punish every rebellion with the death of its ringleaders, even if they believe he was the first to break the fundamental law. For if he knows that he holds *irresistible* supreme power (which must be assumed in every civil constitution, since one who does not have power enough to protect each person in the people against every other also does not have the right to command him), then he need not worry that making his maxim known will defeat his own purpose. It fits perfectly well with this that, if the people's rebellion succeeded, the former head of state would return to the position of a subject, and would likewise have no right to start a rebellion to win his throne back — but would also have nothing to fear from being called to account for his earlier conduct of the state.

2. *As regards the right of nations.* — We can speak of a right of nations only on the assumption of some condition of right (that is, the external condition under which a right can actually be secured to a human being). For as a public right, the right of nations already contains in its very concept the public announcement of a general will that determines what belongs to each. And this *status iuridicus*, this condition of right, must come from some contract — one that need not, like the contract from which a state arises, be founded on coercive laws, but may be at most that of a *continuing free* association, like the federation of different states mentioned above. For without some *condition of right* actively binding the various persons (physical or moral) together — that is, in the state of nature — there can be nothing but private right. — Here, too, a conflict arises between politics and morality (morality taken as the doctrine of right). And here too the criterion of the publicity of maxims is easy to apply, though only on this condition: that the contract binds the states only for the purpose of keeping peace among themselves and together against other states, and by no means for making acquisitions. — The following cases of contradiction between politics and morality now arise, and with each its solution.

*a)* "If one of these states has promised the other something — help, or the cession of certain lands, or subsidies, or the like — the question is whether, in a case on which the welfare of the state depends, it can release itself from keeping its word by having itself regarded as a double person. First, it is *sovereign*, answerable to no one in its state; but then again it is merely the highest *official of the state*, who must give an account to the state. The conclusion is then that what it bound itself to in the first capacity, it is released from in the second." — But if a state (or its head) let this maxim of his be heard, then naturally every other state would either avoid it or unite with others to resist its pretensions. This proves that politics, with all its cunning, would on this footing (of openness) defeat its own purpose, and so that maxim must be wrong.

*b)* "If a neighboring power has grown to a terrifying size (*potentia tremenda*) and arouses alarm, may we assume that because it *can* oppress, it also *will*? And does that give the less powerful a right to attack it (together), even without any prior offense?" — A state that wanted to *announce* its maxim in the affirmative here would only bring on the evil more surely and more quickly. For the greater power would strike first against the smaller ones; and as for the smaller ones uniting, that is only a weak reed against anyone who knows how to use *divide et impera*, divide and rule. — This maxim of political prudence, publicly declared, therefore necessarily defeats its own purpose, and is consequently unjust.

*c)* "If a smaller state, by its position, cuts through the territory of a larger state that needs to be connected for its own preservation, is the larger state not entitled to subjugate the smaller one and unite it with its own?" — It is easy to see that the larger state must certainly not let such a maxim be heard beforehand. For either the smaller states would unite in good time, or other powerful states would fight over this prey. So openness makes the maxim unworkable — a sign that it is unjust, and possibly unjust to a very high degree. For a small object of injustice does not prevent the injustice shown in seizing it from being very great.

3. *As regards cosmopolitan right*, I pass over it here in silence. Because it is analogous to the right of nations, its maxims are easy to state and to judge.

* * *

In the principle that the maxims of the right of nations are incompatible with publicity, we now have a good mark of the *disagreement* of politics with morality (as the doctrine of right). But we also need to be taught the condition under which the maxims of politics *agree* with the right of nations. For we cannot conclude the other way around, that whatever maxims are compatible with publicity are therefore also just — because whoever has decisive superior power has no need to keep his maxims secret. — The condition that makes any right of nations possible at all is that a *condition of right* exist first. For without it there is no public right; all right that one might think of outside it (in the state of nature) is merely private right. Now we saw above that a federative condition of states, aimed solely at keeping war away, is the only *condition of right* compatible with their *freedom*. So politics can agree with morality only in a federative union (which is therefore given and necessary *a priori*, according to principles of right). And all political prudence has as its rightful basis the establishment of such a union, in the widest possible scope; without that end, all its cleverness is folly and veiled injustice. — Now this pseudo-politics has its own *casuistry*, worthy of the best Jesuit school. There is the *reservatio mentalis*, the mental reservation: drawing up public treaties in terms one can interpret to one's advantage whenever one likes (for instance, the distinction between the *status quo de fait*, the actual state of affairs, and *de droit*, the state of affairs in right). There is *probabilism*: inventing evil intentions in others, or making the probability that they might gain the upper hand a legal ground for undermining other, peaceful states. And finally there is the *peccatum philosophicum* (the *peccatillum*, the trifle): treating it as a small, easily forgiven matter to swallow up a *small* state, if a much *larger* one gains by it, for what is supposed to be the greater good of the world.

Footnote: Evidence for such maxims can be found in Privy Councillor Garve's essay *On the Connection of Morality with Politics* (1788). This worthy scholar admits at the very beginning that he cannot give a satisfactory answer to this question. But to approve of the maxims anyway, while confessing that he cannot fully dispose of the objections raised against them, seems to grant more indulgence to those who are very much inclined to abuse them than would be advisable.

What gives politics its opening here is its double-tongued way with morality: using one branch of morality or the other for its own purposes. — Both love of humanity and respect for the *rights* of human beings are duties. But the first is only a *conditional* duty, while the second is an *unconditional*, absolutely commanding duty. Anyone who wants to give himself over to the sweet feeling of doing good must first be fully certain that he has not violated it. Politics easily agrees with morality in the first sense (as ethics), in order to hand over the rights of human beings to their superiors. But with morality in the second sense (as the doctrine of right), before which it would have to bend its knee, politics finds it advisable not to negotiate at all. It prefers to deny morality all reality and to reinterpret all duties as mere benevolence. This cunning of a politics that shuns the light could easily be defeated by philosophy, through making those maxims of politics public — if politics would only dare to allow philosophers the publicity of their own maxims.

With this in mind, I propose another transcendental and affirmative principle of public right, whose formula would be this:

*"All maxims that need publicity (in order not to fail in their purpose) agree with right and politics combined."*

For if they can reach their purpose only through publicity, they must fit the general purpose of the public (happiness), and the proper task of politics is to agree with that purpose (to make the public content with its condition). But if this purpose can be reached *only* through publicity, that is, by removing all distrust of the maxims of politics, then these maxims must also be in harmony with the right of the public; for only in this right is it possible to unite the purposes of all. — I must put off the further development and discussion of this principle to another occasion. That it is a transcendental formula, though, can be seen from the fact that all empirical conditions (the doctrine of happiness) have been removed as the matter of the law, and that it looks only to the form of universal lawfulness.

* * *

If it is a duty, and if at the same time there is well-founded hope, to make a condition of public right real, even if only by an approach that goes on forever, then *perpetual peace*, which will follow what have until now been falsely called treaties of peace (really only truces), is no empty idea. It is a task that, solved little by little, comes steadily closer to its goal — since the times in which equal progress is made will, we hope, keep getting shorter.
