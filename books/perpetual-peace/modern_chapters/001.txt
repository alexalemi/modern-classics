Section One: The Preliminary Articles for Perpetual Peace Between States

Preliminary Article 1

*"No treaty of peace shall count as a treaty of peace if it was made with a secret reservation of material for a future war."*

For then it would be nothing but a truce, a pause in the hostilities, and not *peace*. Peace means the end of all hostilities, so to attach the word *perpetual* to it is already a suspicious piece of redundancy. A treaty of peace wipes out every existing cause of a future war, even causes the parties themselves may not yet know about, and however sharp-eyed the snooping that might one day dig them out of the archives. To hold back, in your own mind, old claims that you will think up properly only later — claims that neither side wants to mention now, because both are too exhausted to go on fighting, while one of them is quietly resolved to use the first good opportunity to press them — is a "mental reservation" (*reservatio mentalis*). That belongs to the hair-splitting moral bookkeeping the Jesuits were notorious for. It is beneath the dignity of a ruler, just as it is beneath the dignity of a ruler's minister to lend himself to that kind of reasoning — if we judge the matter as it really is.

But if, according to the enlightened ideas of modern statecraft, the true honor of a state lies in constantly increasing its power, by whatever means, then this judgment of mine will of course look academic and pedantic.

Preliminary Article 2

*"No independent state, large or small (that makes no difference here), shall be acquired by another state through inheritance, exchange, purchase, or gift."*

A state is not a piece of property (*patrimonium*), the way the ground it stands on might be. It is a society of human beings, and no one but the state itself has the right to command it or dispose of it. It has its own roots, like a tree trunk. To graft it onto another state like a cutting is to abolish its existence as a moral person and turn it into a thing. That contradicts the idea of the original contract, without which no right over a people can even be conceived. Everyone knows the danger into which Europe — for the other parts of the world have never heard of such a thing — has been brought, right down to our own day, by the prejudice in favor of this way of acquiring states: the idea that states can marry one another. It has served partly as a new kind of industry, a way to become overwhelmingly powerful through family alliances without spending any effort, and partly as a way to enlarge one's lands. The hiring out of one state's troops to another, against an enemy the two do not share, belongs in the same class. For the subjects are then used and used up like things, to be handled however one pleases.

Footnote: A hereditary kingdom is not a state that can be inherited by another state. What is inherited is only the right to govern it, which passes to another physical person. The state then acquires a ruler; the ruler, as such (that is, as someone who already possesses another kingdom), does not acquire the state.

Preliminary Article 3

*"Standing armies (miles perpetuus) shall in time be abolished altogether."*

Standing armies constantly threaten other states with war by always appearing ready and equipped for it. They goad states into outdoing one another in the number of armed men, a race that knows no limit. And because the cost of all this eventually makes peace more oppressive than a short war, standing armies themselves become the cause of wars of aggression, fought to get rid of the burden. Besides, to be paid to kill or to be killed seems to involve using human beings as mere machines and tools in the hands of someone else (the state), and that cannot easily be squared with the right of humanity in our own person. It is a completely different matter when citizens voluntarily drill with weapons from time to time, to protect themselves and their country against attack from outside. — Piling up a war chest would have the same effect. Other states would see it as a threat of war and be forced into striking first. (Of the three powers — the power of armies, the power of alliances, and the power of money — money might well be the most reliable instrument of war, if only it were not so hard to find out how much of it another state has.)

Preliminary Article 4

*"No national debt shall be contracted in connection with the foreign affairs of the state."*

There is nothing suspicious about seeking help, from outside the state or from within it, to improve the country's economy — to repair roads, found new settlements, lay up stores of grain against bad harvests, and so on. But a credit system as a machine the powers turn against one another is something else. Picture debts that grow beyond all measure, yet are always secure against any present demand for repayment (since the creditors will never all ask at once) — the ingenious invention of a trading nation in this century. Such a system is a dangerous money power: a war chest bigger than the treasuries of all other states put together, which can be exhausted only when the tax revenues finally fail — and even that day can be put off for a long time, because the borrowed money livens up trade and so feeds back into industry and earnings. This ease in making war, combined with the inclination of rulers to make it, which seems to be built into human nature, is therefore a great obstacle to perpetual peace. Prohibiting it would have to be a Preliminary Article all the more, because a national bankruptcy, which must come in the end, is bound to drag many other states into the damage through no fault of their own, and that would be a public injury to them. So other states are at least entitled to form an alliance against such a state and its pretensions.

Preliminary Article 5

*"No state shall interfere by force in the constitution and government of another state."*

For what could give it the right to? The scandal, perhaps, that the other state gives to its subjects? On the contrary, that state can serve as a warning, by the example of the great evils a people has brought on itself through its lawlessness. And in general, the bad example one free person sets another (as a *scandalum acceptum*, an offense that is taken rather than given) does that other person no injury. — It would be a different case if a state split in two through internal discord, and each part set itself up as a separate state laying claim to the whole. An outside state that gave help to one of them could not then be charged with interfering in the other's constitution, because what exists there is anarchy. But so long as this inner struggle is still undecided, interference by outside powers would violate the rights of a people that depends on no one else and is only wrestling with its own internal illness. It would itself be a scandal given, and it would make the autonomy of every state insecure.

Preliminary Article 6

*"No state at war with another shall permit itself acts of hostility that would make mutual trust impossible in a future peace: such as employing assassins (percussores) or poisoners (venefici), breaking the terms of a surrender, inciting treason (perduellio) in the enemy state, and the like."*

These are dishonorable stratagems. Some trust in the enemy's way of thinking must remain even in the middle of a war, or no peace could ever be concluded, and the hostilities would turn into a war of extermination (*bellum internecinum*). War, after all, is only the sad emergency measure, in the state of nature (where there is no court that could judge with legal force), for asserting one's right by violence. In that condition neither side can be declared an unjust enemy (since that would already presuppose a judge's verdict). Instead the *outcome* of the war decides which side is in the right, much as a so-called "judgment of God" — a trial by ordeal or by combat — was once supposed to. And between states no war of punishment (*bellum punitivum*) can even be conceived, since between them there is no relation of a superior to a subordinate. — From this it follows that a war of extermination, in which both sides might be destroyed at once and all right along with them, would allow perpetual peace only in the vast graveyard of the human race. Such a war, then, and so also the use of the means that lead to it, must be absolutely forbidden. — And that these means lead to it inevitably is clear from this: once those hellish arts come into use, since they are vile in themselves, they do not stay inside the limits of war. Using spies (*uti exploratoribus*) is one example; it exploits only the dishonesty of *others*, which can never be stamped out entirely. But such arts carry over into the state of peace as well, and so would wholly defeat its purpose.

* * *

All the laws listed above are, objectively — that is, in the intention of those in power — *prohibitive laws* (*leges prohibitivae*). But some of them are of the *strict* kind, valid regardless of circumstances (*leges strictae*), which demand that the practice be abolished *at once* (numbers 1, 5, and 6). Others (numbers 2, 3, and 4) are not exceptions to the rule of right, but in view of how the rule is to be *carried out* they are, given the circumstances, *subjectively* broader in what they permit (*leges latae*). They contain permission to *postpone* carrying them out, without losing sight of the goal. For instance, the *restoration* of freedom to certain states that were deprived of it, as described under number 2, must not be put off until a day that never comes (until the Greek calends, *ad calendas graecas*, as the emperor Augustus used to promise — the Greeks had no calends, so the day never arrives). That would mean not restoring it at all. The delay is permitted only so that the restoration does not happen in a rush and so defeat its own purpose. For the prohibition here applies only to the *manner of acquiring* a state, which is no longer to be valid, and not to the *state of possession*. That possession, though it lacks the necessary title in right, was held to be lawful by every state at the time it was acquired (the time of its putative acquisition), according to the public opinion of the day.

Footnote: Whether, besides commands (*leges praeceptivae*) and prohibitions (*leges prohibitivae*), there can also be *permissive laws* (*leges permissivae*) of pure reason has been doubted until now, and not without reason. For laws in general contain a ground of objective practical necessity, while a permission contains a ground of the practical contingency of certain actions. A *permissive law*, then, would contain a necessitation to an action — to something one cannot be necessitated to do — and if the object of the law meant the same thing in both respects, that would be a contradiction. — But in the permissive law here, the presupposed prohibition applies only to a future way of acquiring a right (for instance, by inheritance), while the release from this prohibition, that is, the permission, applies to the present state of possession. In the passage from the state of nature into the civil condition, that possession may continue, under a permissive law of natural right, as possession that is unlawful but nonetheless *honest* (*possessio putativa*, possession held in good faith). This is so even though putative possession is forbidden in the state of nature as soon as it is recognized for what it is, and even though a similar way of acquiring something is likewise forbidden in the subsequent civil condition (once the passage has been made). The right to continue in possession would not hold if such a supposed acquisition had been made inside the civil condition; for then, as an injury, the possession would have to end the moment its unlawfulness was discovered. I have meant only to draw the attention of teachers of natural right, in passing, to the concept of a *lex permissiva*, a permissive law, which presents itself of its own accord to any reason that divides things systematically. I do so especially because use is often made of it in civil (statutory) law — with this difference, that there the prohibitive law stands alone, and the permission is not built into that law as a limiting condition (as it ought to be) but is thrown in among the exceptions. — So the law says: this or that is forbidden — *unless* number 1, number 2, number 3, and so on without end. Permissions are added to the law only by chance, not according to a principle, but by groping around among cases as they come up. Otherwise the conditions would have had to be built *into the formula of the prohibitive law* itself, and it would then have become a permissive law at the same time. — It is a pity, then, that the ingenious but unsolved prize question set by the wise and acute *Count von Windischgrätz*, which pressed precisely for this, was so soon dropped. For the possibility of such a formula (one like the formulas of mathematics) is the only true touchstone of legislation that stays consistent; without it, so-called *ius certum*, certain law, will always remain a pious wish. — Otherwise we will have only *general* laws (which hold *on the whole*), but no universal ones (which hold *without exception*), as the very concept of a law seems to require.
