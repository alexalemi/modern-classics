Second Supplement: A Secret Article for Perpetual Peace

A secret article in negotiations of public right is, objectively — that is, considered by its content — a contradiction. Subjectively, though, judged by the character of the person who dictates it, there may well be a secret in it: that person may find it beneath their dignity to announce publicly that they are its author.

The only article of this kind is contained in this sentence: *The maxims of philosophers on the conditions that make public peace possible shall be consulted by states armed for war.*

Yet it seems humiliating for the lawgiving authority of a state, to which we naturally have to ascribe the greatest wisdom, to seek instruction from its subjects (the philosophers) about the principles of its conduct toward other states. And yet it is very advisable to do so. So the state will invite the philosophers *tacitly* (that is, by making a secret of it). That amounts to saying: it will let them speak freely and publicly about the universal maxims of waging war and making peace (for they will do that on their own, so long as no one forbids them). And no special agreement among states is needed for them to come to an understanding on this point; it lies already in the obligation laid on them by universal human reason, which gives the moral law. — This does not mean that the state must give the principles of the philosopher precedence over the rulings of the lawyer (who represents the power of the state). It means only that the philosopher should be heard. The lawyer has taken as his symbol the scales of right and, alongside them, the sword of justice. He usually uses the sword not merely to keep all outside influences away from the scales, but, when one pan will not sink, to throw the sword into it — *vae victis*, "woe to the conquered," as the Gallic chief said when he tossed his sword onto the scales where the Romans were weighing out their ransom. A lawyer who is not also a philosopher (in morality as well) is under the greatest temptation to do this. For his office is only to apply existing laws, not to examine whether those laws themselves might need improving. And he counts this rank of his faculty, which is in fact the lower one, among the higher ones, because it comes with power (as is the case with the other two faculties as well, theology and medicine). — Philosophy stands on a very low rung beneath this allied power. So it is said of philosophy, for instance, that she is the handmaid of theology (and the same is said with respect to the other two). — But it is not quite clear "whether she carries the torch in front of her gracious ladies or the train behind them."

That kings should philosophize, or philosophers become kings, is not to be expected, and not to be wished for either, because the possession of power inevitably corrupts the free judgment of reason. But that kings, or kingly peoples (peoples who rule themselves by laws of equality), should not let the class of philosophers disappear or fall silent, but let them speak publicly, is indispensable to both, to throw light on their business. And because this class, by its very nature, is incapable of forming gangs and political clubs, it is above suspicion of being accused of spreading propaganda.
