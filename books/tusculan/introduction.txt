In February of 45 BC Cicero's daughter Tullia died after childbirth. He was devastated by it in a way his friends found embarrassing. The same year, Caesar finished off the last armies of the opposition and ruled alone, which left Cicero, born in 106 BC and now sixty-one, with no public career and nothing to do. He went out to his country house at Tusculum, in the hills south of Rome, and wrote philosophy at a speed that is hard to credit. The Tusculan Disputations is one result. On Duties, the letter to his son, followed about a year later, and by the end of 43 BC Cicero had been hunted down and killed.

The book covers five days of argument, one topic a day. Death is not an evil. Pain can be endured. Grief is not compulsory and can be treated. The disorders of the soul have cures. Virtue by itself is enough to make a man happy. Each day opens with an essay in Cicero's own voice, addressed to Brutus, and then turns into a conversation: a young companion states a position, and Cicero spends the rest of the day taking it apart. The Latin gives the speakers no names and no labels, so this edition renders the exchanges as dialogue inside the narration rather than as a script.

Two things are worth knowing before starting. The first is that a passion here is not an emotion in the modern sense. Cicero is using the Stoic term for a disorder of the soul, a judgement gone wrong and hardened into a state, and the whole of Book Four is a catalogue of them arranged like a medical textbook, with treatments. The second is that Book Three, on grief, was written by a man who had just buried his child and who does not mention her anywhere in it. He argues that grief comes from an opinion about what has happened rather than from the loss itself, and that opinions can be changed. He is talking to himself, and the book is more moving for never saying so.

The set pieces are the best things in it. Damocles asks to change places with the tyrant Dionysius for a day and is seated under a sword hung by a single horsehair. Cicero describes finding the lost tomb of Archimedes outside Syracuse when he was a young official there, overgrown in brambles, identified by the sphere and cylinder carved on it. Spartan boys are flogged at the altar without a sound. Alcibiades weeps in front of Socrates.

The translation is from Cicero's Latin, all five books complete.
