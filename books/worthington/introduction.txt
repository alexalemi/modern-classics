Arthur Mason Worthington was born in Manchester in 1852 and went to Rugby School. In 1875 a boy there, his friend H. F. Newall, reported to the school's Natural History Society that ink drops falling by accident on smoked glass left a strange pattern of fine rings, and that drops of the same size falling from the same height always left the same one. Worthington took the question up and was still working on it more than thirty years later. He taught science at Clifton College, was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1893, and when this book appeared he was headmaster and professor of physics at the Royal Naval Engineering College at Devonport.

The difficulty is that a splash is over in a hundredth of a second, far too fast for the eye. His answer was to work in the dark, drop a measured drop, and light it for an instant with an electric spark at a chosen moment. Then he did it again with an identical drop and a spark a thousandth of a second later, and so on, stage by stage. At first he drew what the flash showed him. On 18 May 1894 he gave a Friday Evening Discourse at the Royal Institution in London called The Splash of a Drop, mostly about drops falling onto a flat plate. At the end of it he showed, for the first time, a few photographs of drops falling into liquid, taken with his friend R. S. Cole. The photographs showed him something his drawings had hidden: real splashes are irregular, and his eye had been supplying a symmetry that was not there. The lecture was printed in 1895 and is the second part of this volume.

A Study of Splashes came out in 1908 and is the book the photographs made possible. Drops, and then solid balls, fall into water, often clouded with a little milk so that the photographs show more; a smooth ball and a rough one make two quite different splashes, and he works out why. He wrote it for the general reader, and especially for the young, putting the references for physicists in footnotes. Two conventions are worth knowing before starting. Each series of pictures reads like a strip of film, but every picture is a different drop, photographed at a later moment than the one before. And the times printed under the pictures use a raised decimal point, so 0·002 sec. is two thousandths of a second. The frontispiece shows the same forms left in the steel armour-plate of a battleship where a shell went in.

This is a restored edition, not a retelling: Worthington's words are unchanged. The Project Gutenberg transcriptions it is built from give every plate an empty description. This edition adds a caption and alt text for each of the 247 plates in the two works, keeping the label and the time he printed; it also adds modern typesetting, a proper ebook, and this introduction.
