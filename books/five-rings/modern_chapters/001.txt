The Water Scroll

The spirit of my school, the Two Heavens as One, takes water as its model and practices the methods that work. So I call this the Water scroll, and in it I set down my school's way with the sword. None of this Way can be written down exactly as the mind knows it. But even if my words do not always run smoothly, the principle should come through on its own. Think over every word and every character written here. If you read it roughly, you will go wrong again and again.

I write about the principles of strategy as if it were a duel between one man and another, but it is essential to see them broadly, as the principles of a battle between ten thousand men and ten thousand. In this Way above all, if you mistake the path even a little, if you lose your way at all, you will fall onto a bad road. Just reading what I write here will not bring you to the Way of strategy. When you take what is written here and make it your own, do not think that you are reading it, or that you are learning it, and do not make an imitation of it. Treat it as a principle that you found in your own mind, always put yourself in its place, and think it through.

The Mind in Strategy

In the Way of strategy, your state of mind should be no different from your everyday mind. In daily life and in a fight alike, without the slightest change, keep your mind broad and straight. Do not draw it tight, and do not let it go slack in the least. Do not let it lean to one side; keep it in the middle. Let your mind sway quietly, and never let that swaying stop, not even for an instant. Examine this well.

When your body is still, your mind is not still. When you are moving at top speed, your mind is not hurried at all. The mind is not dragged along by the body, and the body is not dragged along by the mind. Be on your guard in your mind, not in your body. Let your mind lack nothing, and do not let it slip in the least. On the surface your mind may seem weak, but keep it strong at the bottom, and do not let anyone read it.

If you are small, know all about large things in your mind; if you are big, know small things well. Big or small, the important thing is to keep your mind straight and not to be led astray by the size of your own body. Keep your mind clear and wide inside, and put wisdom in that wide place. Polish your wisdom and your mind without ever letting up. Sharpen your wisdom: learn what is right and wrong in the world, know the good and bad of all things, go through all the arts and skills, each in its own Way, and make sure that no one in the world can fool you in the least. Then your mind becomes the wisdom of strategy. The wisdom of strategy is something quite different from other wisdom. On the battlefield, when everything is happening at once, master the principles of strategy and keep your mind unmoved. Examine this well.

The Body in Strategy

Here is how to hold yourself. Do not let your face tip down, or tip up, or lean, or twist. Keep your eyes steady, and do not wrinkle your forehead, but draw your brows together a little. Keep your eyeballs from moving, and try not to blink. Narrow your eyes slightly. Keep your face calm and pleasant, the line of your nose straight, and your chin pushed slightly forward. Keep the back of your neck straight, and put strength into the nape. From the shoulders down, feel your whole body as one piece. Lower both shoulders, keep your spine straight, and do not stick your bottom out. Put strength into your legs from the knees down to the toes, and push your belly out so that your hips do not bend. There is a teaching called "tightening the wedge": brace your belly against the scabbard of your short sword so that your sash does not come loose. In everything, it is essential to make your everyday posture your fighting posture, and your fighting posture your everyday posture. Examine this well.

The Gaze in Strategy

Your gaze should be broad and wide. There are two ways of seeing: the eye that perceives, which is called kan, and the eye that looks, which is called ken. Perceive strongly and look weakly. See what is far away as if it were close, and what is close as if it were far away. This is the heart of strategy. To know your opponent's sword without looking at his sword in the least is one of the great points of strategy. Work this out for yourself.

This way of using your eyes is the same in strategy on the small scale and on the large. It is essential to see to both sides without moving your eyeballs. You cannot learn this in a hurry, in the middle of a crisis. Learn what is written here, make this gaze your gaze at all times, and never let it change, whatever happens. Examine this well.

How to Hold the Long Sword

Hold the long sword with your thumb and forefinger a little loose, your middle finger neither tight nor slack, and your ring finger and little finger tight. Any slack in your grip is bad. Take up your sword thinking that it is a thing for cutting your opponent. Even when you cut, your grip should not change, and your hands should not stiffen. If you have to knock his sword aside, or catch it, or strike it, or press it down, change only your thumb and forefinger a little, and whatever happens, hold your sword thinking that you will cut.

Your grip when you try out a blade by cutting a test object and your grip when you cut in a fight are no different from the grip for cutting a man. In general, whether with the sword or with the hands, I dislike anything that fixes in place. A fixed hand is a dead hand. A hand that does not fix is a living hand. Take this well to heart.

Footwork

When you move your feet, lift your toes a little and step firmly on your heels. Your steps may be long or short, slow or fast, as the moment requires, but always move as if you were walking normally. There are three kinds of footwork I dislike: jumping feet, floating feet, and stamping feet. One of the important things in this Way is called yin-yang footwork, and it is essential. Yin-yang footwork means never moving one foot alone. When you cut, when you draw back, even when you parry, you step right and left, right and left, like yin and yang. Never, never step with one foot only. Examine this well.

The Five Guards

The five guards are the upper guard, the middle guard, the lower guard, the guard on the right side, and the guard on the left side. A guard, or kamae, is the way you hold your sword as you face your opponent. The guards are divided into five, but all of them exist for cutting a man. There are no guards besides these five. Whichever guard you take, do not think about taking a guard; think only about cutting. Whether a guard should be large or small depends on the situation, so choose what serves you best.

The upper, middle, and lower guards are the solid guards. The two side guards are the guards for adapting. You use the right and left guards where the space above you is blocked, or where one side is blocked, and you choose right or left to suit the place. One of the important things in this Way is this: understand that the highest of the guards is the middle guard. The middle guard is what guards are for. Look at it as strategy on the large scale: the middle guard is the commander's seat, and the other four guards follow the commander. Examine this well.

The Path of the Sword

To know the path of the sword means this. If you know its line well, you can swing freely even the sword you always wear at your side, holding it with two fingers. If you try to swing the sword fast, you leave the path of the sword and it becomes hard to swing. Swing the sword calmly, only as fast as it swings well. If you try to swing it fast, the way you would flick a fan or a little knife, you leave the path of the sword and it is hard to swing. That is called "knife-chopping," and you cannot cut a man with a long sword that way.

When you strike downward, bring the sword back up along the path that raises it easily. When you swing it sideways, bring it back sideways, returning it along a good path. Stretch your elbows out wide and swing strongly. That is the path of the sword. If you learn to use the five forms of my strategy, the path of your sword will become settled and your sword will swing easily. Train at this well.

The Five Forms: The First Form

The first form: take the middle guard, with the point of your sword toward your opponent's face. As you close in on him and he strikes at you, turn his sword aside to the right and ride over it. When he strikes again, strike back with the point of your sword flipped over, and leave your sword where it lands after striking his down. When he strikes once more, knock his hands up from below. That is the first form.

To tell the truth, you cannot understand these five forms just from reading them. The forms are something you practice with sword in hand, to learn the path of the sword. Through these five lines of the sword you learn your own sword's path, and you can read any strike your opponent makes. They show you that there are no guards for the two swords other than these five. Train at this.

The Second Form

The second form: take the upper guard, and as your opponent strikes, strike him in the same instant. If you miss him, leave your sword where it is, and as he strikes again, scoop up from below and strike. A further strike is done the same way. Within this form there are all kinds of states of mind and all kinds of rhythm. If you train in my school by means of this form, you will come to know the five paths of the sword in detail, and you will be able to win in any way at all. Practice it.

The Third Form

The third form: hold your sword in the lower guard, as if it were simply hanging from your hand, and as your opponent strikes, knock his hands from below. When you go for his hands and he tries to strike your sword down, let his stroke pass in a rhythm of getting over it, and after his stroke cut across his upper arm. With the lower guard you stop his strike at the very moment he makes it. The lower guard comes into use whether things are moving fast or slow. Take up your sword and train at this.

The Fourth Form

The fourth form: hold your sword level in the guard on your left side, and knock your opponent's striking hands from below. When you knock from below and he tries to strike your sword down, keep your mind on knocking his hands, follow the path of the sword just as it is, and cut upward on a slant, toward the space above your own shoulder. That is the path of the sword. And when he strikes again, you win by following the path of the sword. Examine this well.

The Fifth Form

The fifth form: hold your sword level in the guard on your right side. Read where your opponent's strike is coming from, swing your sword up on a slant from low at your side into the upper guard, and cut straight down from above. This too is for knowing the path of the sword well. Once you have the knack of this form, you can swing even a heavy sword freely.

I will not write about these five forms in detail. You learn the path of the sword as my school teaches it, you learn the rhythms in general, and you learn to read your opponent's sword; and for all of that, these five forms are where you season your hands, over and over. Even in a real fight, by seasoning these paths of the sword you read your opponent's mind, and with all kinds of rhythms you can win in any way. Think carefully about this.

The Guard That Is No Guard

The guard that is no guard means that there should really be no such thing as "taking a guard" with the sword. Still, since there are five places to hold it, you may call them guards. Wherever you hold your sword, hold it according to your opponent, the place, and the situation, so that it is easiest to cut him.

The upper guard, lowered a little as the moment requires, becomes the middle guard. The middle guard, raised a little when that serves, becomes the upper guard. The lower guard, raised a little as the occasion requires, becomes the middle guard. The side guards too, brought a little toward the center depending on the situation, become the middle or lower guard. And so the principle is that there are guards, and there are no guards.

First and foremost, once you have taken up your sword, whatever you do, your mind is on cutting your opponent. You may catch his cutting sword, knock it aside, strike it, stick to it, or touch it, but understand that every one of these is a step toward cutting him. If you think about catching, or about knocking aside, or striking, or sticking, or touching, your cut will fall short. It is essential to think of everything as a step toward cutting. Examine this well.

In strategy on the large scale, the way you draw up your troops is a guard too, and every part of it exists for winning the battle. Fixing in place is bad. Think this through.

The One-Beat Strike

Among the rhythms for striking your opponent there is one called the one-beat strike. You get within the distance where you and he can reach each other, and then, before he has made up his mind, without moving your body and without letting your intention show, you strike as fast and as directly as you possibly can. To strike before he even has the thought of pulling back, dodging, or striking is the rhythm of one beat. Learn this rhythm well, and train at striking fast in the rhythm of the interval.

The Two-Step Rhythm

The two-step rhythm: when you are about to strike and your opponent is the kind who quickly pulls back or quickly knocks your sword away, make as if to strike, and then strike him when he has tensed and then gone slack; strike him when he has pulled back and gone slack. That is the two-step strike. You will hardly manage this strike from what I have written here alone, but once you are shown it, you will understand it at once.

The No-Thought Strike

When your opponent is about to strike and you are about to strike, your body becomes a striking body and your mind a striking mind, and your hands strike from out of nothing, late but fast and strong. This is called the strike of no thought and no design, munen musō, and it is the most important strike of all. You will meet this strike again and again. Learn it well and train at it.

The Running-Water Strike

The running-water strike: when you and your opponent are locked together, sword against sword, and he tries quickly to pull back, quickly to dodge, quickly to knock your sword aside, make your body and your mind large and strike with your sword trailing behind your body, as slowly as you can, as if the water were pooling, broadly and strongly. Once you have learned this strike, it is a sure one. It is essential to read what kind of opponent you face.

The Chance Hit

When you strike out and your opponent tries to block your sword or knock it away, with that one strike you hit his head or his legs, whatever is there, along a single path of the sword. That is the chance hit. Learn this strike very well; it comes up all the time. Exchange blows with partners often, and work it out.

The Flint-Spark Hit

The flint-spark hit is when your opponent's sword and yours are almost touching, and without raising your sword at all, you strike as strongly as you possibly can. You must strike fast, with three things strong: your legs strong, your body strong, your hands strong. You cannot do this strike without practicing it again and again. Train at it well, and it will land hard.

The Red-Leaf Strike

The red-leaf strike means knocking down your opponent's sword and then taking your own sword back in hand, the way a red leaf drops from the tree. When he holds his sword out in front of you and means to strike, to knock your sword aside, or to parry, strike his sword hard with the no-thought strike or the flint-spark strike, and keep pressing after it, your point slanting downward, and his sword will certainly fall. Train at this strike and knocking a sword down becomes easy. Practice it well.

The Body in Place of the Sword

You could also call this the sword in place of the body. In general, when you strike at an opponent, your body and your sword do not strike at the same instant. As the chance for the strike requires, you put your body into striking position first, and your sword strikes on its own, without regard to your body. Sometimes your body stays where it is and your sword does the striking, but usually your body goes first and your sword strikes after. Examine this well and practice the strike.

Striking and Hitting

Striking and hitting are two different things. To strike means that whatever kind of strike it is, you have decided on it and you strike it surely. Hitting is only running into him. However hard you hit, even if your opponent drops dead on the spot, it is still a hit. A strike is something you mean to do. Examine this. Hitting your opponent's hand or leg is a hit; you hit him first, so that you can strike hard afterward. Hitting is about the same as touching. Once you have learned this well, you will see that the two are entirely different. Work this out for yourself.

The Short-Armed Monkey

The body of the short-armed monkey, shūkō, means not reaching out with your hands. When you close in on your opponent, without the slightest thought of reaching out with your hands, you get your body in quickly, before he strikes. If you think about reaching out with your hands, your body will always stay back, so you move your whole body in quickly. In the time it takes for hands to meet, your body can easily get in. Examine this well.

Lacquer and Glue

The body of lacquer and glue, shikkō, means sticking close to your opponent as you close in and not coming away. When you close in on him, stick to him with your head, your body, and your legs, and stick firmly. Everyone gets his face and feet in quickly, but his body hangs back. Press your body tight against his and stick to him with no gap at all between you. Examine this well.

Comparing Heights

Comparing heights means this. Whenever you close in on your opponent, do not let yourself shrink. Stretch your legs, stretch your hips, stretch your neck, and go in strongly. Put your face beside his face, as if the two of you were measuring who is taller, and make yourself so tall that you are sure you have won. Going in strongly like this is essential. Think it through.

Sticking

Your opponent strikes and you strike, and when he parries, you stick your sword to his and go in. Sticking means your sword does not come away from his easily, but go in without too much force. When you stick to his sword and go in, it does no harm to go in as calmly as you like. There is a difference between sticking and tangling: sticking is strong, and tangling is weak. Understand the difference.

The Body Blow

The body blow means going in right up against your opponent and hitting him with your body. Turn your face a little to the side, push your left shoulder forward, and hit him in the chest. Make your body as strong as you can, and go in with a springing rhythm, in time with your breath. Once you have learned to go in like this, it is strong enough to throw your opponent back twelve or eighteen feet. It can hit him hard enough to kill. Train at this well.

The Three Parries

The three parries: when you close in on your opponent and he strikes, parry his sword by sweeping it away over your right shoulder, while moving your sword as if to thrust at his eyes. Next, the "thrusting parry": parry his striking sword by moving as if to thrust at his right eye, thrusting in as if to pin his neck. Last, when he strikes and you are going in with the short sword, do not worry much about parrying his sword; go in as if to punch him in the face with your left hand. These are the three parries. Think of making a fist of your left hand and punching him in the face. Train at this well.

Stabbing at the Face

Stabbing at the face means this. When you and your opponent face each other, sword against sword, in the space between his sword and yours you must always be thinking of thrusting your sword point at his face. If you mean to thrust at his face, his face and body will lean back. Once you make him lean back, you have all sorts of ways to win. Think this through. In a fight, once your opponent's body leans back, you have already won. So never forget to stab at the face. In your practice of strategy, train at this.

Stabbing at the Heart

Stabbing at the heart means this. In a fight, when you are hemmed in above and on the sides, so that you cannot cut at all, you thrust at your opponent. To slip his sword, show him the back of your blade straight on, draw your sword back without letting the point waver, and thrust at his chest. This is especially useful when you are tired, or when your sword will not cut. Think carefully about this.

Katsu-Totsu

Katsu-totsu is two shouts. When you have attacked and are pressing your opponent hard and he is about to strike back, bring your sword up from below as if to thrust at him, and strike with the return stroke. Do both in a quick rhythm: "Katsu!" as you thrust upward, "Totsu!" as you strike. This rhythm comes up all the time in an exchange of blows. The way to do katsu-totsu is to raise your point as if you were going to thrust, and strike in the very same moment. Practice this rhythm well and examine it.

The Knocking Parry

The knocking parry: when you and your opponent are exchanging blows, clang and clang again, knock his striking sword aside with yours and strike. Knocking here does not mean knocking hard, and it does not mean parrying. You meet his striking sword, knock it, and strike him faster than the knock. The essential thing is to take the initiative with the knock and take it again with the strike. If you get the rhythm of the knock right, then however hard he strikes, a small knock is enough to keep your sword point from dropping. Learn this well and examine it.

Fighting Many Opponents

Fighting many opponents is what you do when you fight a crowd by yourself. Draw your long sword and your short sword, and hold them spread wide to the left and right, flung out to the sides. Even though the enemy comes at you from all four directions, the idea is to drive them all around to one side. Read which of them are in front and which behind as they come, meet whoever advances first, and meet him quickly. Keep your eyes wide, read where each of them will strike from, and cut with your right sword and your left sword crossing past each other at once. Waiting is bad. Quickly return to the guards at both sides, cut hard into whoever comes forward, drive them back in confusion, and go straight at the next one who comes forward and cut him down too.

Do everything you can to drive them into a line, like fish strung on a cord, and when you see them piling up on one another, sweep in hard without leaving them an opening. If you only chase them where they are crowded together, you will get nowhere. And if you keep waiting for them to come out one after another, you are waiting, and you will get nowhere. Catch their rhythm, see where they are breaking, and win.

From time to time, gather a large number of partners and drive them about in practice. Once you have the feeling of it, one opponent or ten or twenty are all easy. Practice this well and examine it.

The Principle of Crossing Swords

This principle of crossing swords is where you learn how strategy wins with the sword. I will not write it down in detail; practice well, and learn for yourself how to win. In general, it is the sword that shows the true Way of strategy. This is taught by word of mouth.

The One Strike

By the spirit called "the one strike," you win for certain. It is hard to understand unless you study strategy well. If you train at this well, strategy becomes part of you, and you win as you please. Practice this well.

Direct Transmission

The spirit of direct transmission, jikitsū, is the true Way of the Two Swords as One, received and handed on. It is essential to train well and make your whole body into this strategy. This is taught by word of mouth.

What I have written above is my school's swordsmanship, set down in outline in this scroll. To learn to beat others with the sword in strategy, you first use the five forms to learn the five guards. You learn the path of the sword, and your whole body becomes free. Your mind sharpens, and you come to know the rhythm of the Way. Your sword and your hands grow skilled of themselves, and your body and feet move just as your mind wishes. Then you beat one man, and you beat two, and you come to know what is good and bad in strategy.

Practice what is in this book article by article, fight opponents, and little by little gain the principles of the Way. Keep them in your mind always, but without hurrying; whenever you get your hands on them, learn their worth; cross swords with anyone at all and learn his mind. A journey of a thousand miles is made one step at a time. Take your time, and understand that practicing this law is a warrior's duty. Today, beat yesterday's self; tomorrow, beat those less skilled than you; and later, beat those more skilled. Do as this book says, and never let your mind wander onto a side road.

However many opponents you beat, if you go against what you were taught, it is not the true Way. Once this principle rises in your mind, you will understand how one man can beat dozens. And then, with the wisdom of swordsmanship, you will master strategy on the large scale and on the small. A thousand days of practice is called forging, and ten thousand days of practice is called tempering. Examine this well.
