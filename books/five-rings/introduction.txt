Miyamoto Musashi (about 1584–1645) was a Japanese swordsman who, by his own count, fought more than sixty duels between the ages of thirteen and twenty-nine and never lost one. He also fought in battles, taught his own school of swordsmanship, and was a painter whose ink pictures of birds still hang in Japanese museums. In 1640 he settled in Kumamoto, on the southern island of Kyushu, as a guest of its lord, Hosokawa Tadatoshi. In the tenth month of 1643, at about sixty, he went up to a cave on Mount Iwato outside the town and began this book. He dated the finished scrolls to the fifth month of 1645, early summer, and gave them to his pupil Terao Magonojō a week before he died.

By then Japan had been largely at peace since 1615. The Tokugawa shoguns had ended more than a century of civil war, and a warrior class trained for battle had very little fighting left to do. Fencing schools multiplied, each with its secret techniques and its fees. Musashi wrote against all of that. His book is a manual for the members of one school, in five scrolls named for the five elements of Buddhist teaching. Earth lays out what strategy is and why his school uses two swords. Water gives the technique: posture, gaze, grip, footwork, the five guards, and some thirty named moves. Fire is about fighting itself, timing and initiative and reading an opponent. Wind takes apart the other schools. Void is one page on what is left when the technique is learned.

Two things will help. Musashi uses one word, heihō, for a duel between two men and for a battle between armies, because he holds that they work by the same principles; this edition calls it "strategy" throughout and keeps his phrases "on the small scale" and "on the large scale." And he writes for students standing in front of him with swords in their hands. He repeats himself on purpose, ends almost every article with "examine this well," and sometimes says outright that a point can only be shown, not written.

The original manuscript is lost, and the book survives in copies that differ in small ways. This edition is translated in full from the Japanese text printed in 1909 by a society formed to honor Musashi's memory, which took it from a copy then held by a family in Higo; each scroll ends on its line of transmission, from Musashi to his pupil and on to later copyists, and those lines are kept as printed. No public-domain English translation exists, so none was used. Japanese era dates are given with their Western years, and Japanese measures are converted.
