TEXT ANALYSIS — Miyamoto Musashi, Gorin no Sho (五輪書), The Book of Five Rings, 1643-45

SOURCE AND EDITION
The text is the one printed in 宮本武蔵遺蹟顕彰会 (ed.), 宮本武蔵 (Kinkōdō,
1909), chapter 9, from the copy then held by the Maeda family of Tamana in
Higo. Every scroll closes on the same colophon: dated Shōhō 2 (1645), fifth
month, twelfth day (the Water scroll says thirteenth), signed Shinmen
Musashi, addressed to Terao Magonojō; then a second line, Kanbun 7 (1667),
second month, fifth day, Terao Yumeyo Katsunobu (seal), to Yamamoto
Gensuke. So this is a copy of a copy: Musashi's pupil Terao Magonojō
received the five scrolls a week before Musashi died, and his line passed
them on. Japanese Wikisource and Aozora Bunko do not have the text; the
NDL scan is PDM. The OCR layer was proved against the page images (see
prep.py) and every unreadable glyph resolved from the image.

There is NO public-domain English translation. Harris (1974), Cleary
(1993), Wilson, Bennett and the rest are all in copyright and were not
used. This is a from-the-original translation with no second witness;
the introduction must say so (the sun-tzu / dogen precedent).

WHAT THE BOOK IS
Musashi (1584?-1645), at about sixty, retired in Kumamoto under the
Hosokawa lords, set down the teaching of his school in five scrolls named
for the five elements of Buddhist cosmology (the five-ringed stupa,
gorintō): Earth (the ground plan: what strategy is, the carpenter
analogy, why two swords), Water (the individual technique: posture, gaze,
grip, footwork, the five guards and five forms, thirty-odd named moves),
Fire (fighting: initiative, timing, reading and upsetting the opponent,
the same principles applied to armies), Wind (the faults of other
schools), Void (one page on emptiness: what is left when the technique is
fully learned).

STYLE
Terse, practical, repetitive, with almost no ornament. Sentences run on
with 、 for many clauses; topic shifts mid-run. He defines a term by
repeating it (枕をおさふると云事 / 枕をおさふるとは...). Each article
closes on a refrain. He insists he quotes no Buddhist or Confucian
classics and no old military books, and he doesn't; the voice is a
practitioner's. The difficulty for a modern reader is not vocabulary but
compression: technique is described in a handful of words that assume
you are holding a sword in front of him. Our job is to make each
instruction physically clear (who moves, which hand, where the blade
goes) without inventing technique he does not give.

READABILITY (the Dōgen correction)
Every sentence must be one a bright fourteen-year-old could read aloud and
follow. Break his run-ons into sentences. Say plainly what each move is.
Gloss each technical term ONCE, inside the sentence, at its first use; after
that, use the English. No footnotes. Keep the second-person instruction ("you
must...", "hold your sword so..."), which is what he wrote. Where he says
"this is hard to write down" or "it is taught by word of mouth", keep it --
it is honest and part of the book.

LOCKED TERMS (decided before the first sentence; do not vary)
兵法 heihō — "STRATEGY". Musashi uses one word for single combat and for
  generalship and insists they are the same thing (一人と一人との戰ひも、
  萬と萬との戰ひも同じ道也). "Swordsmanship" loses the armies; "the art of
  war" sounds like a book title (and like Sun Tzu) and will not stretch
  over a duel ("the art of war of one man"). "Strategy" covers both and is
  what English readers already expect of this book. First use glossed:
  "heihō, the way of fighting -- strategy, I will call it, for it covers a
  duel and a battle alike". 兵法者 = "strategist" / "a master of
  strategy"; the 1909 text uses 兵法者 also for the touring duelists he
  fought: "a fencer who called himself a strategist" where contemptuous.
一分の兵法 — "strategy on the small scale" / "single combat" (one man).
大分の兵法 / 大きなる兵法 — "strategy on the large scale" (armies, battles).
  Lock the pair "small scale / large scale" and keep it everywhere.
拍子 hyōshi — "RHYTHM" (timing). One word throughout; first use glossed as
  "rhythm -- the timing of things". あふ拍子 = rhythm that fits / in
  step; ちがふ拍子 / 背く拍子 = rhythm that is out of step / against the
  rhythm; 間の拍子 = the rhythm of the interval.
構 kamae — "GUARD" (the stance of the sword). 五方の構 = the five guards:
  upper, middle, lower, right side, left side (上段中段下段右のわき左のわき).
  有構無構 = "the guard that is no guard" (literally "having a guard,
  having no guard").
表 / おもて — "FORMS" (the five set forms, kata). "The first form" etc.
太刀 tachi — "LONG SWORD"; 刀 katana = "sword"; 脇差 wakizashi = "SHORT
  SWORD". The pair must stay distinct or the Two-Swords argument collapses.
  Musashi notes the old names (太刀・刀) and the present ones (刀・脇差):
  render that sentence literally once ("in the old days they were called
  tachi and katana; today, katana and wakizashi") and then use long
  sword / short sword. In general 太刀 in a technique = "your sword" or
  "the long sword" as context needs; 太刀の道 = "the path of the sword"
  (the line the blade naturally travels).
二刀一流 — "the Two Swords as One school" (Nitō Ichiryū); 二天一流 (the
  preface) = "Niten Ichiryū, the Two Heavens as One". Gloss both once.
  一流 alone = "my school".
道 michi — "the Way" when it is the Way of strategy as a calling ("the
  Way of strategy", "the warrior's Way"); "way" / "path" elsewhere.
  Capitalised only for a Way as a discipline.
観・見 — 観の目 = "the eye that perceives", 見の目 = "the eye that
  looks"; "perceiving strongly, looking weakly". Gloss once in Water.
先 sen — "THE INITIATIVE"; the three: 懸の先 (せんけん) "the initiative
  of attack", 待の先 "the initiative of waiting", 体々の先 "the
  initiative of both together".
心 kokoro — "mind" or "spirit" (mind for the inner state; "the idea is"
  for his frequent ...と云心也 = "the point is").
空 kū — "THE VOID", glossed in the Earth table of contents and again in the
  Void scroll as "emptiness, the void -- what has no form".
鍛錬 — "train" / "training". 朝鍛夕錬 = "forge in the morning, temper in
  the evening"; 千日の稽古を鍛とし萬日の稽古を錬とす = "a thousand days'
  practice to forge, ten thousand days' practice to temper" (keep the
  smith's metaphor: 鍛 forge, 錬 refine/temper).
Named techniques: translate the name and keep it as the subheading; give
  the Japanese only where the name is a pun or untranslatable (秋猴 shūkō,
  the short-armed monkey; 漆膠 shikkō, lacquer and glue; 喝咄 katsu-totsu,
  two shouts; 鼠頭午首 "rat's head, ox's neck").

THE REFRAINS — kept as refrains, in plain English, consistently:
  能々吟味有べし / 吟味すべし   -> "Examine this well." (also "Study this well.")
  能々鍛錬有べし / 鍛錬すべし   -> "Train at this well." / "Practice this well."
  工夫有べし / 工夫すべし       -> "Work this out for yourself." / "Think this through."
  能々分別すべし               -> "Think carefully about this."
  能々稽古すべし               -> "Practice this well."
  口傳                          -> "This is taught by word of mouth."
Short, the last sentence of the article, never inflated.

DATES AND HOURS (converted inline, said so in the introduction)
  寛永二十年十月上旬 -> "early in the tenth month of Kan'ei 20 (late 1643)"
  十月十日の夜、寅の一點 -> "on the night of the tenth day of the tenth
     month, at the first stroke of the hour of the Tiger -- about four in
     the morning"
  正保二年五月十二日 -> "the twelfth day of the fifth month of Shōhō 2
     (June 1645)" [Shōhō 2.5.12 = 5 June 1645 Gregorian]
  寛文七年二月五日 -> "the fifth day of the second month of Kanbun 7
     (1667)"
  Distances: 間 ken -> "about six feet" per ken, converted (二十間 = about
     forty yards; 二間三間 = twelve or eighteen feet).

COLOPHONS
Keep at the end of every scroll, short, set as three lines:
  "Shōhō 2 (1645), fifth month, twelfth day. Shinmen Musashi.
   To Terao Magonojō.
   Copied: Kanbun 7 (1667), second month, fifth day. Terao Yumeyo
   Katsunobu, his seal. To Yamamoto Gensuke."
(The Void scroll's colophon in this print lacks the second transmission
line; render what is printed.)

PEOPLE AND PLACES (fold in, gloss once)
  有馬喜兵衞 Arima Kihei, of the Shintō school (新當流 Shintō-ryū)
  但馬國秋山 a strong man named Akiyama, from Tajima province
  巖殿山 Mount Iwato, in Higo (Kumamoto) -- the Reigandō cave
  新免武藏藤原玄信 Shinmen Musashi, Fujiwara no Genshin
  常陸國かしまかんとり the shrines of Kashima and Katori in Hitachi
    province, whose priests founded fencing schools "on the god's teaching"
  十能七藝 "the ten abilities and the seven arts" (the old list of
    accomplishments); 利方 rikata, "the practical arts"
  なまへいはう大疵のもと "half-learned strategy is the root of grave
    injury" -- the proverb he quotes (生兵法は大疵の基)

SENSITIVE CONTENT
This is a manual of killing with swords, written by a man who killed
dozens. Render it straight, in his measured, practical register: the
cuts, thrusts to the face, and "stabbing the heart" are technique, not
gore, and are not softened or sensationalised. His remark that warriors
are thought to "only practise the way of dying" and his reply (anyone,
monk, woman or farmer, can resolve to die for duty or shame) stays
exactly as argued. "women" there is his example, not an aside to
apologise for. There is nothing here that needs a ruling beyond that.

FAMOUS PASSAGES (must survive; see must_contain.txt)
  - the duel record: first duel at thirteen against Arima Kihei; Akiyama
    at sixteen; more than sixty duels, never losing, between thirteen and
    twenty-eight or twenty-nine
  - "from one thing, know ten thousand things" (一を以て萬を知る)
  - the carpenter analogy (大工 = "great craftsman")
  - "the eye that perceives / the eye that looks"
  - "a thousand days to forge, ten thousand days to temper"
  - the closing of the Void: "In the void there is good and no evil.
    Wisdom exists, principle exists, the Way exists; the mind is void."
    (空有善無惡、智は有也、利は有也、道は有也、心は空也)
  - "the Way of strategy is the Way of the warrior" ... "there is
    nothing outside it" stays in his own words, not the famous English.
