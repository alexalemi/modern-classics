Chapter 7: Harpagus Conquers Ionia, and Cyrus Takes Babylon

162. After Mazares died, Harpagus came down to take his place in command. He too was a Mede by birth. He was the man whom Astyages, king of the Medes, had served that lawless banquet, and who had helped to give the kingdom to Cyrus. Now Cyrus appointed him commander, and he came to Ionia and began taking the cities by building up mounds of earth against them. Whenever he had shut a people up inside their walls, he would pile up mounds against the walls and take the city by storm. The first city in Ionia he attacked was Phocaea.

163. These Phocaeans were the first of the Greeks to make long sea voyages. It was they who discovered the Adriatic, and Tyrrhenia, and Iberia, and Tartessus, out past the Strait of Gibraltar. They did not sail in round-hulled merchant ships but in fifty-oared galleys. They came to Tartessus and made friends with the king of the Tartessians, whose name was Arganthonius. He ruled the Tartessians for eighty years and lived to be a hundred and twenty in all. The Phocaeans became such close friends with this man that first he invited them to leave Ionia and settle anywhere they liked in his own country. When he could not persuade them to do that, and later heard from them how the power of the Mede was growing, he gave them money to build a wall around their city. He gave it without stinting, for the wall runs a good mile or more around, and it is built entirely of large stones closely fitted together.

164. That is how the Phocaeans' wall came to be built. Harpagus marched his army against them and laid siege to the city. At the same time he made them an offer: he would be satisfied, he said, if the Phocaeans would just pull down one battlement of their wall and dedicate one single house to the king. But the Phocaeans hated the thought of slavery. They said they wanted one day to think the matter over, and after that they would give their answer, and they asked him to pull his army back from the wall while they deliberated. Harpagus said he knew perfectly well what they meant to do, but all the same he would let them deliberate. So, while Harpagus had his army pulled back from the wall, the Phocaeans dragged their fifty-oared galleys down to the sea. They put on board their children and women and all their movable goods. They also loaded the statues from the temples and the other offerings, all except the ones made of bronze or stone and the paintings; everything else went into the ships. Then they embarked themselves and sailed for Chios. The Persians took possession of Phocaea, a city empty of its people.

165. As for the Phocaeans, they asked the people of Chios to sell them the islands called the Oenussae, but the Chians would not. They were afraid the islands would become a center of trade and their own island would be shut out of it. So the Phocaeans set out for Cyrnus, the island of Corsica. Twenty years before this, following an oracle, they had founded a city on Cyrnus called Alalia. (By this time Arganthonius was dead.) But before setting out for Cyrnus they first sailed back to Phocaea and slaughtered the Persian garrison that Harpagus had left in charge of the city. When they had done this, they laid a solemn curse on any of their number who stayed behind from the voyage. They also sank a lump of iron in the sea and swore that they would not come back to Phocaea until that lump rose to the surface again. But as they were setting off for Cyrnus, more than half the citizens were seized with such longing and regret for their city and their homeland that they broke their oath and sailed back to Phocaea. Those who still kept the oath weighed anchor from the Oenussae islands and sailed away.

166. When they reached Cyrnus, they lived there for five years alongside the settlers who had come before them, and they built temples there. But they kept plundering the property of all their neighbors. So the Tyrrhenians and the Carthaginians (from Carthage, the Phoenician colony in North Africa) made an agreement and sailed against them, each with sixty ships. The Phocaeans manned their own ships, sixty in all, and went out to meet the enemy in what is called the Sardinian Sea. When they met in the sea battle, the Phocaeans won a kind of Cadmean victory, the kind that ruins the winner too: forty of their ships were destroyed, and the remaining twenty were put out of action, their rams bent aside. So they sailed into Alalia, took on board their children and women and as much of their other property as their ships could carry, left Cyrnus behind, and sailed to Rhegium.

167. As for the crews of the ships that were destroyed, the Carthaginians and Tyrrhenians captured by far the greater number of them. They brought them ashore and stoned them to death. After this the people of Agylla found that every living thing that passed the spot where the stoned Phocaeans lay became twisted, or crippled, or paralyzed: sheep and goats, pack animals and human beings alike. So the people of Agylla sent to Delphi, wanting to make amends for the offense. The Pythia told them to do what the people of Agylla still do today: they offer great sacrifices to the dead men and hold athletic games and horse races in their honor at that place. That was the fate of those Phocaeans. But the ones who took refuge at Rhegium set out from there and took possession of the city in the land of Oenotria that is now called Hyele. They founded it after learning from a man of Poseidonia that the Pythia's answer had meant them to found a shrine to Cyrnus, who was a hero, and not to found a settlement on the island of Cyrnus.

168. That is what happened at Phocaea in Ionia. The people of Teos did almost the same thing. As soon as Harpagus took their wall with a mound, they boarded their ships and sailed straight off to Thrace. There they founded the city of Abdera. Timesius of Clazomenae had founded it before them, but he got no good of it: the Thracians drove him out. Now he is honored as a hero by the Teans in Abdera.

169. These were the only Ionians who left their native cities rather than live in subjection. The other Ionians, except the Milesians, did fight Harpagus just as those who left had done, and they proved themselves brave men, each fighting for his own city. But when they were beaten and their cities taken, they all stayed where they were and did what they were ordered to do. The Milesians, as I said before, had made a sworn agreement with Cyrus himself and stayed out of it. So Ionia was enslaved for the second time. And when Harpagus had conquered the Ionians on the mainland, the Ionians who lived on the islands were so frightened by all this that they surrendered to Cyrus.

170. The Ionians had been badly treated, but they still kept holding their assemblies at the Panionium, as before. I am told that Bias of Priene gave the Ionians a piece of advice there, a most useful one, and if they had followed it they might have been the most prosperous of all the Greeks. He urged the Ionians to set out together in one common fleet, sail to Sardinia, and there found a single city for all Ionians. That way, he said, they would escape slavery and prosper, living on the largest of all islands and ruling over others. But if they stayed in Ionia, he said, he could not see how they would ever be free again. That was Bias of Priene's advice, given after the Ionians had been ruined. But good advice had also been given before Ionia's ruin, by Thales of Miletus, whose family was Phoenician by descent. He advised the Ionians to have one single seat of government and to put it at Teos, since Teos, he said, was at the center of Ionia. The other cities would go on being lived in as before, but would count as if they were only districts of one state.

These, then, were the plans these two men put before them.

171. Harpagus, having subdued Ionia, marched against the Carians and the Caunians and the Lycians, taking Ionians and Aeolians along to help him. The Carians had come to the mainland from the islands. Long ago they had been subjects of Minos, king of Crete, and were called Leleges. They lived on the islands and paid no tribute, as far back as I can find out by hearsay; but whenever Minos asked, they manned his ships for him. And since Minos conquered a great deal of land and was lucky in his wars, the Carians were in his day by far the most famous of all nations. They also produced three inventions that the Greeks took up. The Carians were the ones who started the fashion of fastening crests on helmets and of putting emblems on shields. They were also the first to put handles on their shields. Until then everyone who carried a shield carried it without a handle, steering it with a leather strap slung around the neck and over the left shoulder. Then, a long time afterward, the Dorians and Ionians drove the Carians out of the islands, and that is how they came to the mainland. That is how the Cretans tell the story of the Carians. The Carians themselves do not agree. They believe they have lived on the mainland from the beginning and have always gone by the same name they have now. As evidence they point to an ancient temple of Carian Zeus at Mylasa, which the Mysians and the Lydians share with them as kindred peoples, since they say Lydus and Mysus were brothers of Car. These peoples share the temple, but those of other races who have come to speak the same language as the Carians have no share in it.

172. In my opinion, though, it is the Caunians who have lived there from the beginning, even though they say themselves that they came from Crete. They have come to resemble the Carians in language, or else the Carians have come to resemble the Caunians; I can't say for certain which. But in their customs they are very different from all other people, the Carians included. For example, the finest thing in the world, to their way of thinking, is to gather in large groups for drinking, by age or by friendship, men, women and children alike. Again, they once set up temples to foreign gods, but afterward changed their minds and decided to worship only their own native gods. So all the young men of Caunus put on their armor and marched as far as the borders of the Calyndians, striking at the air with their spears and saying that they were driving the foreign gods out of the country. Those are the customs these people have.

173. The Lycians, on the other hand, came originally from Crete, for in ancient times all of Crete was held by non-Greeks. The two sons of Europa, Sarpedon and Minos, quarreled in Crete over who should be king. Minos won the struggle between their factions and drove out Sarpedon and his supporters. Once expelled, they came to the land of Milyas in Asia. (The land the Lycians live in now was called Milyas in the old days, and the Milyans were then called Solymi.) While Sarpedon reigned over them, they went by the name they had brought with them, which is still what their neighbors call the Lycians today: Termilae. But then Lycus son of Pandion came from Athens to the land of the Termilae and to Sarpedon, after he too had been driven out, by his brother Aegeus. From this Lycus, in time, they came to be called Lycians. Their customs are partly Cretan and partly Carian. But they have one custom all their own, which no other people shares: they take their names from their mothers and not from their fathers. If you ask a Lycian who he is, he will give you his mother's family, and list his mother's mother and her mothers before her. And if a woman who is a citizen marries a slave, her children are counted as nobly born. But if a man who is a citizen, even the first man among them, takes a slave as his wife or his concubine, his children have no citizen rights.

174. The Carians were conquered by Harpagus without doing anything brilliant, and so were the Greeks who live in that country. Among these, besides others, are the people of Cnidus, settlers from Sparta. Their land runs out into the sea; it is the region called Triopium, which begins at the peninsula of Bybassus. All of Cnidus except a small strip is washed by the sea: on the north side it is bounded by the Gulf of Ceramus, and on the south by the sea off Syme and Rhodes. So, while Harpagus was conquering Ionia, the Cnidians began to dig through this small strip, which is about half a mile across, wanting to make their land an island. Everything inside the isthmus was theirs, since the isthmus they were digging across is exactly where Cnidian territory ends on the mainland side. A great many men were at work on it. But it became clear that the workmen were getting hurt far more than you would expect, and in a way that seemed more than natural, in various parts of their bodies and especially in their eyes, whenever the rock was broken up. So they sent men to the oracle at Delphi to ask what was causing the trouble. And the Pythia, as the Cnidians themselves say, gave them this answer, in the three-beat iambic verse:

	Do not wall off the isthmus with towers, and do not dig it through;
	had Zeus wanted your land to be an island, he would have made it one.

When the Pythia had given this oracle, the Cnidians not only stopped digging but surrendered to Harpagus without a fight when he came against them with his army.

175. There were also the Pedasians, who lived inland in the hill country above Halicarnassus. Whenever something bad is about to happen to them or their neighbors, the priestess of Athena there grows a long beard. This has happened to them three times. They were the only people anywhere in Caria who held out against Harpagus for any length of time. They gave him more trouble than anyone else, having fortified a mountain called Lida.

176. In time the Pedasians were conquered. When Harpagus marched his army into the plain of Xanthus, the Lycians came out against him and fought, a few against many, and gave proof of great courage. But they were beaten and shut up inside their city. Then they gathered their wives and children, their property and their servants into the citadel and set fire to it, so that the whole citadel went up in flames. When they had done this, they swore terrible oaths to one another, went out against the enemy, and were killed fighting, all the men of Xanthus. Of the Xanthians who now claim to be Lycians, most are newcomers from abroad, except for eighty households. Those eighty households happened to be away from home at the time, and so they survived. That is how Harpagus took Xanthus, and he took Caunus in much the same way, since the Caunians for the most part did as the Lycians had done.

177. So Harpagus was conquering the coastal regions of Asia, while Cyrus himself was doing the same in the interior, subduing every nation and passing over none. Most of these campaigns I will pass over in silence. But I will tell about the ones that gave him the most trouble and are most worth telling.

178. When Cyrus had brought all the rest of the mainland under his rule, he set about attacking the Assyrians. Assyria has many other great cities, of course, but the most famous and the strongest, where the seat of the kingdom had been set up after Nineveh was destroyed, was Babylon. And this is what kind of city it was. It lies in a great plain, and it is square, each side about thirteen and a half miles long, so that the whole circuit of the city comes to about fifty-four miles. That is the size of the city of Babylon, and it was more magnificent than any other city we know of. First, a moat runs all around it, deep and wide and full of water. Then comes a wall fifty royal cubits thick, about eighty feet, and two hundred cubits, some three hundred feet, high. The royal cubit, by the way, is three finger-widths longer than the ordinary cubit: about twenty inches against eighteen.

179. I must also explain what they did with the earth that came out of the moat, and how the wall was built. As they dug the moat, they made the earth they carried out of it into bricks. When they had molded enough bricks, they baked them in kilns. Then, using hot asphalt for mortar and laying in mats of woven reeds every thirty courses of brick, they built first the sides of the moat and then the wall itself in the same way. Along the top of the wall, at the edges, they built one-story chambers facing each other, and between the rows of chambers they left room to drive a four-horse chariot. All the way around the wall there are a hundred gates, all of bronze, with posts and lintels of bronze as well. There is another city eight days' journey from Babylon, called Is. There is a small river there, also called Is, which flows into the Euphrates. Along with its water, this river Is throws up great quantities of lumps of asphalt, and that is where the asphalt for the wall of Babylon came from.

180. That is how Babylon was walled. The city is in two halves, because a river called the Euphrates runs through the middle of it. It flows from the land of the Armenians, a large, deep, fast river, and empties into the Persian Gulf. On each side, the wall is carried down at its corners to the river, and from there a return wall of baked brick runs along each bank like a rampart. The city itself is full of houses three and four stories high. Its streets run in straight lines, including the cross streets that lead down to the river. At the end of each street there was a gate in the rampart along the river, as many gates as there were streets, and these were bronze too, and opened, like the streets, onto the river itself.

181. This wall I have described is like a breastplate for the city, and inside it another wall runs around, not much weaker for defense than the first but enclosing a smaller space. In the middle of each half of the city stood a building. In one was the king's palace, huge and strongly walled all around. In the other was the temple of Zeus Belus, with bronze gates. This still existed in my time, a square measuring 1,200 feet each way. In the middle of the temple grounds a solid tower is built, 600 feet long and 600 feet wide. On top of this tower stands a second tower, and on that a third, and so on up to eight towers. A ramp has been built winding around the outside of all the towers to climb them. About halfway up there is a landing with seats, where people going up sit down and rest. On top of the last tower there is a large shrine, and in the shrine a large couch is set, richly covered, with a golden table beside it. No statue has been set up there, and no human being spends the night there except one woman of the country, whichever woman the god chooses from all the women. So say the Chaldeans, who are the priests of this god.

182. These same men also say, though I do not believe them, that the god himself often comes to the shrine and rests on the couch. The same thing happens, according to the Egyptians, in Egyptian Thebes, where a woman also sleeps in the temple of Theban Zeus (both these women are said to have nothing to do with men). It happens too with the prophetess of the god at Patara in Lycia, whenever there is one, for there is not always an oracle there; but whenever there is one, she is shut up at night in the temple, inside the shrine.

183. In the temple at Babylon there is also another shrine lower down, with a great seated statue of Zeus made of gold. Beside it stands a large golden table, and his footstool and throne are gold as well. According to the Chaldeans, these things were made from eight hundred talents' weight of gold, about 45,000 pounds. Outside this shrine is a golden altar. There is also another, very large altar, where full-grown animals are sacrificed; on the golden altar it is forbidden to sacrifice anything but young animals still at the breast. On the larger altar, too, the Chaldeans burn a thousand talents' weight of frankincense, about 57,000 pounds, every year at the festival of this god. In those days, in the reign of Cyrus, there was also still standing in the sacred grounds a statue eighteen feet high, of solid gold. I did not see it myself; I am reporting what the Chaldeans say. Darius son of Hystaspes had designs on this statue but did not dare to take it. Xerxes son of Darius did take it, and he killed the priest who told him not to touch it. That is how magnificently this temple is furnished, and there are also many private offerings in it.

184. Babylon had many other rulers, whom I will mention in my Assyrian history, and who improved its walls and temples. Two of them were women. The first, called Semiramis, lived five generations before the other. She built embankments across the plain that are well worth seeing; before that, the river used to flood the whole plain like a sea.

185. The queen who came after her was called Nitocris, and she was wiser than the one who had reigned before. In the first place, she left monuments behind her, which I will describe. Then, seeing that the empire of the Medes was great and restless, and that it had taken many cities, Nineveh among them, she took every precaution against it that she could. First, the river Euphrates, which flows through the middle of their city: before her time it ran straight, but by digging channels upstream she made it so winding that it actually passes the same village in Assyria three times on its way. The village the Euphrates comes to is called Ardericca. Even today, people traveling from our sea, the Mediterranean, to Babylon, as they sail down the Euphrates, arrive at this same village three times, on three different days. That is one thing she did. She also built up an embankment along each bank of the river, amazing for its size and height. And a long way upstream from Babylon she dug a basin for a lake, running alongside the river at a short distance from it. She dug it deep enough everywhere to reach water, and made it so large that its circuit measured about forty-seven miles. The earth dug out of it she used up by piling it into the embankments along the river. When the basin was dug, she brought stones and lined it all around with a stone wall. She did both these things, making the river wind and turning the excavation into a marsh, so that the river would run more slowly with its force broken on its many bends, so that the voyage to Babylon would be a winding one, and so that after the voyage would come a long way around the lake. She carried out these works in the part of the country where the approach to it lay, on the shortest road from Media, so that the Medes could not mix with her people and find out about her affairs.

186. These were the defenses she threw around her city from deep in the country. Along with them she made another improvement that grew out of the same work. The city was in two halves, with the river between them. Under the earlier rulers, anyone who wanted to cross from one half to the other had to go over by boat, which, I imagine, was a nuisance. She took care of this too. While she was digging the basin for the lake, she got this other monument out of the same work. She had very long stones cut, and when the stones were ready and the basin dug, she turned the whole stream of the river into the place she had dug. While the basin was filling with water, the old riverbed dried out. In the meantime she lined the banks of the river with baked brick where it runs through the city, the same way as the wall, and also the steps leading down from the small gates to the river. Then, at about the middle of the city, as far as I can judge, she built a bridge out of the stones she had had quarried, binding the stones together with iron and lead. Across the top she laid squared timbers, which stayed in place during the day, and the people of Babylon used them to cross over. But at night they took the timbers away, for this reason: so that people would not cross back and forth at night and rob one another. When the excavation had been turned into a lake full of river water and the bridge had been finished, she led the Euphrates back out of the lake into its old bed. So the excavation, turned into a marsh, was thought to have served a good purpose, and the people of the city had been given a bridge.

187. This same queen also set the following trap. Over the gate of the city that more people passed through than any other, she had a tomb built for herself, right on top of the gate. On the tomb she had these words carved: "If any king of Babylon after me runs short of money, let him open my tomb and take as much as he wants. But let him not open it for any other reason, unless he is truly in need; for that will not turn out well." The tomb stayed undisturbed until the kingdom came to Darius. But Darius thought it outrageous not to make any use of this gate, and outrageous too, when money was lying there and the money itself was inviting him to take it, not to take it. The reason he would make no use of the gate was that he would have had a corpse over his head as he drove through. So he opened the tomb, and found not money but the corpse, and these words: "If you had not been so greedy for money and so shamelessly grasping, you would not have broken open the resting places of the dead."

188. That, they say, is the kind of woman this queen was. And it was her son, who had the same name as his father, Labynetus, and was ruler of the Assyrians, against whom Cyrus was now marching. When the Great King goes to war, he goes well supplied from home with food for his table and with cattle. He also takes water with him from the river Choaspes, which flows past Susa; this is the only river whose water the king will drink. A great many four-wheeled wagons drawn by mules carry a supply of this Choaspes water, boiled, in silver jars, and follow him wherever he marches.

189. On his way to Babylon, Cyrus came to the river Gyndes. Its springs are in the mountains of the Matienians, and it flows through the land of the Dardanians and into another river, the Tigris, which flows past the city of Opis and out into the Persian Gulf. Cyrus was trying to cross the Gyndes, a river big enough for boats, when one of his sacred white horses, full of spirit and high-handed, went into the river and tried to cross. The current swept it under and carried it away at once. Cyrus was furious with the river for this insolence, and he threatened to make it so weak that from then on even women would be able to cross it easily without wetting their knees. After making this threat, he broke off his march on Babylon and divided his army in two. Then he stretched out lines and marked out straight channels, a hundred and eighty on each bank of the Gyndes, running off in every direction, and he lined up his army along them and ordered them to dig. With so many men working, the job did get done, but they spent the whole summer at it.

190. When Cyrus had taken his revenge on the river Gyndes by splitting it into three hundred and sixty channels, and the next spring was just beginning, he finally went on with his advance on Babylon. The Babylonians had marched out of their city and were waiting for him. When he came near the city, the Babylonians gave battle, were beaten, and were shut up inside their city. But they had known well even before this that Cyrus was not one to sit still, and they had seen him attack every nation alike, so they had brought in provisions in advance for a great many years. So while they thought nothing of the siege, Cyrus did not know what to do, for a long time passed and he was getting nowhere.

191. So, whether someone else suggested it to him when he was at a loss, or whether he saw for himself what he should do, this is what he did. He posted the main body of his army where the river flows into the city, and another division behind the city, where the river flows out of it. He gave his army orders that as soon as they saw the river had become shallow enough to wade, they should go into the city that way. Having posted them like this and given them these orders, he himself marched off with the part of his army that was no use for fighting. When he came to the lake, Cyrus did the same things the queen of Babylon had done with the river and the lake. He led the river off through a channel into the lake, which was by then a marsh, and so the old riverbed became fordable as the water sank. When this had been done, the Persians who had been posted for this very purpose went into Babylon along the bed of the Euphrates, now that the stream had sunk to about the middle of a man's thigh. Now, if the Babylonians had known about this beforehand or had noticed what Cyrus was doing, they would have let the Persians into the city and then destroyed them miserably. For if they had shut all the gates leading down to the river and stood on the ramparts along the banks themselves, they would have caught them as if in a fish trap. But as it was, the Persians came on them unexpectedly. And because the city is so big, as the people who live there say, after the outer parts of the city had been captured, the Babylonians living in the middle had no idea they had been captured. They happened to be holding a festival, and they went on dancing and celebrating all that while, until they learned the truth all too well.

That is how Babylon was taken the first time.

192. How great the resources of the Babylonians are I will show by many other proofs, and among them by this one. Apart from the regular tribute, the whole land the Great King rules is divided into portions to supply him and his army with food. Now, there are twelve months in the year. For four of them he is supplied by the territory of Babylon, and for the other eight by all the rest of Asia put together. So the Assyrian land, in resources, amounts to a third of all Asia. And the governorship of this territory, which the Persians call a satrapy, is by far the best of all the governorships. When Tritantaechmes son of Artabazus held this province from the king, a full artaba of silver came in to him every single day. (The artaba is a Persian measure, about 1 2/3 bushels; it holds three Attic choenixes, about three quarts, more than the Attic medimnus.) As his own private property in this province, apart from his warhorses, he kept eight hundred stallions and sixteen thousand mares, each stallion serving twenty mares. And he kept so many Indian hounds that four large villages in the plain were let off all other taxes and assigned instead to feed the hounds.
