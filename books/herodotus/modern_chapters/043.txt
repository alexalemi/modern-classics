Chapter 43: Thermopylae

201. So King Xerxes was camped in the district of Trachis, in the land of the Malians, and the Greeks were camped inside the pass. Greeks in general call this place Thermopylae, the "Hot Gates," but the people who live there and in the country around it call it simply Pylae, "the Gates." Both sides, then, were camped in this area. The one side held everything beyond Trachis toward the north wind, and the other held everything on this side of the mainland, toward the south wind and the midday sun.

202. These were the Greeks who waited in this place for the Persian attack. From Sparta came three hundred hoplites. From Tegea and Mantinea came a thousand, half from each city; from Orchomenus in Arcadia, a hundred and twenty; and from the rest of Arcadia, a thousand. That was the Arcadian total. From Corinth came four hundred, from Phlius two hundred, and from Mycenae eighty. Those were the men who came from the Peloponnese. From the Boeotians came seven hundred Thespians and four hundred Thebans.

203. Besides these, the Locrians of Opus had been called up to come with their whole force, and the Phocians with a thousand men. The Greeks themselves had sent for them. Their messengers said that they had come ahead as an advance guard for the rest, that the other allies were expected any day, and that the sea was safely guarded, watched over by the Athenians, the Aeginetans and the others assigned to the fleet. There was nothing to fear, they said, because the one coming to attack Greece was not a god but a man. There was no mortal, and never would be one, whose fortune had not had some evil mixed into it from the moment he was born, and the greatest evils fell to the greatest men. So the man marching against them, being mortal, was bound to fall short of what he expected. When the Locrians and Phocians heard this, they came to Trachis to help the others.

204. These troops had other commanders as well, each from his own city, but the man everyone looked up to most, who led the whole army, was the Spartan Leonidas. He was the son of Anaxandrides, son of Leon, son of Eurycratides, son of Anaxander, son of Eurycrates, son of Polydorus, son of Alcamenes, son of Teleclus, son of Archelaus, son of Hegesilaus, son of Doryssus, son of Leobotes, son of Echestratus, son of Agis, son of Eurysthenes, son of Aristodemus, son of Aristomachus, son of Cleodaeus, son of Hyllus, son of Heracles. He had become king of Sparta when no one expected it.

205. He had two brothers older than himself, Cleomenes and Dorieus, so he had been far from any thought of becoming king. But Cleomenes died without a son, and Dorieus was no longer alive either: he too had come to his end, in Sicily. So the kingship came to Leonidas, both because he was older than Cleombrotus (Cleombrotus was the youngest of Anaxandrides' sons) and because he was married to Cleomenes' daughter. At this time, then, he went to Thermopylae with the three hundred men the law assigned to a king, and he chose men who happened to have sons living. On the way there he also picked up the Thebans I counted when I listed the numbers of the troops; their commander was Leontiades son of Eurymachus. Leonidas was eager to bring these men, out of all the Greeks, for one reason: they had been strongly accused of siding with the Persians. So he called them out to the war because he wanted to find out whether they would send troops with him or openly renounce their alliance with the Greeks. They sent men, but all the while they had something else in mind.

206. The Spartans sent Leonidas and his men out first so that the other allies would see them and join the campaign, and so that the allies would not go over to the Persians themselves on hearing that Sparta was holding back. Later, once they had kept their festival (the Carneia, the Spartans' festival of Apollo, stood in their way), they meant to leave a garrison in Sparta and come quickly to help with their whole force. The rest of the allies had planned to do the same, because the Olympic festival happened to fall at the same time as these events. They did not think the fighting at Thermopylae would be decided so soon, so they sent only their advance guards.

207. That was what they intended. Meanwhile the Greeks at Thermopylae, when the Persians came near the pass, were frightened and discussed retreating from their position. The rest of the Peloponnesians thought it best to go back to the Peloponnese and guard the Isthmus. But the Phocians and Locrians were furious at this idea, and Leonidas voted to stay where they were and at the same time to send messengers to the various cities, telling them to come up and help, since they were too few to drive back the army of the Medes.

208. While they were deliberating, Xerxes sent a mounted scout to see how many they were and what they were doing. He had heard while he was still in Thessaly that a small force had gathered at this place, and that its leaders were Spartans, among them Leonidas, a descendant of Heracles. When the horseman rode up toward their camp, he looked them over, though he could not see their whole army. The men posted inside the wall, which they had rebuilt and were guarding, could not be seen. But he did see the ones outside, whose post was in front of the wall, and as it happened, the Spartans were the ones posted outside at that time. He saw some of the men exercising and others combing their long hair. He was amazed at the sight, and at the same time he took note of their numbers. When he had noted everything exactly, he rode back undisturbed. Nobody tried to chase him; they paid him almost no attention at all. When he got back, he told Xerxes everything he had seen.

209. When Xerxes heard this, he could not grasp the truth, which was that the men were getting ready to die and to kill as many of the enemy as they could. It seemed to him that they were behaving in a way that was simply ridiculous. So he sent for Demaratus son of Ariston, who was in his camp. When Demaratus came, Xerxes asked him about each of these things, wanting to understand what the Spartans were doing. Demaratus said, "You heard me speak about these men once before, when we were setting out against Greece. When you heard me, you laughed at me, because I told you how I saw things would turn out. For me, the greatest goal of all is always to tell you the truth, my king. So hear me now as well. These men have come to fight us for the pass, and that is what they are getting ready to do. They have a custom: whenever they are about to risk their lives, they arrange their hair. But you can be sure of this: if you defeat these men and the rest of them who stayed behind in Sparta, there is no other people on earth that will stand and face you, my king, or raise a hand against you. You are now about to fight the finest kingdom and city among the Greeks, and the best men."

What Demaratus said seemed completely unbelievable to Xerxes, and he asked a second time how, being so few, they would fight against his army. Demaratus answered, "My king, treat me as a liar if things do not turn out the way I say."

210. Saying this did not convince Xerxes. He let four days go by, expecting all the time that they would run away. But on the fifth day, when they had not left but were still there, out of sheer impudence and folly, as he thought, he grew angry and sent the Medes and the Cissians against them, with orders to take them alive and bring them before him. The Medes charged and attacked the Greeks, and many of them fell. Others kept coming up in their place, and they were not driven off, though they lost heavily. They made it clear to everyone, and not least to the king himself, that he had many human beings but few men. This fighting went on all day.

211. When the Medes had been badly mauled, they pulled out of the battle. Then the Persians the king called the "Immortals," whose commander was Hydarnes, took their place and came on to the attack, thinking that they at least would beat the enemy easily. But when these too came to grips with the Greeks, they did no better than the Median troops. They did exactly as badly, because they were fighting in a narrow space, with shorter spears than the Greeks, and could not make use of their numbers. The Spartans, meanwhile, fought in a way worth remembering. Among the other skills they showed, as men who knew everything about fighting facing men who knew nothing, they would turn their backs on the enemy and pretend to run away. The enemy, seeing them run, would chase after them with shouting and clashing of weapons. Then, just as they were being caught, the Spartans would turn and face their pursuers, and in turning they killed countless numbers of Persians. A few of the Spartans themselves fell in these clashes too. So, since the Persians could make no headway in trying for the entrance, though they attacked it by divisions and in every way they could think of, they withdrew.

212. During these attacks, it is said, the king, who was watching, leaped up from his seat three times in fear for his army. That was how they fought that day. On the next day the enemy did no better. They went into battle expecting that, since their opponents were so few, they would be crippled by their wounds and unable to lift a hand against them any longer. But the Greeks were drawn up by companies and by nations, and each fought in its turn, except for the Phocians, who had been posted on the mountain to guard the path. So when the Persians found nothing different from what they had seen the day before, they withdrew from the fight.

213. The king was at a loss about what to do next, and then Ephialtes son of Eurydemus, a Malian, came to talk with him, thinking he would get a very large reward from the king. He told him of the path that leads over the mountain to Thermopylae, and so he brought about the destruction of the Greeks who stayed there. Later, out of fear of the Spartans, he fled to Thessaly. While he was in exile, the Amphictyons' delegates, at a meeting of the Amphictyonic assembly at Pylae, put a price on his head. Some time after that he came back to Anticyra and was killed by Athenades, a man of Trachis. This Athenades killed Ephialtes for a different reason, which I will explain later in my history, but the Spartans honored him for it all the same.

214. That is how Ephialtes was killed afterward. But there is another story told: that it was Onetes son of Phanagoras, a man of Carystus, and Corydallus of Anticyra who showed the Persians the way around the mountain. I don't accept this at all. In the first place, we have to judge by this fact: the delegates of the Greeks did not put a price on the heads of Onetes and Corydallus, but on that of Ephialtes of Trachis, and they surely had the most exact information on the matter. In the second place, we know that Ephialtes went into exile to escape this charge. It is true that Onetes could have known about the path, even though he was not a Malian, if he had spent a lot of time in that country. But it was Ephialtes who led them around the mountain by the path, and so it is Ephialtes I write down as the guilty man.

215. Xerxes was delighted with what Ephialtes promised to do, and at once, full of joy, he sent off Hydarnes and the men under Hydarnes' command. They left the camp at about lamp-lighting time. This path had been discovered by the Malians who live in that country, and after discovering it they had used it to lead the Thessalians against the Phocians, back when the Phocians had walled off the pass and were sheltering behind the wall from their attacks. That is how long ago the Malians had shown that the pass was worth nothing.

216. The path runs like this. It begins at the river Asopus, which flows through the gorge. The mountain and the path have the same name, Anopaea. This Anopaea runs along the ridge of the mountain and ends near the town of Alpeni, the first town of the Locrians on the side toward Malis, and near the rock called Black Buttocks and the Seats of the Cercopes. That is where the path is at its very narrowest.

217. Along this path, then, the Persians crossed the Asopus and marched all night, with the mountains of the Oetaeans on their right and those of the Trachinians on their left. At dawn they reached the top of the mountain. On this part of the mountain, as I explained before, a thousand Phocian hoplites were standing guard, to protect their own country and to hold the path. While the pass below was held by the men I have named, the path over the mountain was guarded by the Phocians, who had volunteered to Leonidas for the job.

218. While the Persians were climbing, the Phocians could not see them, because the whole mountain was covered in oak trees. This is how the Phocians found out that they had made the climb. The day was still, and the Persians made a good deal of noise, as you would expect with leaves lying spread over the ground under their feet. At this the Phocians jumped up and began putting on their armor, and by then the enemy was right on top of them. When the Persians saw men arming themselves, they were astonished. They had expected no one to appear against them, and instead they had run into an armed force. Then Hydarnes, afraid that the Phocians might be Spartans, asked Ephialtes what people these troops belonged to. When he had been told exactly, he drew up the Persians for battle. But the Phocians, struck by thick volleys of arrows, fled at once to the highest peak of the mountain, convinced that the enemy had come to attack them in particular, and there they were ready to die. That was what the Phocians had in mind. But the Persians with Ephialtes and Hydarnes paid no attention to the Phocians and went down the mountain as fast as they could.

219. The Greeks at Thermopylae had their first warning from the seer Megistias. He looked into the sacrificed victims and announced the death that would come to them at dawn. After that, deserters brought the news that the Persians were going around. They reported it while it was still night. Third came the lookouts, who ran down from the heights just as day was breaking. Then the Greeks held a council, and their opinions were divided: some urged that they must not abandon their post, while others argued against it. After this they broke up, and some left and scattered, each to his own city, while others were ready to stay there with Leonidas.

220. It is also said, though, that Leonidas himself sent them away, out of concern that they should not be killed, but that he thought it would not be right for himself and the Spartans with him to leave the post they had come to guard in the first place. I lean more toward this second view. I think that Leonidas saw that the allies had lost heart and did not want to face the danger with him to the end, so he ordered them to leave, but he believed it would be dishonorable for him to go. If he stayed, he would leave a great name behind him, and Sparta's prosperity would not be wiped out. For the Pythia had given the Spartans an oracle, when they consulted her about this war just as it was first starting, saying that either Sparta must be destroyed by the invaders or their king must lose his life. She gave them this answer in hexameter verse, and it went like this:

	As for you who live in the wide spaces of Sparta:
	either your glorious city is sacked by the sons of Perses,
	or, if not, then all within the borders of broad Lacedaemon
	will mourn for a dead king of the line of Heracles.
	Neither the strength of bulls nor the fury of lions will stop him,
	for he has the strength of Zeus; I tell you, he will not be held back
	until he has torn one or the other of these to pieces.

I believe Leonidas had this in mind, and wanted to win glory for himself above all the other Spartans, and so he sent the allies away. I don't think the ones who left went off in such disorder simply because they disagreed.

221. To my mind, one of the strongest pieces of evidence for this is the following. It is well known that Leonidas tried to send away the seer as well, the one who was with this army, Megistias the Acarnanian, who was said to be descended from Melampus. He did not want him to die with them after he had read in the victims what was going to happen to them. But when Megistias was told to go, he would not leave himself. Instead he sent away his son, who was serving with him in the army and was his only child.

222. So the allies who were dismissed did as Leonidas said and left, and only the Thespians and the Thebans stayed behind with the Spartans. Of these, the Thebans stayed against their will, not because they wanted to: Leonidas kept them, treating them as hostages. But the Thespians stayed very willingly. They said they would not go away and abandon Leonidas and the men with him. So they stayed behind and died with them. Their commander was Demophilus son of Diadromes.

223. Meanwhile Xerxes poured libations at sunrise and then waited for a while, until about the hour when the marketplace fills up, in mid-morning, and then he advanced on them. Ephialtes had told him to do this, since the way down the mountain is shorter, and the distance much less, than the way around and up. So the invaders with Xerxes moved forward to the attack. And the Greeks with Leonidas, knowing they were marching out to their deaths, now went much farther out than at first, into the wider part of the pass. On the earlier days, while the wall was being guarded, they had fought by falling back into the narrow part of the pass. But now they met the enemy outside the narrows, and a great many of the invaders fell. Behind the enemy ranks, the officers of the divisions stood with whips in their hands, lashing every man and driving them on and on toward the front. Many of them were pushed into the sea and drowned, and even more were trampled alive by their own side, and no one counted how many died. For the Greeks knew that death was coming for them from the men who were going around the mountain, and so they threw all the strength they had against the enemy, as hard as they could, with no thought for danger, as if possessed by a reckless frenzy.

224. By now most of them had broken their spears in this fighting, as it happened, and they were killing Persians with their swords. In this fighting Leonidas fell, having proved himself a very brave man, and with him other Spartans of note. I learned their names, as men who had shown themselves worthy, and in fact I learned the names of all three hundred. Many notable Persians fell here too, among them two sons of Darius, Abrocomes and Hyperanthes, whose mother was Phratagune, daughter of Artanes. Artanes was King Darius' brother and the son of Hystaspes son of Arsames. When he gave his daughter to Darius in marriage, he gave her his whole estate along with her, because she was his only child.

225. So two of Xerxes' brothers fell fighting here. Meanwhile a great struggle broke out between the Persians and the Spartans over the body of Leonidas, until the Greeks, by sheer courage, dragged it away from the enemy, after driving them back four times. This struggle went on until the men with Ephialtes arrived. When the Greeks learned that they had come, from that moment the fight changed character. They fell back to the narrow part of the road, went past the wall, and took up a position on the little hill there, all together in a body, except for the Thebans. This hill is at the entrance to the pass, where the stone lion in honor of Leonidas now stands. On this spot they defended themselves with daggers, those who still had them, and with their hands and teeth, until the enemy buried them under a hail of missiles. Some of the enemy had followed straight after them and torn down the wall, and others had come around and surrounded them on every side.

226. That is how the Spartans and Thespians proved their courage. But the Spartan Dieneces, they say, proved himself the bravest man of all. He is the one who, they report, made this remark before they joined battle with the Medes. A man of Trachis told him that when the enemy fired their arrows, they blotted out the sun with the sheer number of arrows, so huge was their army. Dieneces was not shaken by this at all. Thinking nothing of the numbers of the Medes, he said that their friend from Trachis had brought them very good news: if the Medes hid the sun, they would be fighting in the shade instead of in the sun.

227. They say that Dieneces the Spartan left behind this saying and others like it to be remembered by. After him, they say, the bravest of the Spartans were two brothers, Alpheus and Maron, the sons of Orsiphantus. Of the Thespians, the man who won the most honor was named Dithyrambus son of Harmatides.

228. The men were buried where they fell. For them, and for those who had died before Leonidas sent the allies away, there is an inscription that reads:

	Here once four thousand men from the Peloponnese
	stood and fought against three million.

That inscription is for them all. For the Spartans alone there is this one:

	Stranger, go tell the Spartans that we lie
	here in this place, obedient to their laws.

That one is for the Spartans. And for the seer, this:

	This is the tomb of the famous Megistias, whom the Medes
	killed where the Spercheus flows, as they crossed the stream.
	He was a seer, and he knew clearly the fate that was coming,
	yet he could not bring himself to leave the leaders of Sparta.

It was the Amphictyons who honored the dead with these inscriptions and memorial pillars, except for the seer's inscription. That one, for the seer Megistias, was inscribed by Simonides son of Leoprepes, because the two were guest-friends.

229. Two of the three hundred, it is said, Eurytus and Aristodemus, could have done either of two things if they had agreed with each other. Leonidas had sent them away from the camp, and they were lying at Alpeni, suffering badly from an eye disease. So they could have come home safe to Sparta together, or, if they did not want to go home, they could have died with the rest. They could have done either one, but they would not agree, and they split. When Eurytus, they say, learned that the Persians had gone around, he asked for his armor, put it on, and ordered his helot to lead him to where the fighting was. When the helot had led him there, he ran away and left, and Eurytus plunged into the thick of the fighting and was killed. But Aristodemus lost his nerve and stayed behind. Now if Aristodemus had been the only one who was ill, and had come home to Sparta, or if both of them had come back together, I don't believe the Spartans would have shown any anger toward them. But as it was, one of them had died, and the other, holding on to the same excuse that the first could also have used, had refused to die. So the Spartans were bound to be furious with Aristodemus.

230. Some say Aristodemus came home safe to Sparta in this way and with the excuse I have described. Others say he had been sent from the camp as a messenger, and though he could have gotten back in time to find the battle still going, he chose not to. He lingered on the road and so saved his life, while the man sent with him as a fellow messenger reached the battle and was killed.

231. When Aristodemus came home to Sparta, he met with disgrace and dishonor. This is the dishonor he suffered: no Spartan would give him a light for his fire or speak to him, and he was disgraced with the name "Aristodemus the Coward."

232. But in the battle at Plataea he made up for all the blame laid on him. It is also reported that another of the three hundred survived, a man named Pantites, who had been sent to Thessaly as a messenger. When he came back to Sparta and found himself in disgrace, they say, he hanged himself.

233. The Thebans, whose commander was Leontiades, stayed with the Greeks and fought against the king's army for a while, because they had no choice. But when they saw the Persian side winning, then, and not before, while the Greeks with Leonidas were hurrying to the little hill, they broke away from them. They held out their hands and went up to the enemy, saying something that was perfectly true: that they were on the Persians' side, that they had been among the first to give earth and water to the king, that they had come to Thermopylae because they had been forced to, and that they were not to blame for the losses the king had suffered. By saying this they saved their lives, since they also had the Thessalians to vouch for what they said. Still, their luck was not good all the way through. Some were killed as they came forward, and when they had come over and the enemy had them in their power, most of them were branded with the royal marks on Xerxes' orders, starting with their commander Leontiades. This was the Leontiades whose son Eurymachus was later killed by the Plataeans, when he led four hundred Thebans and seized the city of Plataea.

234. That is how the Greeks fought at Thermopylae. Then Xerxes sent for Demaratus and questioned him, beginning like this: "Demaratus, you are a good man. I judge that by the truth of what you told me, because everything you said turned out just as you said it would. Now tell me: how many Lacedaemonians are left, and how many of them are like these men in war? Or are they all like this?"

Demaratus said, "My king, there are a great many Lacedaemonians and many cities of them, but you will learn what you want to know. In Lacedaemon there is the city of Sparta, with about eight thousand men. These are all the equals of the men who fought here. The other Lacedaemonians (the free men of the Laconian towns, who are not citizens of Sparta) are not equal to these, but they are good men too."

To this Xerxes said, "Demaratus, how can we beat these men with the least trouble? Come, explain it to us. You know the ways they think and plan, since you were once their king."

235. Demaratus answered, "My king, if you are seriously asking my advice, it is right that I tell you the best course. What if you sent three hundred ships from your fleet to attack the coast of Laconia? There is an island lying near it called Cythera. Chilon, who was a very wise man among us, said that it would be better for the Spartans if that island sank beneath the sea than if it stayed above it. He always expected that something would come from it of exactly the kind I am now describing to you. Not that he knew about your expedition in advance; he feared every expedition of men alike. So let your forces set out from this island and keep the Spartans in fear. While they have a war of their own right on their doorstep, you will have nothing to fear from them; they will not come to the rescue while the rest of Greece is being conquered by your land army. Then once the rest of Greece is subdued, from that moment the Spartan power will be left alone, and so it will be weak. But if you don't do this, I will tell you what you can expect. There is a narrow isthmus leading into the Peloponnese, and in that place you can expect other battles, harder than the ones that have been fought already, because all the Peloponnesians have sworn an alliance against you. But if you do the other thing I spoke of, this isthmus and the cities inside it will come over to you without a battle."

236. After him spoke Achaemenes, Xerxes' brother and the commander of the fleet. He happened to be present for this conversation, and he was afraid Xerxes would be persuaded to do it. "My king," he said, "I see you are listening to the words of a man who envies your good fortune, or is even a traitor to your cause. This is just the sort of temper the Greeks delight in: they envy a man his good luck, and they hate whatever is stronger than they are. Four hundred of our ships have been wrecked, on top of our other misfortunes. If you send away another three hundred from the fleet's station to sail around the Peloponnese, your opponents will be a match for you in a fight. But while the fleet is all together, it is hard for them to deal with, and they will not be a match for you at all. Besides, the whole navy will support the army and be supported by it if they move forward together. But if you split them up, you will be no use to them, and they will be no use to you. My view is rather that you should put your own affairs in good order and not worry about the enemy's: where they will make their stand, what they will do, or how many of them there are. It is enough for them to look out for themselves, and for us to do the same for ourselves. And if the Spartans come out to face the Persians in battle, they certainly will not heal the wound they are suffering from now."

237. Xerxes answered him like this: "Achaemenes, I think you are right, and I will do as you say. But Demaratus says what he believes is best for me, even if his advice loses out to yours. I certainly will not accept what you said, that he is not loyal to my cause. I judge that both by what he said to me before this and by the plain truth. It is true that one citizen envies another for his good fortune and shows his hostility by keeping silent. A citizen whom a fellow citizen consults will not suggest what he really thinks is best, unless he has reached a great height of virtue, and such men are surely few. But a guest-friend is more loyal than anything on earth to a guest-friend who is doing well, and if his friend asks his advice, he will give him the best counsel he can. So, as for this slander against Demaratus, who is my guest-friend, I order everyone to stop it from now on."

238. After saying this, Xerxes went out among the bodies of the dead. When he heard that Leonidas had been the Spartans' king and commander, he ordered his men to cut off his head and crucify him. It has become clear to me from many other pieces of evidence, but from none more strongly than this, that King Xerxes was more furious with Leonidas while he lived than with any other man on earth. Otherwise he would never have committed this outrage against his corpse, because of all the people I know, the Persians are the ones most accustomed to honor men who are brave in war. So the men who were given these orders carried them out.

239. Now I will go back to the point in my story where I left it unfinished. The Spartans had been the first of all to learn that the king was preparing an expedition against Greece. That was how they came to send to the oracle at Delphi, where they were given the answer I reported a little earlier. They learned the news in a strange way. Demaratus son of Ariston, after he had fled to the Persians, was not friendly toward the Spartans, in my opinion, and likelihood supports my opinion. But anyone may guess for himself whether he did what follows out of goodwill or to gloat over them. When Xerxes had decided to march against Greece, Demaratus, who was at Susa and had learned of it, wanted to send word to the Spartans. There was no other way he could get the message to them, since he was in danger of being caught, so he came up with this trick. He took a folding writing tablet and scraped off the wax that covered it. Then he wrote the king's plan on the wood of the tablet, and after that he melted the wax and poured it back over the writing, so that the tablet, carried blank, would not get its bearer into trouble with the guards on the roads. When it reached Sparta, the Spartans could not make anything of it, until finally, as I am told, Gorgo, the daughter of Cleomenes and wife of Leonidas, worked it out herself and suggested a plan: she told them to scrape off the wax, and they would find writing on the wood. They did as she said, found the writing and read it, and then sent word to the rest of the Greeks. This, they say, is how it happened.
