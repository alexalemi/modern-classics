Chapter 41: Athens and the Wooden Wall, and the Greeks Appeal to Argos and to Gelon

138. In name, the king's expedition was marching against Athens, but in fact it was aimed at the whole of Greece. The Greeks had known this for a long time, but they did not all take it the same way. Those who had given earth and water to the Persian felt confident, supposing that the invader would do them no harm. Those who had not given it were terrified. There were not enough ships in Greece to meet the invader in battle, and most of the cities were unwilling to take up the war and were eager to go over to the Persians.

139. Here I am forced to state an opinion that most people will resent. Even so, I will not hold back from saying what I clearly see to be the truth. If the Athenians had been gripped by fear of the danger coming at them and had abandoned their land, or if they had stayed without leaving it and surrendered to Xerxes, then no one would have tried to oppose the king at sea. And if no one had opposed Xerxes at sea, this is roughly what would have happened on land. The Peloponnesians might have thrown wall after wall across the Isthmus, layer on layer like so many tunics, but the Spartans would still have been deserted by their allies. The allies would not have left them willingly but because they had no choice, since the Persian fleet would have conquered them city by city. So the Spartans would have been left alone, and after doing great deeds of courage, they would have died nobly. Either they would have suffered that fate, or else, seeing the rest of the Greeks also going over to the Persians, they would have come to terms with Xerxes before it came to that. Either way, Greece would have fallen under Persian rule. For I cannot discover what good the walls across the Isthmus would have done, once the king was master of the sea.

As it is, anyone who says that the Athenians were the saviors of Greece would hit the truth exactly. Whichever side they took, the scales were likely to tip that way. They chose that Greece should go on living in freedom, and it was they who roused the whole of Greece that was left, all of it, that is, that had not gone over to the Persians. And it was they who, after the gods at least, drove back the king. Not even the terrifying oracles that came from Delphi and filled them with dread could persuade them to abandon Greece. They stayed where they were and stood their ground to meet the invader of their land.

140. For the Athenians had sent messengers to Delphi and were ready to consult the oracle. When these men had performed the usual rites in the sacred precinct and had gone into the inner shrine and sat down, the Pythia, whose name was Aristonice, gave them this oracle:

	Wretched ones, why do you sit here? Run to the ends of the earth,
	leave your homes and the heights of your city, round as a wheel!
	For neither the head nor the body is safe any longer,
	nor the feet below, nor the hands, nor the middle is left you:
	all is destroyed together. Fire and the fierce god of war,
	driving his Syrian chariot hard, will hurl them to ruin.
	Not yours alone: he will bring down many more strongholds,
	and hand many temples of the gods to the ravening fire,
	temples that stand even now with the sweat of terror streaming down them,
	shaking with dread; and look, from the top of the roof to the floor
	dark blood is trickling, foretelling the evil no one can escape.
	Out, get out of the shrine, and steep your souls in sorrow!

141. When the men the Athenians had sent to consult the oracle heard this, they were deeply distressed. As they sat there in despair at the disaster that had been foretold for them, Timon son of Androbulus, a man of Delphi as highly respected as anyone there, advised them to take a suppliant's branch and go in a second time to consult the oracle as suppliants. The Athenians did as he advised and said: "Lord Apollo, give us some better oracle about our homeland, out of respect for these suppliant branches we have brought you. Otherwise we will not leave the shrine. We will stay right here, where we are now, until we die." When they said this, the priestess gave them a second oracle, as follows:

	Pallas cannot win over great Zeus of Olympus,
	though she pleads with him in many words and closely woven cunning.
	But I will tell you this as well, and make it fast as adamant.
	All else will be taken that the borders of Cecrops
	hold within them, and the dark glens of holy Cithaeron.
	At the last Zeus grants to Athena, the Trito-born, a wooden wall
	alone to stand unsacked, and it will help you and your children.
	Do not wait there for the horsemen and the foot soldiers without number;
	do not sit still as the army comes from the mainland, but pull back
	and turn your back to the enemy: one day you will face him yet.
	Divine Salamis, you will bring death to the sons of women,
	whether the grain is being sown or gathered in.

142. This seemed to them to be a milder answer than the first, as in truth it was. So they had it written down and went back to Athens with it. When the messengers had returned and reported it to the people, many different opinions were offered by those trying to work out what the oracle meant. Two of them stood most directly opposed to each other. Some of the older men said they thought the god had prophesied that the Acropolis would survive. In the old days the Acropolis of Athens had been fenced with a hedge of thorns, and so they guessed that the "wooden wall" meant that fence. Others said, on the contrary, that the god meant their ships, and they urged the Athenians to let everything else go and get the ships ready. But those who said the ships were the wooden wall were thrown off by the last two lines the priestess had spoken:

	Divine Salamis, you will bring death to the sons of women,
	whether the grain is being sown or gathered in.

These lines upset the view of those who said the ships were the wooden wall. For the interpreters of oracles took them to mean that if the Athenians prepared for a sea battle, they were fated to be defeated off Salamis.

143. Now there was a man among the Athenians who had lately been making his way into the front rank, whose name was Themistocles; he was called the son of Neocles. This man said that the interpreters of oracles had not worked out the whole of it correctly. If the words really referred to the Athenians, he argued, he did not think the oracle would have put it so gently. It would have said "Cruel Salamis" instead of "Divine Salamis," at least if the people living there were fated to die around it. The truth was that the god had spoken the oracle about the enemy, if one understood it rightly, and not about the Athenians. So he advised them to get ready to fight at sea, since the ships were their wooden wall. When Themistocles had declared his view this way, the Athenians judged that it was better to follow him than the interpreters of oracles. The interpreters told them not to prepare for a sea battle, and in short not to lift a hand against the enemy at all, but to leave the land of Attica and settle somewhere else.

144. Before this, another proposal of Themistocles' had also turned out to be the best one at the critical moment. The Athenians had a great sum of money in the public treasury, which had come in from the mines at Laurium, and they were about to share it out among themselves, ten drachmas to each man. Themistocles persuaded them to give up this plan of dividing it, and to use the money instead to build two hundred ships for the war, meaning the war with the Aeginetans. That war, by breaking out when it did, turned out to be the saving of Greece at that time, because it forced the Athenians to become a sea power. The ships were never used for the purpose they were built for, and so they were there for Greece when she needed them. So the Athenians had these ships already, built beforehand, and they needed to build more besides. After the oracle was given, they took counsel and resolved to obey the god: they would meet the invader of Greece at sea with their whole force, together with any of the other Greeks who were willing to join them.

145. Those were the oracles that had been given to the Athenians. Now the Greeks who had the better attitude toward Greece came together in one place, talked over their situation, and exchanged pledges with one another. In their deliberations they decided that the first thing to do, before anything else, was to put an end to their quarrels and the wars they were fighting among themselves. There were wars going on between various cities, but the biggest was the one between the Athenians and the Aeginetans. Next, when they learned that Xerxes was at Sardis with his army, they decided to send spies to Asia to look over the king's forces. They also resolved to send envoys to Argos to make an alliance against the Persian, to send others to Sicily to Gelon son of Deinomenes and to Corcyra to urge them to come to the help of Greece, and others again to Crete. Their aim was that, if possible, the whole Greek people might be united and might all act together toward the same goal, since the danger threatened all the Greeks alike. And Gelon's power was said to be great, far greater than that of any other Greek.

146. After they had made these decisions, they settled their quarrels and then, first of all, sent three men to Asia as spies. These men reached Sardis and gathered information about the king's army, but they were caught. The generals of the land army questioned them and they were being led away to be executed. Their death had been decided. But when Xerxes heard of it, he found fault with the generals' decision and sent some of his bodyguard of spearmen, with orders to bring the spies before him if they found them still alive. They found them still living and brought them before the king. Then Xerxes, once he had learned what they had come for, ordered the spearmen to take them around and show them the whole army, infantry and cavalry both. When they had looked their fill, the guards were to let them go unharmed to whatever country they wished.

147. That was his order, and he gave his reason for it at the same time. If the spies had been put to death, he said, the Greeks would not have learned in advance how far beyond all description his power was, while killing three men would not do the enemy much harm. But when these men went back to Greece, he thought it likely that the Greeks would hear about his power and hand over their freedom to him of their own accord, before the expedition now being set in motion ever took place, and then there would be no need for the trouble of marching an army against them at all.

This opinion of his is like his way of thinking on other occasions too. When Xerxes was at Abydos, he saw ships carrying grain from the Black Sea sailing out through the Hellespont on their way to Aegina and the Peloponnese. The men sitting beside him, when they learned that the ships belonged to the enemy, were ready to capture them, and kept looking at the king to see when he would give the word. But Xerxes asked where the men were sailing. They answered: "To your enemies, master, carrying grain to them." And he replied: "Aren't we sailing to the same place ourselves, supplied with grain as well as everything else we need? So how are they wronging us, if they're carrying provisions there for our use?"

148. So the spies, after looking at everything in this way and then being sent off, returned to Europe. Meanwhile, after sending out the spies, the Greeks who had sworn alliance against the Persian next sent envoys to Argos.

Now the Argives say that their part in this happened as follows. They learned, they say, at the very beginning about the movement the Persians were setting on foot against Greece. Once they knew of it, and saw that the Greeks would try to win them as allies against the Persians, they sent messengers to Delphi to ask the god what they should do to come off best. For not long before, six thousand of them had been killed by the Spartans and by Cleomenes son of Anaxandrides, and that in fact was why they sent to ask. When they asked, the Pythia answered them this way:

	Hated by your neighbors, beloved by the immortal gods,
	keep your spear drawn in, and sit well guarded behind it.
	Guard the head, and the head will keep the limbs and the body safe.

This, they say, was the answer the Pythia had given them before. Afterward, when the envoys of the Greeks came to Argos, as I said, they went into the Council chamber and delivered the message they had been given. To this the Council replied that the Argives were ready to do what was asked, on two conditions: that they could make a thirty years' peace with the Spartans, and that they could have half the leadership of the whole alliance. And yet by strict right, they said, the whole leadership belonged to them; even so, half was enough for them.

149. That, they say, is the answer the Council gave, even though the oracle had forbidden them to make the alliance with the Greeks. They were eager to have the thirty-year truce, they say, even though they were afraid of the oracle, so that, as they claim, their sons might grow to manhood in those years. Without a truce, they were afraid that if a second disaster struck them in fighting the Persian, on top of the one that had already befallen them, they might be subject to the Spartans for all time to come.

Those of the envoys who came from Sparta answered what the Council had said. As for the truce, they would refer it to their public assembly. But as for the leadership, they had been given authority to answer themselves, and so they said this: the Spartans had two kings and the Argives had one, and it was not possible to remove either of Sparta's two from the leadership, but nothing prevented the Argive king from having an equal vote with each of their two. At that, the Argives say, they could not stand the greed of the Spartans. They chose to be ruled by the non-Greeks rather than give way at all to the Spartans, and they told the envoys to leave Argive territory before sunset, or else they would be treated as enemies.

150. That is how much the Argives themselves report about these matters. But there is another story told in Greece. According to it, Xerxes sent a herald to Argos before he set out on his expedition against Greece, and when this herald arrived, they say, he spoke as follows: "Men of Argos, King Xerxes says this to you. We believe that Perses, our ancestor, was the son of Perseus son of Danae, and that his mother was Andromeda, daughter of Cepheus. If that is so, it seems we are descended from you. So it would not be right for us to march against our own forefathers, or for you to oppose us by helping others. It would be right for you to keep quiet and stay out of it. For if things go the way I intend, I will hold no people in higher honor than you." The Argives, it is said, thought a great deal of this offer. So at first they offered no help and asked for no share in anything. But later, when the Greeks tried to bring them in, they knew perfectly well that the Spartans would not give them a share of the command, and so they demanded it only to have an excuse for keeping out of the war.

151. Some of the Greeks also report that something happened many years later which agrees with this account. Envoys of the Athenians, they say, happened to be at Susa, the city of Memnon, on some other business: Callias son of Hipponicus and those who had gone up with him. At that same time the Argives had also sent envoys to Susa. These envoys asked Artaxerxes son of Xerxes whether the friendship they had formed with Xerxes still held good, as they wished it to, or whether he now regarded them as enemies. And King Artaxerxes said that it most certainly still held, and that there was no city he considered a better friend than Argos.

152. Now whether Xerxes really did send a herald to Argos with the message that has been reported, and whether envoys of the Argives went up to Susa and asked Artaxerxes about their friendship, I can't say for certain, and I offer no opinion on these matters beyond what the Argives themselves say. But I do know this much. If all the peoples of the world were to bring their own troubles together into one place, wanting to trade them with their neighbors, each of them, after taking a close look at the troubles of the others, would gladly carry back home the ones they had brought. So the Argives are not the ones who acted most shamefully of all. I am obliged to report what is said, but I am not obliged to believe all of it, and let that hold for this whole history. For I must add that this too is said: that it was actually the Argives who invited the Persian to invade Greece, because their war with the Spartans had gone badly for them, and they were willing to suffer anything at all rather than the misery they were in at the time.

153. So much for the Argives. Meanwhile envoys from the allies had come to Sicily to confer with Gelon, and among them, from Sparta, was Syagrus. Now Gelon's ancestor, the first of his family to settle at Gela, came from the island of Telos, which lies off Triopium. When Gela was founded by the Lindians from Rhodes together with Antiphemus, he went along too. In the course of time his descendants became priests of the mysteries of the Earth goddesses, and they kept that office. It had been won by Telines, one of their ancestors, in the following way. Certain men of Gela had been beaten in a struggle between factions and had fled to Mactorium, the town that stands above Gela. Telines brought these men back to Gela from exile, with no force of men at all, only the sacred objects of these goddesses. Where he got them from, or whether he came by them on his own, I cannot say. In any case, trusting in them, he brought the men back from exile on condition that his descendants would be priests of the goddesses' mysteries.

It has also surprised me, given what I am told, that Telines was able to do such a great deed. Deeds like that, I think, do not come from just anyone, but from a man with a brave spirit and manly strength, whereas the people of Sicily say that Telines was just the opposite: a soft, effeminate man with rather a poor spirit.

154. So that was how he won this privilege. Cleander son of Pantares had been tyrant of Gela for seven years when he came to his end, killed by Sabyllus, a man of Gela. Then Hippocrates, Cleander's brother, took over the sole rule. While Hippocrates was tyrant, Gelon, a descendant of Telines the priest of the mysteries, was one of Hippocrates' bodyguard of spearmen, along with many others, among them Aenesidemus son of Pataecus. Not long after, he was made commander of the whole cavalry because of his courage. For when Hippocrates besieged Callipolis, Naxos, Zancle and Leontini one after another, and Syracuse too, and many towns of the non-Greeks, Gelon proved himself a brilliant fighter in those wars. Of the cities I just named, not one except Syracuse escaped being conquered by Hippocrates. The Syracusans were beaten in battle at the river Elorus, but they were rescued by the Corinthians and the Corcyreans. These rescued them and settled the quarrel on the condition that the Syracusans hand over Camarina to Hippocrates. In ancient times Camarina had belonged to Syracuse.

155. Then it was Hippocrates' turn to die, after being tyrant for the same number of years as his brother Cleander. He was killed at the city of Hybla, where he had gone on campaign against the Sicels. When the citizens of Gela refused to obey any longer, Gelon made a show of standing up for Hippocrates' sons, Eucleides and Cleander. But in fact, once he had beaten the men of Gela in battle, he robbed Hippocrates' sons of the power and ruled himself. After this stroke of luck, Gelon brought back the Syracusans called the "landholders," who had been driven into exile by the common people and by their own slaves, the ones called Cyllyrians. He brought them back from the town of Casmene to Syracuse, and so he got hold of that city too. For when Gelon marched against them, the common people of Syracuse handed over their city and themselves to him.

156. Once he had Syracuse in his power, he cared less about Gela, which he also ruled, and he put it in the charge of his brother Hieron while he built up Syracuse. Right away the city shot up and flourished. First he brought all the people of Camarina to Syracuse and made them citizens, and razed the town of Camarina to the ground. Second, he did the same to more than half the citizens of Gela as he had done to the people of Camarina. Then there were the Megarians of Sicily. When they were besieged and surrendered on terms, Gelon brought their wealthy men to Syracuse and made them citizens, even though they had started the war against him and expected to be put to death for it. But the common people of Megara had no part in causing this war and expected no harm. Gelon brought them to Syracuse too, and sold them as slaves to be carried out of Sicily. He did the same to the Euboeans in Sicily, making the same distinction between them. He treated these two cities this way because he thought the common people were the most unpleasant people to have in a state.

157. That is how Gelon had become a powerful tyrant. At this time the envoys of the Greeks arrived at Syracuse, and when they were given a hearing they said: "The Spartans and their allies have sent us to win you to our side against the Persian. You must know, surely, about the man who is about to invade Greece. A Persian means to bridge the Hellespont and lead all the armies of the East out of Asia against Greece. He pretends he is marching on Athens, but in fact he means to bring all Greece under his rule. You have risen to great power, and as ruler of Sicily you hold no small share of Greece. So come and help those who are working to free Greece, and join in making her free. For if all Greece is gathered together as one, it makes a great force, and we are a match in battle for those coming against us. But if some of us go over to the enemy and others refuse to help, and the healthy part of Greece is small, then right away there is a danger that all Greece will fall. Do not hope that if the Persian beats us in battle he will not come on to you. Guard yourself against that in advance. By helping us you are helping yourself. And a plan that is wisely made usually turns out well in the end."

158. That is what the envoys said. Gelon came down on them hard, answering like this: "Men of Greece, this is a selfish speech you have dared to come here with, inviting me to be your ally against the foreigner. Not long ago I asked you to join me in fighting an army of foreigners, when I had a quarrel with the Carthaginians. I urged you to take vengeance on the people of Egesta for the death of Dorieus son of Anaxandrides, and I offered at the same time to help free the trading ports that have brought you such great profit and gain. And you did not come to help me, either for my own sake or to avenge the death of Dorieus. As far as you are concerned, all that country would still be under the rule of foreigners now. But things turned out well for us, and better than that. And now that the war has come around and reached you, you have finally remembered Gelon.

"But though you treated me with contempt, I will not behave the way you did. I am ready to come to your help, with two hundred triremes and 20,000 hoplites, along with 2,000 horsemen, 2,000 archers, 2,000 slingers, and 2,000 light-armed men to run beside the horsemen. What is more, I promise to supply grain for the whole Greek army until we have finished the war. I promise all this on one condition: that I am the commander and leader of the Greeks against the foreigner. On any other condition I will neither come myself nor send anyone else."

159. When Syagrus heard this, he could not contain himself, and he said: "Agamemnon son of Pelops would groan aloud indeed, if he heard that the Spartans had been robbed of the leadership by Gelon and the Syracusans! No, don't mention this condition again, that we should hand the leadership over to you. If you want to come to the help of Greece, understand that you will be under the command of the Spartans. And if you really refuse to take orders, then don't come to help us at all."

160. Gelon saw how hostile Syagrus' speech was, and so he laid out his final offer to them: "My Spartan friend, insults that sink into a man's heart usually stir him to anger in return. But though you have insulted me in what you said, you will not provoke me into answering you in an unseemly way. Since you lay claim to the leadership so hard, it would be fair for me to claim it even more than you, since I lead an army many times larger and far more ships. But since this condition is so distasteful to you, we will give up something of our first proposal. Suppose you lead the land army and I lead the fleet. Or if you would rather lead at sea, I am willing to lead on land. Either you must be content with these terms, or go home without the alliance I have to give."

161. Those were the offers Gelon made, and the Athenian envoy, speaking before the Spartan could, answered him this way: "King of the Syracusans, Greece did not send us to you because she needs a leader, but because she needs an army. But you hold out no hope that you will send an army unless you lead Greece, and what you are really after is becoming commander of the Greek armies. As long as you were demanding to lead the whole Greek army, it was enough for us Athenians to keep quiet, knowing that the Spartan could speak up for both of us. But now, since you have been turned down for the whole and are asking to command the fleet, here is how things stand. Even if the Spartan lets you command it, we will not. The fleet at least is ours, if the Spartans do not want it themselves. If they wish to lead it, we do not oppose them, but we will allow no one else to command the ships. Otherwise it would be pointless for us to own the largest navy of all the Greeks, if we, Athenians, handed the leadership over to Syracusans. We can boast that ours is the most ancient people of all, the only Greeks who have never moved from one land to another. One of our own was the man whom Homer, the epic poet, called the best of all who came to Troy at drawing up an army and setting it in battle order. So no one can fairly blame us for saying these things."

162. Gelon answered: "My Athenian friend, it seems you have plenty of commanders, but you will not have anyone to command. Since you will not give way at all and want to have everything, you had better go home as fast as you can and tell the Greeks that the spring has been taken out of their year." This is what the saying means. The spring is plainly the finest part of the year, and he meant that his army was the finest part of the Greek army. So Greece, deprived of his alliance, was, he said, like a year with the spring taken out of it.
