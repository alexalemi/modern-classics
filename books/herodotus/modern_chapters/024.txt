Chapter 24: The War in the Empty Land, the Scythian Riddle, and the Bridge on the Danube

118. These, then, are the nations I have listed in order, and it was to their kings that the Scythians' messengers came. They found them gathered together in one place, and told them what was happening. The Persian king, they said, had already brought everything in the other continent under his power. He had laid a bridge across the narrow neck of the Bosporus and crossed over to this continent, and once across he had conquered the Thracians. Now he was building a bridge over the Danube, because he wanted to bring all these lands under his power as well. "So whatever you do," they said, "don't stand aside and let us be destroyed. Let's all be of one mind and stand together against the man who is coming at us. If you won't do this, we will be forced by necessity either to leave our land, or to stay in it and come to terms with the invader. What else can we do, if you refuse to help us? And after that, things will be no easier for you. The Persian has come against you just as much as against us, and once he has conquered us he won't be content to leave you alone. We can give you strong proof that what we say is true. If the Persian were making this expedition against us alone, because he wanted revenge for the time when his people were our subjects, he would have left everyone else alone and come straight to invade our land. That way he would have made it clear to all that he was marching against the Scythians and not against anyone else. But in fact, ever since he crossed over to this continent, he has forced everyone in his path to submit to him. He already holds the rest of the Thracians, and the Getae too, who are our nearest neighbors."

119. When the Scythians made this proposal, the kings who had come from the various nations talked it over, and their opinions were divided. The kings of the Gelonians, the Budini and the Sauromatae agreed with one another and accepted the proposal: they would help the Scythians. But the kings of the Agathyrsians, the Neuri, the Androphagi, the Melanchlaeni and the Tauri gave the Scythians this answer: "If you had not been the first to wrong the Persians and to start the war, then we would certainly think you were in the right to ask for what you are asking now. We would have agreed to your request and shared your fortunes. As it is, though, you invaded their land without us, and you ruled over the Persians for as long as heaven allowed you to. Now they, stirred up by the same heaven, are paying you back in kind. As for us, we did these men no wrong back then, and we will not start wronging them now, unprovoked. If the Persians come against our land too, and wrong us first, then we too will refuse to submit. But until we see that happen, we will stay where we are. In our opinion the Persians have not come against us at all, but against the people who started the wrong."

120. When this answer was reported to the Scythians, they decided not to fight an open, pitched battle, since these nations would not join them as allies. Instead they would fall back before the Persians and drive their cattle away ahead of them. As they went, they would choke up the wells and springs they passed with earth and destroy the grass on the ground. For this they split themselves into two bodies. They decided that the Sauromatae should be added to one of their divisions, the one Scopasis was king of. If the Persians turned in their direction, this force was to move straight toward the river Don, retreating before the enemy along the shore of Lake Maeotis; and when the Persian marched back again, they were to follow and harry him. That was one division of their kingdom, assigned to the route I have described. The other two kingdoms, the large one ruled by Idanthyrsus and the third, ruled by Taxacis, were to join together into one force, with the Gelonians and the Budini added to them. They too were to fall back before the Persians, staying one day's march ahead of them, getting out of their way and carrying out the plan. First they were to head straight for the lands of the nations that had refused to be their allies, so as to drag them into the war as well. These peoples had not taken up the war with the Persians willingly, but they would be dragged into it all the same, against their will. After that the Scythians would turn back to their own land and attack the enemy, if they decided in council that this was the thing to do.

121. With this plan made, the Scythians went out to meet Darius' army, sending their best horsemen ahead as scouts. But all the wagons that their children and women lived in they sent on ahead, along with all their cattle, except for as much as they needed for food, and ordered them to keep moving steadily toward the north. So the wagons were carried on ahead.

122. When the scouts riding in front of the Scythians found the Persians, about three days' march from the Danube, the Scythians, having found them, kept pitching their camp one day's march ahead of them, utterly destroying everything that grew from the ground. When the Persians saw that the Scythian horsemen had appeared, they came after them, following in their tracks, while the Scythians kept moving on. After this, since the Persians had set off after the first of the divisions, they kept on pursuing eastward, toward the river Don. When the Scythians crossed the Don, the Persians crossed after them and kept up the pursuit, until they had gone right through the land of the Sauromatae and come to that of the Budini.

123. Now as long as the Persians were marching through Scythia and the land of the Sauromatae, they had nothing to destroy, since the land was bare. But when they invaded the land of the Budini, they came upon the wooden-walled city there. The Budini had abandoned it and left it completely empty, and the Persians burned it down. After that they kept following the enemy's tracks onward, until they had passed through the whole of that land and reached the desert. This desert has no people living in it at all. It lies beyond the land of the Budini and stretches for seven days' journey. Beyond the desert live the Thyssagetae, and four large rivers flow down from their country through the land of the Maeotians and empty into what is called Lake Maeotis. Their names are the Lycus, the Oarus, the Don and the Syrgis.

124. So when Darius came to the desert, he stopped his advance and halted his army on the river Oarus. Then he began to build eight large forts, at equal distances from one another, about seven miles apart; their ruins were still standing in my own time. While he was busy with this, the Scythians he was chasing circled around through the country to the north and went back into Scythia. Since they had vanished completely and the Persians could no longer see them anywhere, Darius left the forts half finished, turned back, and began to march west. He supposed that these were the whole body of the Scythians, and that they were fleeing toward the west.

125. He marched his army as fast as he could, and when he reached Scythia he ran into the two combined divisions of the Scythians. When he came upon them he kept up the pursuit, while they kept withdrawing out of his way, always a day's march ahead. Since Darius would not stop coming after them, the Scythians, following the plan they had made, kept retreating before him toward the lands of the nations that had refused to be their allies, and first toward that of the Melanchlaeni. When the Scythians and the Persians together had invaded the Melanchlaeni and thrown them into turmoil, the Scythians led the way into the country of the Androphagi. When these had been thrown into turmoil too, they went on into the land of the Neuri; and while these too were in turmoil, the Scythians kept retreating before the enemy toward the Agathyrsians. But the Agathyrsians saw their neighbors fleeing from the Scythians in turmoil, and before the Scythians could invade their land they sent a herald. He warned the Scythians not to set foot across their borders: if they tried to invade the country, they would first have to fight the Agathyrsians. After giving this warning, the Agathyrsians marched out in arms to their borders, meaning to drive back anyone who came at them. But the Melanchlaeni, the Androphagi and the Neuri, when the Persians and Scythians invaded them together, did not put up a brave defense. They forgot their earlier threats and fled in confusion, farther and farther north, into the desert. As for the Scythians, once the Agathyrsians had warned them off, they made no further attempt to go there. Instead they led the Persians out of the country of the Neuri and back into their own land.

126. This went on for a long time and showed no sign of ending, so Darius sent a horseman to Idanthyrsus, the king of the Scythians, with this message: "You strange, extraordinary man, why do you keep running away, when you could do one of two things? If you think you are strong enough to stand up to my power, then stop wandering about, stand still, and fight. But if you admit you are too weak, then in that case too stop running, and come and talk with your master, bringing him the gifts of earth and water that show you submit."

127. Idanthyrsus, the king of the Scythians, answered him this way: "This is how things stand with me, Persian. I have never yet run away out of fear, not from any other man before now, and not from you now. What I am doing now is no different from what I do in peacetime too. And I will tell you why I don't fight you right away. We have no cities and no farmland that we need to worry might be captured or laid waste, which would make us join battle with you sooner. But if you absolutely must come to a fight soon, then know this: we have tombs where our fathers are buried. So come on, find them, and try to destroy them, and then you will see whether we fight you for the tombs or not. Until then, unless we feel like it, we will not join battle with you. That is enough said about fighting. As for masters, I recognize none but Zeus, my forefather, and Hestia, the queen of the Scythians. Instead of gifts of earth and water, I will send you the kind of gifts that you deserve to get. And in return for your calling yourself my master, I say: be damned to you." This is what is proverbially called "the Scythian saying."

128. So the herald had gone off to report this to Darius. The kings of the Scythians, hearing talk of being subject to a master, were filled with fury. They sent off the division that had been assigned to join the Sauromatae, the one commanded by Scopasis, with orders to go and talk with the Ionians, the ones guarding the bridge over the Danube. Meanwhile those who stayed behind decided to stop leading the Persians around in circles, and to attack them constantly whenever they were out gathering food. So they watched Darius' soldiers as they foraged and carried out what they had decided. The Scythian cavalry always routed the Persian cavalry. But the Persian horsemen, as they fled, would fall back on their foot soldiers, and the infantry would come up to help them; and at that point the Scythians, having driven in the cavalry, would turn back, because they were afraid of the men on foot. The Scythians made attacks like these by night as well.

129. Now I will tell you the thing that, strange to say, most helped the Persians and most hindered the Scythians when they attacked Darius' camp: it was the braying of the donkeys and the look of the mules. For Scythia produces neither donkeys nor mules, as I have explained before, and because of the cold there is not a single donkey or mule anywhere in the Scythian country. So the donkeys, braying wildly, would throw the Scythian cavalry into confusion. Often, just as the Scythians were in the middle of a charge against the Persians, their horses would hear the donkeys' voices and turn back in confusion, amazed, pricking up their ears. They had never heard a sound like that before, or seen a creature of that shape.

130. So for a small part of the war the Persians had the upper hand in this. But whenever the Scythians saw that the Persians were growing uneasy, they did something to keep them in Scythia longer, so that by staying they would suffer from running short of everything. They would leave some of their own cattle behind with the herdsmen and ride off somewhere else, and the Persians would come upon the cattle and take them, and having taken them they were pleased with themselves.

131. This happened again and again, until at last Darius began to be in difficulties. When the kings of the Scythians saw this, they sent a herald bringing Darius gifts: a bird, a mouse, a frog and five arrows. The Persians asked the man who brought them what the gifts meant. But he said that his only orders were to hand them over and get away as fast as possible. If the Persians were clever, he said, they could work out for themselves what the gifts were meant to say.

132. When they heard this, the Persians discussed it among themselves. Darius' opinion was that the Scythians were surrendering to him, giving him both themselves and their earth and water. He reasoned it out this way: a mouse is born in the earth and eats the same food from the earth as a man does; a frog lives in the water; a bird is very like a horse; and in giving the arrows they were handing over their own fighting strength. That was the opinion Darius put forward. But Gobryas, one of the seven men who killed the Magus, disagreed. He guessed that the gifts were saying this: "Unless you turn into birds, Persians, and fly up into the sky, or turn into mice and sink down under the earth, or turn into frogs and leap into the lakes, you will never get back home. You will be shot down by these arrows."

133. So the Persians were trying to guess the meaning of the gifts. Meanwhile the single division of the Scythians, the one that had first been assigned to keep guard along Lake Maeotis and then to go to the Danube and talk with the Ionians, reached the bridge and said this: "Ionians, we have come to bring you freedom, if only you are willing to listen to us. We are told that Darius ordered you to guard the bridge for sixty days only, and if he had not arrived within that time, to go home to your own land. So now, if you do what we say, neither he nor we will have anything to blame you for. Stay the number of days you were given, and after that, go." When the Ionians promised to do this, the Scythians hurried back again by the fastest route.

134. Meanwhile, after the gifts had come to Darius, the Scythians who had stayed behind drew themselves up against the Persians, foot and horse together, meaning to give battle. But when the Scythians were in battle order, a hare darted out through their ranks into the space between the two armies. Every company of them that saw the hare started to chase it. The Scythians fell into disorder and raised a great shouting, and Darius asked what all the noise from the enemy was about. When he heard that they were chasing the hare, he said to the men he usually talked things over with: "These men think very little of us. I see now that Gobryas was right about the Scythian gifts. Since I now think so myself, we need good advice on how to make our way home safely." Gobryas answered him: "My king, even from what people said, I was nearly sure how hard these men would be to deal with. Since I came here I have learned it even better, because I could see they were making fun of us. So here is my advice. As soon as night falls, let us light the campfires as we always do, and deceive with some false story the men least able to bear hardship. Let us tie up all the donkeys and get away, before the Scythians make for the Danube to destroy the bridge, or the Ionians decide on something that could be the end of us."

135. That was Gobryas' advice, and when night came Darius acted on it. He left behind in the camp the men who were worn out with fatigue and whose loss mattered least, and he left the donkeys there too, tied up. His reasons for leaving the donkeys and the weaker part of his army were these: the donkeys, so that their noise would be heard; the men, in reality because of their weakness, but on the pretext, which he announced openly, that he was going to attack the Scythians with the fit part of the army, and that they were to guard the camp in the meantime. Once he had given these instructions to the men he was leaving, and had the campfires lit, Darius hurried away by the fastest route toward the Danube. The donkeys, no longer having the usual crowd around them, brayed all the more loudly because of it. So the Scythians, hearing the donkeys, were quite sure the Persians were still where they had been.

136. But when day came, the men who had been left behind realized that Darius had betrayed them. They held out their hands to the Scythians in surrender and told them how things stood. When the Scythians heard this, they gathered as fast as they could, the two combined divisions of the Scythians and the single division together, with the Sauromatae, the Budini and the Gelonians too, and set off after the Persians, heading straight for the Danube. But most of the Persian army was on foot, and it did not know the roads, since the roads there are not marked out with tracks; while the Scythian army was all on horseback and knew the shortcuts. So the two armies missed each other, and the Scythians reached the bridge long before the Persians. Finding out that the Persians had not yet arrived, they said to the Ionians, who were in their ships: "Ionians, the number of your days is up, and you are not doing right by staying here still. Before, it was fear that kept you here. Now break up the crossing as fast as you can and go away free and unharmed, and thank the gods and the Scythians for it. As for the man who used to be your master, we will deal with him so thoroughly that he will never again march with an army against anyone."

137. At this the Ionians held a council. Miltiades the Athenian, who was the commander and tyrant of the people of the Chersonese on the Hellespont, the Gallipoli peninsula, was for taking the Scythians' advice and setting Ionia free. But Histiaeus of Miletus held the opposite opinion. At present, he said, each one of them ruled his city as tyrant thanks to Darius. If Darius' power were destroyed, neither he himself would be able to go on ruling the Milesians, nor would any of the others be able to go on ruling any other city, because every one of the cities would choose to be governed by its people rather than by a tyrant. When Histiaeus put forward this view, they all went over to it at once, though at first they had been siding with Miltiades.

138. The men who cast their votes between the two views, and who counted for something in the king's eyes, were these. First, the tyrants of the Hellespont: Daphnis of Abydos, Hippoclus of Lampsacus, Herophantus of Parium, Metrodorus of Proconnesus, Aristagoras of Cyzicus and Ariston of Byzantium; these were the ones from the Hellespont. From Ionia there were Strattis of Chios, Aeaces of Samos, Laodamas of Phocaea, and Histiaeus of Miletus, whose view was the one set against Miltiades'. Of the Aeolians the only man of importance there was Aristagoras of Cyme.

139. Once these men had adopted Histiaeus' view, they decided to add to it the following words and deeds. They would break up the part of the bridge on the Scythian side, as far out as an arrow can fly. That way they would seem to be doing something, when in fact they were doing nothing, and it would also stop the Scythians from trying to force their way across the Danube by the bridge. And while they were breaking up the part of the bridge toward Scythia, they would say that they would do everything the Scythians wanted. They added this to the view they had adopted, and then Histiaeus stepped forward from among them and answered the Scythians this way: "Scythians, you have brought good news, and you have hurried to bring it just in time. You are giving us good guidance, and we are doing you good service in return. As you can see, we are breaking up the crossing, and we will show every eagerness in our wish to be free. While we break up the bridge, you should be out searching for those men you talk about, and when you find them, take revenge on them, for us as well as for yourselves, the way they deserve."

140. So the Scythians, believing a second time that the Ionians were telling the truth, turned back to search for the Persians, but they missed their line of march through the country altogether. The Scythians themselves were to blame for this, since they had destroyed the horse pastures in that region and choked up the springs with earth. If they had not done that, they could easily have found the Persians if they had wanted to. As it was, the very measures they thought they had planned best were what made them fail. The Scythians went through the parts of their own land where there was grass for horses and springs of water and searched for the enemy there, thinking that the Persians too would be retreating through country like that. But in fact the Persians marched carefully along the track they had made before, and so they found the crossing of the river, though only with difficulty. They arrived by night, and when they found the bridge broken up they were terrified that the Ionians had deserted them.

141. Now Darius had with him an Egyptian with the loudest voice of any man on earth. Darius ordered this man to stand on the bank of the Danube and call for Histiaeus of Miletus. He did so, and Histiaeus heard the very first shout. He brought up all the ships to ferry the army across, and put the bridge back together as well.

142. That is how the Persians escaped, and the Scythians, searching for them, missed them a second time. And this is the Scythians' verdict on the Ionians: if you look at them as free men, they are the most worthless and cowardly of all men; but if you look at them as slaves, they are the most devoted to their master and the least likely to run away of all slaves. That is the insult the Scythians throw at the Ionians.

143. Darius marched through Thrace and came to Sestos in the Chersonese. From there he crossed over with his ships to Asia himself, but he left Megabazus, a Persian, in command of his army in Europe. This was the man Darius once honored, in Persia, with the following remark. Darius was starting to eat some pomegranates, and as soon as he opened the first one, his brother Artabanus asked him what he would like to have as many of as there were seeds in the pomegranate. Darius said he would rather have that many men like Megabazus than have Greece under his rule. That was how he honored him in Persia, with those words; and now he left him in command, with 80,000 men of his army.

144. This Megabazus made one remark that left an undying memory of him among the peoples of the Hellespont. Once, when he was at Byzantium, he heard that the people of Chalcedon, across the strait, had settled there seventeen years before the Byzantines. When he heard it, he said that the people of Chalcedon must have been blind at the time. Surely they would never have chosen the worse site, when they could have settled on the better one, if they had not been blind. It was this Megabazus who was now left in command in the land of the Hellespont, and he set about conquering everyone who was not on the Persian side.
