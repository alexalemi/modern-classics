Chapter 11: Sacred Animals, Embalming, and Life along the Nile

65. That is how these other peoples defend what they do, though I for my part do not accept it. But the Egyptians are extremely careful in their religious observances, both in other matters that concern the sacred rites and in the ones I am about to describe. Egypt borders on Libya, but it does not have a great many wild animals. Those it does have are all, every one of them, held sacred by the Egyptians, some living alongside people and others not. But if I were to say why the sacred animals have been dedicated in this way, I would find myself talking about the gods, which is the subject I most want to avoid; and whatever I have actually said that touches on it lightly, I said only because I had no choice. As for these animals, the custom is this. Certain Egyptians, both men and women, have been appointed to provide the food for each kind of animal separately, and the office passes down from father to son. The people in the various cities fulfill their vows to these animals in this way. When a man makes a vow to the god the animal belongs to, he shaves his children's heads, either the whole head or a half or a third of it. Then he weighs the hair on a scale against silver, and whatever it weighs, he gives that much silver to the woman who looks after the animals. She cuts up fish of the same value and feeds it to them. That is how food has been arranged for their keep. If anyone kills one of these animals on purpose, the penalty is death; if he kills one by accident, he pays whatever penalty the priests decide. But anyone who kills an ibis or a hawk, whether on purpose or by accident, must die.

66. There are a great many animals that live with people, and there would be many more if it were not for what happens to the cats. When the females have had kittens, they no longer go to the males, and the males, who want to mate with them, cannot. So the males have worked out a trick. They take the kittens away from the females, by force or by stealth, and kill them (though they do not eat them after killing them). The females, robbed of their young and wanting more, then come back to the males, since the cat is an animal that loves its young. And when a fire breaks out, something strange, almost supernatural, takes hold of the cats. The Egyptians stand around at intervals keeping watch on the cats and take no trouble to put out the fire, but the cats slip through or leap over the men and jump into the flames. When this happens, the Egyptians go into deep mourning. In any house where a cat has died a natural death, everyone who lives there shaves off their eyebrows, and only their eyebrows. But in a house where a dog has died, they shave their whole body and their head as well.

67. When cats die they are carried to sacred buildings in the city of Bubastis, where they are embalmed and buried. Dogs are buried by each people in their own city, in sacred tombs, and ichneumons (a kind of mongoose) are buried in just the same way as dogs. Shrews and hawks, however, are carried off to the city of Buto, and ibises to Hermopolis. Bears, which are rare there, and wolves, which are not much bigger than foxes, they bury on the spot where they find them lying.

68. The nature of the crocodile is as follows. During the four coldest months of winter it eats nothing. It has four feet and lives both on land and in water: it lays its eggs and hatches them on land, and it spends most of the day on dry ground, but the whole night in the river, because the water is actually warmer than the open air under a clear sky and the dew. Of all the living creatures we know of, this one grows from the smallest beginning to the greatest size. The eggs it lays are not much bigger than goose eggs, and the newly hatched young one is in proportion to the egg; but as it grows it reaches as much as twenty-five feet long, and sometimes even more. It has eyes like a pig's and large teeth like tusks, in proportion to the size of its body. Unlike every other animal, it grows no tongue. Nor does it move its lower jaw: it brings the upper jaw down onto the lower, and in this too it is unlike every other animal. It also has strong claws, and a scaly hide on its back that cannot be pierced. It is blind in the water, but in the air its sight is very sharp. Since it lives in the water, the inside of its mouth is always full of leeches. All other birds and animals run from it, but the trochilus is at peace with it, because the crocodile gets some good from her. When the crocodile has come out of the water onto land and opened its mouth (it usually does this facing the west wind), the trochilus goes into its mouth and gulps down the leeches. The crocodile is glad of the help and does the trochilus no harm.

69. To some of the Egyptians crocodiles are sacred; others do not hold them sacred at all but, on the contrary, treat them as enemies. The people around Thebes and around Lake Moeris consider them very sacred indeed. Each of these two peoples keeps one particular crocodile, chosen out of all the rest and trained to be tame. They hang earrings of glass and gold in its ears and put bracelets around its front feet. They give it special food and sacrificial offerings and treat it as well as they possibly can while it lives, and when it dies they embalm it and bury it in a sacred tomb. But the people around the city of Elephantine actually eat crocodiles, since they do not consider them sacred. Their name for them is not "crocodiles" but "champsae." It was the Ionians who called them crocodiles, comparing their shape to the lizards in their own country that live in stone walls, which they call crocodiles.

70. There are many different ways of catching them. I will describe the one that seems to me most worth telling. A man baits a hook with a pig's back and lets it out into the middle of the river, while he himself stands on the bank holding a live young pig, which he beats. The crocodile hears its squeals and heads toward the sound, and when it comes on the pig's back it swallows it. Then the men pull. When it has been dragged out onto land, the first thing the hunter does is plaster its eyes shut with mud. Once he has done that, he can master it very easily; but if he does not, he has a great deal of trouble.

71. The hippopotamus, the "river horse," is sacred in the district of Papremis, but not to the rest of the Egyptians. This is what it looks like. It has four feet and split hooves like an ox. It has a snub nose and a mane like a horse, and it shows tusk-like teeth. It has a horse's tail and a horse's voice, and it is as big as the biggest ox. Its hide is so extremely thick that once it is dried, it is used to make javelin shafts.

72. There are also otters in the river, which they consider sacred. Among fish they consider sacred the one called the lepidotus, and also the eel; these, they say, are sacred to the Nile. Among birds, the fox-goose is sacred.

73. There is another sacred bird too, called the phoenix. I have never seen one myself, except in paintings, because it comes to them very rarely: once every five hundred years, so the people of Heliopolis say. They say it comes regularly when its father dies. If it looks like the paintings, then this is its size and appearance: some of its feathers are the color of gold and others are red, and in outline and size it is very much like an eagle. They say this bird does something that I cannot believe. It sets out from Arabia, they say, carrying its father to the temple of the Sun (Helios), all plastered up in myrrh, and buries him in the temple of the Sun. This is how it carries him. First it shapes an egg out of myrrh, as big as it can carry. Then it tries carrying it. When it has tested it enough, it hollows out the egg and puts its father inside, and it plasters over with more myrrh the part of the egg it hollowed out to put him in. With the father inside, they say, the egg weighs exactly what it weighed before. After plastering it up, the bird carries the whole thing to Egypt, to the temple of the Sun. That, they say, is what this bird does.

74. Around Thebes there are also sacred snakes, which are not at all harmful to people. They are small, and have two horns growing from the top of the head. When they die, they are buried in the temple of Zeus, because they are sacred, they say, to that god.

75. There is also a place in Arabia, lying roughly opposite the city of Buto, where I went to ask about the winged serpents. When I got there I saw bones and spines of serpents in such enormous quantities that it is impossible to give the number. There were heaps of spines, some large, some smaller, and others smaller still, and there were a great many of these heaps. The place where the spines lie scattered on the ground is something like an entrance from a narrow mountain pass into a great plain, and this plain adjoins the plain of Egypt. The story goes that at the beginning of spring winged serpents fly from Arabia toward Egypt, and the birds called ibises meet them at the entrance to this country and do not let the serpents pass, but kill them. It is because of this deed, say the Arabians, that the ibis has come to be greatly honored by the Egyptians; and the Egyptians themselves also agree that this is why they honor these birds.

76. This is what the ibis looks like. It is jet black all over, with legs like a crane's and a strongly hooked beak, and it is about the size of a rail. That is the appearance of the black kind, the ones that fight the serpents. There are two distinct kinds of ibis, however, and the other kind, the ones that crowd most around people's feet, have a bare head and a bare throat all the way down. Their feathers are white except on the head and neck and the tips of the wings and the rump; in all the parts I have mentioned they are jet black. In their legs and the shape of their head they look like the other kind. As for the serpent, it looks like a water snake. It has wings without feathers, very much like a bat's wings. Let that be enough about sacred animals.

77. As for the Egyptians themselves, those who live in the farming part of Egypt cultivate memory more than any other people, and are by far the most learned in history of all the peoples I have come across. This is how they live. For three days in a row every month they purge themselves, seeking health through emetics and enemas, because they think that all the diseases people get come from the food they eat. In fact, the Egyptians are, for other reasons as well, the healthiest of all people after the Libyans. In my opinion this is because of their climate, since their seasons do not change; for it is changes in things generally, and above all changes in the seasons, that are most likely to bring on disease. As for their diet, it is this. They eat bread, making loaves out of spelt, which they call cyllestis. They regularly drink a wine made from barley, because they have no vines in their country. Some of their fish they dry in the sun and eat raw; others they eat cured in brine. Of birds, they eat quails, ducks and small birds raw, after first curing them in salt. Everything else they have in the way of birds or fish, apart from the ones they have set aside as sacred, they eat roasted or boiled.

78. At the dinner parties of the rich, when the meal is over, a man carries around a wooden model of a corpse in a coffin, painted and carved to look as real as possible, about eighteen inches or three feet long. He shows it to each of the guests in turn and says, "Look at this, then drink and enjoy yourself, for when you are dead this is what you will be." That is what they do at their drinking parties.

79. The customs they practice are the ones they inherited from their fathers, and they do not take on any others. Among their other customs worth mentioning, they have one song, the song of Linus, the same Linus who is sung of in Phoenicia, in Cyprus and elsewhere, though he has a different name among each people. This song is exactly the same as the one the Greeks sing when they call on the name of Linus. So of the many things that amaze me about Egypt, this amazes me most of all: where did they get the song of Linus? It is clear, though, that they have been singing it since time out of mind. In the Egyptian language Linus is called Maneros. The Egyptians told me that he was the only son of the first king of Egypt, that he died young, and that the Egyptians honored him with this lament, and that this was their first and only song.

80. In another respect the Egyptians agree with some of the Greeks, namely the Spartans, though not with the rest. When younger men meet their elders, they make way for them and step aside from the path, and when their elders come near, they stand up from their seats. But in the following custom they agree with none of the Greeks at all: instead of greeting each other in the street with words, they bow, lowering a hand down to the knee.

81. They wear linen tunics with fringes around the legs, which they call calasiris, and over these they throw cloaks of white wool. But woolen clothing is never taken into the temples, and no one is buried in it, because religion forbids it. In this they agree with the rites called Orphic and Bacchic, which are really Egyptian, and also with those of the Pythagoreans: anyone who takes part in these mysteries is likewise forbidden by religious law to be buried in woolen clothing. There is a sacred story told about this.

82. Besides these things, the Egyptians have also worked out which god each month and each day belongs to, and what fortune a man born on a given day will meet with, how he will die, and what sort of man he will be. These discoveries were taken up by those Greeks who busied themselves with poetry. The Egyptians have also discovered more omens than all the rest of mankind put together. Whenever an omen occurs, they watch and write down what comes of it, and if anything like it ever happens again, they believe the outcome will be the same.

83. Their prophecy is arranged like this. The art of prophecy does not belong to any human being, but to certain of the gods. In their country there are oracles of Heracles, of Apollo, of Athena, of Artemis, of Ares and of Zeus, and also the one they honor most of all, the oracle of Leto in the city of Buto. But the method of prophecy is not yet the same everywhere among them; it differs from place to place.

84. Medicine among them is divided up like this. Each doctor is a doctor for one disease and no more, and the whole country is full of doctors. Some set up as doctors for the eyes, others for the head, others for the teeth, others for disorders of the stomach, and others for the less obvious illnesses.

85. Their customs of mourning and burial are these. Whenever a household loses a man of any standing, all the women of that house at once smear their heads, or even their faces, with mud. Then they leave the body in the house and go roaming around the city, beating their breasts, with their clothes tied up by a belt and their breasts bare, and with them go all the women who were related to the dead man. On the other side the men beat their breasts, they too with their clothes tied up by a belt. When they have done this, they take the body to be embalmed.

86. There are men who practice this as their regular trade and inherit it as a craft. Whenever a body is brought to them, they show the people who brought it wooden models of corpses, painted to look real. The finest method of embalming, they say, is that of the one whose name I think it irreverent to mention in a matter of this kind. The second method they show is not as good as the first, and cheaper; and the third is the cheapest of all. After explaining all this, they ask the family which method they want used for the body of their loved one. Then the family agree on a price and go away, and the embalmers, left behind in their workshops, embalm the body. The most perfect way is this.

First, with a hooked iron tool, they draw the brain out through the nostrils, taking out part of it this way and the rest by pouring in drugs. Next, with a sharp Ethiopian stone, they make a cut along the side and take out all the contents of the belly. They clean out the cavity and rinse it with palm wine, and then rinse it again with crushed spices. Then they fill the belly with pure crushed myrrh, cassia and other spices, all except frankincense, and sew it up again. After this they cover the body with natron, a natural salt, and keep it for embalming for seventy days; they are not allowed to embalm it for longer than that. When the seventy days are over, they wash the body and wrap the whole of it in bandages cut from fine linen, smearing their undersides with gum, which the Egyptians generally use instead of glue. Then the relatives take the body back from them and have a wooden case made in the shape of a man. When it has been made, they shut the body up inside it and store it in a burial chamber, standing it upright against the wall.

87. That is how they deal with the bodies prepared in the most expensive way. For those who want the middle method and wish to avoid great expense, they prepare the body as follows. They fill their syringes with oil made from cedarwood and pump it straight into the belly of the corpse, without cutting it open or taking out the intestines: they inject the oil through the anus and stop it from running back out. Then they keep the body the set number of days for embalming, and on the last day they let out of the belly the cedar oil they put in earlier. The oil is so powerful that it brings out with it the intestines and inner organs, dissolved; and the natron dissolves the flesh, so that nothing is left of the body but the skin and bones. When they have done this, they hand the body back just as it is, without doing any more work on it.

88. The third kind of embalming, which is used for the bodies of those with less money, is this. They clean out the belly with a purge and then keep the body for embalming for the seventy days, and right after that they give it back to the people who brought it, to take away.

89. When the wives of prominent men die, they are not handed over to be embalmed right away, nor are women who are very beautiful or more highly regarded than others. They are given to the embalmers only on the third or fourth day after death, and not before. They do this so that the embalmers will not have sex with these women. For they say that one embalmer was once caught doing this with the body of a woman who had just died, and a fellow craftsman reported him.

90. Whenever anyone, whether an Egyptian or a foreigner, is found to have been carried off by a crocodile or killed by the river itself, the people of whatever city he washes up near are obliged to embalm him, lay him out as finely as they can, and bury him in a sacred tomb. None of his relatives or friends may touch him. Instead the priests of the Nile handle the body themselves and bury it, as if it were the body of someone more than human.

91. They refuse to follow Greek customs at all, and, to put it generally, they follow the customs of no other people whatever. Most of the Egyptians keep to this rule. But there is a large city called Chemmis in the Theban district, near Neapolis, and in this city there is a square temple of Perseus, the son of Danae, with date palms growing all around it. The gateway of the temple is built of stone and is very large, and at its entrance stand two great stone statues. Inside this enclosure is a shrine, and in it stands a statue of Perseus. The people of Chemmis say that Perseus often appears in their country, and often inside the temple, and that sometimes one of his sandals is found, which he has worn, three feet long; and whenever it turns up, the whole of Egypt prospers. That is what they say. And they honor Perseus in the Greek fashion, like this: they hold athletic games that include every kind of contest, and they offer cattle, cloaks and hides as prizes. I asked them why Perseus appeared to them alone, and why they were different from all the other Egyptians in holding athletic games. They said that Perseus was by descent a man of their city. Danaus and Lynceus, they said, were men of Chemmis who had sailed to Greece, and they traced the line of descent from them down to Perseus. They told me that he came to Egypt for the reason the Greeks also give, to bring the Gorgon's head from Libya, and that on that journey he visited them too and recognized all his relatives. They said he already knew the name of Chemmis well before he came to Egypt, because he had heard it from his mother, and that they hold the athletic games for him because he ordered it himself.

92. All these are the customs of the Egyptians who live above the marshes. Those who are settled in the marshland have mostly the same customs as the other Egyptians, both in other matters and in this one: each man lives with one wife only, as the Greeks do. But to make their food go further they have come up with these additional things. When the river is full and the plains are flooded, a great many lilies grow in the water, which the Egyptians call lotus. They cut these with a sickle and dry them in the sun. Then they pound up the part in the middle of the lotus that looks like a poppy head and make loaves out of it, baked over a fire. The root of this lotus is also good to eat and tastes rather sweet; it is round and about the size of an apple. There are other lilies too, with flowers like roses, which also grow in the river. Their fruit grows in a separate pod on a stalk that springs from the root beside the plant itself, and it looks very much like a wasps' nest. Inside it grow a great many edible seeds about the size of an olive pit, and these are eaten either fresh or dried. Besides this, they pull up from the marshes the papyrus that grows there every year. They cut off the upper parts and put them to other uses, but the lower part that is left, about eighteen inches long, they eat or sell. Those who want their papyrus at its very best bake it in a red-hot oven and then eat it. Some of these people live on fish alone. They catch them, take out the guts and dry them in the sun, and then, when they are dry, they eat them.

93. Fish that swim in schools are not much bred in the rivers; they are bred in the lakes, and this is what they do. When the urge to spawn comes over them, they swim out in schools toward the sea. The males lead the way, shedding their milt as they go, and the females follow behind and swallow it up, and from it they become pregnant. When they are full of young in the sea, they swim back up again, each school to its own home waters. But now the males no longer lead the way as before: the lead passes to the females. Going ahead in schools, they do just what the males did: they shed their eggs, a few grains at a time, and the males follow behind and swallow them up. Now these grains are fish, and from the grains that survive and are not swallowed, the fish grow that are later raised. The fish caught as they swim out to sea are found to be rubbed on the left side of the head, while the ones caught as they swim back up are rubbed on the right. This happens to them because, as they swim down to the sea, they keep close to the land on the left side of the river, and as they swim back up they keep to the same bank, coming as close to it and touching it as much as they can, no doubt for fear of being swept off course by the current. When the Nile begins to rise, the hollows in the land and the low places beside the river are the first to fill, as the water seeps through from the river. As soon as they are full of water, they are all at once full of little fish. I think I can see where these most likely come from. In the year before, when the Nile goes down, the fish lay their eggs in the mud and then leave with the last of the retreating water. When the season comes around again and the water once more comes over the land, the fish I am talking about are hatched at once from these eggs.

94. So much for the fish. For anointing themselves, the Egyptians who live in the marshes use oil from the castor-oil plant, which the Egyptians call kiki. This is how they do it. They plant it along the banks of the rivers and ponds. In Greece this plant grows wild by itself, but in Egypt it is planted, and it bears a great many berries, though they smell bad. When they have gathered them, some people chop them up and press the oil out of them; others first roast them and then boil them down, and collect what runs off. The oil is thick and greasy, and no worse than olive oil for lamps, but it gives off an unpleasant smell.

95. Against the gnats, which are very plentiful, they have come up with these defenses. Those who live above the marshland are helped by towers, which they climb up to when they go to bed, because the wind keeps the gnats from flying up high. But those who live in the marshland have worked out another method instead of towers. Every man of them owns a casting net, which he uses during the day to catch fish. At night he puts it to this use: he puts the net all around the bed he sleeps in, then crawls in underneath it and goes to sleep. If he sleeps wrapped up in a cloak or a linen sheet, the gnats bite right through it, but through the net they do not even try to bite.

96. The boats they use to carry cargo are made of the thorny acacia, a tree very much like the lotus of Cyrene in shape, which gives off a gum. From this tree they cut planks about three feet long and lay them like bricks, fastening the boat together by running a great many long bolts through the three-foot planks. When they have fastened the boat together this way, they lay crossbeams over the top. They use no ribs for the sides, and on the inside they caulk the seams with papyrus. They make a single steering oar for it, which passes through the bottom of the boat. They have a mast of acacia and sails of papyrus. These boats cannot sail upstream unless a strong wind is blowing, and they are towed from the bank. Downstream, though, they travel like this. They have a raft shaped like a door, made of tamarisk wood and reed mats sewn together, and also a stone with a hole bored through it, weighing about two talents, or about 115 pounds. The boatman lets the raft float ahead of the boat, tied on with a rope, and lets the stone drag behind on another rope. As the force of the current presses on the raft, it moves along quickly and pulls the baris (that is what these boats are called), while the stone, dragging behind and sunk deep in the water, keeps the boat on a straight course. They have great numbers of these boats, and some of them carry many thousands of talents' weight.

97. When the Nile floods the land, only the cities can be seen standing above the water, looking more like the islands in the Aegean Sea than anything else. All the rest of Egypt turns into a sea, and only the cities rise above it. So whenever this happens, people travel by boat no longer along the channels of the river but straight across the middle of the plain. For example, anyone sailing up from Naucratis to Memphis then passes right by the pyramids, though that is not the usual route even here: the usual route goes by the point of the Delta and the city of Cercasorus. And if you sail across the plain from the sea and from Canopus to Naucratis, you will pass by Anthylla and the city named after Archander.

98. Of these, Anthylla is a well-known city, and it is set aside especially for the wife of whoever rules Egypt, to keep her in shoes. This has been the arrangement ever since Egypt came under the Persians. The other city, it seems to me, takes its name from Archander, the son-in-law of Danaus and the son of Phthius, the son of Achaeus; for it is called the City of Archander. There may of course have been another Archander, but in any case the name is not Egyptian.
