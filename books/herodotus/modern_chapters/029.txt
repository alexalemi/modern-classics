Chapter 29: Athens Frees Itself: the End of the Tyrants and the Reforms of Cleisthenes

55. So Aristagoras, driven out of Sparta, went on to Athens. Athens had been set free from its tyrants, and I will tell how. Hipparchus, the son of Pisistratus and brother of the tyrant Hippias, had seen a vision in a dream that plainly foretold his fate, and after it he was killed by Aristogeiton and Harmodius, who were Gephyraeans by descent. But for four years after this the Athenians went on living under tyranny just as before, and in fact under a harsher one.

56. This was the dream Hipparchus had. On the night before the Panathenaea, the great festival of Athena, he dreamed that a tall, handsome man came and stood over him and spoke these riddling verses:

	"Endure, lion, with an enduring heart, what cannot be endured:
	no man who does wrong will escape the punishment appointed for him."

As soon as it was day he told these verses openly to the interpreters of dreams. But afterward he put the vision out of his mind and took his part in the procession, and it was during that procession that he was killed.

57. The Gephyraeans, the family the murderers of Hipparchus belonged to, say that they came originally from Eretria. But as I find when I trace the matter back, they were Phoenicians, part of the company who came with Cadmus to the land now called Boeotia, and they lived in the district of Tanagra, which had been allotted to them there. The Cadmeans were driven out first, by the Argives; then these Gephyraeans in their turn were driven out by the Boeotians, and they turned toward Athens. The Athenians took them in as citizens on certain fixed terms, laying down rules that shut them out of a number of things not worth mentioning here.

58. These Phoenicians who came with Cadmus, the Gephyraeans among them, settled in this land of Boeotia and brought many skills to the Greeks. Above all they brought writing, which, as it seems to me, the Greeks did not have before this. At first they used the same letters all Phoenicians use, but as time went on they changed the shape of the letters along with their language. In most of the places around them at that time the Greeks who lived nearest were Ionians. The Ionians learned the letters from the Phoenicians, changed their shape a little, and used them, and when they did they called them "phoenicians," which was only fair, since the Phoenicians had brought them into Greece. The Ionians have also called papyrus "skins" from ancient times, because once, when papyrus was scarce, they wrote on the skins of goats and sheep. Even in my own time many non-Greeks still write on such skins.

59. I have seen Cadmean letters myself, in the temple of Ismenian Apollo at Thebes in Boeotia, carved on some tripods, and for the most part they look like the Ionian letters. One of these tripods carries the inscription:

	"Amphitryon dedicated me, home from the land of the Teleboans."

This inscription would date from about the time of Laius son of Labdacus, son of Polydorus, son of Cadmus.

60. A second tripod says, in hexameter verse:

	"Scaeus dedicated me to you, Apollo who shoots from afar,
	when he won the boxing match, a beautiful gift in your honor."

This Scaeus would be the son of Hippocoon, if it really was he who dedicated it and not another man with the same name as the son of Hippocoon, and he would have lived about the time of Oedipus son of Laius.

61. The third tripod, also in hexameter verse, says:

	"Laodamas, while he was king, dedicated me to you, Apollo of the true aim,
	from his own wealth, a beautiful gift in your honor."

It was in the reign of this very Laodamas son of Eteocles that the Cadmeans were driven out by the Argives and went off to the Enchelians. The Gephyraeans were left behind then, and later the Boeotians forced them to withdraw to Athens. They have temples of their own established in Athens, in which the other Athenians have no share. These are different from the rest, and the chief of them is a temple of Demeter Achaea, with her secret rites, the Mysteries.

62. I have now told about the dream Hipparchus saw, and where the Gephyraeans came from, the family his murderers belonged to. Now I must go back and continue the story I set out to tell at the start: how the Athenians were freed from their tyrants.

Hippias was tyrant, and he was treating the Athenians harshly because of Hipparchus' death. The Alcmaeonids, an Athenian family who had been exiled by the sons of Pisistratus, had joined the other Athenian exiles in an attempt to come home by force and set Athens free. They fortified Leipsydrium, above Paeonia, but the attempt failed and they suffered a heavy defeat. After that the Alcmaeonids, still trying every means against the sons of Pisistratus, took the contract to build the temple at Delphi, the one that stands there now, which did not yet exist then. They were wealthy and had been men of standing from ancient times, and they built the temple more beautifully than the plan required. In particular, though the contract was for a temple of ordinary limestone, they built its front of Parian marble.

63. Then, as the Athenians say, these men stayed at Delphi and bribed the Pythia to tell any Spartans who came to consult the oracle, whether on private business or public, that they should set Athens free. Since the Spartans got the same answer every time, they sent Anchimolius son of Aster, a man of standing among their citizens, with an army to drive the sons of Pisistratus out of Athens. They did this even though the sons of Pisistratus were bound to them by close ties of guest-friendship, because they held that the god's business must come before men's. They sent this force by sea, in ships.

Anchimolius put in at Phalerum, the harbor of Athens, and landed his army. But the sons of Pisistratus had learned of it in advance and called for help from Thessaly, since they had an alliance with the Thessalians. At their request the Thessalians, by public decree, sent a thousand cavalry and their king Cineas, a man of Conium. With these allies in hand, the sons of Pisistratus made a plan. They cut down the trees on the plain of Phalerum to make the ground fit for horses, and then they sent the cavalry against the enemy's camp. The cavalry fell on it and killed many of the Spartans, Anchimolius himself among them, and they shut the survivors up in their ships. That was how the first expedition from Sparta ended. Anchimolius' tomb is at Alopece in Attica, near the temple of Heracles at Cynosarges.

64. After this the Spartans fitted out a larger expedition and sent it against Athens. They made their king Cleomenes son of Anaxandrides its commander, and this time they sent it by land instead of by sea. When they invaded Attica, the Thessalian cavalry met them first, and before long the Thessalians were routed and lost more than forty men. The survivors went straight home to Thessaly without more ado. Then Cleomenes, along with those Athenians who wanted to be free, came to the city and began to besiege the tyrants, who had shut themselves up behind the Pelasgian Wall.

65. The Spartans would never have captured the sons of Pisistratus at all. They had no plans for a long blockade, and the defenders were well supplied with food and drink, so after a siege of a few days the Spartans would have gone back home to Sparta. But as it happened, something occurred just then that was bad luck for one side and help for the other: the children of the sons of Pisistratus were captured as they were being smuggled out of the country. When that happened, all their plans were thrown into confusion. To get their children back they surrendered on the Athenians' terms, which were that they should leave Attica within five days. So they left the country and went to Sigeum on the Scamander, after their family had ruled the Athenians for thirty-six years. They too were originally Pylians, descendants of Neleus, from the same ancestors as the family of Codrus and Melanthus, who had come from abroad earlier and become kings of Athens. That was why Hippocrates had named his son Pisistratus, as a reminder, after Pisistratus the son of Nestor.

So the Athenians were freed from their tyrants. Before I go any further, I will first set out the things worth telling that they did or suffered after they were freed, up to the time when Ionia revolted from Darius and Aristagoras of Miletus came to Athens and asked them for help.

66. Athens had been great even before, but now, rid of its tyrants, it grew greater still. Two men held power there. One was Cleisthenes, a descendant of Alcmaeon, the man who is said to have bribed the Pythia. The other was Isagoras son of Tisander, of a highly respected family; I can't say what his family's origins were, but his relatives sacrifice to Carian Zeus. These two men fought each other for power, and when Cleisthenes was getting the worst of it, he made the common people his partners. After this he divided the Athenians into ten tribes instead of the old four. He did away with the old tribal names, which came from the sons of Ion (Geleon, Aegicores, Argades and Hoples), and found new names for them after other heroes, all of them native Athenians except Ajax. Ajax he added, although he was no Athenian, as a neighbor and an ally.

67. In this, it seems to me, this Cleisthenes was imitating his mother's father, Cleisthenes the tyrant of Sicyon. When that Cleisthenes went to war with Argos, the first thing he did was stop the contests of the rhapsodes, the reciters of Homer, in Sicyon, because Homer's poems are full of praise for the Argives and Argos almost from end to end. The second thing was this. In the marketplace of Sicyon there was (and there still is) a shrine to the hero Adrastus son of Talaus, and Cleisthenes wanted to throw him out of the country, because he was an Argive.

So he went to Delphi and asked the oracle whether he should throw Adrastus out. The Pythia answered that Adrastus was king of the Sicyonians, and Cleisthenes was only fit to stone them. Since the god would not let him do it, he went home and thought about how to make Adrastus leave of his own accord. When he thought he had found a way, he sent to Thebes in Boeotia and said he wanted to bring Melanippus son of Astacus into his city, and the Thebans let him. So Cleisthenes brought Melanippus in, gave him a sacred precinct in the town hall itself, and set him up there in the strongest spot. Cleisthenes brought in Melanippus (I must tell this too) because he had been Adrastus' worst enemy: he had killed both Adrastus' brother Mecisteus and his son-in-law Tydeus. Once he had given Melanippus his precinct, he took the sacrifices and festivals away from Adrastus and gave them to Melanippus.

The Sicyonians had been in the habit of honoring Adrastus very greatly. This land had once belonged to Polybus, and Adrastus was Polybus' grandson, his daughter's son; and when Polybus died without sons, he left his kingdom to Adrastus. So the Sicyonians gave Adrastus other honors, and in particular they honored him for his sufferings with tragic choruses, paying that honor not to Dionysus but to Adrastus. Cleisthenes, however, gave the choruses back to Dionysus, and the rest of the rites he gave to Melanippus.

68. That is what he did to Adrastus. He also changed the names of the Dorian tribes, so that the Sicyonians would not have the same tribes as the Argives. In this he made a great mockery of the Sicyonians, because he took the new names from the words for "pig" and "donkey," changing only the endings, except for his own tribe, which he named after his own rule. So the men of his tribe were called Archelai, the "Rulers of the People," while of the rest, one tribe was called the Hyatae, the "Hog-men," another the Oneatae, the "Donkey-men," and the last the Choereatae, the "Swine-men." The men of Sicyon used these tribal names not only while Cleisthenes ruled but for sixty years after his death. Then, however, they talked it over and changed them to Hylleis, Pamphyli and Dymanatae, and added a fourth tribe, which they called the Aegialeis after Aegialeus son of Adrastus.

69. That is what Cleisthenes of Sicyon had done. And the Athenian Cleisthenes, his daughter's son, who was named after him, despised the Ionians, I suppose, as his grandfather despised the Dorians, and so he imitated his namesake to keep the Athenians from having the same tribes as the Ionians. At the time I am speaking of, he had won over to his side the whole body of the Athenian common people, whom he had looked down on before. So he changed the names of the tribes and made more of them than there had been: he made ten tribal chiefs instead of four, and he divided the demes among the tribes, ten to each. With the common people on his side, he was far stronger than his rivals.

70. Then Isagoras, losing in his turn, came up with a countermove. He called in Cleomenes of Sparta to help him. Cleomenes had been his guest-friend ever since the siege of the sons of Pisistratus, and Cleomenes was also accused of having an affair with Isagoras' wife. First Cleomenes sent a herald to Athens demanding that Cleisthenes be banished, and many other Athenians with him, calling them "the accursed." He sent this message on Isagoras' instructions, because the Alcmaeonids and their party were blamed for the killing this name referred to, while Isagoras and his friends had no part in it.

71. This is how the Athenians "under the curse" got their name. There was a man in Athens named Cylon, a victor at the Olympic games. He grew arrogant and wanted to make himself tyrant. He formed a band of young men his own age and tried to seize the Acropolis, but when he could not take it, he sat down as a suppliant at the statue of the goddess. The presidents of the naucraries, the officials who ran Athens at that time, persuaded them to leave sanctuary on a promise that they would face any punishment short of death. But they were killed, and the Alcmaeonids are blamed for killing them. All this happened before the time of Pisistratus.

72. When Cleomenes sent his demand that Cleisthenes and the accursed be banished, Cleisthenes himself slipped away. But even so, Cleomenes came to Athens afterward with a fairly small force. When he arrived, he banished seven hundred Athenian families as accursed, families whose names Isagoras had given him. Having done this, he next tried to dissolve the Council, and he put the offices of state in the hands of three hundred of Isagoras' supporters. But the Council resisted and refused to obey, and so Cleomenes, Isagoras and his supporters seized the Acropolis. Then the rest of the Athenians, all of one mind, joined together and besieged them for two days. On the third day all of them who were Spartans left the country under a truce.

So the ominous words that had been spoken to Cleomenes came true. When he went up to the Acropolis intending to take it over, he was going into the shrine of the goddess to pray to her. But the priestess rose from her seat before he had got through the door and said: "Spartan stranger, go back. Do not enter the temple. It is not lawful for Dorians to come in here." He said: "Woman, I am not a Dorian. I am an Achaean." So, paying no attention to the omen, he made his attempt, and then he was thrown out again along with the Spartans. The rest of the men the Athenians put in chains, to be executed. Among them was Timesitheus of Delphi, about whom I could tell of very great deeds of strength and courage.

73. So these men were put in chains and executed. After this the Athenians called back Cleisthenes and the seven hundred families Cleomenes had banished. Then they sent envoys to Sardis, wanting to make an alliance with the Persians, because they were sure that Cleomenes and the Spartans were now their deadly enemies. When the envoys arrived at Sardis and were delivering the message they had been given, Artaphernes son of Hystaspes, the governor of Sardis, asked who these people were who wanted to be allies of the Persians, and where on earth they lived. When the envoys had told him, he gave them his answer in short: if the Athenians would give earth and water to Darius, he would make an alliance with them; if not, they could leave. The envoys, acting on their own authority, said they would do it, because they wanted the alliance.

74. When they got home, they were sharply condemned for it. Meanwhile Cleomenes, who felt that the Athenians had insulted him outrageously in word and deed, was raising an army from the whole of the Peloponnese. He did not say what he was raising it for, but he wanted revenge on the Athenian people, and he meant to make Isagoras tyrant, since Isagoras too had come out of the Acropolis with him. So Cleomenes marched into Eleusis with a large army. At the same time the Boeotians, by agreement with him, took Oenoe and Hysiae, the demes on the very edge of Attica, and on the other side the Chalcidians invaded and began plundering various parts of Attica. The Athenians were attacked on more than one side, but they decided they would remember the Boeotians and the Chalcidians later, and they drew up their army to face the Peloponnesians at Eleusis.

75. Then, just as the armies were about to engage, the Corinthians were the first to change their minds. They decided among themselves that they were not doing right, and they left. After them Demaratus son of Ariston did the same. He was the other king of Sparta besides Cleomenes, and he had led the army out from Sparta together with him, and until then he had never been at odds with Cleomenes. Because of this split, a law was made at Sparta that when an army went out, both kings might not go with it, as they had both done up to then. And since one of the kings was now released from service, one of the sons of Tyndareus was to be left at home as well; before this both of them had been called on for help and had gone out with the armies.

76. So at Eleusis the rest of the allies, seeing that the kings of Sparta disagreed and that the Corinthians had left their place in the line, also left and hurried away. This was the fourth time the Dorians had come into Attica: twice they had invaded it to make war, and twice they had come to help the Athenian common people. The first was when they also founded Megara (this expedition may rightly be dated to the time when Codrus was king of Athens); the second and third were when they marched from Sparta to drive out the sons of Pisistratus; and the fourth was this one, when Cleomenes led the Peloponnesians into Eleusis. So this was the fourth time the Dorians invaded Athens.

77. Once this army had broken up so ingloriously, the Athenians wanted revenge, and they marched first against the Chalcidians. The Boeotians came to the Euripus, the strait between the mainland and Euboea, to help the Chalcidians. When the Athenians saw the Boeotian relief force, they decided to attack the Boeotians before the Chalcidians. So they fought the Boeotians and beat them soundly. They killed a great many and took seven hundred prisoners. On that very same day the Athenians crossed over to Euboea and fought the Chalcidians as well. They beat them too, and they left 4,000 settlers, each given an allotment of land, on the land of the "Horse-breeders," as the wealthier Chalcidians were called.

All the Chalcidians they captured they kept in prison along with the Boeotian prisoners, bound in fetters. Later they let them go, setting their ransom at two minas a head. But they hung up the fetters they had been bound with on the Acropolis, and these were still there in my own time, hanging on walls scorched by the Persians' fire, facing the shrine that looks toward the west. They also dedicated a tenth of the ransom as an offering and made a bronze four-horse chariot out of it. It stands on the left as you enter the Propylaea, the great gateway of the Acropolis, and on it is this inscription:

	"When the tribes of Boeotia and Chalcis met them in the work of war,
	the sons of Athens won, and broke them in battle.
	In dark chains of iron they quenched their arrogance,
	and from the ransom they give these horses to Athena, a tenth."

78. So the Athenians grew in power. And it is clear, not from one example only but in every way, that equality is an excellent thing. While the Athenians were ruled by tyrants they were no better at war than any of their neighbors, but once they were rid of the tyrants they became by far the best. This proves that while they were held down they held back on purpose, because they were working for a master, but once they were free, each man was eager to achieve something for himself.

79. That was how the Athenians were doing. After this the Thebans sent to the god at Delphi, wanting to be avenged on the Athenians. The Pythia said that they could not get revenge by their own strength alone. She told them to lay the matter before "the many-voiced" and to ask help from "those nearest" to them. So when the men sent to the oracle returned, the Thebans held a general assembly and the envoys reported the answer. When the Thebans heard that they were to ask help from those nearest to them, they said: "Surely the people who live nearest to us are the men of Tanagra and Coronea and Thespiae. They always fight hard on our side and see the war through with us to the end. Why should we need to ask them? Perhaps that isn't what the oracle means."

80. While they were discussing it this way, at last someone saw the point and said: "I think I understand what the oracle means to tell us. Asopus, they say, had two daughters, Thebe and Aegina. Since these two are sisters, I think the god's answer means that we should ask the men of Aegina to be our helpers." No one could offer a better opinion than this, so they sent at once to the Aeginetans and asked them for help, calling on them in the name of the oracle. When the Thebans made their request, the Aeginetans said they would send the sons of Aeacus, their heroes, to help them.

81. The Thebans tried their luck with the sons of Aeacus as allies and were roughly handled by the Athenians. So they sent again, gave the Aeginetans back the sons of Aeacus, and asked them for men instead. The Aeginetans, riding high on great prosperity and remembering an old grudge against the Athenians, answered the Thebans' request by starting a war on the Athenians without a declaration. While the Athenians were busy with the Boeotians, they sailed against Attica in warships and ravaged Phalerum and many demes along the rest of the coast, and by doing this they made the Athenians very angry indeed.
