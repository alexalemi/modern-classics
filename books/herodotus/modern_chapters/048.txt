Chapter 48: Mardonius Takes Athens Again, and the Armies Gather at Plataea

1. After Alexander came back and reported to Mardonius what the Athenians had said, Mardonius set out from Thessaly and led his army toward Athens with all speed, and in every country he came to he took the people of that land along with him. The leaders of Thessaly, meanwhile, felt no regret at all for what they had already done. On the contrary, they urged the Persian on even more. Thorax of Larissa, who had helped escort Xerxes on his flight, now openly offered Mardonius a way through to invade Greece.

2. When the army on its march reached Boeotia, the Thebans tried to keep Mardonius there. They advised him that there was no better place for him to make his camp than this one. They urged him not to go any farther, but to settle there and try to bring the whole of Greece under his power without a fight. To beat the Greeks by open force while they were united, as they had been of one mind before, would be hard, they said, even for the whole world put together. "But," they went on, "if you do what we advise, you will have all their plans for resistance in your hands with very little trouble. Send money to the men who hold power in their cities. By sending it you will split Greece into two parties, and after that, with the help of your own party, you will easily bring down those who are not on your side."

3. That was their advice, but he did not take it. A great desire had taken hold of him to capture Athens a second time, partly out of stubbornness, and partly because he meant to signal to the king in Sardis, by beacon fires across the islands, that he held Athens. Yet even this time, when he came to Attica, he did not find the Athenians there. He learned that most of them were either on Salamis or on the ships, and he took the city empty. The king's capture of the city had taken place ten months before this later expedition of Mardonius against it.

4. When Mardonius reached Athens, he sent Morychides, a man from the Hellespont, over to Salamis with the same proposals that Alexander the Macedonian had carried to the Athenians. He sent them this second time knowing already that the Athenians were not friendly to him. But he hoped they would give way and drop their stubbornness, now that the land of Attica had been captured and was in his hands.

5. That is why he sent Morychides to Salamis. Morychides came before the Council and reported Mardonius' message. Then one of the councillors, Lycidas, gave his opinion that it would be better to accept the proposal Morychides had brought and to put it before the assembly of the people. He gave this opinion either because he had taken money from Mardonius or because it was what he really thought. But the Athenians, both the members of the Council and those outside it, were furious the moment they heard it. They surrounded Lycidas and stoned him to death, though they sent Morychides the Hellespontine away unharmed. With all the uproar there was on Salamis about Lycidas, the Athenian women heard what was happening. One woman passed the word to the next, and each brought another along with her, and on their own they went to Lycidas' house and stoned his wife and his children to death.

6. This is how the Athenians had come to cross over to Salamis. As long as they expected an army to come from the Peloponnese to help them, they stayed in Attica. But the Peloponnesians were very slow and kept delaying, and the invader was already said to be in Boeotia, so they moved everything out of harm's way and crossed over to Salamis themselves. At the same time they sent envoys to Sparta. The envoys were to reproach the Spartans for letting the invader into Attica instead of going with the Athenians to meet him in Boeotia, and to remind them how much the Persian had promised to give the Athenians if they changed sides. And they were told to warn the Spartans that if they did not help the Athenians, the Athenians would find some protection for themselves.

7. The Spartans, in fact, were holding a festival at this time, the Hyacinthia, their feast in honor of Apollo and his beloved Hyacinthus, and they thought nothing more important than doing what they owed the god. At the same time, the wall they had been building across the Isthmus was just then being finished with its battlements. When the envoys from Athens arrived at Sparta, bringing envoys from Megara and Plataea with them, they went before the ephors and said: "The Athenians have sent us to tell you this. The king of the Medes offers to give us back our land. He also wants to make us his allies on fair and equal terms, without trickery or treachery, and he is willing to give us another country besides our own, whichever we choose. But we respect Zeus, the god of all the Greeks, and we would not stoop to betray Greece. So we did not agree; we refused. We refused even though the other Greeks have treated us unjustly and left us to be destroyed, and even though we knew it would pay us better to make a treaty with the Persian than to go on fighting. Nor will we make a treaty in the future, if we have any say in it. That is how honestly we have done our duty by the Greeks. But you? Back then you were terrified that we would make a treaty with the Persian. Then you learned for certain what our spirit was, that we would never betray Greece, and your wall across the Isthmus is all but finished. So now you think nothing of the Athenians. You agreed with us to go to Boeotia and face the Persian there, and you have deserted us, and you let the invader into Attica besides. For now, then, the Athenians are angry with you, because you did not do what you ought to have done. And now they ask you to send out an army with us as fast as you can, so that we can meet the invader in Attica. Since we have missed our chance in Boeotia, the best place to fight in our own country is the Thriasian plain, the plain around Eleusis."

8. When the ephors heard this, they put off their answer to the next day, and then on the next day to the day after that. They kept this up for as many as ten days, putting the matter off from one day to the next, while all the Peloponnesians were working hard on the wall across the Isthmus and had it nearly finished. I can't say why, when Alexander the Macedonian came to Athens, they were so very anxious that the Athenians might go over to the Persians, and now they did not care at all. The only reason I can see is that their wall across the Isthmus was now built and they thought they had no more need of the Athenians, whereas when Alexander came to Attica the wall was not yet finished, and they were working on it in great fear of the Persians.

9. In the end, though, the answer was given and the Spartans marched out, and it happened like this. On the day before the envoys' last hearing was due, Chileus, a man of Tegea who had more influence at Sparta than any other foreigner, heard from the ephors everything the Athenians were saying. And he, it seems, said this to them: "This is how things stand, ephors. If the Athenians are not our friends but allies of the invader, then however strong a wall has been built across the Isthmus, a wide door has been opened for the Persian into the Peloponnese. So listen to them, before the Athenians decide on something else that will bring Greece down."

10. That was his advice, and they took his words to heart at once. Without saying anything to the envoys who had come from the cities, they sent out five thousand Spartans while it was still night, with no fewer than seven helots assigned to attend each man, and they appointed Pausanias son of Cleombrotus to lead them out. The command properly belonged to Pleistarchus son of Leonidas, but he was still a boy, and Pausanias was his guardian and his cousin. For Cleombrotus, Pausanias' father and the son of Anaxandrides, was no longer alive: after leading home from the Isthmus the army that had built the wall, he died not long afterward. The reason Cleombrotus led the army home from the Isthmus was this: while he was offering sacrifice for a battle against the Persian, the sun went dark in the sky. Pausanias chose as his fellow commander Euryanax son of Dorieus, a man of the same royal house.

11. So Pausanias and his army had left Sparta. When day came, the envoys, who knew nothing about the march, went before the ephors meaning to go home themselves, each to his own city. When they came in they said: "You Spartans are staying here celebrating this Hyacinthia of yours and enjoying yourselves, and you have left your allies to be destroyed. The Athenians, wronged by you and with no allies, will make peace with the Persians on whatever terms they can get. And once we have made peace, we will plainly become the king's allies, and so we will march with him against any land the Persians lead us to. Then you will find out what comes of all this for you." When the envoys said this, the ephors told them, and swore an oath to it, that by now they supposed the men were at Orestheum on their way against the strangers. ("Strangers" was what they used to call the non-Greeks.) The envoys did not understand and asked what this meant, and by asking they learned the whole truth. They were astonished, and they set off after the army as fast as they could. And five thousand picked hoplites of the Lacedaemonian "dwellers around," who were not Spartan citizens, did the same and went with them.

12. So they were hurrying toward the Isthmus. As soon as the Argives heard that Pausanias and his army had marched out of Sparta, they sent a herald to Attica, the best long-distance runner they could find. They did this because they had earlier promised Mardonius, of their own accord, that they would stop the Spartans from marching out. When the herald reached Athens, he said: "Mardonius, the Argives have sent me to tell you that the young men have marched out of Lacedaemon, and the Argives are not able to stop them. So, with this in mind, may you have the good fortune to plan well."

13. After saying this, the herald left and went back. Once Mardonius had heard the message, he had no wish at all to stay in Attica any longer. Before he heard it, he had been waiting there because he wanted to find out what the Athenians were going to do. He had not been damaging or ravaging the land of Attica, because he kept hoping they would make a treaty with him. But since he could not persuade them, and now knew the whole situation, he began to withdraw from the country before Pausanias' force reached the Isthmus. First, though, he set fire to Athens, and he knocked down and destroyed whatever was still standing of the walls, the houses and the temples. He marched away for these reasons. First, Attica was not country where cavalry could operate freely. And second, if he were beaten in a battle in Attica, the only way out was through a narrow pass, where a few men could stop his army. So he meant to fall back to Thebes and fight near a friendly city, in country where cavalry could move freely.

14. So Mardonius was withdrawing, and he was already on the road when a message reached him that another force, an advance guard of a thousand Lacedaemonians, had come to Megara. On hearing this he thought it over, wanting, if he could, to capture these men first. So he turned back and led his army toward Megara, and his cavalry, riding ahead of the rest, overran the land of Megara. That was the farthest point in Europe toward the setting sun that this Persian army ever reached.

15. After this a message reached Mardonius that the Greeks were gathered at the Isthmus. So he marched back by way of Decelea. The chiefs of Boeotia had sent for the Asopians who lived near his line of march, and these men guided him along the road to Sphendaleis and from there to Tanagra. He camped for the night at Tanagra, and the next day he turned toward Scolus, and so he was in Theban territory. There he began cutting down the trees on the Thebans' lands, even though they were on the Persian side. He did not do it out of any hostility toward them. He was driven by urgent need: he wanted to build a defense for his camp, and he was also making it a place of refuge in case the battle, when it came, did not go the way he wished. His army's camp stretched from Erythrae past Hysiae and reached down to the river Asopus. But he did not make the wall run as far as that; each side of it measured something like 6,000 feet, a little over a mile.

16. While the invaders were busy with this work, Attaginus son of Phyrnon, a Theban, made lavish preparations and invited Mardonius himself and fifty of the most important Persians to a banquet. They accepted, and the dinner was held at Thebes. What follows I heard from Thersander, a man of Orchomenus who was very highly respected there. Thersander said that he too was invited to this dinner by Attaginus, and that fifty Thebans were invited as well. Their host did not seat them nation by nation, each on separate couches, but put a Persian and a Theban on every couch. When dinner was over and they were drinking toasts to one another, the Persian who shared his couch asked him in Greek where he was from, and he answered that he was from Orchomenus. The Persian said: "Since you have now shared my table and my drink offering, I want to leave you something to remember my opinion by, so that you yourself may know ahead of time and be able to make whatever plans are best for you. Do you see these Persians feasting here, and the army we left camped by the river? A little while from now, of all these you will see only a very few still alive." As the Persian said this, he wept freely, so Thersander reported. Thersander was amazed at his words and said to him: "Surely, then, you ought to tell this to Mardonius, and to the Persians who rank next after him." To this the Persian said: "My friend, what is fated to come from heaven no man can turn aside. No one is willing to listen to advice, even when what he hears is sensible. Many of us Persians know well what I am telling you, and yet we go along, bound by necessity. The bitterest pain a man can have is this: to understand much and have power over nothing." This I heard from Thersander of Orchomenus, and this as well: that he told it to various people right away, before the battle at Plataea took place.

17. While Mardonius was camped in Boeotia, all the other Greeks living in those parts who had sided with the Persians were supplying troops, and they had joined in the invasion of Attica. Only the Phocians had not joined in the invasion. The Phocians too, I should explain, were now actively on the Persian side, though not willingly, but under compulsion. Not many days after Mardonius arrived at Thebes, however, a thousand of their hoplites came, led by Harmocydes, the most respected man among their citizens. When these too had come to Thebes, Mardonius sent horsemen and ordered the Phocians to take up a position by themselves on the plain. As soon as they had done so, the whole cavalry appeared. At that a rumor ran through the army of Greeks who were with the Persians that the cavalry was going to shoot the Phocians down with javelins, and the same report spread among the Phocians themselves. Then their commander Harmocydes encouraged them with these words: "Phocians, it is plain that these men mean to hand us over to a death we can clearly see coming, because the Thessalians have slandered us, I suspect. So now every one of you must prove himself a brave man. It is better to end our lives doing brave deeds and defending ourselves than to be destroyed by a shameful death, offering ourselves up to be slaughtered. Let each of them learn that they are barbarians, and that we, the men they have plotted to murder, are Greeks."

18. While he was encouraging them this way, the horsemen surrounded them and rode at them as if to destroy them. They were already aiming their missiles as if about to throw them, and some perhaps actually did throw. Meanwhile the Phocians stood facing them, gathered together and with their ranks closed up as tight as they could on every side. Then the horsemen turned and rode back. I can't say for certain which it was. Perhaps they came to destroy the Phocians at the Thessalians' request, and then, when they saw them turning to defend themselves, were afraid of taking losses themselves, and so rode away, because those were Mardonius' orders. Or perhaps he wanted to test them and see whether they had any fighting spirit in them. When the horsemen had ridden back, Mardonius sent a herald with this message: "Take heart, Phocians. You have shown yourselves to be brave men, and not what I was told you were. Now carry on with this war eagerly, and you will not outdo either me or the king in good turns." So much for the Phocians.

19. When the Lacedaemonians reached the Isthmus, they camped there. Hearing of this, the rest of the Peloponnesians, those who favored the better cause, and some also because they saw the Spartans marching out, did not think it right to be left behind by the Lacedaemonians in taking the field. So when the sacrifices turned out favorable, they marched out from the Isthmus all together and came to Eleusis. There they sacrificed again, and when the signs were favorable they marched on, and the Athenians with them, who had crossed over from Salamis and joined them at Eleusis. When they reached Erythrae in Boeotia, they learned that the invaders were camped on the Asopus. Seeing this, they drew themselves up facing them on the lower slopes of Cithaeron.

20. Then, since the Greeks would not come down into the plain, Mardonius sent all his cavalry against them. Their commander was Masistius (the Greeks call him Macistius), a man of reputation among the Persians, who rode a Nesaean horse with a golden bridle and fine trappings everywhere else too. When the horsemen rode up to the Greeks, they attacked them squadron by squadron, and with their attacks they did them a great deal of damage, and they jeered at them besides, calling them women.

21. It happened that the Megarians had been posted in the most exposed part of the whole position, where the cavalry could most easily get at them. So as the cavalry kept attacking, the Megarians, hard pressed, sent a herald to the Greek commanders. The herald came and said: "The Megarians say this: 'Allies, we cannot hold off the Persian cavalry by ourselves in the position where we were posted at the start. Even up to now we have held out against them only by endurance and courage, hard pressed as we are. And now, unless you send some others to take over our position from us, understand that we are going to leave it.'" That was the herald's report. At this Pausanias tested the Greeks, to see whether any others would volunteer to go to that place and take over the post from the Megarians. The rest did not want to go, but the Athenians took the job on: of the Athenians, the three hundred picked men whose captain was Olympidorus son of Lampon.

22. These were the men who took on the task and were posted at Erythrae, out in front of all the other Greeks who were there, and they chose to take the archers along with them. They fought for some time, and in the end the fighting finished like this. While the cavalry were attacking squadron by squadron, Masistius' horse, riding out ahead of the rest, was hit in the side by an arrow. In pain, it reared straight up and threw Masistius off. When he was down, the Athenians rushed at him at once. They took his horse, and they killed him as he fought back, though at first they could not manage it, because of the armor he wore. He had on a breastplate of golden scales underneath, and over the breastplate he had put on a crimson tunic. So their blows on the breastplate did nothing, until somebody saw what the trouble was and stabbed him in the eye. Then at last he fell and died. Somehow the other horsemen had not seen this happen. They had not seen him fall from his horse or seen him killed, because it happened while they were pulling back and wheeling round, and they did not notice what was going on. But when they halted their horses, they missed him at once, because there was no one giving them orders. When they realized what had happened, they called to one another and all rode in together, to try to recover the body.

23. When the Athenians saw that the cavalry were no longer coming at them squadron by squadron but all together, they shouted to the rest of the army to help them. While the whole body of the infantry was coming to their help, a fierce fight broke out over the body. As long as the three hundred were on their own, they had much the worse of it and were about to abandon the body. But when the main army came to help them, the horsemen could hold out no longer, and they did not manage to recover the body; and besides him they lost others of their own men as well. Then they pulled back about a quarter of a mile and talked over what to do. Since they had no commander, they decided to ride back to Mardonius.

24. When the cavalry reached the camp, Mardonius and the whole army mourned Masistius deeply. They cut off their own hair and the manes of their horses and pack animals, and they wailed without restraint, so that the sound of it filled all Boeotia, because a man had died who, after Mardonius, counted for most with the Persians and with the king.

25. So the invaders were honoring the dead Masistius in their own way. The Greeks, though, having stood up to the cavalry's attack and, after standing up to it, driven them back, took much greater heart. First they put the body in a cart and carried it along their lines. The body was well worth seeing for its size and its beauty, and that is why the men kept leaving their places in the ranks and coming one after another to look at Masistius. After this they decided to move down farther toward Plataea. The country around Plataea looked much better for a camp than the country around Erythrae, for other reasons and because it had better water. So they decided they must go to this country and to the spring of Gargaphia, which is there, and make camp in their separate positions. They took up their arms and went along the lower slopes of Cithaeron, past Hysiae, into the territory of Plataea. When they got there, they took up their positions nation by nation near the spring of Gargaphia and the sacred precinct of the hero Androcrates, on low hills and level ground.

26. Then, while the positions were being assigned, a long argument broke out between the Tegeans and the Athenians. Each claimed the right to hold the other wing of the army themselves, and each pointed to deeds both recent and ancient. The Tegeans said: "All the allies have always judged us worthy of this post, in every joint campaign the Peloponnesians have made before now, whether long ago or lately. It has been so ever since the sons of Heracles tried to return to the Peloponnese, which they claimed as their father's inheritance, after the death of Eurystheus, the king who had driven them out. We won this honor at that time through the following event. Together with the Achaeans and the Ionians, who were living in the Peloponnese then, we had gone out to the Isthmus to defend it, and we were camped facing the men who wanted to return. Then, it is said, Hyllus, the son of Heracles who led them, made a speech. He said it was not right for the two armies to risk everything by fighting a battle against each other, and he urged that the Peloponnesians choose whichever man of their army they judged the best, to fight him in single combat on terms agreed between them. The Peloponnesians decided that this should be done, and they swore an oath with one another on this condition: if Hyllus defeated the Peloponnesians' champion, the sons of Heracles would return to their father's inheritance; but if he was defeated, the sons of Heracles would go away and lead off their army, and not try to return to the Peloponnese for a hundred years. Out of all the allies, the man chosen, at his own offer, was Echemus son of Aeropus son of Phegeus, who was our commander and our king. He fought the single combat and killed Hyllus. Because of this deed we won from the Peloponnesians of that time many other great privileges, which we still have, and this one too: that we always lead the other wing of the army whenever a joint campaign is made. We do not oppose you, Spartans. We give you your free choice, and you may command whichever wing you want. But we say that the other wing is ours to lead, as it was in the past. And apart from the deed we have just told, we are more worthy of this post than the Athenians. We have fought many glorious contests against you, Spartans, and many against others as well. So it is right that we should have the other wing rather than the Athenians, because they have not done deeds like ours, either recent or ancient."

27. That is what they said, and the Athenians answered: "We know this gathering was assembled to fight the invader, not to make speeches. But since the man from Tegea has set us the task of speaking of things old and new, of the brave deeds each of our two peoples has done in all time, we must show you how it comes about that the first place belongs to us, who have always been brave men, as our inheritance, rather than to the Arcadians. First, the sons of Heracles, whose leader they say they killed at the Isthmus. Back then, they were fleeing slavery under the Mycenaeans and were driven away by every Greek people they came to, and we alone took them in. Together with them we broke the arrogance of Eurystheus, defeating in battle the people who lived in the Peloponnese at that time. Then again, when the Argives who marched against Thebes with Polynices had been killed and were lying unburied, we say that we led an army against the Cadmeans of Thebes, recovered the bodies and buried them in our own land, at Eleusis. We have another glorious deed to our name as well, against the Amazons, who once came from the river Thermodon and invaded Attica. And in the hard struggle at Troy we were second to none. But there is no use in bringing these things up. Men who were brave in those days might have become worthless now, and men who were worthless then might be better now. So enough about ancient deeds. Even if we had shown no other deed (and we have shown many, and glorious ones, as many as any Greek people), still, for what we did at Marathon alone we deserve this privilege and others besides. We alone of all the Greeks fought the Persian single-handed, and having taken on so great a task, we overcame him and defeated forty-six nations. Do we not deserve this post for that deed alone? But at a time like this it is not right to quarrel over positions. We are ready to do as you say, Spartans, and to stand wherever you think best, and opposite whomever you think best. Wherever we are posted, we will try to be brave men. Give us your orders, and we will obey." That was their answer. And the whole Lacedaemonian army shouted out that the Athenians were more worthy than the Arcadians to hold the wing. So the Athenians got the wing, and they won out over the Tegeans.

28. After this the Greeks were drawn up as follows, both those who kept arriving later and those who had come at the start. The right wing was held by 10,000 Lacedaemonians. Of these, the 5,000 who were Spartans were attended by 35,000 helots serving as light-armed troops, seven assigned to each man. Next to themselves the Spartans chose to place the Tegeans, both to honor them and because of their courage; there were 1,500 of these hoplites. After them stood 5,000 Corinthians, who had got permission from Pausanias to have the 300 men present from Potidaea in Pallene stand beside them. Next to these stood 600 Arcadians from Orchomenus, and next to them 3,000 Sicyonians. After these came 800 Epidaurians, and beside them 1,000 Troezenians. Next to the Troezenians were 200 Lepreans, next to these 400 men of Mycenae and Tiryns, and then 1,000 Phliasians. Beside these stood 300 Hermionians, and next to the Hermionians 600 Eretrians and Styrians. Next to these were 400 Chalcidians, and next to them 500 men of Ambracia. After these stood 800 Leucadians and Anactorians, and next to them 200 from Pale in Cephallenia. After these were 500 Aeginetans, beside them 3,000 Megarians, and next to these 600 Plataeans. Last, or first if you like, came the Athenians, holding the left wing, 8,000 strong, and their commander was Aristides son of Lysimachus.

29. All of these, except the men assigned to attend the Spartans, seven to each man, were hoplites, 38,700 in all. That was the whole number of hoplites gathered against the invader. The light-armed troops numbered as follows: in the Spartan division, 35,000 men, at the rate of seven for each Spartan, and every one of them was equipped for fighting; and the light-armed troops of the other Lacedaemonians and of the other Greeks, about one for each man, came to 34,500.

30. So the whole number of light-armed fighting men was 69,500, and the whole Greek force gathered at Plataea, counting both the hoplites and the light-armed fighting men, was 110,000 less 1,800. With the Thespians who were there, the full 110,000 was made up. For the Thespians who had survived were also present in the army, about 1,800 of them, and these too had no heavy armor. These, then, were drawn up in order and camped on the river Asopus.

31. Meanwhile, when the invaders with Mardonius had mourned Masistius enough, they learned that the Greeks were at Plataea, and they too came to the stretch of the Asopus that flows past there. When they arrived, Mardonius drew them up against the enemy as follows. Against the Lacedaemonians he posted the Persians. Since the Persians far outnumbered them, they were drawn up in deeper ranks, and even so their line stretched out in front of the Tegeans as well. He arranged them like this: he picked out all the strongest part of that force and posted it opposite the Lacedaemonians, and he set the weaker part beside it, opposite the Tegeans. He did this on the information and advice of the Thebans. Next to the Persians he posted the Medes, and these faced the Corinthians, Potidaeans, Orchomenians and Sicyonians. Next to the Medes he posted the Bactrians, and these faced the Epidaurians, Troezenians, Lepreans, Tirynthians, Mycenaeans and Phliasians. After the Bactrians he placed the Indians, and these faced the Hermionians, Eretrians, Styrians and Chalcidians. Next to the Indians he posted the Sacae, who faced the men of Ambracia, the Anactorians, Leucadians, Palians and Aeginetans. Next to the Sacae, opposite the Athenians, Plataeans and Megarians, he posted the Boeotians, Locrians, Malians, Thessalians, and the thousand Phocians. For not all the Phocians had gone over to the Persians. Some of them were even supporting the Greek cause, shut up on Parnassus, and from there they made raids and plundered Mardonius' army and the Greeks who were with him. He also posted the Macedonians and the peoples living around the borders of Thessaly opposite the Athenians.

32. These I have named were the greatest of the nations Mardonius drew up, by which I mean the most famous and important ones. But there were also men of several other nations mixed in among them in his army: Phrygians, Thracians, Mysians, Paeonians and the rest, and among them also some Ethiopians, and the Egyptians called Hermotybians and Calasirians, carrying knives, who are the only warriors among the Egyptians. These he had taken off the ships while he was still at Phalerum, where they had been serving as fighting men on board; for the Egyptians had not been assigned to the land army that came to Athens with Xerxes. Of the non-Greeks, then, there were 300,000, as I said before. But no one knows how many Greek allies Mardonius had, because they were never counted. As a guess, I judge that about 50,000 of them were gathered there. All these drawn up side by side were infantry; the cavalry was posted apart, in a separate body.
