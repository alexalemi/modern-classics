Chapter 38: The Canal at Athos, Pythius the Lydian, and the Bridges on the Hellespont

20. For four full years after the conquest of Egypt, Xerxes was getting the army ready, along with everything the army would need. Then, in the course of the fifth year, he set out on campaign with an enormous host. Of all the armies we know of, this one was by far the greatest. Next to it, the army Darius led against the Scythians looks like nothing at all. So does the Scythian host, back when the Scythians chased the Cimmerians, invaded the land of the Medes, and conquered and occupied nearly all of upper Asia, the invasion Darius later tried to punish them for. So does the army the sons of Atreus, Agamemnon and Menelaus, led to Troy, to judge by what is reported of that expedition. And so does the army of the Mysians and Teucrians before the Trojan War, who crossed over into Europe by the Bosporus, conquered all the Thracians, and came down as far as the Ionian Sea and as far south as the river Peneus.

21. All these expeditions put together, with any others there may have been added to them, do not equal this one alone. For what nation of Asia did Xerxes not lead out against Greece? And what water was not drunk dry by his host, except for the great rivers? Some peoples supplied ships, and others were assigned to serve in the land army. Some were ordered to provide cavalry, and others ships to carry horses, while also serving in the expedition themselves. Others were told to provide warships for the bridges, and others again ships loaded with provisions.

22. First of all, since the earlier fleet had come to disaster sailing around Athos, preparations had been going on at Athos for about three years. Triremes lay at anchor at Elaeus in the Chersonese, and with this as their base, men of every nation in the army were made to dig, driven to it with whips. They went to the work in regular shifts, one group after another. The people who lived around Athos dug too. Two Persians were put in charge of the work: Bubares son of Megabazus and Artachaees son of Artaeus.

Athos is a great and famous mountain that runs down into the sea, and people live on it. Where the mountain ends on the landward side, the place is like a peninsula, with an isthmus about a mile and a third across. This isthmus is flat ground or low hills, running from the sea off Acanthus to the sea off Torone. On this isthmus, where Athos ends, stands a Greek city called Sane. Beyond Sane, out on the peninsula of Athos itself, are other cities, and at this time the Persian king had decided to make them island cities instead of mainland ones. They are Dion, Olophyxus, Acrothoum, Thyssus and Cleonae.

23. Those are the cities on Athos. And this is how they dug. The ground was divided among the various peoples of the army for the work. At the city of Sane they drew a straight line across the isthmus. When the channel got deep, the men standing at the bottom dug, and handed the earth as it came out to other men standing above them, as if on steps. These passed it up to others in turn, until it reached the men at the very top, who carried it away and dumped it. All of them except the Phoenicians had twice the work, because the steep sides of their trench kept caving in. They were trying to make the opening at the top the same width as the bottom, so something like that was bound to happen as they worked. But the Phoenicians, who show their skill in everything they build, showed it in this work too. When they had been given their share by lot, they started digging with the opening at the top twice as wide as the channel itself was meant to be. As the work went down, they kept narrowing it, so that by the time they reached the bottom their section was the same width as everyone else's. There is a meadow there, where a market was set up for them, a place for buying and selling, and large amounts of grain came for them regularly from Asia, already ground into flour.

24. Making my own guess about this work, it seems to me that Xerxes ordered the canal dug out of a love of grandeur. He wanted to show off his power and leave a monument behind him. For they could have dragged the ships across the isthmus without much trouble, but he ordered them to dig a channel for the sea wide enough for two triremes to be rowed through it side by side. The same men who were assigned the digging were also assigned to bridge the river Strymon, joining its two banks.

25. So that was how Xerxes was going about these things. Meanwhile he also had cables prepared for the bridges, made of papyrus and of white flax, and gave this job to the Phoenicians and the Egyptians. He was also arranging to store up provisions along the route, so that neither the army nor the pack animals would go short as they marched against Greece. He found out by inquiry which places were best, and ordered depots set up in the most convenient spots, with supplies shipped in to different points by merchant ships and ferryboats from every country in Asia. Most of the grain was carried to a place in Thrace called Leuce Acte, "White Cape." Others carried supplies to Tyrodiza in the territory of Perinthus, others to Doriscus, others to Eion on the Strymon, and others to Macedonia, the work being divided among them.

26. While these people were working at the jobs they had been given, the whole land army had been gathered and was marching with Xerxes to Sardis, setting out from Critalla in Cappadocia. That was where the whole army that was to go with Xerxes himself by land had been ordered to assemble. Which of the provincial governors brought the best-equipped force and won the prizes the king had offered, I can't say, because I do not know that it ever even came to a judging. After they crossed the river Halys and entered Phrygia, they marched through that country and came to Celaenae. There the springs of the river Maeander rise, and also those of another river no smaller than the Maeander, called the Catarractes. This one rises right in the marketplace of Celaenae and runs into the Maeander. Also in this city hangs the skin of Marsyas the Silenus, the satyr who challenged Apollo to a music contest; the Phrygians say Apollo flayed it off him and hung it up there.

27. In this city a Lydian, Pythius son of Atys, was waiting for the king. He entertained Xerxes himself and his whole army with the most lavish hospitality, and declared he was ready to supply money for the war as well. When Pythius offered money, Xerxes asked the Persians who were with him who this Pythius was and how much money he had, to make an offer like that. They said: "My king, this is the man who gave your father Darius the golden plane tree and the golden vine. And even now he is the richest man we know of, after you alone."

28. Xerxes was amazed at the last part of this, and next he asked Pythius himself how much money he had. Pythius said: "My king, I will not hide the truth from you, or pretend I don't know what I own. I will count it out for you exactly, since I know the truth of it. As soon as I heard that you were coming down to the Greek sea, I wanted to give you money for the war, so I found out the facts. When I added it up, I found I had 2,000 talents of silver, and of gold, 4,000,000 daric staters, the Persian gold coin, less 7,000. All of this I give to you. For myself, my slaves and my estates give me enough to live on."

29. That is what he said. Xerxes was pleased with his words and replied: "My Lydian host, since I left the land of Persia I have met no one else up to now who wanted to entertain my army, or who came before me and offered of his own free will to contribute money to me for the war, no one except you. You have not only entertained my army magnificently, you are now offering me huge sums of money as well. So in return I give you these rewards. I make you my guest-friend. And I will make up your 4,000,000 staters by giving you the 7,000 myself, so that your 4,000,000 will not fall 7,000 short, and you will have a round sum, made complete by me. Keep what you have earned for yourself, and be sure always to act like this. If you do, you will never be sorry for it, either now or later."

30. Having said this and kept his promise, he marched on. He passed a Phrygian city called Anaua and a lake where salt is harvested, and came to Colossae, a great city of Phrygia. There the river Lycus drops into an opening in the earth and disappears from sight. Then, after about half a mile, it comes up into view again, and this river too flows into the Maeander. Setting out from Colossae toward the border of Phrygia and Lydia, the army reached the city of Cydrara. A pillar stands there, set up by Croesus, with an inscription declaring that the border is at that spot.

31. From Phrygia, then, he entered Lydia. Here the road splits in two. The left-hand road leads toward Caria, and the right-hand one to Sardis. Taking the right-hand road, you have to cross the river Maeander and pass the city of Callatebus, where the men make honey for a living, out of the tamarisk tree and wheat flour. Xerxes took this road, and on it he found a plane tree so beautiful that he adorned it with gold and appointed a man to guard it forever, one keeper after another. On the next day he came to the Lydians' capital.

32. When he reached Sardis, the first thing he did was send heralds to Greece to demand earth and water, and also to give notice in advance that dinners should be prepared for the king. He sent to every state except Athens and Sparta; to those two he did not send to ask for earth. The reason he sent a second time to ask for earth and water was this: he thought that all those who had not given it when Darius sent before would certainly give it now, out of fear. He wanted to know this for certain, and so he sent.

33. After this he got ready to march to Abydos. Meanwhile his men were bridging the Hellespont from Asia to Europe. In the Chersonese on the Hellespont, between the city of Sestos and Madytus, there is a broad headland running down into the sea directly opposite Abydos. This is the place where, not long afterward, the Athenians under Xanthippus son of Ariphron captured Artayctes, a Persian who was governor of Sestos, and nailed him alive to a plank with his hands and feet stretched out. (He was the man who used to bring women into the temple of Protesilaus at Elaeus and do things there that are forbidden.)

34. It was to this headland that the men given the job built their bridges, starting from Abydos. The Phoenicians built one with cables of white flax, and the Egyptians the other, made with papyrus cables. From Abydos to the opposite shore is a distance of about 4,200 feet, a little under a mile. But when the strait had been bridged, a great storm blew up, smashed all the work to pieces and broke it apart.

When Xerxes heard this he was furious. He ordered the Hellespont to be given three hundred lashes with the whip and a pair of shackles to be dropped into the sea. I have even heard that he sent branders along with them to brand the Hellespont. In any case, he ordered them, as they whipped it, to say these barbarous and arrogant words: "You bitter water, your master lays this punishment on you, because you wronged him when he had done you no wrong. And King Xerxes will cross you whether you like it or not. It is right, it seems, that no man offers you sacrifice, since you are a treacherous and salty river." That was how he ordered them to punish the sea. And he ordered that the men in charge of bridging the Hellespont should have their heads cut off.

36. So the men who had been given this ugly duty carried it out, and meanwhile other master builders set to work on the bridges. This is how they built them. They lashed together fifty-oared galleys and triremes, 360 to go under the bridge on the Black Sea side and 314 under the other. The ships lay along the line of the Hellespont's current (though crosswise to the Black Sea), to take the strain of the cables. They set them side by side like this and let down very large anchors: on the one side, toward the Black Sea, because of the winds that blow out from it, and on the other side, toward the west and the Aegean, because of the southeast and south winds. They also left a gap to pass through, so that anyone who wanted could sail into the Black Sea in small boats, and out of it again.

When this was done, they stretched the cables tight from the shore, hauling them taut with wooden windlasses. This time they did not use the two kinds of cable separately. Instead they gave each bridge two cables of white flax and four of papyrus. The thickness and the quality of the work were the same for both, but the flax cables were heavier in proportion: every foot and a half of this cable weighed a talent, about fifty-seven pounds. When the strait was bridged, they sawed up logs, cut them to the width of the bridge, and laid them across the taut cables. Once they had set them in a row, they fastened them down on top. When this was done, they brought on brushwood, and when the brushwood was in place, they brought earth on top of that. When they had stamped the earth down hard, they built a fence along each side, so that the pack animals and horses would not be frightened by looking out over the sea.

37. When the bridges had been finished, and word came that the works at Athos were fully complete, both the canal itself and the breakwaters at its two mouths (these were built to keep the surf from filling up the mouths), the army, which had spent the winter at Sardis, set out from there at the beginning of spring, fully equipped, to march to Abydos. Just as it was setting out, the sun left its place in the sky and vanished, though there were no clouds and the sky was perfectly clear, and day turned into night. When Xerxes saw and understood this, it worried him, and he asked the Magi what the sign meant. They declared that the god was showing the Greeks in advance that their cities would be abandoned. The sun, they said, foretold the future for the Greeks, but the moon for the Persians. When he heard this, Xerxes went on with the march overjoyed.

38. As he was leading his army out, Pythius the Lydian, alarmed by the sign in the sky and made bold by the gifts he had received, came to Xerxes and said: "Master, I would like to ask you for something. It happens to be an easy thing for you to grant, but a great thing for me, if I get it." Xerxes, who thought the request would be anything but what it turned out to be, said he would grant it, and told him to say what he wanted. When Pythius heard this, he took courage and said: "Master, it happens that I have five sons, and it is their lot to be marching with you, all of them together, against Greece. So, my king, take pity on me, now that I have come to such an old age, and release one of my sons from serving in the expedition, the eldest, so that he can take care of me and of my property. Take the other four with you, and when you have done what you have in mind, may you come home safe."

39. Xerxes was furiously angry and answered him: "You miserable man! I am marching against Greece myself, taking my sons and my brothers and my relatives and friends, and you dare to mention a son of yours? You, who are my slave, and who ought to be following me yourself with your whole household, your wife included? Now understand this: a man's spirit lives in his ears. When it hears good things it fills the body with delight, but when it hears the opposite it swells up with rage. When you did us good service before and offered to do more of the same, you could not boast of having outdone the king in generosity. And now that you have turned shameless, you will not get what you deserve, but less than you deserve. Your hospitality will save you and four of your sons from death. But you will pay the penalty with the life of the one you cling to most." After giving this answer, he immediately ordered the men whose job it was to find Pythius' eldest son and cut him in half through the middle. When they had cut him in two, they were to set the halves on either side of the road, one on the right and one on the left, and the army was to march out between them.

40. When they had done this, the army marched out between the halves. First came the baggage carriers with their pack animals, and after them the host of every kind of nation, all mixed together without distinction. When more than half of these had gone by, a space was left, to keep them apart from the king. In front of the king came first a thousand horsemen, picked from all the Persians. After them came a thousand spearmen, also picked from all the Persians, carrying their spears with the points turned down toward the ground. Then came ten sacred horses called "Nesaean," in the finest possible harness. The horses are called Nesaean for this reason: there is a wide plain in Media called the Nesaean plain, and it breeds the big horses I am talking about. Behind these ten horses came the sacred chariot of Zeus, drawn by eight white horses. Behind the horses followed the charioteer on foot, holding the reins, since no human being ever climbs up onto the seat of that chariot. Behind this came Xerxes himself, in a chariot drawn by Nesaean horses, and beside him rode a charioteer whose name was Patiramphes, son of Otanes, a Persian.

41. That was how Xerxes marched out of Sardis, and whenever he felt like it he would change from the chariot to a covered carriage. Behind him came a thousand spearmen, the best and noblest of the Persians, holding their spear points up in the usual way. After them came another thousand horsemen picked from the Persians, and after the horsemen ten thousand men picked from the rest of the Persians. These went on foot. A thousand of them had golden pomegranates on their spears instead of the spikes on the butt end, and these thousand surrounded the others on the outside, while the other nine thousand marched inside them and had silver pomegranates. The men with their spear points turned toward the ground also had golden pomegranates, and those who followed right behind Xerxes had golden apples. Behind the ten thousand came a body of ten thousand Persian cavalry. After the cavalry a gap of as much as 1,200 feet was left, and then the rest of the host came marching along without distinction.

42. So the army made its way from Lydia to the river Caicus and the land of Mysia. Setting out from the Caicus, and keeping Mount Cane on its left, it marched through the region of Atarneus to the city of Carene. From there it went through the plain of Thebe, passing the city of Adramyttium and Antandrus of the Pelasgians. Then, keeping Mount Ida on its left, it came into the land of Troy. The first thing that happened there was that when it stopped for the night right under Mount Ida, thunder and lightning bolts fell on it and killed a great many men on that very spot.

43. Then the army came to the river Scamander. Of all the rivers they had reached since setting out from Sardis on their march, this was the first whose stream gave out and was not enough for the army and its animals to drink. When Xerxes had come to this river, he went up to the citadel of Priam, because he wanted to see it. When he had seen it and asked about everything there, one thing after another, he sacrificed a thousand heifers to Athena of Troy, and the Magi poured offerings to the heroes. After they had done this, a panic fell on the army in the night. At daybreak he marched on from there, keeping on his left the cities of Rhoeteum, Ophryneum and Dardanus, which borders on Abydos, and on his right the Gergithian Teucrians, a people descended from the old Trojans.

44. When Xerxes had reached the middle of Abydos, he wanted to see his whole army. A raised throne of white stone had already been built for him on purpose on a hill there; the people of Abydos had made it on the king's orders, given in advance. There he sat, looking down at the shore, and gazed at both the land army and the ships. As he looked at them he wanted to see the ships race. The race was held, and the Phoenicians of Sidon won it, and he was delighted both with the race and with his whole force.

45. And when he saw the whole Hellespont covered with ships, and all the shores and plains of Abydos full of men, Xerxes called himself a happy man, and after that he began to weep.

46. His uncle Artabanus noticed. This was the same man who had at first spoken his mind so boldly, advising Xerxes not to march against Greece. Seeing that Xerxes was weeping, he asked: "My king, how different from each other are what you did just now and what you did a moment ago! You called yourself a happy man, and now you are in tears." Xerxes said: "Yes, because as I was counting them up, it came into my mind to feel pity at how short the whole of a man's life is, since out of all these thousands, not one will be alive a hundred years from now." Artabanus answered: "There is something else we suffer in the course of our lives that is more pitiful than that. Short as life is, no man, whether among these here or anywhere else, is born so happy that the wish to be dead rather than alive will not come to him many times, and not just once. Misfortunes fall on us, and diseases trouble us, and they make life seem long, short as it really is. So, since life is full of trouble, death has become the most welcome refuge for a man. And heaven, which has given us a taste of how sweet life is, turns out in this to be full of jealousy."

47. Xerxes answered: "Artabanus, human life is what you say it is, but let us stop talking about it, and let's not remember bad things when we have good things in hand. Tell me this instead. If the vision in the dream had not appeared so clearly, would you still hold your earlier opinion and be trying to stop me from marching against Greece, or would you have changed your mind? Come, tell me exactly." Artabanus answered: "My king, may the vision in the dream come true the way we both want it to! But even now I am full of fear and can hardly contain myself. I am taking many things into account, and above all I see that the two greatest things of all are your bitter enemies."

48. Xerxes answered: "You strangest of men, what are these two things you say are my bitter enemies? Is there something wrong with the size of the land army? Do you think the Greek army will turn out to be many times bigger than ours? Or that our fleet will fall short of theirs? Or both together? If you think our power falls short in these ways, another force could be gathered at once."

49. Artabanus answered: "My king, no one with any sense would find fault with this army, or with the number of the ships. In fact, if you gather more, the two things I mean will become even more your enemies. These two things are the land and the sea. On the sea, as I imagine, there is no harbor anywhere big enough to take in this fleet of yours if a storm blows up, and to keep the ships safe until it is over. And there ought to be not just one such harbor, but harbors like it all along the coast of the mainland you are sailing past. Since there are no harbors to take in your ships, understand that men do not rule chance; chance rules men. Now that I have told you one of the two things, I will tell you the other. The land is your enemy in this way. If nothing comes to oppose you, the land grows more hostile to you the farther you advance, always drawn on a little farther, for men are never satisfied with good fortune. What I am saying is this: with no one standing against you, the land you cover will keep growing as time goes on, and it will bring you famine. A man is at his best when he is afraid while he is making his plans, counting on suffering everything that could possibly happen, but bold when he is carrying them out."

50. Xerxes answered: "Artabanus, what you say about these things is reasonable. But don't fear everything, or weigh every danger equally. If you were to weigh every danger equally in every matter that came up, you would never do anything at all. It is better to have courage about everything and suffer half the evils that threaten than to be afraid of everything in advance and suffer nothing at all. And if you argue against everything that is proposed, but never point out the safe course, then you are just as open to the charge of failure as the man who argues the opposite. So the two are evenly matched. But how can a mere man know the safe course? I don't think he can. As a rule, the people who are willing to act are the ones who gain, while those who weigh everything and hold back don't gain much. You see the power of the Persians, how great it has grown. If the kings before me had held opinions like yours, or, without holding them, had had advisers like you, you would never have seen it come this far. As it is, they brought it this far by taking risks, because great power is generally won by taking great risks. So we are following their example. We are marching at the finest season of the year, and when we have conquered all Europe we will come home again without having met famine anywhere or suffered anything else unpleasant. For one thing, we are marching with great stores of food of our own. For another, we will have the grain crops of every people whose land we come to. And we are marching against men who farm the soil, not against nomads."

51. Then Artabanus said: "My king, since you will not let us fear anything, at least take one piece of advice from me. When there is a lot to discuss, it takes a lot of talk. Cyrus son of Cambyses conquered all of Ionia except Athens, and made it pay tribute to the Persians. My advice is that you should on no account lead these Ionians against the people they came from. We are strong enough to beat our enemies even without them. Suppose they come with us. Then they must either prove themselves great wrongdoers, by helping to enslave their mother city, or great doers of right, by helping to set her free. If they turn out great wrongdoers, they don't add very much to our gains. But if they turn out great doers of right, they are in a position to do your army a great deal of damage. So take to heart this old saying too, how true it is: at the beginning of a thing, its end is never fully in view."

52. Xerxes answered: "Artabanus, of all the opinions you have given, this one is the most mistaken. You are afraid the Ionians will change sides. But about them we have the surest proof there is. You are a witness to it yourself, and so are all the others who went with Darius on his march against the Scythians. At that time the safety of the whole Persian army depended on these men, whether they chose to destroy it or to save it. And they showed honesty and loyalty, and nothing hostile at all. Besides, since they have left their children and wives and property in our land, we must not even imagine that they will rebel. So don't be afraid of this either. Keep up your courage, and keep my house and my rule safe. For to you, out of all men, I am handing over my scepter."

53. After saying this, Xerxes sent Artabanus back to Susa. Next he summoned the most distinguished of the Persians, and when they had come before him he said to them: "Persians, I have brought you together because I want this from you: that you show yourselves brave men and do not disgrace the deeds the Persians have done before, which are great and glorious. Let each one of us on his own, and all of us together, put our hearts into this enterprise, because what we are working for is the common good of all. And I urge you to keep at this war without letting up, because, as I am told, we are marching against brave men. If we beat them, there is no other army in the world that will ever stand against us. So let us begin the crossing, after we have prayed to the gods who have Persia in their keeping."

54. All that day they were getting ready to cross. On the next day they waited for the sun, wanting to see it rise, and meanwhile they burned all kinds of incense on the bridges and strewed the road with myrtle branches. Then, as the sun was rising, Xerxes poured an offering into the sea from a golden cup and prayed to the sun that no accident would happen to him that would stop him from conquering Europe until he had reached its farthest borders. After this prayer he threw the cup into the Hellespont, and with it a golden mixing bowl and a Persian sword of the kind they call an akinakes. But whether he threw them into the sea as an offering to the sun, or whether he regretted whipping the Hellespont and wanted to give the sea a gift to make up for it, I can't say for certain.

55. When Xerxes had done this, they began to cross. The whole fighting army, foot and horse, went over by one bridge, the one on the Black Sea side, while the pack animals and the camp followers went over by the other, toward the Aegean. First came the ten thousand Persians, all wearing wreaths, and after them the mixed host of every kind of nation. These crossed on the first day. On the next day the first to cross were the horsemen and the men who carried their spear points turned down, these also wearing wreaths. After them came the sacred horses and the sacred chariot, then Xerxes himself with the spearmen and the thousand horsemen, and after them the rest of the army. Meanwhile the ships too put out from shore and crossed to the other side. But I have also heard another account, which says that the king crossed over last of all.

56. When Xerxes had crossed into Europe, he watched his army crossing under the whip. His army took seven days and seven nights to cross, going on without a break. And then, it is said, when Xerxes had crossed the Hellespont, a man who lived there on the shore cried out: "Zeus, why have you taken the shape of a Persian man and the name of Xerxes instead of Zeus, and why are you bringing all the nations of the world with you to lay Greece waste? You could have done it without any of them."
