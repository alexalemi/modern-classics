Chapter 15: Cambyses Conquers Egypt, and the March against the Ethiopians

1. It was against this Amasis that Cambyses son of Cyrus was now marching. He took with him the other nations he ruled, and Greeks as well, both Ionians and Aeolians. The cause of the expedition was this. Cambyses sent an envoy to Egypt and asked Amasis for his daughter. He made the request on the advice of an Egyptian, who brought this trouble on Amasis because he had a grudge against him. The grudge went back to the time when Cyrus sent to Amasis and asked him for an eye doctor, the best there was in Egypt. Amasis had picked this man out of all the doctors in Egypt, torn him away from his wife and children, and handed him over to Persia. With this grievance in mind, the Egyptian kept urging Cambyses to ask Amasis for his daughter, so that Amasis would either be hurt if he gave her up or, if he refused, would make an enemy of Cambyses. So Amasis, who was both annoyed by the power of the Persians and afraid of it, could neither bring himself to give her nor see how to refuse. He was quite certain that Cambyses meant to take her not as his wife but as a concubine. So, having thought it through, he did this. There was a daughter of Apries, the king before him, a very tall and good-looking girl and the only member of her family still alive. Her name was Nitetis. Amasis dressed this girl in fine clothes and gold and sent her off to Persia as his own daughter. But some time later, when Cambyses greeted her by her father's name, the girl said to him: "My king, you don't realize how Amasis has fooled you. He dressed me up in finery and sent me off to you as if I were his own daughter. But in truth I am the daughter of Apries, his lord and master, against whom Amasis rose up with the Egyptians and whom he murdered." It was these words, and the occasion they gave him, that led Cambyses son of Cyrus, in a towering rage, to march against Egypt.

2. That is the Persians' account. The Egyptians, though, claim Cambyses as one of their own. They say he was born to this very daughter of Apries, and that it was Cyrus, not Cambyses, who sent to Amasis for his daughter. But they are wrong to say so. And they cannot have failed to notice (for the Egyptians know the laws and customs of the Persians as well as anyone does) two things: first, that among the Persians a son born outside marriage does not become king when there is a son born of a true marriage; and second, that Cambyses was the son of Cassandane, the daughter of Pharnaspes, a man of the Achaemenid family, and not of the Egyptian woman. They twist the truth of history because they want to claim kinship with the house of Cyrus. So much for that.

3. There is also another story told, which for my part I do not believe. One of the Persian women, it goes, came to visit the wives of Cyrus. When she saw the tall, good-looking children standing beside Cassandane, she praised them loudly and could not admire them enough. Then Cassandane, the wife of Cyrus, said: "And yet, even though I am the mother of children like these, Cyrus treats me with contempt and honors the woman he has brought in from Egypt." She said this, they say, out of resentment of Nitetis. At that Cambyses, the elder of her sons, said: "Then, mother, when I am grown up, I will turn Egypt upside down: what is on top I will put at the bottom, and what is at the bottom I will put on top." He is said to have said this when he was about ten years old, perhaps, and the women were astonished. And he, they say, never forgot it, so that when at last he had grown up and become king, he made the expedition against Egypt.

4. Something else also helped bring about this expedition, and it was this. Among Amasis' foreign mercenaries there was a man from Halicarnassus named Phanes, who was both sound in judgment and brave in war. This Phanes had some quarrel with Amasis (or so we may suppose), and he fled from Egypt by ship, hoping to get a hearing with Cambyses. Since he was a man of no small standing among the mercenaries and knew every detail of Egypt's affairs, Amasis went after him and thought it a matter of real importance to catch him. He sent the most trusted of his eunuchs after him in a trireme. The eunuch caught him in Lycia, but having caught him, he did not bring him back to Egypt, because Phanes outwitted him: he got his guards drunk and escaped to Persia. Now Cambyses had made up his mind to march on Egypt, but he was at a loss about the march itself, and how he could get safely across the waterless desert. Phanes arrived just then. Besides telling him everything else about Amasis' affairs, he advised him about the march too: he should send to the king of the Arabians and ask him for safe passage through this region.

5. This is the only known way into Egypt. From Phoenicia to the borders of the city of Cadytis the land belongs to the Syrians who are called the Syrians of Palestine. Cadytis is a city not much smaller than Sardis, I would guess. From Cadytis, the trading towns along the coast as far as the city of Ienysus belong to the king of Arabia. Then from Ienysus the land belongs to the Syrians again, as far as Lake Serbonis, along whose shore Mount Casius stretches down toward the sea. From Lake Serbonis, where Typhon, the monster, is said to lie hidden, the land is Egypt. The stretch between the city of Ienysus on one side and Mount Casius and Lake Serbonis on the other is no small one: it is a full three days' journey, and it is terribly short of water.

6. And now I will tell of something that few of those who sail to Egypt have noticed. Twice every year, earthenware jars full of wine are shipped into Egypt from all over Greece and from Phoenicia as well. And yet you could almost say that you will not see a single empty wine jar anywhere in the country.

7. So what becomes of them, you will ask? I will tell you this too. The headman of each district has to collect all the earthenware jars from his own town and carry them to Memphis. The people at Memphis have to fill them with water and carry them to that same waterless region of Syria. So the jars that keep arriving in Egypt and being emptied there are carried off to Syria to join the ones that went before. It was the Persians who prepared this route into Egypt in this way, supplying it with water as I have described, from the time they first took possession of Egypt. But at the time I am speaking of, no water had yet been provided. So Cambyses, following the advice of his guest from Halicarnassus, sent envoys to the Arabian king and asked him for safe passage. He got it, after giving the king pledges of friendship and receiving pledges from him in return.

8. The Arabians respect pledges of friendship as much as any people in the world, and this is how they make them. A third man, not one of the two who want to exchange pledges, stands between them. With a sharp stone he cuts the palms of their hands along the base of the thumb. Then he takes a thread from each man's cloak and with their blood smears seven stones that lie between them, and as he does this he calls on Dionysus and Urania. When he has finished these ceremonies, the man who has given the pledge commends the stranger to his friends (or his fellow tribesman, if he is giving the pledge to a member of his own tribe), and his friends consider themselves bound to honor the pledge as well. The only gods the Arabians believe in are Dionysus and Urania. They say that they cut their hair the same way Dionysus himself had his cut: they crop it in a circle all the way around and shave the temples. They call Dionysus Orotalt, and Urania they call Alilat.

9. So when the Arabian king had given his pledge of friendship to the men who had come to him from Cambyses, he did this. He took camel skins, filled them with water, and loaded them onto every live camel he had. Then he drove them to the waterless region and waited there for Cambyses' army. That is the more believable of the two accounts. But the less believable one must be told too, since it is the one people tell. There is a great river in Arabia called the Corys, which flows out into the sea called the Erythraean, the Indian Ocean. From this river, it is said, the king of the Arabians had a pipeline made of raw oxhides and other skins sewn together, long enough to reach the waterless region. Through this, if you please, he brought the water, and he had great cisterns dug in the waterless region to take the water in and store it. Now it is a twelve days' journey from the river to this waterless region. And what is more, the story says he brought the water by three pipelines to three different parts of it.

10. Meanwhile Psammenitus son of Amasis was camped at the Pelusian mouth of the Nile, waiting for Cambyses to come. For when Cambyses marched on Egypt, he did not find Amasis still alive. Amasis had died after a reign of forty-four years in which no great misfortune had befallen him. When he died he was embalmed and buried in the tomb he had built for himself in the temple. While Psammenitus son of Amasis was king, the Egyptians witnessed the greatest omen that had ever happened to them: rain fell at Thebes in Egypt. It had never rained there before, and it has never rained there since, down to my own time, as the Thebans themselves say; for in Upper Egypt no rain falls at all. But at the time I am speaking of, rain fell at Thebes in a light drizzle.

11. The Persians crossed the whole of the waterless region and camped near the Egyptians, ready to give battle. Then the Egyptian king's foreign mercenaries, who were Greeks and Carians, turned on Phanes. They hated him for having led a foreign-speaking army against Egypt, and they came up with this plan against him. Phanes had left his children behind in Egypt. The mercenaries brought them to their camp, where their father could see them, and set up a mixing bowl between the two camps. Then they led the children up one by one and cut their throats, so that the blood ran into the bowl. When they had gone through all the children, they brought wine and water and poured them into the bowl, and only when every one of the mercenaries had drunk some of the blood did they go into battle. The battle was hard fought, and a great many fell on both sides, but in the end the Egyptians broke and ran.

12. And I saw a great wonder here, which the people of the place pointed out to me. The bones of the men who fell in this battle lie scattered about, each side's apart, for the Persian bones lie on one side, where they were separated at the start, and the Egyptian bones on the other. The skulls of the Persians are so thin that if you just hit one with a pebble you will make a hole in it, while the skulls of the Egyptians are so tough that you could hardly break one if you struck it with a big rock. The reason, they told me, and I readily believe them, is this. The Egyptians shave their heads from early childhood, and the bone thickens in the sun. This is also why they do not go bald: you see fewer bald men among the Egyptians than among any other people. That, then, is why their skulls are strong. The reason the Persians' skulls are weak is that they keep their heads soft and shaded from the start by wearing tiaras, which are felt caps. So much for that. I also saw the same sort of thing at Papremis, among those who were killed along with Achaemenes son of Darius by Inaros the Libyan.

13. After their rout in the battle, the Egyptians fled in disorder and shut themselves up in Memphis. Cambyses sent a ship from Mytilene up the river carrying a Persian herald to call on the Egyptians to come to terms. But when they saw the ship come into Memphis, they poured out of the fortress all together, wrecked the ship, tore the men on board limb from limb, and carried them back into the fortress. After this the Egyptians were besieged, and in time they surrendered. The Libyans who live on the borders of Egypt were terrified by what had happened to Egypt, and they gave themselves up without a fight. They agreed to pay tribute and sent gifts. The people of Cyrene and Barca were just as terrified as the Libyans and did the same. Cambyses accepted the Libyans' gifts graciously, but he found fault with those that came from Cyrene, because, I suppose, they were too small (the Cyreneans in fact sent five hundred pounds of silver). He grabbed the silver by handfuls and scattered it among his soldiers with his own hand.

14. Ten days after he took the surrender of the fortress at Memphis, Cambyses had Psammenitus, the king of the Egyptians, who had reigned for six months, sit in the outskirts of the city to humiliate him. He set him there along with other Egyptians, and he put his spirit to the test in this way. He dressed Psammenitus' daughter in a slave's clothes and sent her out with a pitcher to fetch water. With her he sent other girls, chosen from the daughters of the leading men, dressed the same way as the king's daughter. As the girls passed their fathers, crying and wailing, all the other men began to cry out and wail aloud at the sight of their children so mistreated. But Psammenitus, when he saw it with his own eyes and understood, bowed his head to the ground. When the water carriers had gone by, Cambyses next sent out Psammenitus' son, along with two thousand other Egyptians of the same age, with ropes tied around their necks and bits in their mouths. They were being led off to execution in revenge for the men of Mytilene who had been killed at Memphis with their ship. For the Royal Judges had ruled that for each man, ten of the noblest Egyptians should die in return. Psammenitus saw them go past him and understood that his son was leading the way to his death. But while the other Egyptians sitting around him wept and showed their grief, he did just what he had done at the sight of his daughter. When these too had gone by, it happened that one of his old table companions, a man getting on in years, came past Psammenitus son of Amasis and the Egyptians sitting in the outskirts of the city. This man had lost everything he owned and had nothing left but what a beggar has, and he was begging from the soldiers. When Psammenitus saw him, he let out a great cry of grief, called his friend by name, and beat his own head. Now there were men, it seems, posted to watch him, who reported to Cambyses everything he did each time a group went past. Cambyses was amazed at what he did, and he sent a messenger to ask him this: "Psammenitus, your master Cambyses asks why, when you saw your daughter mistreated and your son going to his death, you did not cry out or weep for them, yet you honored with these signs of grief the beggar who, he hears from others, is no relation of yours at all." That was the question, and Psammenitus answered: "Son of Cyrus, my own troubles were too great for me to weep over aloud. But my friend's trouble called for tears: he has lost great wealth and fallen into beggary on the threshold of old age." When the messenger reported this answer, they all thought it well said. And according to the Egyptians, Croesus wept (for he too, as fortune would have it, had come with Cambyses to Egypt), and the Persians who were there wept as well. Even Cambyses himself felt some pity. At once he ordered that Psammenitus' son be saved from among those who were being put to death, and that Psammenitus himself be raised from his place in the outskirts of the city and brought before him.

15. As for the son, the men sent to fetch him found that he was no longer alive: he had been the first to be cut down. But they raised Psammenitus himself from his place and brought him before Cambyses, and he lived with him from then on without suffering any violence. And if he had known how to keep out of trouble, he would have been given Egypt back to rule. For the Persians make a practice of honoring the sons of kings; even if the kings have revolted against them, they still give the power back to their sons. That this is their settled rule can be seen from many other cases, and especially from that of Thannyras son of Inaros, who got back the power his father had held, and that of Pausiris son of Amyrtaeus, who also got back his father's power. And yet it is certain that no one up to this time ever did the Persians more harm than Inaros and Amyrtaeus. As it was, though, Psammenitus plotted mischief and got what he deserved. He was caught stirring up the Egyptians to revolt. When Cambyses found out, Psammenitus drank bull's blood and died on the spot. That was how he met his end.

16. From Memphis Cambyses went to the city of Sais, meaning to do exactly what he then did. When he had entered Amasis' palace, he at once ordered Amasis' corpse to be brought out of its tomb. When this had been done, he ordered them to whip it, pull out its hair, stab it, and treat it with every other kind of indignity. They went on doing this until they were worn out, for the corpse had been embalmed and held out against their violence without falling apart anywhere. Then Cambyses ordered it to be burned. In giving this order he was commanding something forbidden by religion, for the Persians hold fire to be a god. Burning the dead is not the custom of either people at all. It is not the Persians' custom, for the reason I have given, since they say it is wrong to give a man's dead body to a god. And the Egyptians believe that fire is a living wild beast that devours everything it catches, and that when it has eaten its fill it dies along with what it is devouring. But it is certainly not their custom to give a man's corpse to wild beasts; that is why they embalm it, so that worms will not eat it as it lies in the tomb. So Cambyses was ordering them to do something the customs of neither people allow. The Egyptians, however, say that it was not Amasis who suffered this outrage, but another Egyptian of the same height and build. The Persians, they say, did their outrage to him, thinking they were doing it to Amasis. For Amasis, they say, had learned from an oracle what was going to happen to him after his death. So to ward off the threatened harm, he buried the body of this man, the one who was whipped, just inside the doors of his own burial chamber, and instructed his son to lay his own body as far back in the innermost part of the chamber as he could. In my opinion these instructions, which Amasis is supposed to have given about his burial and about this man, were never given at all. I think the Egyptians just pretend they were, out of pride, and with no good reason.

17. After this Cambyses planned three separate expeditions: one against the Carthaginians, another against the Ammonians, and a third against the "Long-lived" Ethiopians, who live in the part of Libya that lies on the Southern Sea, the Indian Ocean. As he worked out these plans, he decided to send his fleet against the Carthaginians and a picked force from his land army against the Ammonians. To the Ethiopians he decided to send spies first. They were to see whether the Table of the Sun, which is said to exist among these Ethiopians, was really there, and to spy out everything else besides, while pretending to be bringing gifts for their king.

18. The Table of the Sun is said to be like this. In the outskirts of their city there is a meadow full of the boiled meat of every kind of four-legged animal. Here, it is said, the citizens who are in office at the time take care to lay out the meat by night, and by day anyone who wants to can come and eat his fill. The natives, it is reported, say that the earth itself produces these things, over and over.

19. That is what the so-called Table of the Sun is said to be like. So when Cambyses had made up his mind to send the spies, he at once sent to the city of Elephantine for some of the Fish-Eaters (the Ichthyophagi, as the Greeks call them) who knew the Ethiopian language. While men were going to fetch them, he ordered the fleet to sail against Carthage. But the Phoenicians said they would not do it. They were bound by solemn oaths not to, they said, and it would be an impious thing to make war on their own children, since Carthage was their colony. And since the Phoenicians were unwilling, the rest of the fleet was not strong enough to make the attempt. That is how the Carthaginians escaped being enslaved by the Persians. Cambyses did not think it right to force the Phoenicians, both because they had handed themselves over to the Persians of their own accord and because the whole navy depended on them. The Cyprians too had handed themselves over to the Persians and were taking part in the expedition against Egypt.

20. As soon as the Fish-Eaters came to Cambyses from Elephantine, he sent them to the Ethiopians, telling them what to say and giving them gifts to take: a purple robe, a twisted gold necklace with bracelets, an alabaster jar of perfume, and a jar of palm wine. These Ethiopians to whom Cambyses was sending them are said to be the tallest and most handsome people in the world. Besides their other customs, which are reported to differ from those of other peoples, there is this one in particular, it is said, about their kingship: whichever man of their nation they judge to be the tallest, and to have strength to match his size, is the one they make their king.

21. So when the Fish-Eaters reached this people, they presented their gifts to the king who ruled them, and said: "Cambyses, king of the Persians, wishes to become your friend and guest-friend. He has sent us with orders to speak with you, and he gives you as gifts these things, which are the things he himself most enjoys." But the Ethiopian saw that they had come as spies, and he said to them: "The king of the Persians did not send you with gifts because he thought it very important to become my guest-friend, and you are not telling the truth (for you have come to spy on my kingdom), and he is not a just man either. If he were just, he would not have coveted any land but his own, and he would not be leading into slavery people who have done him no wrong. Now, though, give him this bow and tell him this: The king of the Ethiopians advises the king of the Persians that when the Persians can draw bows as big as this one as easily as I do, then he should march against the Long-lived Ethiopians, provided he has the larger army. But until then he should thank the gods that they do not put it into the minds of the sons of the Ethiopians to win more land in addition to their own."

22. With that he unstrung the bow and handed it over to the men who had come. Then he picked up the purple robe and asked what it was and how it was made. When the Fish-Eaters told him the truth about the purple shellfish and how the cloth is dyed, he said that the men were deceitful and so were their clothes. Next he asked about the twisted gold of the necklace and the bracelets. When the Fish-Eaters explained how they were made, the king burst out laughing; he took them for shackles, and he said that they had stronger shackles than these in his country. Third, he asked about the perfume, and when they told him how it was made and how people anoint themselves with it, he said the same thing he had said about the robe. Then he came to the wine and learned how it was made, and he was so delighted with the taste of the drink that he went on to ask what the king ate and what was the longest a Persian lived. They told him that the king ate bread, explaining first how wheat is grown, and they said that eighty years was the longest life a Persian could expect. To this the Ethiopian replied that he was not surprised that they lived so few years when they ate dung. They would not even live as long as that, he said, if they did not revive themselves with this drink, and he pointed out the wine to the Fish-Eaters. In this one thing, he said, his own people fell well short of the Persians.

23. Then the Fish-Eaters asked the king in turn how long his people lived and how they lived. He answered that most of them reached a hundred and twenty years, and some lived even longer, and that their food was boiled meat and their drink was milk. When the spies marveled at the number of years, he took them to a certain spring. When they washed in its water their skin became sleeker, as if it were a spring of oil, and it gave off a scent like violets. The water of this spring, the spies said, was so thin that nothing would float on it, neither wood nor anything lighter than wood: everything sank to the bottom. If this water of theirs is really what it is said to be, it would surely be the reason the people live so long, since they use it for everything. When they left the spring, he took them to a prison for men, and there all the prisoners were bound in chains of gold. For among these Ethiopians bronze is the rarest and most precious thing of all. When they had seen the prison, they saw the so-called Table of the Sun as well.

24. After that, last of all, they saw their coffins, which are said to be made of crystal, in this way. They dry the corpse, either the Egyptian way or some other, then coat it all over with plaster and paint it, making the figure look as much like the living man as they can. After this they put it inside a hollowed-out block of crystal, which they dig up in great quantities and which is very easy to work. The body sits in the middle of the block and can be seen through it. It gives off no unpleasant smell and nothing else offensive, and every part of it can be seen as plainly as the body itself. The dead man's closest relatives keep the block in their house for a year, giving him the first share of everything and offering sacrifices to him. After that they carry it out and set it up around the city.

25. When they had seen everything, the spies left to go back. And when they reported all this, Cambyses flew into a rage at once and marched his army against the Ethiopians. He gave no orders for any supply of food, and he never stopped to think that he was about to march an army to the farthest ends of the earth. Instead, like a madman out of his right mind, he set out the moment he heard the Fish-Eaters' report. He ordered the Greeks who were with him to stay behind in Egypt and took his whole land army with him. When in the course of the march he reached Thebes, he split off about fifty thousand men from his army and ordered them to enslave the Ammonians and burn down the oracle of Zeus, while he himself went on against the Ethiopians with the rest of the army. But before the army had covered a fifth of the way, all the food they had ran out completely, and after the food, the pack animals too were eaten until they were gone. Now if Cambyses, when he saw this, had changed his mind and led his army back, he would have been a wise man in spite of his first mistake. As it was, he paid no attention but kept on going forward. So the soldiers kept themselves alive by eating grass, as long as they could find anything growing in the ground. But when they reached the sand, some of them did a terrible thing: out of every ten men they chose one by lot and ate him. When Cambyses heard of this, he was frightened by the cannibalism. He gave up the expedition against the Ethiopians and started back, and he reached Thebes having lost a great part of his army. From Thebes he came down to Memphis and let the Greeks sail home.
