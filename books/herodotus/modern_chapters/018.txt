Chapter 18: The Debate on Government, Darius Becomes King, and the Riches of India and Arabia

80. When the uproar had died down and more than five days had passed, the men who had risen against the Magi began to discuss the state of the whole country. Speeches were made then that some Greeks do not believe were really spoken, but spoken they were all the same. Otanes urged that they should hand the government over to the whole body of the Persians, and this is what he said: "I think none of us should be sole ruler from now on, because that is neither pleasant nor good. You saw how far Cambyses' arrogance went, and you have had a taste of the Magus' arrogance too. How can the rule of one man be a well-ordered thing, when a monarch can do whatever he likes without answering to anyone for what he does? Put even the best man in the world in that position and it would change him from what he used to be. The good things he has breed arrogance in him, and envy is planted in human beings from the start. With those two he has every vice, because he does many reckless and wicked things, some out of arrogance born of having too much, some out of envy. And yet a tyrant, of all people, ought to be free from envy, since he has every good thing there is. But by nature he behaves toward his subjects in just the opposite way. He resents the best men for being alive at all, he enjoys the company of the worst citizens, and he is readier than anyone to listen to slander. And he is the most inconsistent of all creatures. If you admire him in moderation, he is offended that you are not courting him hard enough; if you court him extravagantly, he is offended at you for being a flatterer. But the most important thing is what I am about to say. He overturns the customs handed down from our fathers, he forces himself on women, and he puts men to death without trial. The rule of the many, on the other hand, first of all has the finest name there is: Equality. And second, the many do none of the things a monarch does. Offices are filled by lot, officials have to answer for what they do in office, and every question is brought before the public assembly. So my opinion is that we let monarchy go and raise up the power of the people, because everything is in the many."

81. That was the opinion Otanes gave. But Megabyzus urged that they hand things over to the rule of a few, and he said: "What Otanes said against tyranny, count it as said by me too. But when he urged us to hand power to the masses, he missed the best advice. Nothing is more senseless or more arrogant than a worthless mob. For men to escape the arrogance of a tyrant only to fall into the arrogance of an unruly people is not to be endured at all. When the tyrant does something, at least he knows what he is doing; the people cannot even know. How could anyone know who has never been taught anything good by others and has never seen anything good for himself, but rushes at things headlong and without sense, like a river in flood? Let the enemies of the Persians have rule by the people. But let us choose a group of the best men and give the power to them. We ourselves will be among them, and it is likely that the best men will make the best decisions."

82. That was the opinion Megabyzus gave. Third, Darius declared his opinion, saying: "I think that what Megabyzus said about the masses was right, but what he said about the rule of a few was not. Three things are set before us, and let us suppose each is the best of its kind: the best democracy, the best oligarchy, and the best monarchy. I say the last is far better than the others. Nothing better can be found than the rule of one man, if he is the best man. Using his own best judgment, he would guard the people without fault, and the plans he made against enemies would be best kept secret that way. In an oligarchy, many men work at doing good for the state, and so strong private hatreds often spring up among them. Each one wants to be the leader himself and to have his own way in the council, so they come to hate each other bitterly. From that come factions, from factions come murders, and from murders comes the rule of one man. And that shows how much the rule of one is the best. Then again, when the people rule, corruption is bound to arise. And when corruption arises in public life, the corrupt men do not come to hate each other; they become close friends, because those who are robbing the state put their heads together to do it in secret. This goes on until someone takes the lead of the people and puts a stop to such men. Because of that, this man is admired by the people, and being admired, he suddenly turns out to be a monarch. So he too proves that the rule of one is best. Finally, to sum it all up in a word: where did our freedom come from, and who gave it to us? Was it the people, or an oligarchy, or a monarch? My opinion, then, is that since one man set us free, we should keep that form of rule. And apart from that, we should not do away with our fathers' customs when they are good ones. That is not the better way."

83. These three opinions had been put forward, and the other four of the seven sided with the last. When Otanes, who wanted to give the Persians equality, saw that his view had lost, he spoke to the gathering: "My fellow conspirators, it is clear that one of us has to become king, whether he is chosen by lot, or by leaving the decision to the whole mass of the Persians and taking whoever they choose, or in some other way. Well, I will not compete with you. I want neither to rule nor to be ruled. I withdraw my claim to rule on this condition: that I shall not be ruled by any of you, neither I nor my descendants for all time to come." When he had said this, the six agreed to his terms. He did not compete with them but withdrew from the gathering. And to this day his house is the only free one among all the Persians. It obeys only as far as it chooses, though it does not break the laws of the Persians.

84. The rest of the seven went on discussing how to set up a king in the fairest way. They decided that if the kingdom went to any of the others among the seven, Otanes and his descendants after him should receive, as special gifts, a suit of Median clothes every year and all the presents the Persians count most valuable. They decided to give him these because he was the first to propose the business and the one who brought them together. These were the special gifts for Otanes. They also decided this for all of them in common: any one of the seven who wished could walk into the royal palace without anyone going in ahead to announce him, unless the king happened to be sleeping with his wife; and the king was not allowed to marry from any family except those of the men who had risen with him. About the kingdom itself they decided this: they would ride out on their horses into the outskirts of the city, and the man whose horse neighed first after sunrise would have the kingdom.

85. Now Darius had a clever groom named Oebares. When they had left their meeting, Darius said to him: "Oebares, we have decided this about the kingdom: the man whose horse neighs first after sunrise, when we are mounted, will be king. So if you have any cleverness in you, find a way for us to win this prize, and not someone else." Oebares answered: "Master, if it really depends on this whether you become king or not, take heart and don't worry about it. Nobody else will be king before you. I have just the charm for it." Then Darius said: "If you really have a trick like that, it's time to work it out, not to put it off, because our trial is tomorrow." When Oebares heard this, here is what he did. As night was coming on, he took one of the mares, the one Darius' stallion liked best, led her into the outskirts of the city, and tied her up. Then he brought Darius' stallion to her. For some time he led him around her, close enough to brush against the mare, and at last he let the stallion mount her.

86. At dawn the six rode up on their horses, as they had agreed. As they were riding through the outskirts of the city, they came near the spot where the mare had been tied up the night before, and Darius' horse ran up to the spot and neighed. Just as the horse did this, lightning flashed and thunder sounded out of a clear sky. These signs, coming to Darius on top of the neighing, made his claim complete, because they seemed to have happened by some design. The others leapt down from their horses and bowed down before Darius.

87. Some say this was Oebares' trick, but others tell it another way (the Persians tell the story both ways). In this version, he rubbed his hand on the mare's genitals and then kept his hand hidden inside his trousers. At sunrise, when they were about to let the horses go, Oebares pulled out his hand and held it to the nostrils of Darius' horse. The horse caught the smell, snorted, and neighed.

88. So Darius son of Hystaspes was proclaimed king. In Asia everyone except the Arabians was subject to him, since Cyrus had conquered them and Cambyses after him. The Arabians, though, were never subjects of the Persians. They had become their guest-friends when they let Cambyses pass through on his way to Egypt, because the Persians could not have invaded Egypt against the Arabians' will. Darius also made the most distinguished marriages a Persian could make. He married two daughters of Cyrus, Atossa and Artystone. Atossa had been the wife first of her brother Cambyses and after that of the Magus, while Artystone was a virgin. Besides them he married the daughter of Smerdis son of Cyrus, whose name was Parmys. And he also married the daughter of Otanes, the man who had found out the Magus. His power filled everything. First of all, he had a stone relief carved and set up. On it was the figure of a man on horseback, and he had it inscribed with these words: "Darius son of Hystaspes, by the excellence of his horse" (and here the horse's name was given) "and of his groom Oebares, won the kingdom of the Persians."

89. After he had done this in Persia, he set up twenty provinces, which the Persians themselves call satrapies. Once he had set up the provinces and put governors over them, he fixed the tribute each was to pay him, people by people. To each of the main peoples he joined its neighbors, or else, skipping over the nearest peoples, he assigned the more distant ones to various groups. He divided up the provinces and the yearly tribute as follows. Those who paid in silver were ordered to pay by the standard of the Babylonian talent, and those who paid in gold by the Euboean talent. The Babylonian talent is equal to seventy-eight Euboean minas, and since a mina is a sixtieth of a talent, that makes one Babylonian talent worth 1.3 Euboean talents. In the reigns of Cyrus and Cambyses nothing had been fixed about tribute; people used to bring gifts. Because Darius fixed the tribute, and did other things of the same kind, the Persians say that Darius was a shopkeeper, Cambyses a master, and Cyrus a father. Darius, because he ran all his affairs like a shopkeeper; Cambyses, because he was harsh and cared for no one; and Cyrus, because he was gentle and worked out every good thing for them.

90. From the Ionians, the Magnesians who live in Asia, the Aeolians, the Carians, the Lycians, the Milyans and the Pamphylians (he set one single sum as the tribute for all of these together) there came in four hundred talents of silver. He made this the first province. From the Mysians, the Lydians, the Lasonians, the Cabalians and the Hytennians there came in five hundred talents. This is the second province. From the Hellespontians who live on the right as you sail in, the Phrygians, the Thracians who live in Asia, the Paphlagonians, the Mariandyni and the Syrians, the tribute was three hundred and sixty talents. This is the third province. From the Cilicians there came in three hundred and sixty white horses, one for every day of the year, and five hundred talents of silver besides. Of this silver, one hundred and forty talents were spent on the cavalry that guarded the land of Cilicia, and the remaining three hundred and sixty came in to Darius year by year. This is the fourth province.

91. The province that begins at the city of Posideium, which Amphilochus son of Amphiaraus founded on the border between the Cilicians and the Syrians, runs all the way to Egypt. It does not include the territory of the Arabians, which was free from tribute. From it came three hundred and fifty talents. In this province are all of Phoenicia, the part of Syria called Palestine, and Cyprus. This is the fifth province. From Egypt and the Libyans who border on Egypt, and from Cyrene and Barca, which were assigned to the Egyptian province, there came in seven hundred talents. That is not counting the money from Lake Moeris, which came from its fish. Without counting that, I say, or the grain they also contributed by measure, there came in seven hundred talents. As for the grain, they supply 120,000 medimni of it (about 180,000 bushels) for the Persians stationed in the "White Fortress" at Memphis and for their foreign mercenaries. This is the sixth province. The Sattagydians, the Gandarians, the Dadicae and the Aparytae, grouped together, paid one hundred and seventy talents. This is the seventh province. From Susa and the rest of the land of the Cissians there came in three hundred. This is the eighth province.

92. From Babylon and the rest of Assyria there came in to him a thousand talents of silver and five hundred boys to be made eunuchs. This is the ninth province. From Ecbatana and the rest of Media, and from the Paricanians and the Orthocorybantians, came four hundred and fifty talents. This is the tenth province. The Caspians, the Pausicae, the Pantimathi and the Daritae paid together two hundred talents. This is the eleventh province. From the Bactrians as far as the Aegli the tribute was three hundred and sixty talents. This is the twelfth province.

93. From Pactyice, the Armenians, and the peoples bordering on them as far as the Black Sea came four hundred talents. This is the thirteenth province. From the Sagartians, the Sarangians, the Thamanaeans, the Utians, the Mycians, and the people who live on the islands of the Persian Gulf, where the king settles the people called the "Removed," those he has deported from their homes, from all these together came a tribute of six hundred talents. This is the fourteenth province. The Sacae and the Caspians paid two hundred and fifty talents. This is the fifteenth province. The Parthians, the Chorasmians, the Sogdians and the Areians paid three hundred talents. This is the sixteenth province.

94. The Paricanians and the Ethiopians of Asia paid four hundred talents. This is the seventeenth province. The Matienians, the Saspeirians and the Alarodians were assessed at two hundred talents. This is the eighteenth province. The Moschi, the Tibarenians, the Macrones, the Mossynoeci and the Mares were ordered to pay three hundred talents. This is the nineteenth province. The Indians are by far the most numerous people of any we know of, and they paid a tribute larger than all the rest: three hundred and sixty talents of gold dust. This is the twentieth province.

95. Now if we convert the Babylonian talents into Euboean ones, the silver comes to 9,880 talents. And if we count gold at thirteen times the value of silver, weight for weight, the gold dust comes to 4,680 Euboean talents. Add all these together, and the total collected every year in tribute for Darius comes to 14,560 Euboean talents. Sums smaller than these I leave out and do not mention.

96. This was the tribute that came in to Darius from Asia and from a small part of Libya. But as time went on, more tribute came in from the islands as well, and from the peoples of Europe as far as Thessaly. The king stores this tribute in his treasury in the following way. He melts it down and pours it into clay jars, and when the jars are full he breaks the clay away from the metal. Then, whenever he needs money, he cuts off as much as he needs at the time.

97. Those were the provinces and the tribute each was assessed. The land of Persia alone I have not listed as paying anything, because the Persians hold their land free of tribute. The following peoples had no tribute fixed for them, but brought gifts instead. First, the Ethiopians who border on Egypt, whom Cambyses conquered on his march against the Long-Lived Ethiopians, and those who live around sacred Nysa and hold festivals in honor of Dionysus. These Ethiopians and their neighbors use the same grain as the Callantian Indians, and they live in underground houses. These two peoples together brought every other year, and still bring down to my own time, two quarts of unrefined gold, two hundred logs of ebony, five Ethiopian boys, and twenty large elephant tusks. The Colchians also counted themselves among those who brought gifts, together with their neighbors as far as the Caucasus Mountains. (Persian rule reaches as far as these mountains, but the peoples who live beyond the Caucasus, toward the north wind, pay no attention to the Persians.) These peoples went on bringing the gifts they had fixed for themselves every four years, down to my own time: a hundred boys and a hundred girls. Finally, the Arabians brought a thousand talents' weight of frankincense every year (about 57,000 pounds). These were the gifts these peoples brought the king, apart from the tribute.

98. Now here is how the Indians get the great quantity of gold from which they bring the king the gold dust I mentioned. The part of India toward the sunrise is sand. Of all the peoples of Asia that we know of, or about whom anything certain is reported, the Indians live farthest to the east, toward the rising sun, since the country east of the Indians is desert because of the sand. There are many tribes of Indians, and they do not speak the same language. Some of them herd animals and some do not. Some live in the marshes of the river and eat raw fish, which they catch from boats made of cane, each boat made from a single joint of cane. These Indians wear clothes made of rushes. They gather and cut rushes from the river, weave them into a sort of mat, and wear it like a breastplate.

99. Other Indians, living to the east of these, are herders and eat raw meat. They are called the Padaei, and these are their customs. Whenever one of their tribe falls sick, woman or man, if it is a man, the men who are closest to him kill him. They say that the disease is wasting him and his flesh is being spoiled for them. All the while he flatly denies that he is sick, but they do not accept that, and after they have killed him they feast on his flesh. If it is a woman who falls sick, the women closest to her do the same to her as the men do in the other case. In fact, they even kill and feast on anyone who reaches old age. But very few of them ever get to be counted as old, because they kill everyone who falls sick before he reaches old age.

100. Other Indians, on the contrary, live like this. They kill no living thing, they sow no crops, and it is not their custom to have houses. They live on plants. They have a grain about the size of millet, in a husk, which grows wild from the ground. They gather it, boil it husk and all, and eat it. Whenever one of them falls sick, he goes off into the wilderness and lies there, and none of the others pays any attention, either to the dead or to the sick.

101. All these Indians I have spoken of have sex in the open, like cattle. They all have the same skin color, much like that of the Ethiopians. And the semen they ejaculate is not white like other peoples' but black, like their skin; the Ethiopians are the same in this respect. These tribes of Indians live beyond the reach of Persian power, toward the south wind, and they were never subjects of Darius.

102. Other Indians live on the borders of the city of Caspatyrus and the country of Pactyice, to the north of the rest of the Indians, and their way of life is much like that of the Bactrians. These are the most warlike of the Indians, and they are the ones who go on expeditions for the gold. In the parts where they live it is desert because of the sand. In this sandy desert there are ants smaller than dogs but bigger than foxes; in fact some of them, caught out there, are kept at the palace of the king of Persia. These ants make their homes underground and bring up the sand just the way the ants in Greece do, and they look very much like them too. And the sand they bring up has gold in it. To get this sand, the Indians set out into the desert, each man with three camels yoked together: a female in the middle and a male on each side, pulling like a trace horse. The man rides on the female himself, and he takes care that the one he yokes has been taken away from young she has just given birth to, the younger the better. Their female camels are as fast as horses, and they can carry much heavier loads besides.

103. I will not describe what the camel looks like, since the Greeks I am writing for already know what it looks like. But I will tell what is not commonly known about it. The camel has four thighs and four knees in its hind legs, and its genitals are between its hind legs, pointing back toward the tail.

104. So the Indians ride out for the gold in the way I have described, with their camels yoked like that. They time it so that they are carrying the gold off during the hottest part of the day, because the heat drives the ants underground. In those lands the sun is hottest in the morning, not at midday as it is elsewhere. From sunrise until the hour when the market closes, it gives off much more heat than the midday sun in Greece, so much that people there are said to soak themselves in water then. At midday the heat is about the same for the Indians as for everyone else. After midday their sun becomes like the morning sun elsewhere, and from then on, as it moves farther away, it gets cooler and cooler, until at sunset it is very cool indeed.

105. When the Indians reach the place, carrying bags, they fill them with the sand and ride back as fast as they can. The ants, the Persians say, catch the scent and at once start to chase them. This animal, they say, is faster than any other creature, so that if the Indians did not get a head start while the ants were gathering, not one of them would get away. The male camels are slower runners than the females, so if they start to drag, they are cut loose from the female's side, first one and then the other. The females, though, remembering the young they left behind, do not slacken at all. That is how the Indians get most of their gold, the Persians say. There is other gold in their country too, which they mine, but less of it.

106. It seems that the farthest parts of the inhabited world have been given the finest things, just as Greece has been given far the best-balanced climate. First, India, as I said a little earlier, is the farthest of the inhabited lands toward the east. In that land the animals, both the birds and the four-footed beasts, are much bigger than anywhere else, except for the horses, which are outdone by the Median horses called Nesaean, the famous breed of Media. There is also gold in abundance there, some of it mined, some carried down by rivers, and some stolen in the way I just explained. And there the trees that grow wild bear wool that is finer and better than sheep's wool, and the Indians wear clothes made from these trees.

107. Then again, Arabia is the farthest of the inhabited lands toward the south. It is the only land in the world where frankincense, myrrh, cassia, cinnamon and gum ladanum grow. All of these except myrrh the Arabians get only with great difficulty. They gather frankincense by burning storax, a fragrant gum, which the Phoenicians bring to Greece. They burn this to make smoke, and so they get the frankincense. The trees that bear frankincense are guarded by winged snakes, small and of many colors, great numbers of them around each tree. These are the same kind of snakes that try to invade Egypt. Nothing will drive them away from the trees except the smoke of storax.

108. The Arabians also say that the whole world would be full of these snakes by now if the same thing did not happen to them that I know happens to vipers. It seems that the forethought of the divine, wise as one would expect it to be, has made all the timid animals that are good to eat breed abundantly, so that they will not all be eaten up and die out, while it has made the fierce and harmful ones have few young. For example, because the hare is hunted by every beast and bird, and by man as well, it breeds very abundantly. It is the only animal that becomes pregnant again before its earlier young are born. It carries in its womb some young that already have fur, others still bare, and while one is just being formed in the womb, another is being conceived. That is how it is with the hare. The lioness, on the other hand, the strongest and boldest of creatures, gives birth to one cub only once in her life, because when she gives birth she casts out her womb along with her cub. The reason is this. When the cub inside its mother begins to move, its claws, which are far sharper than any other animal's, tear the womb, and the bigger it grows, the more it scratches through. At last, when the birth is near, there is nothing of the womb left whole at all.

109. In the same way, if vipers and the winged snakes of Arabia were born in the ordinary course of nature, people could not live on the earth. But as it is, when they mate and the male is in the act and releasing his seed, the female seizes him by the neck, fastens on, and does not let go until she has bitten clean through it. So the male dies in the way I said. But the female pays the price for the male in this way: while the young are still inside her, they avenge their father by eating through their mother, and once they have eaten through her belly, they make their way out. Other snakes, which do no harm to people, lay eggs and hatch a great many young from them. Now vipers are found all over the earth. But the winged snakes are found in great numbers together in Arabia and in no other land, and that is why they seem so numerous.

110. That, then, is how the Arabians get their frankincense, and here is how they get cassia, a spice bark much like cinnamon. They wrap their whole body and face, all but their eyes, in cowhide and other skins, and then they go out for the cassia. It grows in a shallow lake. Around the lake and in it there live, it seems, winged creatures very like bats, which screech horribly and fight fiercely. The men have to keep these away from their eyes while they cut the cassia.

111. Cinnamon they gather in an even more remarkable way. Where it grows and what land produces it, they cannot say, except that some say, and it is a likely story, that it grows in the regions where Dionysus was brought up. They say that large birds carry these dry sticks, the ones we have learned from the Phoenicians to call cinnamon, up to nests made of mud and stuck on the sheer faces of mountains, which no one can find any way to climb. So the Arabians have worked out this trick. They cut up the oxen and donkeys and other pack animals that die into pieces as large as they can handle, carry them to these places, and lay them down not far from the nests. Then they move off a good distance. The birds fly down and carry the pieces of the pack animals up to their nests. The nests cannot bear the weight, and they break and fall to the ground. Then the men come up and collect the cinnamon. That is how cinnamon is gathered, and from this people it reaches the other countries of the world.

112. Gum ladanum, which the Arabians call ladanon, comes about in a still stranger way. Though it is the sweetest-smelling of all things, it comes from the worst-smelling thing there is: it is found in the beards of billy goats, forming there like resin on wood. It is used in making many perfumes, and the Arabians burn it as incense more than anything else.

113. That is enough about spices. A wonderfully sweet scent of them blows from the land of Arabia. The Arabians also have two kinds of sheep worth marveling at, which are found in no other land. One kind has a long tail, no less than four and a half feet long. If the sheep were left to drag their tails behind them, they would get sores from the tails rubbing against the ground. But as it is, every shepherd there knows enough carpentry to make little carts, which they fasten under the tails, each animal's tail tied to a little cart of its own. The other kind of sheep has a broad tail, as much as a foot and a half across.
