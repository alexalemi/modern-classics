Chapter 2: The Son of Croesus, the Testing of the Oracles, and Athens under Pisistratus

34. After Solon had gone, a great retribution from heaven fell on Croesus, probably because he had judged himself the happiest of all men. First a dream came and stood over him as he slept, and it showed him the truth about the evils that were about to happen to his son. Croesus had two sons. One of them was disabled, since he was deaf and unable to speak. The other far surpassed the young men of his own age in everything, and his name was Atys. About this Atys, then, the dream told Croesus that he would lose him, struck by an iron spear-point. When Croesus woke up and thought the matter over, he was terrified by the dream. First he found his son a wife. Then, although the young man had been in the habit of leading the Lydian armies, Croesus no longer sent him out anywhere on any such business. And he had the javelins and lances and every other weapon men use for fighting taken out of the men's quarters and piled up in the inner storerooms, for fear that something hanging on the wall might fall down on his son.

35. While Croesus was busy with his son's wedding, a man arrived at Sardis who was under a misfortune and whose hands were not clean of blood. He was a Phrygian by birth and of the royal family. This man came to Croesus' house and, following the custom of that country, asked to be purified of the killing, and Croesus purified him. (The Lydian way of purifying a man is almost the same as the Greek one.) When Croesus had done what custom required, he asked the man where he came from and who he was. "Stranger," he said, "who are you? From what part of Phrygia have you come to sit as a suppliant at my hearth? And what man or woman did you kill?" The man answered: "My king, I am the son of Gordias son of Midas, and my name is Adrastus. I killed my own brother without meaning to. That is why I am here: my father drove me out and stripped me of everything I had." Croesus answered: "You happen to come from a family who are our friends, and you have come to friends. As long as you stay in our country you will want for nothing. And you will find it best for yourself to bear this misfortune as lightly as you can." So Adrastus made his home with Croesus.

36. During this time a monstrous boar appeared on Mount Olympus in Mysia. It came down from that mountain and ravaged the fields of the Mysians. The Mysians went out against it many times, but they could do it no harm; instead it did harm to them. So at last messengers from the Mysians came to Croesus and said: "My king, a monstrous boar has appeared in our country and is destroying our fields. We are eager to kill it, but we can't. So now we ask you to send your son with us, and a picked band of young men with dogs, so that we can rid our land of it." That was their request. But Croesus remembered what the dream had said, and he answered them: "As for my son, don't mention him again in this matter. I will not send him with you, since he has just been married and that is what concerns him now. But I will send picked men of the Lydians with you, and my whole pack of hunting dogs, and I will order those who go to do everything they can to help you rid your land of the beast."

37. That was his answer. The Mysians were satisfied with it, but just then Croesus' son came in, having heard what the Mysians had asked. When Croesus said that he would not send his son with them, the young man said: "Father, in the past the finest and noblest part was ours: to go out again and again to war and to the hunt and to win a good name. But now you have shut me out of both, though you have never seen any cowardice or lack of spirit in me. How am I supposed to show my face now when I walk to and from the marketplace? What sort of man will the citizens think I am? What sort of man will my new wife think I am? What kind of husband will she think she has been married to? So either let me go to the hunt, or give me reasons and persuade me that things are better for me the way they are now."

38. Croesus answered: "My son, I am not doing this because I have seen any cowardice in you, or anything else unworthy. It is because a vision came and stood by me in a dream as I slept, and told me that you would die young, and that you would be killed by an iron spear-point. Because of this vision I hurried on your marriage, and because of it I now refuse to send you on this business. I am watching over you, to see if I can somehow steal you from your fate at least for as long as I live. For you are, as it happens, my only son: the other one I do not count, since he is deaf."

39. The young man answered: "Father, after a vision like that, no one could blame you for watching over me. But there is something you don't understand, a point in the dream whose meaning you have missed, and it is only right that I explain it to you. You say the dream told you I would die by an iron spear-point. But what hands does a boar have? What iron spear-point is there for you to be afraid of? If the dream had told you I would die by a tusk, or by anything else like that, then you would be right to do what you are doing. But it said 'by a spear-point.' So since our fight will not be against men, let me go."

40. Croesus answered: "My son, you have partly won me over with what you say about the dream. Since you have persuaded me, I will change my mind and let you go to the hunt."

41. Having said this, Croesus sent for Adrastus the Phrygian, and when he came Croesus said to him: "Adrastus, when you were struck by a terrible misfortune (and I do not hold it against you), I purified you, and I took you into my house and have paid for everything you need. Now, since you received kindness from me first, you owe me kindness in return. So I ask you to be the guardian of my son as he goes out to the hunt, in case some wicked robbers come upon you on the road to do you harm. And besides, you ought to go where you can win fame by your deeds yourself. It is your inheritance from your fathers to do so, and you have the strength for it too."

42. Adrastus answered: "My king, if it were not for this I would not be going to any contest of courage like this one. In the first place, it is not right for a man who has suffered such a misfortune as mine to seek the company of men his age who are prospering. In the second place, I have no wish to, and for many reasons I would have kept away. But now, since you urge me and I ought to please you (for I owe you kindness in return), I am ready to do it. So you may expect your son, whom you tell me to guard, to come home to you unharmed, as far as it depends on his guardian to keep him safe."

43. After Adrastus had answered Croesus with words like these, they set out, supplied with picked young men and with dogs. When they reached Mount Olympus they tracked the animal down. They found it, took their stand around it in a circle, and began hurling their spears at it. Then the guest, the man who had been purified of killing, whose name was Adrastus, threw his spear at the boar, missed it, and hit Croesus' son. So the young man was struck by the spear-point and fulfilled what the dream had foretold. Someone ran to tell Croesus what had happened, and when he reached Sardis he told him about the fight and about his son's fate.

44. Croesus was terribly shaken by his son's death, and he took it all the harder because his son had been killed by the very man he himself had purified of killing. Overwhelmed by the disaster, he called on Zeus the Purifier, calling him to witness what he had suffered at the hands of his guest. He called on the same god as Protector of Suppliants and as Guardian of Friendship too. He called him Protector of Suppliants because when he took the guest into his house he had, without knowing it, been sheltering his son's killer; and Guardian of Friendship because he had sent the man as a guardian and found him his worst enemy.

45. After this the Lydians arrived carrying the body, and behind it came the killer. He took his stand in front of the body and gave himself up to Croesus. He held out his hands and begged the king to kill him over the body. He spoke of his earlier misfortune, and said that now, on top of it, he had destroyed the man who had purified him of it, and that life was no longer worth living for him. When Croesus heard this he took pity on Adrastus, even though he was suffering so great a sorrow of his own, and said to him: "My friend, I have already had from you all the justice I am owed, since you condemn yourself to death. You are not the cause of this disaster, except in so far as you were its instrument, against your will. The cause is one of the gods, I suppose, the same one who warned me long ago of what was going to happen." So Croesus buried his son with proper rites. But Adrastus son of Gordias son of Midas, the man who had killed his own brother and had now killed the son of the man who purified him, waited until everyone had gone and the tomb was quiet. Then, knowing himself to be the most unfortunate of all the men he had ever heard of, he killed himself on the grave.

46. For two years after the loss of his son Croesus sat quietly in mourning. But after that, two things made Croesus put an end to his mourning: the overthrow of the rule of Astyages son of Cyaxares by Cyrus son of Cambyses, and the growing greatness of the Persians. Now he began to think how he might cut short the power of the Persians, if he could find any way, while it was still growing and before they became great.

With this plan in mind he set out at once to test the oracles, both the Greek ones and the one in Libya (as the Greeks call Africa). He sent messengers in different directions: some to Delphi, some to Abae in Phocis, some to Dodona. Some were sent to the shrine of the hero Amphiaraus and to the shrine of Trophonius, and others to Branchidae in the territory of Miletus. These are the Greek oracles Croesus sent to for prophecies. Others he sent to ask at the shrine of Ammon in Libya. He was sending the messengers out to test the oracles and to find out what they really knew. Then, if they turned out to know the truth, he would send a second time to ask whether he should go to war against the Persians.

47. He gave the Lydians he sent to test the oracles these instructions. Starting from the day they set out from Sardis, they were to count the days, and on the hundredth day they were to consult the oracles. They were to ask what Croesus son of Alyattes, king of the Lydians, happened to be doing at that moment. Whatever each oracle answered, they were to have it written down and bring it back to him. What the other oracles answered nobody reports. But at Delphi, the moment the Lydians entered the temple to consult the god and asked the question they had been told to ask, the Pythia spoke these words in hexameter verse:

	I know the number of the grains of sand and the measure of the sea;
	I understand the mute, and I hear the one who does not speak.
	There has come to my senses the smell of a hard-shelled tortoise
	boiling in a bronze cauldron, with the flesh of a lamb mixed in;
	bronze lies beneath it, and bronze is laid over it like a cloak.

48. When the Pythia had spoken this oracle, the Lydians had the prophecy written down and went straight back to Sardis. When the others who had been sent around arrived with the answers of the oracles, Croesus unrolled the writings one by one and looked them over. At first none of them pleased him. But when he heard the one from Delphi, he immediately bowed in worship to the god and accepted the answer. He decided that the oracle at Delphi was the only true one, because it had found out what he himself had been doing. For after he had sent his messengers out to the various oracles to consult the gods, he kept the appointed day carefully in mind and came up with this trick. He thought of something it would be impossible for anyone to discover or to guess: he cut up a tortoise and a lamb and boiled them together himself in a bronze cauldron, with a bronze lid on top.

49. That, then, was the answer Croesus received from Delphi. As for the answer of Amphiaraus, I cannot say what he told the Lydians after they had performed the customary rites at his temple. There is no record of it, any more than of the others, except that Croesus believed that his oracle was a true one too.

50. After this Croesus tried to win the favor of the god at Delphi with great sacrifices. Of every kind of animal fit for sacrifice he offered three thousand. He heaped up couches plated with gold and silver, cups of gold, and purple robes and tunics, and made a great pyre of them and burned it, hoping this would win the god even more to the Lydians' side. And he proclaimed that every Lydian should sacrifice whatever he had. When he had finished the sacrifice, he melted down a vast quantity of gold and cast it into ingots, each eighteen inches long, nine inches wide and three inches high. There were one hundred and seventeen of them. Four were of pure gold, weighing two and a half talents each (about 140 pounds apiece). The others were of gold mixed with silver, weighing two talents each (about 115 pounds). He also had a lion made of pure gold, weighing ten talents (about 570 pounds). When the temple at Delphi burned down, this lion fell off the ingots, since it had been set on top of them. It now stands in the treasury of the Corinthians and weighs six and a half talents (about 370 pounds), because three and a half talents (about 200 pounds) of it melted away.

51. When Croesus had finished all these things he sent them to Delphi, and these other gifts along with them. There were two enormous mixing bowls, one of gold and the other of silver. The gold one stood on the right as you enter the temple and the silver one on the left. These too were moved after the temple burned down. The gold bowl now stands in the treasury of the people of Clazomenae, and it weighs eight and a half talents and twelve pounds more (close to 500 pounds in all). The silver one stands in the corner of the temple's front hall. It holds six hundred amphoras, about 6,000 gallons, and the Delphians fill it with wine at the feast of the Theophania. The people of Delphi say it is the work of Theodorus of Samos, and I think they are right, because the workmanship is clearly out of the ordinary. Croesus also sent four silver wine jars, which stand in the treasury of the Corinthians, and two basins for holy water, one of gold and the other of silver. The gold one is inscribed "From the Spartans," and the Spartans say it is their offering. But they are not right about that. This one too is from Croesus, but one of the Delphians wrote the inscription on it because he wanted to please the Spartans. I know his name, but I will not mention it. The statue of a boy through whose hand the water flows is from the Spartans, but neither of the basins is. Croesus sent many other offerings with these that were not specially marked out. Among them are some round silver castings, and also a gold figure of a woman four and a half feet tall, which the Delphians say is a statue of Croesus' baker. Croesus also dedicated the necklaces from his wife's neck and her belts.

52. Those are the things he sent to Delphi. To Amphiaraus, having heard of his courage and of his terrible fate (he was the seer who rode against Thebes and was swallowed up by the earth), Croesus dedicated a shield made entirely of gold, and a spear all of solid gold, the shaft as well as both points. Both of these were still there in my own time, at Thebes, in the temple of Ismenian Apollo.

53. Croesus told the Lydians who were to carry these gifts to the temples to ask the oracles another question as well: should Croesus go to war against the Persians, and if so, should he add an army of allies to his own? When the Lydians reached the places they had been sent to and had dedicated the offerings, they consulted the oracles and said: "Croesus, king of the Lydians and of other nations, believes that yours are the only true oracles in the world. He has given you gifts worthy of your discoveries, and now he asks you whether he should go to war against the Persians, and if so, whether he should add an army of men to his own as allies." That was their question, and the answers of both oracles agreed. Both told Croesus that if he went to war against the Persians, he would destroy a great empire. And they advised him to find out which Greeks were the most powerful and make them his friends.

54. When the answers were brought back and Croesus heard them, he was delighted with the oracles. Fully expecting to destroy the kingdom of Cyrus, he sent to Pytho (the old name for Delphi) once again. He found out how many men of Delphi there were and gave each of them two gold staters. In return the Delphians gave Croesus and the Lydians the right to consult the oracle first, freedom from all fees, and front seats at the games. They also gave them a privilege for all time: any of them who wished could become a citizen of Delphi.

55. After making these presents to the men of Delphi, Croesus consulted the oracle a third time. Once he had learned that the oracle told the truth, he used it again and again. This time he asked whether his kingdom would last a long time. The Pythia gave him this answer:

	But when the day comes that a mule is king of the Medes,
	then, soft-footed Lydian, run by the pebbly Hermus;
	run and do not stay, and do not be ashamed to be called a coward.

56. When these lines reached him, Croesus was more pleased with them than with all the rest. He supposed that a mule would never rule the Medes in place of a man, and that he and his descendants would therefore never lose their power. After this he set about finding out which of the Greeks he should count as the most powerful and win over as his friends. When he looked into it, he found that the Spartans and the Athenians were the leaders, the Spartans of the Dorian people and the Athenians of the Ionian. These were the two most distinguished peoples in ancient times: the Ionians were originally Pelasgian (from the people who lived in Greece before the Greeks), and the Dorians Greek. The Ionian people never moved from their land in any direction. The Dorian people, on the other hand, were great wanderers. In the reign of Deucalion they lived in Phthiotis. In the time of Dorus son of Hellen they lived in the land below Mount Ossa and Mount Olympus, which is called Histiaeotis. When the sons of Cadmus drove them out of Histiaeotis, they settled in the Pindus mountains and were called Macednians. From there they moved again to Dryopis, and from Dryopis they came at last to the Peloponnese, where they began to be called Dorians.

57. What language the Pelasgians used to speak I can't say for certain. But if we must judge by the Pelasgians who are still left, we can draw a conclusion. There are the Pelasgians of the city of Creston, above the Tyrrhenians, who were once neighbors of the people now called Dorians and lived at that time in the land now called Thessaliotis. There are the Pelasgians who settled at Placia and Scylace on the Hellespont, who had earlier lived alongside the Athenians. And there are the people of the various other towns that really are Pelasgian, though they have lost the name. If we must judge by these, the Pelasgians used to speak a non-Greek language. So if the whole Pelasgian people was like these, then the people of Attica, being Pelasgian, must have unlearned their language at the same time as they changed and became Greek. For the people of Creston do not speak the same language as any of their neighbors, and neither do the people of Placia; but they speak the same language as each other. This proves that they still keep the form of language they brought with them when they moved to these places.

58. As for the Greek people, it seems clear to me that they have always used the same language since they first began. They split off from the Pelasgian people and were weak at first. But from that small beginning they have grown into the great number of peoples we see today, chiefly because many non-Greek peoples have joined them. And I think it is also true of the Pelasgian people that as long as they remained non-Greek, they never grew much.

59. Of these peoples, then, Croesus learned that the Athenians were held down and torn apart by faction under Pisistratus son of Hippocrates, who was tyrant of Athens at that time. A great wonder had happened to Hippocrates when, as a private citizen, he went to watch the Olympic games. After he had offered sacrifice, the cauldrons standing on the hearth, full of meat and water, boiled over without any fire under them. Chilon the Spartan happened to be there and saw the wonder. He advised Hippocrates, first, not to take a wife into his house to bear him children; second, if he had one already, to send her away; and if he happened to have a son, to disown him. Hippocrates, they say, would not take Chilon's advice, and afterward this Pisistratus was born to him. At Athens there was a feud between the people of the coast and the people of the plain. Megacles son of Alcmaeon led the coast faction, and Lycurgus son of Aristolaides led the faction of the plain. Pisistratus, aiming at tyranny for himself, raised a third party. He gathered supporters, called himself the leader of the hill-country men, and came up with this trick. He wounded himself and his mules, and then drove his cart into the marketplace as though he had just escaped from his enemies, who, he claimed, had tried to kill him as he was driving out to the country. He asked the people to give him a bodyguard. Before this he had won a reputation as a commander in the war against the Megarians, when he captured Nisaea and did other great services. The Athenian people were taken in and gave him a band of men picked from the citizens. They became not his spearmen but his club-bearers, because they followed him around carrying wooden clubs. These men rose up with Pisistratus and seized the Acropolis. After that Pisistratus ruled the Athenians. He did not upset the existing officials or change the old laws. He ran the city under the constitution that was already in place, and he governed it fairly and well.

60. But not long after this the followers of Megacles and those of Lycurgus joined forces and drove him out. That was how Pisistratus took Athens the first time, and how he lost power before it had taken firm root. But the men who had driven him out soon fell to feuding with one another again. Harassed by the party strife, Megacles sent a message to Pisistratus asking whether he would take his daughter as his wife, on condition that he would become tyrant. Pisistratus accepted the offer and agreed to the terms. Then, to bring him back, they worked out the silliest scheme ever practiced, as I think, by far. After all, the Greeks had been set apart from non-Greeks long ago as the cleverer people, further removed from foolish simplicity; and this was done among the Athenians, who are counted the first of the Greeks in intelligence. In the village of Paeania there was a woman named Phya. She was almost six feet tall (four cubits less three finger-widths), and good-looking as well. They dressed this woman in a full suit of armor, put her up in a chariot, and showed her the pose that would make her look most like the part. Then they drove to the city. They had sent heralds running ahead, and when the heralds reached the city they said what they had been told to say: "Athenians, welcome Pisistratus with goodwill. Athena herself has honored him above all men and is bringing him home to her Acropolis." So the heralds went all over the place saying this. At once a report spread to the villages of the countryside that Athena was bringing Pisistratus back, and meanwhile the people in the city, convinced that the woman was the goddess herself, were worshipping this human being and welcoming Pisistratus.

61. Having got back the tyranny in this way, Pisistratus married Megacles' daughter, as he had agreed with Megacles. But he already had sons who were grown men, and the descendants of Alcmaeon were said to be under a curse. Since he did not want to have children by his new wife, he had sex with her in an unnatural way. At first the woman kept this to herself, but later she told her mother, whether because her mother asked or not I cannot say, and the mother told her husband Megacles. Megacles was furious that Pisistratus had dishonored him, and in his anger he immediately went and made up his quarrel with the men of the other faction. When Pisistratus learned what was being done against him, he left the country altogether and went to Eretria, where he talked things over with his sons. Hippias' advice won out: they should try to win back the tyranny. So they began to collect gifts of money from the cities that owed them favors. Many gave large sums, but the Thebans gave more money than all the rest. Then, to make a long story short, time passed, and at last everything was ready for their return. Some Argives came from the Peloponnese as hired soldiers, and a man of Naxos named Lygdamis had come to them of his own accord and showed great zeal in supplying both money and men.

62. So they set out from Eretria and came back after ten years. The first place they seized in Attica was Marathon. While they were camped there, their supporters from the city came to join them, and others streamed in from the villages, people who preferred tyranny to freedom. So these were gathering. As for the Athenians in the city, they had thought nothing of it as long as Pisistratus was collecting money, and nothing of it even after he took Marathon. But when they heard that he was marching from Marathon toward the city, they went out to stop him. So they were marching out in full force to fight the returning exiles. Meanwhile Pisistratus' forces had set out from Marathon toward the city, and the two armies met at the temple of Athena Pallenis, where they camped facing each other. Then, guided by the gods, Amphilytus the Acarnanian, a seer, came to Pisistratus. He came up to him and spoke an oracle in hexameter verse:

	The cast is made, and the net is spread out wide;
	the tunnies will dart through it in the moonlit night.

63. He spoke this oracle under divine inspiration. Pisistratus understood it, said he accepted the prophecy, and led his army against the enemy. The Athenians from the city were busy with their morning meal just then, and after eating some of them had turned to dice and others to sleep. Pisistratus' forces fell on them and routed them. As they fled, Pisistratus came up with a very clever plan to keep the Athenians from gathering together again and to keep them scattered. He put his sons on horseback and sent them on ahead. They caught up with the men who were running away and said what Pisistratus had told them to say: that they should not be afraid, and that each man should go home.

64. The Athenians did just that. So Pisistratus took Athens for the third time, and this time he rooted his tyranny firmly. He did it with many hired foreign soldiers and with a large income of money, which came partly from Attica itself and partly from the region around the river Strymon. He also took as hostages the sons of the Athenians who had stayed and had not fled at once, and put them on Naxos; for Pisistratus had conquered Naxos too in war and handed it over to Lygdamis. Besides this, he purified the island of Delos as the oracles commanded. This is how he purified it: as far as one could see from the temple, he dug up all the dead who were buried in that area and moved them to another part of Delos. So Pisistratus was tyrant of Athens. Of the Athenians, some had fallen in the battle, and others had gone into exile from their native land with the sons of Alcmaeon.
