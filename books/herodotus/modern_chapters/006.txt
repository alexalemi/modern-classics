Chapter 6: The Customs of the Persians, the Greeks of Asia, and the Revolt of Pactyes

131. These are the customs the Persians follow, as far as I know them. They do not think it right to set up statues of the gods, or temples, or altars. In fact they say that people who do these things are fools. The reason, as it seems to me, is that they do not believe the gods have human form, as the Greeks do. Their custom is to go up to the highest mountains and offer sacrifices to Zeus there, and the whole circle of the sky is what they call Zeus. They also sacrifice to the Sun and the Moon and the Earth, to Fire and to Water and to the Winds. These are the only gods they have sacrificed to from the very beginning. But they have also learned to sacrifice to Aphrodite Urania, and they learned it from the Assyrians and the Arabians. The Assyrians call Aphrodite Mylitta, the Arabians call her Alitta, and the Persians call her Mitra.

132. Now this is the way the Persians are accustomed to sacrifice to the gods I have named. They build no altars and they light no fire. When they mean to sacrifice, they use no libation, no music of the pipe, no garlands and no barley-meal for sprinkling. Instead, when a man wants to sacrifice to any one of the gods, he leads the animal to a clean place, one free of any pollution, and calls on the god, usually with a wreath of myrtle wound around his tiara, the soft cap the Persians wear. The man who sacrifices is not allowed to pray for good things for himself alone. He prays that all will be well with all the Persians and with the king, since he himself is of course counted among all the Persians. When he has cut the victim up into pieces and boiled the meat, he spreads out a bed of the freshest grass, clover above all, and lays all the pieces of meat on it right away. Once he has laid them out in order, a Magus, one of the Magi, the Persian priestly caste, stands beside them and chants a song over them about the birth of the gods (that, they say, is what their chant is), since it is against their law to sacrifice without a Magus. Then, after waiting a short time, the man who made the sacrifice carries the meat away and uses it for whatever he likes.

133. Of all the days of the year, each of them most honors the day he was born. On that day he thinks it right to serve a bigger feast than on other days. At this feast the richer Persians set on the table an ox or a horse or a camel or a donkey, roasted whole in an oven, while the poor set out smaller animals, cooked the same way. They eat few main dishes but many desserts, and these are not served all at once but one after another. That is why the Persians say that the Greeks get up from dinner still hungry: after dinner the Greeks are served nothing worth mentioning in the way of dessert, whereas if they were served a good dessert they would not stop eating so soon. The Persians are very fond of wine. It is not allowed for a man to vomit or to urinate in front of anyone else, and that is how they guard against these things. It is also their custom to discuss their most important business while they are drinking heavily. Whatever they decide in that discussion, the master of the house where they happen to be meeting puts before them again the next day, when they are sober. If they still like the decision when they are sober, they act on it; if they do not, they drop it. And whatever they discuss first when they are sober, they consider again when they are drunk.

134. When they meet each other in the street, you can tell whether the two who meet are of equal rank, because instead of greeting each other with words, equals kiss each other on the mouth. If one of them is a little lower in rank than the other, they kiss on the cheek. If one is of much humbler birth than the other, he throws himself down on the ground before him and bows in worship. After themselves, they honor most the nations that live nearest to them, then the ones that live next nearest, and so on, giving honor in proportion to distance. They honor least the people who live farthest away from them, since they think that they themselves are by far the best of all human beings in every way, that others have a share of merit in the proportion I have just described, and that those who live farthest from them are the worst. Under the rule of the Medes the different nations also governed one another by the same rule the Persians follow in giving honor. The Medes ruled over all of them, and directly over those who lived nearest to them; these in turn ruled over their own neighbors, and those again over the nations next to them. So the Median people extended their power step by step, from ruling directly to ruling through others.

135. The Persians take up foreign customs more readily than anyone else in the world. They wear Median clothes, because they think them better-looking than their own, and in war they wear the Egyptian breastplate. They also take up every kind of luxury as soon as they hear of it, and in particular they have learned from the Greeks to have sex with boys. Each of them marries several lawful wives, and they keep many more concubines besides.

136. Next to being brave in battle, the mark of a real man is to be able to show many sons, and every year the king sends gifts to the man who has the most, since they think that numbers are strength. They educate their sons from the age of five to the age of twenty in three things only: riding, shooting with the bow, and telling the truth. Before a boy is five he never comes into his father's sight, but lives with the women. This is done so that if the child dies while he is being raised, his death will cause his father no grief.

137. I approve of this custom of theirs, and also of the next one I am going to mention. The king himself does not put anyone to death for a single offense, and no other Persian does any harm that cannot be undone to any of his own servants for a single offense either. Only if he weighs things up and finds that the wrongs are more numerous and greater than the services does he let his anger loose. They also say that no one has ever killed his own father or mother. Whenever anything of that kind has been done, they say, it will always turn out on inquiry that the killer was a changeling or the child of adultery. For it is not reasonable, they say, that a true parent should be killed by his own son.

138. Whatever they are not allowed to do, they are not allowed even to talk about. The most shameful thing of all, in their view, is to tell a lie, and the next most shameful is to owe money. They have many reasons for this, but the chief one is that a man who owes money, they say, is bound to tell lies sometimes too. Any citizen who has leprosy, or the disease that turns the skin white, may not enter a city or mix with the other Persians. They say he has these diseases because he has sinned against the Sun in some way. A foreigner who catches these diseases is driven out of the country altogether in many parts of it, and so are white doves, which they charge with the same offense. They never urinate or spit into a river, and they do not wash their hands in one or let anyone else do these things. They hold rivers in very great reverence.

139. Something else is also true of them, which the Persians themselves have never noticed, but I have. Their names, which describe their bodies or their high position, all end in the same letter: the letter the Dorians call san and the Ionians call sigma, our s. If you look into it, you will find that every Persian name ends in this letter. Not some in this one and others in other letters, but all alike.

140. All this I can say for certain from my own knowledge. But what follows about their dead is told as a secret mystery and not openly: that the body of a Persian man is not buried until it has been torn by a bird or a dog. (I know for certain that the Magi do this, because they do it openly.) Be that as it may, the Persians coat the body in wax and then bury it in the ground. The Magi are very different from other men, and from the priests in Egypt in particular. The Egyptian priests make it a point of purity to kill nothing alive except the animals they sacrifice. But the Magi kill every kind of creature with their own hands, except dogs and human beings. They even make a great point of it, killing ants and snakes and every other creature that crawls or flies. As for this custom, let it stay as it was established from the beginning. Now I will go back to my earlier story.

141. As soon as the Lydians had been conquered by the Persians, the Ionians and the Aeolians sent messengers to Cyrus at Sardis. They wanted to be his subjects on the same terms as they had been subjects of Croesus. When Cyrus heard what they proposed, he told them a fable. A piper, he said, saw some fish in the sea and played his pipe to them, thinking they would come out onto the land. When his hopes came to nothing, he took a casting net, caught a great many of the fish in it, and hauled them out of the water. And when he saw them jumping about, he said to the fish: "You can stop dancing now. You wouldn't come out and dance before, when I piped to you." Cyrus told the Ionians and Aeolians this fable for a reason. Earlier, when Cyrus himself had sent a messenger asking the Ionians to revolt from Croesus, they had refused; and now, when the conquest was over, they were ready to obey Cyrus. So he spoke to them in anger. When the Ionians heard this answer brought back to their cities, each of them built a wall around itself, and they met together at the Panionium, the shrine of all the Ionians, all except the people of Miletus. With the Milesians alone Cyrus had sworn a treaty on the same terms the Lydians had granted them. The rest of the Ionians decided together to send messengers to Sparta, to ask the Spartans to help the Ionians.

142. These Ionians, the ones the Panionium belongs to, happen to have built their cities in the finest climate and the best seasons of any people we know. For no land, whether north of Ionia or south of it, east of it or west, does as well as Ionia itself. The lands in one direction suffer from cold and wet, and those in the other from heat and drought. These Ionians do not all speak the same language; they have four different dialects. The southernmost of their cities is Miletus, and next to it come Myus and Priene. These were settled in Caria, and they speak the same dialect as each other. The following cities are in Lydia: Ephesus, Colophon, Lebedus, Teos, Clazomenae and Phocaea. These cities speak nothing like the ones I just mentioned, but they agree with one another. There are three more Ionian cities left. Two of them are on islands, Samos and Chios, and one, Erythrae, is built on the mainland. The people of Chios and Erythrae speak the same dialect, but the Samians have one all their own. So that makes four separate dialects.

143. Of these Ionians, then, the Milesians were sheltered from danger, since they had sworn a treaty. The islanders had nothing to fear, because the Phoenicians were not yet subjects of the Persians, and the Persians themselves were not seafarers. Now these twelve cities had split off from the rest of the Ionians for no other reason than this. The whole Greek nation was weak at that time, but of all its peoples the Ionians were by far the weakest and the least regarded; apart from Athens they did not have a single city of any importance. The other Ionians, and the Athenians among them, avoided the name and did not want to be called Ionians; even now, I notice, most of them seem ashamed of it. But these twelve cities took pride in the name. They set up a temple of their own, which they named the Panionium, and they decided not to let any other Ionians share in it. (Nor did any others ask to share in it, except the people of Smyrna.)

144. In the same way, the Dorians of the district that is now called the Five Cities, though it used to be called the Six Cities, take care not to admit any of the neighboring Dorians to their temple at Triopium. They even shut out those of their own number who break the temple's rules. For example, at the games of Triopian Apollo they used to give bronze tripods as prizes to the winners, and the rule was that the winners must not carry them out of the temple, but must dedicate them to the god then and there. Then there was a man from Halicarnassus named Agasicles. When he won, he paid no attention to this rule. He carried the tripod off to his own house and hung it up there on a nail. For this the other five cities, Lindus, Ialysus and Camirus, Cos and Cnidus, shut the sixth city, Halicarnassus, out of any share in the temple.

145. That was the penalty they laid on the Halicarnassians. As for the Ionians, I think the reason they made themselves twelve cities and would not take any more into their league is this. When they lived in the Peloponnese, they were divided into twelve parts, just as the Achaeans, who drove the Ionians out, are divided into twelve parts now. Starting from the side nearest Sicyon, first comes Pellene, then Aegira, and Aegae, which has the river Crathis, a river that never runs dry (the river of the same name in Italy was named after it), and Bura, and Helice, where the Ionians fled for refuge when the Achaeans beat them in battle, and Aegium, and Rhypes, and Patrae, and Pharae, and Olenus, where the great river Peirus is, and Dyme, and Tritaea, the only one of them that lies inland. These are now the twelve divisions of the Achaeans, and in the old days they were the divisions of the Ionians.

146. That is why the Ionians, too, made themselves twelve cities. For it would be sheer folly to say that these are any more Ionian than the other Ionians, or that they come of any nobler descent. A large part of them are Abantians from Euboea, who do not even share the name of Ionia. Minyans of Orchomenus are mixed in with them, and Cadmeans, and Dryopians, and Phocians who broke away from their own state, and Molossians, and Pelasgians from Arcadia, and Dorians from Epidaurus, and many other peoples besides. And those who set out to found their colonies from the Prytaneum, the town hall of Athens, and who think themselves the noblest-born of the Ionians, brought no women with them to their new home. Instead they took Carian women, after killing their parents. Because of this slaughter the women made a rule for themselves, bound one another by oaths to keep it, and passed it on to their daughters: they would never eat with their husbands, and no wife would ever call her own husband by his name. The reason was that these men had killed their fathers and husbands and children, and then, after doing that, had taken them as wives. This happened at Miletus.

147. Some of them, too, chose Lycian kings to rule them, descendants of Glaucus and Hippolochus, while others were ruled by Cauconians from Pylos, descendants of Codrus son of Melanthus, and others again by kings from both families together. Still, since these twelve cities cling to the name more than the other Ionians, let them be called, if they like, the Ionians of truly pure descent. But in fact all are Ionians who are descended from Athens and keep the festival of the Apaturia. And all of them keep it except the people of Ephesus and Colophon. These alone of all the Ionians do not keep the Apaturia, and their reason is a certain murder.

148. The Panionium is a holy place on the north side of Mycale, set apart by the common agreement of the Ionians for Poseidon of Helice. Mycale is a headland of the mainland that runs out westward toward Samos. The Ionians used to gather there from their cities and hold a festival they called the Panionia. (And not only the festivals of the Ionians but those of all the Greeks alike follow the same rule: their names all end in the same letter, just like the names of the Persians.)

Those, then, are the Ionian cities.

149. The cities of Aeolia are these: Cyme, which is called Phriconis, Larisa, Neonteichus, Temnus, Cilla, Notium, Aegiroessa, Pitane, Aegaeae, Myrina and Gryneia. These are the ancient cities of the Aeolians, eleven in number, since one of them, Smyrna, was taken away from them by the Ionians. For these cities, the ones on the mainland, used to number twelve as well. These Aeolians happened to settle in a land that is more fertile than Ionia but does not have as good a climate.

150. This is how the Aeolians lost Smyrna. Some men of Colophon had lost out in a struggle between factions and been driven from their city, and the people of Smyrna took them in as refugees. Later these Colophonian exiles waited for a time when the people of Smyrna were holding a festival for Dionysus outside the walls. Then they shut the gates against them and took the city for themselves. After this, when all the Aeolians came to the rescue, the two sides made an agreement: the Ionians would hand over everything that could be carried away, and on that condition the Aeolians would give up Smyrna. When the people of Smyrna had done this, the other eleven cities divided them among themselves and made them their own citizens.

151. These, then, are the Aeolian cities on the mainland, apart from those on Mount Ida, which are separate from the rest. As for those on the islands, there are five on Lesbos. The sixth city on Lesbos, Arisba, was enslaved by the people of Methymna, even though its citizens were of the same blood as they were. There is one city on Tenedos, and another in what are called the Hundred Isles. Now the people of Lesbos and Tenedos, like the Ionians who lived on islands, had nothing to fear. But the rest of the cities agreed together to follow the Ionians wherever they led.

152. When the messengers from the Ionians and Aeolians reached Sparta (for all this was done in a hurry), they chose a Phocaean named Pythermus to speak for all of them. He put on a purple cloak, so that as many Spartans as possible would hear about it and come to listen. Then he was brought before the assembly, and he made a long speech asking the Spartans to help them. But the Spartans would not listen to him. On the contrary, they decided not to help the Ionians. So the messengers went away. But even though they had sent the Ionians' messengers away, the Spartans still sent some men off in a fifty-oared ship, to find out, as I suppose, how things stood with Cyrus and with Ionia. When these men reached Phocaea, they sent the most respected man among them, whose name was Lacrines, to Sardis, to give Cyrus the Spartans' message: he was to do no harm to any city in Greece, because they would not allow it.

153. When the herald had said this, Cyrus is said to have asked the Greeks he had with him who these Spartans were, and how many of them there were, that they dared make such a declaration to him. When he heard their answer, he said to the Spartan herald: "I have never yet been afraid of men like these, who have a place set aside in the middle of their city where they get together and cheat each other with false oaths. If I stay healthy, they will have their own troubles to talk about, and not the troubles of the Ionians." Cyrus threw out these words as an insult to all the Greeks, because they have marketplaces and buy and sell in them. The Persians themselves do not use markets, and they have no marketplace at all. After this Cyrus put Sardis in the charge of a Persian named Tabalus, and gave a Lydian named Pactyes the job of looking after the gold of Croesus and of the other Lydians. Then he marched off himself to Ecbatana, taking Croesus with him, and for the moment took no account of the Ionians. For Babylon still stood in his way, and so did the Bactrian nation, the Sacae and the Egyptians. He meant to lead the campaigns against these himself, and to send some other general against the Ionians.

154. But when Cyrus had marched away from Sardis, Pactyes led the Lydians in a revolt against Tabalus and against Cyrus. He went down to the coast, and since he had all the gold that had been in Sardis, he hired mercenaries and persuaded the people of the coast to march with him. So he marched on Sardis and besieged Tabalus, who had shut himself up in the citadel.

155. Cyrus heard about this on his march, and he said to Croesus: "Croesus, where will all this end? It looks as if the Lydians will never stop making trouble for me and having trouble themselves. I wonder whether it would be best to sell them all as slaves. As it is, I think I have acted like a man who kills the father and then spares the sons. That is just what I have done: I took you prisoner, and I am carrying you away, you who were much more than a father to the Lydians, and I handed the Lydians back their city. And after that, can I be surprised that they have revolted from me?" That is how he said what was on his mind. But Croesus was afraid that Cyrus would destroy Sardis, and he answered: "My king, what you say is reasonable. But do not give way to your anger completely, and do not destroy an ancient city that is innocent both of what happened before and of what is happening now. As for what happened before, I did it, and I carry the consequences on my own head. As for what is happening now, the wrongdoer is Pactyes, the man you put in charge of Sardis, so let him pay the penalty. But pardon the Lydians, and give them these orders, so that they will not revolt or be a danger to you. Send word forbidding them to own weapons of war. Tell them instead to wear tunics under their cloaks and soft high boots on their feet, and order them to teach their sons to play the lyre and the harp and to keep shops. Soon, my king, you will see that they have become women instead of men, and there will be no fear that they will revolt from you."

156. Croesus suggested this to him because he saw that it was better for the Lydians than being made slaves and sold. He knew that if he did not offer a good enough reason, he would not persuade Cyrus to change his mind. And he was afraid that at some later time, if the Lydians escaped the present danger, they might revolt from the Persians and be destroyed. Cyrus was very pleased with the suggestion. His anger cooled, and he said he agreed with Croesus' advice. Then he called for Mazares, a Mede, and ordered him to proclaim to the Lydians what Croesus had suggested. He also ordered him to sell as slaves all the others who had joined the Lydians in the march on Sardis, and above all, whatever it took, to bring Pactyes himself to Cyrus alive.

157. Cyrus gave these orders on the road and then marched on to the Persians' homeland. But when Pactyes heard that an army was coming to fight him, he was terrified and fled at once to Cyme. Then Mazares the Mede marched on Sardis with a part of Cyrus' army. He no longer found Pactyes or his followers at Sardis, so first he forced the Lydians to carry out Cyrus' orders, and by his orders the Lydians changed their whole way of life. After this Mazares sent messengers to Cyme, ordering the people to hand over Pactyes. The people of Cyme decided to refer the question of what to do to the god at Branchidae. There was an oracle there, set up long ago, which all the Ionians and Aeolians used to consult. The place is in the territory of Miletus, above the harbor of Panormus.

158. So the people of Cyme sent messengers to Branchidae to consult the god, and they asked what they should do about Pactyes in order to please the gods. When they asked, the answer they were given was that they should hand Pactyes over to the Persians. When they heard this answer reported, the people of Cyme were ready to hand him over. But when most of the people were ready to do it, Aristodicus son of Heraclides, a man of standing among the citizens, stopped them. He did not trust the answer, and he thought the messengers who had gone to consult the oracle were not telling the truth. In the end other messengers were sent to the oracle to ask a second time about Pactyes, and Aristodicus was one of them.

159. When they reached Branchidae, Aristodicus stepped forward from the others and put this question to the oracle: "Lord, Pactyes the Lydian came to us as a suppliant, asking for protection, running from a violent death at the hands of the Persians. The Persians are demanding him, and they order the people of Cyme to give him up. But although we are afraid of the power of the Persians, we have not yet dared to hand the suppliant over to them, not until your advice is made clear to us, telling us which of the two things we ought to do." That was his question, but the god gave them the same answer again, telling them to hand Pactyes over to the Persians. At this Aristodicus deliberately did the following. He went all around the temple, destroying the nests of the sparrows and of all the other kinds of birds that had hatched their young on the temple. While he was doing this, it is said, a voice came out of the inner shrine, speaking to Aristodicus: "You most wicked of men, how dare you do this? Are you dragging my suppliants by force out of my temple?" And Aristodicus, they say, was not at a loss at all, and answered: "Lord, is this how you come to the help of your own suppliants, while you tell the people of Cyme to hand over theirs?" And the god answered him again: "Yes, I do tell you to, so that you will be destroyed all the sooner for your wickedness, and so that you will never again come to the oracle to ask about handing over suppliants."

160. When the people of Cyme heard this reply reported, they did not want to be destroyed for handing him over, or to be besieged for keeping him. So they sent him away to Mytilene. But when Mazares sent messages to the Mytilenians, they got ready to hand Pactyes over for a price. What the price was I can't say for certain, because the deal was never completed. For when the people of Cyme learned what the Mytilenians were doing, they sent a ship to Lesbos and carried Pactyes off to Chios. There he was dragged by force out of the temple of Athena Poliuchus, Athena Guardian of the City, by the Chians, and handed over. The Chians handed him over and got Atarneus in return (Atarneus is a district of Mysia, opposite Lesbos). So the Persians took charge of Pactyes and kept him under guard, meaning to bring him before Cyrus. And for a long time afterward, none of the Chians would use barley grown in this district of Atarneus for sprinkling at a sacrifice to any god, or bake sacrificial cakes from the grain that grew there. Everything the land produced was kept out of every kind of sacred rite.

161. So the Chians had handed over Pactyes. After this Mazares marched against those who had joined in besieging Tabalus. First he enslaved the people of Priene. Then he overran the whole plain of the Maeander, letting his army plunder it, and he did the same to Magnesia. And right after this he fell sick and died.
