Chapter 27: Thrace, the Persian Envoys in Macedonia, and Histiaeus Called to Susa

1. Meanwhile the Persians whom Darius had left behind in Europe, under the command of Megabazus, had conquered the people of Perinthus. They were the first of the peoples on the Hellespont to be subdued, because they refused to become Darius' subjects. The Perinthians had been roughly handled once before, in earlier times, by the Paeonians. The Paeonians from the river Strymon had been commanded by an oracle of their god to march against the Perinthians. If the Perinthians, when they were camped facing them, shouted aloud and called them by their name, the Paeonians were to attack; but if they did not shout to them, they were not to attack. And that is what the Paeonians did. The Perinthians camped facing them in the outskirts of their city, and a challenge was made to single combat in three different forms: they matched man against man, horse against horse, and dog against dog. The Perinthians were winning two of the three contests, and in their delight they raised the victory shout "Paean!" The Paeonians guessed that this was the very thing the oracle had spoken of, and no doubt they said to one another, "Now surely the oracle is coming true for us. Now it is time to act." So the Paeonians attacked the Perinthians after they had raised the shout of "Paean!", and they had much the better of the fight and left only a few of them alive.

2. That is what had happened to the Perinthians at the hands of the Paeonians in earlier times. This time, although the Perinthians proved themselves brave men in defense of their freedom, the Persians under Megabazus got the better of them by sheer numbers. After Perinthus had been conquered, Megabazus marched his army through the length of Thrace, forcing every city and every people living there to submit to the king. Those were Darius' orders to him: to subdue Thrace.

3. The Thracians are the most numerous people in the world, after the Indians. If they were ruled by one man, or could agree among themselves, they would be unbeatable in war and by far the strongest of all nations, in my opinion. But this is impossible for them, and there is no way it will ever happen; and that is why they are in fact weak. They have many names, one for each of their tribes in its own region, but they all follow customs that are nearly the same in every respect, except for the Getae, the Trausians, and the people who live north of the Crestonians.

4. Of these, I have already described the practices of the Getae, who believe they are immortal. The Trausians do everything else the same way as the other Thracians, but at a birth and a death they do as follows. When a child is born, its nearest relatives sit around it and mourn over all the troubles it will have to live through, now that it is born, listing every one of the sorrows of human life. But when a man dies, they bury him in the earth with laughter and rejoicing, telling one another how many great troubles he has escaped and how he is now completely happy.

5. The people who live north of the Crestonians do this. Each man has many wives, and when a man dies, there is a great contest among his wives, with their friends working hard on their behalf, over which of them their husband loved best. The one who is judged the winner, and so honored, is first praised by both the men and the women; then her nearest relative cuts her throat over the grave, and she is buried with her husband. The other wives are bitterly upset, because for them this is the greatest disgrace.

6. Among the other Thracians, the custom is to sell their children to be taken abroad. They do not watch over their unmarried girls, but let them have sex with any men they please; their wives, though, they watch very closely. They buy their wives from the women's parents for large sums of money. To be tattooed is considered a mark of noble birth, and to have no tattoos is a sign of low birth. Not working is thought the most honorable thing of all, and working the land the most dishonorable. Living by war and plunder is the most honorable life there is.

7. Those are their most remarkable customs. Of the gods, they worship only Ares, Dionysus, and Artemis. Their kings, however, unlike the rest of the people, worship Hermes above all the gods and swear by him alone, and they say they are descended from Hermes.

8. This is how the rich among them are buried. For three days they lay out the corpse for people to see, and they sacrifice every kind of animal and feast, after first mourning. Then they bury the body, either burning it or putting it in the ground unburned. When they have heaped up a mound over it, they hold games with every kind of contest, and the biggest prizes, as is only reasonable, go to single combat. That is how the Thracians bury their dead.

9. As for the region farther north of this country, no one can say for certain who lives there. But the land just beyond the Danube is known to be empty and enormous. The only people I can hear of who live beyond the Danube are said to be called the Sigynnae, and they dress in the Median fashion. Their horses, it is said, are covered all over with shaggy hair as much as five fingers (nearly four inches) long. The horses are small and snub-nosed and too weak to carry men, but yoked to chariots they are very spirited, so the people of the country drive chariots. This people's territory reaches, it is said, as far as the land of the Eneti, who live on the Adriatic. They say the Sigynnae are colonists from the Medes. How they could have come to be colonists from the Medes I cannot work out for myself, but anything can happen in the long course of time. However that may be, the Ligurians who live inland above Massalia (the Greek city that is now Marseille) call traders "sigynnae," and the people of Cyprus use the same word for spears.

10. The Thracians say that the land on the other side of the Danube is full of bees, and that because of them it is impossible to get through and go any farther. But to me, when they say this, they seem to be saying something unlikely. Bees are known to hate the cold, and it seems to me that it is the cold climate that makes the lands toward the north uninhabitable. These, then, are the stories told about this country. However that may be, Megabazus was now bringing its coastal regions under Persian rule.

11. Meanwhile Darius, as soon as he had crossed the Hellespont and reached Sardis, remembered the service Histiaeus of Miletus had done him and the advice of Coes of Mytilene. He sent for them to come to Sardis and offered them a choice of rewards. Histiaeus, since he was already tyrant of Miletus, did not ask to rule anywhere else in addition. Instead he asked for Myrcinus, a district in the land of the Edonians, because he wanted to found a city there. That was Histiaeus' choice. Coes, who was not a tyrant but an ordinary citizen, asked to be made tyrant of Mytilene.

12. Both men got what they wanted, and each went off to what he had chosen. At this same time it happened that Darius saw something that made him want to order Megabazus to conquer the Paeonians and move them by force out of Europe into Asia. This is what it was. There were two Paeonians named Pigres and Mantyas. After Darius had crossed back into Asia, they came to Sardis, because they wanted to become rulers of the Paeonians themselves, and they brought their sister with them, a tall, good-looking woman. They waited for a time when Darius was sitting in public in the outskirts of the Lydian city. Then they dressed up their sister as well as they could and sent her out to fetch water. She carried a water jar on her head, led a horse behind her by a bridle looped over her arm, and was spinning flax as she went. As the woman passed by him on her way out of the city, Darius took notice, because what she was doing was not the Persian way, nor the Lydian way, nor the custom of any people in Asia. So he sent some of his spearmen and told them to watch what the woman would do with the horse. They followed her. When she reached the river, she watered the horse. After she had watered it and filled her jar, she came back the same way, carrying the water on her head, leading the horse behind her by the bridle looped over her arm, and turning her spindle as she went.

13. Darius was amazed both at what he heard from the men he had sent to watch and at what he saw for himself, and he ordered them to bring her before him. When she was brought, her brothers came too; they had been watching all this from not far off. Darius asked what country she came from, and the young men said that they were Paeonians and that she was their sister. He replied, "Who are these Paeonians, and where on earth do they live?" He also asked them what they wanted, that they had come to Sardis. They told him that they had come to put themselves in his hands; that Paeonia was a country on the river Strymon; that the Strymon was not far from the Hellespont; and finally that they were colonists from the Teucrians of Troy. They told him all of this, point by point. Then he asked whether all the women of their country were as hardworking as their sister. They were very quick to say that yes, they were, because that was the very point of what they had been doing.

14. Then Darius wrote a letter to Megabazus, whom he had left in command of his army in Thrace, ordering him to uproot the Paeonians from their homes and bring them to the king, the men themselves together with their children and their wives. At once a horseman set off at full speed to carry the message to the Hellespont. He crossed over to the other side and gave the letter to Megabazus. Megabazus read it, got guides from Thrace, and set out to march on Paeonia.

15. When the Paeonians learned that the Persians were coming against them, they gathered all their forces and marched out toward the sea, thinking that the Persians would make their attack from that side when they invaded. So the Paeonians were ready, as I said, to drive back Megabazus' army when it came. But the Persians heard that the Paeonians had gathered their forces and were guarding the approach from the sea. With their guides, they took the upper road instead, slipped past the Paeonians without being seen, and fell on their cities, which had been left empty of men. Finding them undefended, they took them easily. When the Paeonians heard that their cities were in enemy hands, they scattered at once, each tribe to its own home, and began to surrender to the Persians. And so it was that these tribes of the Paeonians, namely the Siropaeonians, the Paeoplians, and all the tribes as far as Lake Prasias, were uprooted from their homes and taken to Asia.

16. But the peoples around Mount Pangaeum, around the Doberians, the Agrianians, and the Odomantians, and around Lake Prasias itself, were not conquered by Megabazus at all. He did try to remove even the people who live on the lake itself. Their homes are built like this. A platform, fastened together and set on tall piles, stands out in the middle of the lake, and a single narrow bridge leads to it from the shore. The piles that hold up the platform were no doubt set up in the first place by all the members of the community working together. But since then they have gone on setting them by this rule: every man who marries brings three piles from the mountain called Orbelus for each wife and sets them up as supports, and each man takes many wives. This is how they live. Each man has a hut of his own on the platform, where he lives, and a trapdoor leading down through the platform to the lake. They tie their small children by the foot with a rope, for fear they will roll into the water. They feed their horses and pack animals on fish. There are so many fish that if a man opens his trapdoor and lets an empty basket down into the lake on a cord, he waits only a very short time and then pulls it up full of fish. There are two kinds of fish, which they call paprax and tilon.

17. So the Paeonians who had been conquered were being taken off to Asia. Meanwhile Megabazus, after conquering the Paeonians, sent seven Persians as envoys to Macedonia; after himself, they were the most distinguished men in the army. They were sent to Amyntas to demand earth and water for King Darius. From Lake Prasias it is a very short way into Macedonia. First, right next to the lake, there is the mine that later brought Alexander a talent of silver every single day. Past the mine, once you have crossed the mountain called Dysorum, you are in Macedonia.

18. When the Persians sent to Amyntas arrived, they came before him and demanded earth and water for King Darius. He was willing to give it, and he also invited them to be his guests. He prepared a magnificent dinner and received the Persians with every kindness. When dinner was over and they were drinking toasts to one another, the Persians said, "Our Macedonian host, it is the custom among us Persians, when we give a great dinner, to bring in our concubines and our lawful wives as well, to sit beside us. You have welcomed us readily, you are entertaining us magnificently as your guests, and you are willing to give King Darius earth and water. So agree to follow our custom." Amyntas answered, "Persians, that is not our custom here. With us men and women are kept apart. But since you are our masters and you ask this as well, you shall have this too." With that Amyntas sent for the women. When they had been summoned and came, they sat down in a row facing the Persians. Then the Persians, seeing that the women were beautiful, spoke to Amyntas and told him that what he had done was not at all well thought out. It would have been better for the women not to come at all than to come and not sit beside them, but sit across from them and be a torment to their eyes. So Amyntas, under pressure, told the women to sit beside the Persians. As soon as the women did so, the Persians, who were very drunk, began to fondle their breasts, and some, no doubt, also tried to kiss them.

19. Amyntas saw this and kept quiet, angry as he was, because he was terribly afraid of the Persians. But his son Alexander, who was there and saw it, was young and had never known disaster, and he could not stand it any longer. Unable to bear it, he said to Amyntas, "Father, give in to your age: go and rest, and don't stay on drinking any longer. I will stay here and see that our guests have everything they need." Amyntas understood that Alexander meant to do something violent, and he said, "Son, you are burning with anger, and I think I understand what you are saying: you want to send me away and then do something violent. So I am asking you not to do anything violent to these men, or it will be the ruin of us. Put up with what is happening and watch it. As for my leaving, though, I will do as you say."

20. When Amyntas had made this request and gone, Alexander said to the Persians, "Guests, you are completely free to do as you like with these women: sleep with all of them, if you wish, or with as many of them as you want. You shall have the say in this. But now, since it is almost time for you to go to bed and I can see you have had a good deal to drink, let these women go and bathe, if that suits you, and when they have bathed, take them back into your company." With that, since the Persians readily agreed, he sent the women out, and when they had gone he sent them to the women's quarters. Then Alexander took young men with no beards yet, as many as there were women, dressed them in the women's clothes, gave them daggers, and led them into the banquet hall. As he led them in, he said to the Persians, "Persians, I think you have been treated to a feast that lacked nothing. Everything we had, and everything besides that we could find and provide, has all been set before you. And on top of that there is this, the greatest gift of all: we freely give you our own mothers and sisters as well, so that you may see that we honor you as fully as you deserve, and so that you may report to the king who sent you that a Greek, his governor of the Macedonians, entertained you well at table and in bed." With that, Alexander seated a Macedonian man dressed as a woman beside each Persian, and when the Persians tried to lay hands on them, the Macedonians killed them.

21. So the envoys died this way, they and their servants with them. For they had brought carriages and servants and all the usual baggage of a great embassy, and all of it disappeared along with them. Not long afterward the Persians made a great search for these men. But Alexander was clever enough to stop it. He gave a large sum of money, and his own sister, whose name was Gygaea, to Bubares, a Persian who commanded the men searching for the missing envoys. By giving these things to Bubares, Alexander stopped the search.

22. And so the death of these Persians was hushed up. Now, that these descendants of Perdiccas are Greeks, as they themselves say, I happen to know for myself, and what is more, I will prove it later in my history. The Hellanodicae, the judges who run the games at Olympia, have also ruled that they are Greeks. When Alexander wanted to compete in the games and had come down into the arena for this purpose, the Greeks who were to run against him tried to keep him out, saying that the contest was for Greeks, not for barbarians. But Alexander proved that he was of Argive descent, and he was judged to be a Greek. He entered the footrace, and his lot was drawn to run in the first heat.

23. That is how these things turned out. At the same time Megabazus arrived at the Hellespont with the Paeonians, crossed the strait from there, and came to Sardis. Histiaeus of Miletus was already building a wall around the place he had asked for and been given by Darius as a reward for keeping the bridge of boats safe. This place was the one called Myrcinus, on the bank of the river Strymon. Megabazus had seen what Histiaeus was doing, and as soon as he reached Sardis with the Paeonians he said to Darius, "My king, what is this you have done? You have let a clever, cunning Greek found a city in Thrace, in a place where there is plenty of timber for shipbuilding, a great deal of wood for oars, and silver mines, and where huge numbers of Greeks and non-Greeks live all around. Once they have a leader, they will do whatever he tells them, day and night. So stop this man from doing it, so that you won't find yourself in a war at home. Stop him by sending for him politely. But once you have him in your hands, make sure he never goes back to Greek lands again."

24. With these words Megabazus easily persuaded Darius, who thought he had seen clearly what was likely to happen. So Darius sent a messenger to Myrcinus with this message: "Histiaeus, King Darius says this. Thinking it over, I find there is no one more truly loyal than you to me and to my power, and I know this from deeds, not words. Now, since I have great plans in mind, you must come to me, so that I can share them with you." Histiaeus believed these words, and he also thought it a great thing to become one of the king's advisers, so he came to Sardis. When he arrived, Darius said to him, "Histiaeus, this is why I sent for you. When I came back from Scythia and you were gone out of my sight, I never wanted anything so soon as I wanted then to see you and to talk with you. For I have come to see that the most valuable thing a man can have is a friend who is both wise and truly loyal, and I know you have both these qualities. I can bear witness to them from my own affairs. So now, since you have done well to come, this is what I offer you. Leave Miletus and your new city in Thrace, come with me to Susa, and share everything I have. Eat at my table and be my adviser."

25. So said Darius. He appointed Artaphernes, his brother by the same father, governor of Sardis, and marched off to Susa, taking Histiaeus with him, after first naming Otanes commander of the people along the coast. This Otanes' father, Sisamnes, had been one of the Royal Judges. King Cambyses had put him to death because he had taken money to give an unjust verdict, and had flayed off all his skin. Once the skin was torn off, Cambyses had it cut into strips of leather and stretched them across the chair where Sisamnes used to sit to give judgment. When he had stretched them across the seat, Cambyses appointed the son of the Sisamnes he had killed and flayed to be judge in his father's place, and told him to remember what seat he was sitting in when he gave judgment.

26. This Otanes, then, who was made to sit in that chair, now succeeded Megabazus in the command. He conquered the Byzantines and the Chalcedonians, and he conquered Antandrus in the Troad, and Lamponium. Then, with ships he got from the Lesbians, he conquered Lemnos and Imbros, both of which were still inhabited at that time by Pelasgians.

27. Of these, the Lemnians fought well and held out for a long time, but in the end they were ruined. Over those who survived, the Persians set up as governor Lycaretus, the brother of the Maeandrius who had been king of Samos. This Lycaretus ruled Lemnos until he died. And the reason was this: he went on enslaving and subduing everyone, accusing some of deserting to the Scythians and others of attacking Darius' army as it came back from Scythia.
