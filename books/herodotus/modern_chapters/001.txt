Chapter 1: The First Wrongs, Gyges and Candaules, Arion, and Solon's Visit to Croesus

This is the account of the inquiry of Herodotus of Halicarnassus, set down so that the deeds of men may not be forgotten with time, and so that the great and marvelous works done by Greeks and non-Greeks alike may not lose their fame; and above all, so that the reason they went to war with each other may be remembered.

1. The Persians who know their history say that the Phoenicians started the quarrel. The Phoenicians, they say, came from the sea the Greeks call the Erythraean, the Persian Gulf, to our sea, the Mediterranean. They settled in the land where they still live today, and right away they took to making long voyages. They carried Egyptian and Assyrian goods to many places, and one of the places they came to was Argos. At that time Argos was in every way the leading city of the country now called Greece. So the Phoenicians arrived at Argos and began to sell off their cargo. On the fifth or sixth day after they arrived, when nearly all their goods were sold, a large crowd of women came down to the shore, and among them was the king's daughter. Her name, and the Greeks agree on this, was Io, the daughter of Inachus. The women were standing near the stern of the ship, buying whatever wares they liked best, when suddenly the Phoenicians passed the word to one another and rushed at them. Most of the women ran off and got away, but Io and some others were seized. The Phoenicians put them on board and sailed off at once for Egypt.

2. That, according to the Persians, is how Io came to Egypt; here they do not agree with the Greeks. And this, they say, was the first wrong. After this, they say, some Greeks (they can't say which people) put in at Tyre in Phoenicia and carried off the king's daughter Europa. These would presumably have been Cretans. That made them even for the first injury. But after this, they say, it was the Greeks who committed the second wrong. They sailed in a warship, the Argo of Jason and his crew, to Aea in Colchis, on the river Phasis at the far eastern end of the Black Sea, and when they had finished the other business they came for, they carried off the king's daughter Medea. The king of Colchis sent a herald to Greece to demand compensation for the kidnapping and to ask for his daughter back. But the Greeks answered that since the non-Greeks (the "barbarians," as the Greeks called anyone whose language was not Greek) had paid them no compensation for kidnapping Io of Argos, they would pay the non-Greeks none for this one.

3. In the next generation after this, they say, Alexander the son of Priam, the prince of Troy also called Paris, heard of these things and decided to get himself a wife from Greece by force. He was quite sure he would never have to pay for this wrong, since the Greeks had not paid for theirs. So he carried off Helen, the wife of Menelaus of Sparta. The Greeks decided first to send messengers to demand her back and to ask compensation for the kidnapping. When they made this demand, the other side threw the kidnapping of Medea back at them: the Greeks now wanted compensation from others, they said, though they had paid none themselves and had refused to hand the girl back when asked.

4. Up to this point, they say, it was only a matter of women being carried off on both sides. But after this the Greeks were very much to blame, because they started the fighting: they invaded Asia before the non-Greeks ever invaded Europe. Now, in the Persians' judgment, carrying off women by force is the act of wrongdoers, but making a great business of revenge for it is the act of fools; the sensible thing is to pay no attention once the women are gone, since obviously they would never be carried off if they did not want to go. And the Persians say that they, the people of Asia, made nothing of it when their women were carried off, but that the Greeks, for the sake of one woman from Sparta, gathered a great army, came to Asia, and destroyed the kingdom of Priam. From that time on, they say, they have always regarded the Greeks as their enemies. For the Persians claim Asia and the non-Greek peoples who live there as their own, while they consider Europe and the Greeks to be a separate world.

5. That is how the Persians say it happened, and they trace the start of their hostility to the Greeks back to the taking of Troy. But about Io the Phoenicians do not tell the story the way the Persians do. They deny that they took her to Egypt by force. They say instead that while they were at Argos she slept with the captain of their ship, and when she found she was pregnant she was ashamed to tell her parents, so she sailed away with the Phoenicians of her own free will, so as not to be found out. Those are the stories the Persians and the Phoenicians each tell. I am not going to say that these things happened one way or the other. Instead I will point out the man who, to my own knowledge, first began to wrong the Greeks, and then I will go on with my story, covering the cities of men, small and great alike. For most of the cities that were great long ago have become small, and the ones that were great in my own time used to be small. So, since I know that human prosperity never stays in one place for long, I will give both kinds their due.

6. Croesus was a Lydian by birth, the son of Alyattes, and ruler of the nations on this side of the river Halys. That river flows up from the south between the Syrians and the Paphlagonians and empties northward into the sea called the Euxine, the Black Sea. Croesus was the first non-Greek we know of who conquered some of the Greeks and made them pay tribute, while he won others over as friends. The ones he conquered were the Ionians, the Aeolians, and the Dorians who live in Asia; the ones he made his friends were the Spartans. Before Croesus' reign all the Greeks were free. The raid of the Cimmerians, a nomad people from north of the Black Sea, which struck Ionia before Croesus' time, was not a conquest of the cities, only a plundering expedition.

7. Now this is how the throne, which had belonged to the Heraclids, passed to Croesus' family, the Mermnads. Candaules, whom the Greeks call Myrsilus, was ruler of Sardis and a descendant of Alcaeus son of Heracles. Agron son of Ninus son of Belus son of Alcaeus was the first of the Heraclids to become king of Sardis, and Candaules son of Myrsus was the last. The kings who ruled this land before Agron were descended from Lydus son of Atys, after whom this whole people came to be called Lydians; before that they were called Meonians. The Heraclids, descended from Heracles and a slave girl of Iardanus, took over the government from these earlier kings; it was entrusted to them because of an oracle. They reigned for twenty-two generations, five hundred and five years, handing the power down from father to son, until the time of Candaules son of Myrsus.

8. This Candaules fell passionately in love with his own wife, and being in love, he was convinced that she was by far the most beautiful woman in the world. Believing this, he used to talk to Gyges son of Dascylus, the one among his bodyguards he liked best. He shared his most important business with Gyges, and he also used to praise his wife's beauty to him, far beyond all measure. Not long after, since disaster was fated to fall on Candaules, he said this to Gyges: "Gyges, I don't think you believe me when I tell you how beautiful my wife is. People's ears are less trusting than their eyes. So find a way to see her naked." Gyges cried out and said: "Master, what a foolish thing to say, telling me to look at my mistress naked! When a woman takes off her clothes, she takes off her modesty with them. Men worked out long ago the good sayings we ought to learn from, and one of them is this: each man should look at what is his own. I do believe she is the most beautiful of all women, and I beg you not to ask me to do what is wrong."

9. With words like these he resisted, afraid that some harm would come to him from it. But the king answered him: "Don't worry, Gyges, and don't be afraid, either of me, that I'm saying this to test you, or of my wife, that she might do you some harm. I will arrange it from the start so that she won't even know you have seen her. I'll put you in the room where we sleep, behind the open door. After I go in, my wife will come to bed too. There is a chair near the entrance of the room, and she will lay her clothes on it as she takes them off one by one. That way you will be able to look at her at your leisure. Then, when she walks from the chair to the bed and her back is to you, make sure she doesn't see you as you slip out through the door."

10. Since there was no way out, Gyges agreed. When Candaules thought it was time for bed, he led Gyges to the bedroom, and right after that his wife came in too. Gyges watched her come in and lay down her clothes. When her back was turned to him as she went to the bed, he slipped out of his hiding place and started to leave. But as he went out, the woman caught sight of him. She understood what her husband had done. She did not cry out, though she felt the shame of it, and she acted as if she had noticed nothing, because she meant to take revenge on Candaules. For among the Lydians, as among most other non-Greek peoples, it is a disgrace even for a man to be seen naked.

11. So for the moment she kept quiet, as I said, and gave no sign. But as soon as day came, she got ready the servants she knew were most loyal to her, and then sent for Gyges. He had no idea she knew anything of what had happened, and he came when she called, since he was used to going whenever the queen sent for him. When Gyges arrived, the woman said to him: "Gyges, two roads lie open to you now, and I give you the choice of which you will take. Either you kill Candaules and have both me and the kingdom of Lydia, or you must be killed yourself, here and now, so that you never again obey Candaules in everything and see what you should not. Either he must die, the man who planned this, or you must, the man who looked at me naked and did what is not allowed." For a while Gyges was stunned by what she said. Then he began to beg her not to force him to make such a choice. But he could not move her, and he saw that he truly had to choose: kill his master, or be killed by others himself. He chose to live. Then he asked her: "Since you are forcing me to kill my master against my will, tell me how we are going to attack him." She answered: "The attack will come from the same place where he showed me naked. We will attack him while he sleeps."

12. So they made their plan, and when night came (Gyges was not allowed to leave, and there was no escape for him: either he must be killed or Candaules must), he followed the woman to the bedroom. She gave him a dagger and hid him behind that very same door. Later, while Candaules was asleep, Gyges crept up to him and killed him, and so he won both the wife and the kingdom. The poet Archilochus of Paros, who lived at about that time, also mentioned Gyges in a poem in iambic verse.

13. Gyges won the kingdom, though, and was made secure in it, by the oracle at Delphi. The Lydians were furious about what had been done to Candaules and took up arms. But Gyges' followers and the rest of the Lydians came to an agreement: if the oracle declared that Gyges was to be king of the Lydians, he would be king; if not, he would give the power back to the descendants of Heracles. The oracle gave its answer, and so Gyges became king. Yet the Pythia, Apollo's priestess at Delphi who speaks his oracles, also said this: that vengeance for the Heraclids would come upon Gyges' descendants in the fifth generation. The Lydians and their kings paid no attention to this prophecy until it actually came true.

14. So the Mermnads drove out the Heraclids and took the throne. When Gyges became king he sent a great many offerings to Delphi. Of all the silver offerings at Delphi, more are his than anyone else's. Besides the silver he gave an enormous amount of gold, and in particular one gift more worth mentioning than the rest: six golden mixing bowls, the great bowls the Greeks mixed their wine and water in, which stand there as his offering. They weigh thirty talents, something like 1,700 pounds (the talent is a weight as well as a sum of money, and a talent of silver, about fifty-seven pounds of it, is a fortune). The bowls stand in the treasury of the Corinthians, though in truth this treasury does not belong to the people of Corinth but to Cypselus son of Aetion. This Gyges was the first non-Greek we know of to dedicate offerings at Delphi, except only Midas son of Gordias, king of Phrygia, who dedicated the royal throne he used to sit on when he judged cases in public. The throne is well worth seeing, and it stands in the same place as Gyges' bowls. The people of Delphi call the gold and silver Gyges dedicated "Gygian," after the man who gave it.

As soon as he became king, Gyges also led an army against Miletus and Smyrna, and he took the lower town of Colophon. But he did no other great deed in his reign, which lasted thirty-eight years, so we will pass him by with no more than has already been said.

15. Now I will speak of Ardys son of Gyges, who became king after him. Ardys took Priene and invaded the territory of Miletus. While he was ruling at Sardis, the Cimmerians, driven from their homes by the nomad Scythians, came into Asia and took Sardis, all except the citadel.

16. After Ardys had been king for forty-nine years, his son Sadyattes succeeded him and reigned twelve years, and after him came Alyattes. Alyattes made war on Cyaxares, the descendant of Deioces, and on the Medes. He drove the Cimmerians out of Asia, he took Smyrna, which had been founded from Colophon, and he invaded the territory of Clazomenae. From there he did not come back the way he wanted to, but with heavy losses. During his reign, though, he did other things very much worth mentioning, as follows.

17. He made war on Miletus, a war he had inherited from his father. This is how he used to invade their land and besiege Miletus. Whenever the crops in the fields were ripe, he would lead an army into their territory, marching to the music of pipes and harps and flutes, both low-pitched and high-pitched. When he came into the land of Miletus, he did not knock down the houses out in the countryside, or set fire to them, or tear off their doors; he left them standing as they were. But he destroyed the trees and the crops in the fields, and then went back the way he had come. The Milesians controlled the sea, so there was no point in his army trying to blockade them. And he left the houses standing so that the Milesians would have places to live while they sowed and worked the land, and so that their labor would give him something to destroy whenever he invaded.

18. He kept up this war against them for eleven years, and in the course of those years the Milesians suffered two great defeats: once in a battle at Limenion in their own territory, and again on the plain of the Maeander River. For six of the eleven years Sadyattes son of Ardys was still king of the Lydians, and he was the one who invaded Milesian land at those times of year; for it was this Sadyattes who started the war. For the five years that followed those first six, the war was carried on by Alyattes son of Sadyattes, who inherited it from his father (as I said before) and threw himself into it hard. None of the Ionians helped the Milesians carry the burden of this war except the people of Chios. The Chians came to their aid to repay a favor in kind, because the Milesians had earlier helped the Chians all through their war with the people of Erythrae.

19. Then in the twelfth year of the war, while the Lydian army was burning the standing grain, this happened. As soon as the grain caught fire, a strong wind drove the flames onto the temple of Athena called Athena of Assesus, and the temple caught fire and burned to the ground. At the time nobody thought anything of it. But later, after the army had gone back to Sardis, Alyattes fell sick. His illness dragged on, so he sent messengers to consult the oracle at Delphi, either because someone advised him to or because he himself thought it best to send and ask the god about his illness. But when the messengers reached Delphi, the Pythia said she would give them no answer until they had rebuilt the temple of Athena they had burned at Assesus in the territory of Miletus.

20. This much I know from what the people of Delphi told me. But the Milesians add to it that Periander son of Cypselus, who was a close guest-friend of Thrasybulus, then the tyrant of Miletus (a tyrant being a man who has seized sole power outside the law, not necessarily a cruel one), heard of the oracle given to Alyattes. Periander sent a messenger to tell Thrasybulus, so that he would know about it ahead of time and could plan whatever the situation required. That is the story the Milesians tell.

21. When this answer was reported to Alyattes, he sent a herald to Miletus at once, wanting to make a truce with Thrasybulus and the Milesians for as long as it took him to build the temple. So the envoy was on his way to Miletus. Meanwhile Thrasybulus, who had been told the whole story in advance and knew what Alyattes intended to do, came up with this trick. He gathered into the marketplace all the food that could be found in the city, both his own and what belonged to private citizens, and he announced to the Milesians that when he gave the signal they were all to start drinking and celebrating together.

22. Thrasybulus did this and gave this order so that the herald from Sardis would see a huge quantity of food piled up carelessly and the people feasting, and would report it to Alyattes. And that is just what happened. The herald saw all this, delivered to Thrasybulus the message the king of Lydia had given him, and went back to Sardis; and the peace that followed came about, as I am told, for this reason alone. Alyattes believed there was a severe famine in Miletus and that the people had been worn down to the depths of misery, and when the herald came back from Miletus he heard the opposite of what he had expected. After this the two sides made peace, on terms that they would be guest-friends and allies to each other. Alyattes built two temples to Athena at Assesus instead of one, and he recovered from his illness. That is how things went in the war Alyattes waged against the Milesians and Thrasybulus.

23. As for Periander, the man who told Thrasybulus about the oracle, he was the son of Cypselus and tyrant of Corinth. The Corinthians say (and the people of Lesbos agree with them) that a very great wonder happened in his lifetime: Arion of Methymna was carried ashore at Taenarum on the back of a dolphin. This Arion was a lyre-player second to none of his day, and the first, as far as we know, to compose a dithyramb, the choral hymn to Dionysus; he gave it that name and trained a chorus to perform it at Corinth.

24. This Arion, they say, spent most of his time at Periander's court, but he got the urge to sail to Italy and Sicily. There he made a great deal of money, and then he wanted to go back to Corinth. So he set out from Taras, in southern Italy, and since he trusted Corinthians more than anyone else, he hired a ship with a Corinthian crew. Out on the open sea, the story goes, the crew plotted to throw Arion overboard and keep his money. He found out about it and begged them, offering them his money and asking them to let him live. But he could not persuade them. The men taking him told him either to kill himself, so that he could have a burial on land, or to jump into the sea at once. Driven into this corner, Arion begged them, since that was their decision, to let him stand on the deck in his full singer's costume and sing, and he promised to kill himself after he had sung. They were delighted at the thought of hearing the best singer in the world, and they moved back from the stern to the middle of the ship. He put on his full costume, took up his lyre, stood on the deck, and performed the Orthian strain, a high, ringing hymn. When the song was over he threw himself into the sea just as he was, costume and all. The crew sailed on to Corinth. But a dolphin, they say, took Arion on its back and carried him ashore at Taenarum. When he reached land he went on to Corinth, still in his costume, and when he got there he told the whole story. Periander did not believe him. He kept Arion under guard and would not let him go anywhere, while he kept a careful lookout for the men who had carried him. When they arrived, Periander called them in and asked whether they had any news of Arion. They said he was safe and well in Italy, and that they had left him doing nicely at Taras. At that moment Arion suddenly appeared in front of them, looking just as he had when he jumped from the ship. They were so astonished that, when they were questioned, they could no longer deny it. That is the story the Corinthians and the people of Lesbos both tell, and at Taenarum there is an offering of Arion's, not very large: a bronze figure of a man riding on a dolphin.

25. After waging this war against the Milesians, Alyattes the Lydian eventually died, having reigned fifty-seven years. When he recovered from his illness, this king had dedicated an offering at Delphi, the second of his family to do so: a great silver mixing bowl with a stand of welded iron. The stand is a sight worth seeing above all the offerings at Delphi. It is the work of Glaucus of Chios, the first man in the world to discover the art of welding iron.

26. After Alyattes died, his son Croesus inherited the kingdom at the age of thirty-five. As I said, he fought against the Greeks, and the first of them he attacked were the Ephesians. When he besieged them, the Ephesians dedicated their city to Artemis by tying a rope from her temple to the city wall. The distance between the old city, which was the one under siege then, and the temple is about 4,200 feet, a little under a mile. These were the first Greeks Croesus attacked, but afterward he did the same to the other Ionian and Aeolian cities, one by one, accusing them of one thing or another. Where he could find serious grounds he brought serious charges; against some of them he charged only trivial offenses.

27. When the Greeks in Asia had been conquered and made to pay tribute, he planned next to build ships and attack the islanders. When everything was ready for the shipbuilding, they say that Bias of Priene came to Sardis (or, according to another version, Pittacus of Mytilene). Croesus asked him whether there was any news from Greece, and his answer put an end to the shipbuilding. "My king," he said, "the islanders are hiring 10,000 cavalry, and they mean to march on Sardis and fight you." Croesus thought he was telling the truth and said: "If only the gods would put it into the islanders' heads to come against the sons of the Lydians on horseback!" The other answered: "My king, I can see you are eager to catch the islanders on horseback on the mainland, and it is natural you should want that. But what do you think the islanders want? Ever since they heard you were going to build ships against them, what have they been praying for except to catch the Lydians at sea, so they can take revenge on you for the Greeks on the mainland whom you hold as slaves?" Croesus, they say, was delighted with this ending, and he took the advice, since he thought it was well said. He stopped building ships, and instead he made a treaty of friendship with the Ionians who lived on the islands.

28. As time went on, nearly all the peoples on this side of the river Halys were conquered. Except for the Cilicians and the Lycians, Croesus conquered every nation there and kept it under his rule: the Lydians, Phrygians, Mysians, Mariandynians, Chalybians, Paphlagonians, the Thracians both Thynian and Bithynian, the Carians, Ionians, Dorians, Aeolians and Pamphylians.

29. Once these had been conquered, and while Croesus was still adding to his Lydian empire, all the wise men of Greece who happened to be alive at the time came to Sardis, which was then at the height of its wealth, each brought there by his own reasons. One of them was Solon the Athenian. Solon had made laws for the Athenians at their request, and then he left home for ten years. He sailed off saying that he wanted to see the world, but really it was so that he would not be forced to repeal any of the laws he had made. The Athenians could not repeal them on their own, because they had bound themselves by solemn oaths to live for ten years under whatever laws Solon gave them.

30. So for this reason, and to see the world, Solon left home and visited Amasis, the king of Egypt, and then Croesus at Sardis. When he arrived, Croesus welcomed him as a guest in the royal palace. Then, on the third or fourth day, on Croesus' orders his servants took Solon around to see his treasuries and showed him everything, how great and splendid it all was. When Solon had looked at all of it and examined it as he pleased, Croesus asked him: "My Athenian friend, we have heard a great deal about you, both about your wisdom and about your travels: how you have gone through many lands to see them, in your search for wisdom. So now I have a great wish to ask you whether, of all the people you have seen, there is anyone you think is the happiest of all." He asked this because he thought he himself was the happiest man in the world. But Solon did not flatter him at all; he told the truth. "Yes, my king," he said: "Tellus the Athenian." Croesus was amazed at this and demanded: "Why do you judge Tellus to be the happiest?" Solon said: "First, Tellus lived when his city was prospering, and he had fine, good sons, and he saw children born to every one of them, and all of them lived to grow up. Second, he was well off by our standards, and at the end of his life he had a most glorious death. When the Athenians fought a battle at Eleusis against their neighbors, he brought up help, routed the enemy, and died there the finest of deaths. The Athenians gave him a public burial on the spot where he fell, and paid him great honor."

31. When Solon had stirred up Croesus with the story of Tellus and all the ways in which he had been happy, the king asked again whom he had seen who deserved second place after him. He was sure that he would at least get second place. But Solon answered: "Cleobis and Biton. They were from Argos, and they had enough to live on, and on top of that they had the bodily strength I am about to describe. Both of them had won prizes at the games, and this story is also told about them. There was a festival of Hera at Argos, and their mother absolutely had to be driven to the temple in a cart. But the oxen did not come back from the field in time. So the young men, with no time for anything else, put themselves under the yoke and pulled the cart themselves, with their mother riding in it. They pulled it for about five miles, and brought it to the temple. When they had done this, and the whole gathered crowd had seen them do it, their lives came to the best of ends; and in this the god showed that for a human being it is better to die than to live. The men of Argos stood around them praising the young men's strength, and the women of Argos praised the mother for having such sons. Their mother was overjoyed, both at what they had done and at the praise it won. She stood in front of the goddess's statue and prayed that she would give her sons Cleobis and Biton, who had honored her so greatly, the best gift a human being can receive. After this prayer, when they had sacrificed and feasted, the young men lay down to sleep in the temple itself, and they never got up again; that was the end that held them. The Argives had statues made of them and dedicated them at Delphi, because they had shown themselves to be the best of men."

32. So Solon gave these two second place for happiness. Croesus was angry, and said: "My Athenian friend, do you think so little of our prosperity that you rank us below even ordinary private citizens?" Solon answered: "Croesus, you are asking about human life, and you are asking someone who knows very well that heaven is jealous and loves to throw things into confusion. In a long lifetime a man gets to see many things he would rather not see, and to suffer many things he would rather not suffer. I set the limit of a man's life at seventy years. Those seventy years add up to 25,200 days, not counting any extra months. But if every other year is made a month longer, so that the seasons come around at the right time of year, there will be 35 extra months in those seventy years, and those months make 1,050 days. Of all these days, 26,250 in the seventy years, not one brings anything at all like what another brings. So you see, Croesus, a human being is nothing but chance. As for you, I can see that you are very rich and king over many people. But what you asked me about, I cannot call you yet, not until I learn that you have brought your life to a good end. The very rich man is not one bit happier than the man who has only enough for each day, unless his luck stays with him and he ends his life well, with all his good things still around him. Many very rich men are unhappy, while many with only modest means are lucky. In fact the rich man who is unhappy has only two advantages over the poor man who is lucky, while the lucky man has many over the rich man who is unhappy. The rich man is better able to satisfy his desires, and better able to bear a great disaster if one falls on him. But the other has the advantage of him in these ways. True, he cannot satisfy his desires or bear a disaster as well as the rich man can, but his good luck keeps those things away from him. He is sound in body, free of disease, untouched by suffering, the father of fine children, and good-looking himself. And if on top of all this he ends his life well, then he is the one you are looking for, the one who deserves to be called happy. But until he is dead, hold back: do not call him happy yet, only lucky. Now, no mere human being can have all these things at once. Just as no single country can supply everything it needs for itself, but has one thing and lacks another, and the country that has the most is the best, so it is with a man: no one person is complete in himself. He has one thing and lacks another. But whoever keeps the greatest number of these good things to the end, and then dies a gracious death, that man, my king, I think deserves the name. In everything, though, we have to look at the end, and see how it turns out at the last. For heaven gives many people a glimpse of happiness, and then tears them up by the roots and throws them down."

33. That is what Solon said, and he did not please Croesus. Croesus sent him away and thought nothing of him; he decided Solon was a complete fool, a man who ignored the good things in front of him and told people to look at the end of everything.
