Chapter 9: The Oldest People, and the Land and River of Egypt

1. When Cyrus came to the end of his life, Cambyses took over the royal power after him. He was the son of Cyrus and of Cassandane, the daughter of Pharnaspes. She had died before Cyrus did, and Cyrus had mourned her deeply himself and had also ordered all the peoples he ruled to go into mourning for her. Cambyses, then, the son of this woman and of Cyrus, looked on the Ionians and the Aeolians as slaves he had inherited from his father. He began to march an army against Egypt, and as his helpers he took with him not only the other nations he ruled but also those of the Greeks who were under his power.

2. Now before Psammetichus became their king, the Egyptians used to believe that they were the first of all people to come into being. But ever since Psammetichus became king and wanted to know which people really had come first, they have believed that the Phrygians came into being before them, though they themselves came before everyone else. Psammetichus had not been able to find any way of learning, by asking questions, who the first people were, so he came up with a scheme. He took two newborn children belonging to ordinary people and gave them to a shepherd to raise among his flocks. He told him how they were to be raised: no one was to say a single word in their presence; they were to lie by themselves in a room where no one could come in; at the proper times he was to bring goats to them; and when he had filled them up with milk, he was to do whatever else they needed. Psammetichus did this, and gave the shepherd these orders, because he wanted to hear what word the children would burst out with first, once they had stopped their meaningless crying. And that is just what happened. After two years had passed, with the shepherd carrying on in this way, one day he opened the door and went in, and both children fell down in front of him, stretched out their hands, and said the word "bekos." At first, when he heard this, the shepherd kept quiet about it. But the word came again and again whenever he visited them and looked after them, which he did constantly. So at last he told his master about it, and at the king's command he brought the children before him. Psammetichus heard the word himself, and then he began to inquire which nation of men called anything "bekos." In the course of his inquiries he found that it was the Phrygians' word for bread. In this way, and on the strength of this evidence, the Egyptians came to admit that the Phrygians were an older people than they were.

3. That this is how it happened I heard from the priests of Hephaestus at Memphis. The Greeks, though, tell many other silly stories, and one of them is that Psammetichus cut out the tongues of certain women and then had the children live with these women.

So much, then, for what they told me about the raising of the children. But I heard other things too at Memphis, when I talked with the priests of Hephaestus. I also went to Thebes and to Heliopolis for this very reason: I wanted to know whether the priests in those places would agree with the account of the priests at Memphis, since the men of Heliopolis are said to be the most learned in history of all the Egyptians. As for the stories I heard about the gods, I am not eager to tell them in full. I will only mention their names, because I think all people know equally little about such things. Whatever I do record about them I will record only because the course of my story forces me to.

4. But about human affairs the priests all agreed with one another. They said the Egyptians were the first people on earth to discover the course of the year, dividing it into twelve parts to make up the whole. They said they found this out from the stars. And in my opinion they calculate more wisely than the Greeks do. The Greeks throw in an extra month every other year to keep the seasons in line, while the Egyptians count twelve months of thirty days each and then add five days every year beyond that number, and so the circle of their seasons comes round to the same point it started from. They also said that the Egyptians were the first to use names for the twelve gods, and that the Greeks took the practice over from them; and that they were the first to give the gods altars and statues and temples, and the first to carve figures in stone. For most of these things they showed me actual proof that it was so. They said, too, that the first man to become king of Egypt was Min. In his time, they said, all of Egypt except the district of Thebes was a swamp, and none of the land that now lies below Lake Moeris was above water then. That lake is a seven days' voyage up the river from the sea.

5. And I thought what they said about the land was right. For it is plain to anyone who sees it, even if he has not heard it beforehand, as long as he has any sense, that the Egypt that Greek ships sail to is land the Egyptians have been given as an addition: it is a gift of the river. The same is true of the country above this lake for a distance of three days' sail, though they did not go on to say anything of the kind about it. That too is another example of the same thing. For this is what the land of Egypt is like. First, when you are still approaching it by ship and are a day's run from land, if you let down a sounding line you will bring up mud, and you will find yourself in sixty-six feet of water. This much shows that the land is being built out forward into the sea.

6. Second, there is Egypt itself. Its length along the sea is sixty schoeni, taking Egypt, as we define it, to stretch from the Gulf of Plinthine to Lake Serbonis, along which Mount Casius runs. It is from this lake that the sixty schoeni are measured. For people who have little land measure their country in fathoms (a fathom is six feet); those who are less poor in land, in stades (a stade is six hundred feet); those who have a lot of land, in parasangs; and those who have land in great abundance, in schoeni. A parasang equals thirty stades, about three and a third miles, and a schoenus, which is an Egyptian measure, equals sixty stades, about six and two-thirds miles. So the coastline of Egypt would be 3,600 stades long, about four hundred miles.

7. From there inland as far as Heliopolis, Egypt is broad. The land is all flat, without springs, and made of mud. The road inland from the sea up to Heliopolis is about the same length as the road from the altar of the twelve gods at Athens to Pisa and the temple of Olympian Zeus. If you worked it out, you would find that the difference between these two roads is very small, no more than a mile and two-thirds. The road from Athens to Pisa falls short of about 167 miles by that much, while the road from the sea to Heliopolis comes to the full 167.

8. From Heliopolis on up, though, Egypt is narrow. On one side a range of mountains belonging to Arabia runs alongside it, stretching from the north toward the south, where the noonday sun and the south wind are, and running on upward without a break to the sea called the Erythraean, the Indian Ocean. In this range are the stone quarries that were used to cut the stone for the pyramids at Memphis. On this side, then, the mountains end where I said, and then they turn back. Where the range is widest, as I was told, it is a two months' journey across from east to west, and its eastern edges are said to produce frankincense. That is what this mountain range is like. On the Libyan side of Egypt another range stretches out, rocky and covered in sand. The pyramids are in this range, and it runs in the same direction as the part of the Arabian mountains that goes toward the south. So, from Heliopolis on, the land that belongs to Egypt is no longer very wide. For about four days' sail up the river, Egypt proper is narrow. The land between the mountain ranges I have mentioned is level plain, but where it is narrowest it did not seem to me to be more than about twenty-two miles across, from the Arabian mountains to the ones called the Libyan. After this Egypt widens out again.

9. That is what this land is like. From Heliopolis to Thebes is a nine days' voyage up the river, and the distance is about 540 miles, or eighty-one schoeni. If you add up these measurements of Egypt, the result is as follows. I have already shown that the distance along the sea comes to about four hundred miles. Now I will tell you the distance inland from the sea to Thebes: it is about 680 miles. And from Thebes to the city called Elephantine is another two hundred miles.

10. As for most of this land I have been describing, I too thought, just as the priests said, that it had been won by the Egyptians as an addition. It was clear to me that the space between the mountain ranges I mentioned, the ones above the city of Memphis, was once a gulf of the sea. It was like the country around Troy and Teuthrania and Ephesus and the plain of the Maeander, if I may compare small things with great. And small those places are by comparison, since none of the rivers that piled up the soil in those regions deserves to be compared in volume with even one of the mouths of the Nile, and the Nile has five mouths. There are other rivers too, not at all equal to the Nile in size, that have done great things. I could name several, and above all the Achelous, which flows through Acarnania and out into the sea and has already turned half of the Echinades islands into mainland.

11. Now in Arabia, not far from Egypt, there is a gulf that runs in from the sea called the Erythraean. It is very long and narrow, as I am about to explain. As for its length, someone setting out from its innermost point to sail out through it into the open sea would spend forty days on the voyage, rowing. As for its width, where the gulf is broadest it is half a day's sail across. And it has a tide that ebbs and flows every day. I believe Egypt was once another gulf just like this one. The one ran in from the northern sea, the Mediterranean, toward Ethiopia. The other, the Arabian Gulf that I am describing, our Red Sea, ran from the south toward Syria. The two gulfs bored inland until their ends almost met, passing alongside each other with only a little space left between them. So if the Nile were to turn aside and flow into this Arabian Gulf, what would stop the river from filling that gulf up with silt as it kept on flowing, within twenty thousand years at any rate? For my part, I think it would be filled up within ten thousand. So in all the ages that passed before I was born, why should a gulf even much bigger than this one not have been filled up by a river so great and so hard-working?

12. So as for Egypt, I believe the people who say that this is how things are, and I am also strongly convinced of it myself. I have seen that Egypt juts out into the sea farther than the land on either side of it. Shells are found on its mountains. A crust of salt forms on the surface of the ground, so that even the pyramids are being eaten away by it. And of all the mountains of Egypt, the range above Memphis is the only one that has sand. Besides this, I notice that Egypt is not like the land of Arabia that borders it, nor like Libya, nor like Syria (it is Syrians who live in the parts of Arabia along the sea). Its soil is black and crumbles easily, because it really is mud and silt brought down from Ethiopia by the river. The soil of Libya, as we know, is reddish and rather sandy, while the soil of Arabia and Syria is more clay and rock.

13. The priests also gave me strong evidence about this land. In the reign of King Moeris, they said, whenever the river rose at least twelve feet it watered Egypt below Memphis. And when I heard this from the priests, not yet nine hundred years had passed since Moeris died. Now, though, unless the river rises twenty-four feet, or twenty-two and a half at the very least, it does not spread over the land. I think too that if that land keeps on growing in height at this rate, and keeps on increasing in area in the same way, then the Egyptians who live below Lake Moeris, especially in the region called the Delta, will suffer for all time to come, once the Nile no longer floods their land, the very thing they themselves said would one day happen to the Greeks. For when they heard that the whole land of the Greeks is watered by rain and not by rivers as theirs is, they said that someday the Greeks would be cheated of a great hope and would suffer terribly from famine. What this saying means is that if the god does not send them rain, but lets a drought go on for a long time, the Greeks will be wiped out by hunger, since in fact they have no other source of water to save them except Zeus alone.

14. That was a fair thing for the Egyptians to say about the Greeks. But now let me explain how things stand with the Egyptians themselves. If, as I said before, their land below Memphis (for this is the land that is growing) keeps on rising at the same rate as in the past, then certainly the Egyptians who live there will go hungry, since their land gets no rain and the river will not be able to spread over their fields. It is true, though, that at present they gather the fruits of the earth with less work than any other people, and with less work than the rest of the Egyptians too. They don't have to labor at breaking up furrows with a plow, or hoeing, or any of the other jobs that other people do to raise a crop. Instead, when the river has risen of its own accord and watered their fields, and after watering them has gone down again, each man sows his own field and turns pigs loose on it. When the pigs have trampled the seed into the ground, he waits for the harvest. Then he has the pigs thresh the grain, and so he gathers it in.

15. Suppose we wanted to follow the opinion of the Ionians about Egypt. They say that the Delta alone is Egypt. They count its coastline as running from what is called the Watchtower of Perseus to the fish-salting works of Pelusium, a distance of forty schoeni, about 267 miles, and they count it as reaching inland as far as the city of Cercasorus, where the Nile divides and runs to Pelusium and to Canopus. The rest of Egypt they assign partly to Libya and partly to Arabia. If, I say, we followed this account, we would be declaring that in earlier times the Egyptians had no land to live in at all. For, as we have seen, their Delta at least is land laid down by the river, and it has appeared only lately, so to speak, as the Egyptians themselves say and as I believe. So if at first there was no land for them to live in, why did they waste their effort trying to prove that they had come into being before all other people? They had no need to test the children to see what language they would speak first. But I do not believe that the Egyptians came into being at the same time as what the Ionians call the Delta. I believe they have always existed, ever since the human race came into being, and that as their land moved forward, many of them stayed behind in their first homes while many others gradually came down into the lower country. At any rate it is certain that in old times Thebes was called Egypt, and the distance around Thebes measures about 680 miles.

16. If our judgment on these matters is right, then the Ionians' opinion about Egypt is not sound. But if the Ionians' judgment is right, then I say that neither the Greeks nor the Ionians themselves know how to count. They say that the whole earth is made up of three parts, Europe, Asia and Libya. But by that reckoning they ought to count the Delta of Egypt as a fourth, since it belongs neither to Asia nor to Libya. For by their reckoning it cannot be the river Nile that divides Asia from Libya. The Nile splits at the point of the Delta and flows around it on both sides, and so this land would lie between Asia and Libya.

17. So we set aside the opinion of the Ionians and give a judgment of our own on this question as well. Egypt, we say, is all the land where Egyptians live, just as Cilicia is the land where Cilicians live and Assyria the land where Assyrians live. And we know of no boundary between Asia and Libya, properly speaking, except the borders of Egypt. But if we adopt the opinion the Greeks generally hold, we must suppose that the whole of Egypt, starting from the Cataract and the city of Elephantine, is split in two and so takes a share of both names, one side belonging to Libya and the other to Asia. For from the Cataract onward the Nile flows to the sea cutting Egypt down the middle. As far as the city of Cercasorus the Nile flows in a single stream, but from that city on it splits three ways. One branch, called the Pelusian mouth, turns east. The second branch heads west, and this is called the Canopic mouth. But the branch that runs straight on goes like this: when the river, flowing downward, comes to the point of the Delta, it cuts through the middle of the Delta and so runs out to the sea. This branch carries a share of the river's water that is neither the smallest nor the least famous, and it is called the Sebennytic mouth. There are also two other mouths that split off from the Sebennytic and run to the sea, and these are called the Saitic and the Mendesian. The Bolbitinic and Bucolic mouths, on the other hand, are not natural; they were dug.

18. The answer given by the oracle of Ammon, out in the Libyan desert, also bears out my opinion that Egypt is as large as I make it out to be in my account. And I heard about this answer only after I had formed my own opinion about Egypt. The people of the cities of Marea and Apis, who live in the part of Egypt that borders on Libya, believed that they themselves were Libyans and not Egyptians. They also found the rules of the Egyptian religion a burden, because they did not want to be forbidden to eat beef. So they sent to Ammon, saying that they had nothing in common with the Egyptians, since they lived outside the Delta and agreed with them about nothing, and they said they wanted to be allowed to eat everything without restriction. But the god would not allow them to do so. He said that Egypt was all the land that the Nile spread over and watered, and that Egyptians were all those who lived below the city of Elephantine and drank from that river. That was the answer the oracle gave them about this.

19. And when the Nile is in flood, it spreads not only over the Delta but also over parts of the land called Libyan and the land called Arabian, sometimes as far as two days' journey on each side, and at times even more than this, or at times less.

As for the nature of the river, I could not learn anything about it, either from the priests or from anyone else. And I was especially eager to learn from them about these things: why the Nile comes down rising in volume for a hundred days, starting from the summer solstice, and then, when it has reached that number of days, turns back and goes down again, its stream failing, so that it stays low all through the winter until the summer solstice comes round again. I could not get any explanation of any of this from the Egyptians, when I asked them what power the Nile has that makes it behave the opposite way from other rivers. I also asked because I wanted to know, besides what I have just said, why it is the only river that sends out no breezes blowing off it.

20. However, some Greeks who wanted to make a name for themselves for cleverness have explained this water in three different ways. Two of these I do not think worth even talking about, except to point out what they are. One of them says that the Etesian winds, the steady north winds of summer, are what make the river rise, by keeping the Nile from flowing out into the sea. But the Etesian winds often fail, and the Nile still does its usual work. Besides, if these winds were the cause, then all the other rivers that flow against the Etesian winds should have been affected in the same way as the Nile, and even more so, since they are smaller and push against the winds with a weaker current. But there are many such rivers in Syria and many in Libya too, and none of them behaves at all like the Nile.

21. The second explanation is even more ignorant than the one I have mentioned, and more of a marvel to tell. It says that the river does these things because it flows from the Ocean, and that the Ocean flows around the whole earth.

22. The third explanation sounds by far the most convincing, but it is the most mistaken of all. In fact it has no more truth in it than the others. It claims that the Nile flows from melting snow, when in fact it flows out of Libya through the middle of the Ethiopians and so comes out into Egypt. So how could it flow from snow, when it flows from the hottest places to cooler ones? Most of the facts are enough to convince a man, at least one who is capable of reasoning about such things, that it is not at all likely to flow from snow. The first and greatest piece of evidence is the winds, which blow hot from those regions. The second is that the land never has rain or frost, whereas after snow falls, rain must come within five days; so if it snowed in those parts, it would rain there. The third piece of evidence is the people who live there, who are dark-skinned because of the burning heat. On top of that, kites and swallows stay there all year round and never leave the country, and cranes fleeing the cold weather that comes on in the region of Scythia come regularly to these parts to spend the winter. So if it snowed even a little in the land the Nile flows through and where it rises, none of these things would happen, as the facts force us to admit.

23. As for the man who talked about the Ocean, he has carried his story off into the unknown, and so there is no need to refute him. For my part, I know of no river called Ocean. I think Homer, or one of the poets before him, invented the name and brought it into his poetry.

24. But if, after finding fault with the opinions others have put forward, I must give an opinion of my own on these puzzling questions, I will say what I think is the reason the Nile rises in summer. In the winter the Sun is driven out of his old path through the sky by the storm winds, and he comes to the upper parts of Libya. To put it as briefly as possible, that is the whole explanation. For whatever country this god comes closest to and stands directly over, that country, it is reasonable to suppose, is the most in need of water, and its own rivers dry up the most.

25. But to explain it at greater length, it works like this. As the Sun passes over the upper parts of Libya, this is what he does. The air in those parts is always clear and the land is always warm, since there are no cold winds, and so as he passes through it the Sun does just what he usually does in summer, when he travels through the middle of the sky. That is, he draws the water up to himself, and when he has drawn it up he drives it away to the upper parts of the country, and the winds catch it up, scatter it, and turn it into rain. So it is natural that the winds that blow from this region, the south and southwest winds, should be by far the rainiest of all winds. I think, though, that the Sun does not send away from himself all the water he draws each year from the Nile, but keeps some of it with him. Then, when the winter grows milder, the Sun goes back again to the middle of the sky, and from then on he draws water equally from all rivers. In the meantime, though, these other rivers flow full, because a great deal of rainwater mixes with them, since their country gets rain then and is full of torrents. In summer, however, they are weak, because the rain fails them and the Sun is drawing on them as well. But the Nile, the only river that gets no rain and is drawn on by the Sun all winter, naturally flows during that time with much less than its proper volume, much less than in summer. For in summer it is drawn on equally with all the other waters, but in winter it carries the burden alone. So I believe that the Sun is the cause of all this.

26. The Sun is also, in my opinion, the reason the air in those parts is dry, since he dries it out by scorching his path through the sky. So in the upper parts of Libya it is always summer. But suppose the stations of the seasons had been switched around. Suppose that where the north wind and winter now stand in the sky, there stood the south wind and the noonday, and where the south wind now stands, there stood the north. If that were so, then the Sun, driven out of the middle of the sky by winter and the north wind, would go to the upper parts of Europe, just as he now comes to the upper parts of Libya. And as he passed over the whole of Europe, I believe he would do to the Danube what he now does to the Nile.

27. As for the breeze, and why none blows off the river, my opinion is that nothing would naturally blow from very hot places, and that a breeze usually blows from something cold.

28. Let these things be, then, as they are and as they were from the beginning. But as for the sources of the Nile, not one of the Egyptians or Libyans or Greeks who talked with me claimed to know anything about them, except the scribe who kept the sacred treasury of Athena in the city of Sais in Egypt. And even this man seemed to me to be joking when he said he knew for certain. Here is what he said. There were two mountains with sharply pointed peaks, standing between the city of Syene, in the district of Thebes, and Elephantine. The names of the mountains were Crophi and Mophi. From between these two mountains, he said, flowed the springs of the Nile, which were bottomless. Half of the water flowed to Egypt and the north, and the other half to Ethiopia and the south. As for the springs being bottomless, he said that Psammetichus, king of Egypt, had put this to the test. He had a rope twisted many thousands of fathoms long, tens of thousands of feet, and let it down there, and it found no bottom. By this the scribe gave me to understand (if what he told me was really true) that there were powerful whirlpools there and a current flowing back on itself, and that because the water was dashing against the mountains, the sounding line could not get to the bottom when it was let down.

29. I was not able to learn anything about this from anyone else. But I learned as much as follows by the most careful inquiry, going as far as the city of Elephantine and seeing for myself, and from there on gathering what I know by hearsay. As you go up the river from the city of Elephantine, the country climbs steeply. So here you have to tie ropes to both sides of the boat, the way you would harness an ox, and make your way on like that. If the rope breaks, the boat is gone at once, swept away by the force of the current. This stretch takes about four days to sail through. In this part the Nile winds like the river Maeander, and the distance you have to travel in this way is twelve schoeni, about eighty miles. Then you come to a level plain, where the Nile flows around an island called Tachompso. (In the country above Elephantine, Ethiopians begin to live right away; they hold half of the island, and Egyptians the other half.) Next to this island there is a great lake, with nomad Ethiopian tribes living all around it. When you have sailed across this, you come to the stream of the Nile again, which flows into the lake. After this you get out and travel overland for forty days, because sharp rocks stick up out of the water in the Nile and there are many reefs, so that no boat can get through. Then, after you have crossed this country in the forty days I mentioned, you get on board another boat and sail for twelve days, and after that you come to a great city called Meroe. This city is said to be the mother city of all the other Ethiopians. The people who live there worship Zeus and Dionysus alone of the gods, and honor them greatly. They have an oracle of Zeus established there, and they go to war whenever this god commands them through his prophecies, and wherever he commands them to go.

30. Sailing on from this city, you will come to the "Deserters" in the same length of time again as it took you to get from Elephantine to the mother city of the Ethiopians. The name of these "Deserters" is the Asmach, and this word means, in Greek, "those who stand on the king's left hand." They were 240,000 Egyptians of the warrior class who revolted and went over to the Ethiopians, and this is why. In the reign of Psammetichus garrisons were posted: one at the city of Elephantine against the Ethiopians, another at Daphnae of Pelusium against the Arabians and Assyrians, and another at Marea against Libya. Even in my own time the Persians keep their garrisons posted in the same way as they were in the reign of Psammetichus, since the Persians have outposts both at Elephantine and at Daphnae. Now the Egyptians I am speaking of had been on guard duty for three years, and no one had come to relieve them. So they talked it over among themselves, agreed on a plan, revolted from Psammetichus all together, and set off for Ethiopia. When he heard this, Psammetichus went after them. When he caught up with them, he pleaded with them at length and tried to persuade them not to abandon the gods of their country and their children and wives. At that, the story goes, one of them pointed to his genitals and said that wherever these were, there they would have both children and wives. When they reached Ethiopia, they put themselves at the service of the king of the Ethiopians, and he rewarded them like this. There were certain Ethiopians who had quarreled with him, and he told the Egyptians to drive these people out and live in their land. And since these men settled among the Ethiopians, the Ethiopians have become gentler in their ways, having learned the customs of the Egyptians.

31. So, besides the part of its course that lies in Egypt, the Nile is known as far as a four months' journey by river and land. That is how many months you find, if you count them up, are spent in going from Elephantine to these "Deserters." And there the river flows from the west, from the setting of the sun. But what lies beyond that, no one can say for sure, because that country is desert on account of the burning heat.

32. This much, though, I heard from some men of Cyrene. They told me they had gone to the oracle of Ammon and had talked with Etearchus, king of the Ammonians. After talking about other things, it happened, they got onto the subject of the Nile, and how no one knew its sources. And Etearchus told them that once some men of the Nasamonians had come to him. (This is a Libyan people that lives in the Syrtis and in the land a short way to the east of the Syrtis.) When the Nasamonians arrived, he asked them whether they could tell him anything more than he knew about the desert parts of Libya. They told him that there had been among them some sons of chief men, unruly young fellows. When they grew up, these young men came up with various wild schemes, and among other things they chose five of their number by lot to go and look at the deserts of Libya, and see whether they could discover more than those who had explored the farthest before them. For along the part of Libya that lies on the northern sea, from Egypt as far as Cape Soloeis, which is the farthest point of Libya, Libyans live all along the whole coast, many peoples of them, except for what the Greeks and Phoenicians hold. But in the upper parts, above the seacoast and above the peoples whose land comes down to the sea, Libya is full of wild beasts. And beyond the land of wild beasts it is all sand, terribly waterless and utterly desert. So these young men (the Nasamonians said), sent off by their companions well supplied with water and food, first went through the inhabited country. When they had passed through that, they came to the country of wild beasts, and after that they crossed the desert, traveling westward, toward the west wind. They crossed a great stretch of sand over many days, and at last they saw trees growing on a level plain. They went up to them and began to pick the fruit that was on the trees. But just as they started picking it, some small men came at them, men shorter than average, and seized them and carried them off. The Nasamonians could not understand a word these people said, and their captors could not understand a word of the Nasamonians' speech either. They led them (so the story went) through enormous swamps, and after crossing these they came to a city where all the men were the same size as their captors and dark-skinned. Past the city flowed a great river, running from the west toward the sunrise, and crocodiles could be seen in it.

33. So much for the account of Etearchus the Ammonian, except to add that, as the men of Cyrene told me, he said that the Nasamonians got home safely, and that the people they had come to were all sorcerers. Etearchus guessed that this river flowing past the city was the Nile, and reason compels us to think so too. For the Nile flows out of Libya and cuts Libya through the middle. And as I guess, judging what is unknown by what can be seen, it starts as far from its mouth as the Danube does from its own. For the Danube begins among the Celts and at the city of Pyrene, and runs so as to divide Europe down the middle. (The Celts live beyond the Pillars of Heracles and border on the Cynesians, who live farthest west of all the peoples who make their homes in Europe.) And the Danube ends, after running through the whole of Europe, by flowing into the Black Sea at the place where the Milesians have their colony of Istria.

34. Now the Danube, since it flows through inhabited land, is known from the reports of many people. But no one can give an account of the sources of the Nile, because the part of Libya it flows through is uninhabited desert. About its course, though, I have told as much as it was possible to learn by the most careful inquiry, and it runs out into Egypt. Now Egypt lies roughly opposite the mountain country of Cilicia. From there to Sinope on the Black Sea is a five days' journey in a straight line for a man traveling light. And Sinope lies opposite the place where the Danube runs out into the sea. So I think that the Nile crosses the whole of Libya and is equal in length to the Danube.

So much for the Nile, then.
