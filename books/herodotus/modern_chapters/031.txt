Chapter 31: Athens Joins the Revolt, the Burning of Sardis, and the Death of Aristagoras

97. The Athenians were thinking this way, and had already been set at odds with the Persians, when at this very moment Aristagoras of Miletus arrived in Athens, after Cleomenes the Spartan had ordered him out of Sparta. He came to Athens because it was the most powerful of all the other cities, after Sparta. Aristagoras came before the assembly of the people and said the same things he had said at Sparta: how much wealth there was in Asia, and how the Persians made war, using neither shield nor spear, so that they were easy to beat. That is what he said, and he added this too: the Milesians were colonists from Athens, and it was only reasonable that the Athenians, with all their power, should rescue them. There was nothing he did not promise, so hard did he press his request, until at last he won them over. It seems it is easier to deceive many people than one: he could not deceive Cleomenes the Spartan on his own, but he managed it with thirty thousand Athenians. So the Athenians, persuaded, voted to send twenty ships to help the Ionians, and they appointed as commander Melanthius, one of their citizens, a man highly respected in every way. These ships were the beginning of troubles for Greeks and non-Greeks alike.

98. Aristagoras, however, sailed on ahead and came to Miletus. There he worked out a plan that was not likely to do the Ionians any good. In fact that was not why he was doing it; he did it to annoy King Darius. He sent a man to Phrygia, to the Paeonians whom Megabazus had carried off as captives from the river Strymon, and who were living by themselves in a district and village of Phrygia. When the messenger reached the Paeonians, he said: "Paeonians, Aristagoras the tyrant of Miletus has sent me to offer you a way to save yourselves, if you are willing to do as he says. All Ionia has now revolted from the king, and you have a chance to get home safe to your own land. Getting to the sea is your business; after that, it will be ours." The Paeonians welcomed this as the best news they could have heard. They took their children and their women and fled for the sea, though some of them were afraid and stayed where they were. When the Paeonians reached the coast they crossed over to Chios. They were already on Chios when a large force of Persian cavalry arrived on their trail, chasing them. Since the horsemen had not caught them, they sent over to Chios to order the Paeonians to come back. The Paeonians refused. Instead the people of Chios carried them from Chios to Lesbos, the Lesbians brought them to Doriscus, and from there they went on by land and reached Paeonia.

99. Meanwhile the Athenians arrived with their twenty ships, bringing with them five triremes from Eretria as well. The Eretrians had not joined the expedition for the Athenians' sake but for the Milesians', to repay a debt they owed them. In earlier times the Milesians had stood with the Eretrians through the whole of their war with the Chalcidians, at the time when the Samians were helping the Chalcidians against the Eretrians and Milesians. So when these ships had arrived and the other allies were on the spot, Aristagoras launched a march on Sardis. He did not go on this march himself. He stayed in Miletus and put others in command of the Milesians: his brother Charopinus, and one of the other citizens, Hermophantus.

100. With this force the Ionians came to Ephesus. They left their ships at Coresus in Ephesian territory and went inland themselves in a large body, with Ephesians to guide them on the march. They marched along the river Cayster, crossed the range of Tmolus, and when they arrived they took Sardis without a fight, all except the citadel. The citadel Artaphernes saved from capture himself, since he had a sizable force of men with him.

101. They took the city, but they were kept from plundering it, and this is why. Most of the houses in Sardis were built of reeds, and even the ones built of brick had roofs thatched with reeds. One of these houses was set on fire by a soldier, and at once the fire spread from house to house over the whole town. As the town burned, the Lydians and all the Persians who were in the city were cut off: the fire had hold of the outskirts all around them, and they had no way out of the town. So they streamed together into the marketplace and to the river Pactolus. The Pactolus carries down gold dust for them from Tmolus. It flows right through the middle of their marketplace and then runs out into the river Hermus, and the Hermus into the sea. At the Pactolus and in the marketplace, then, the Lydians and Persians gathered and were forced to defend themselves. When the Ionians saw some of the enemy standing their ground and others coming on to attack in great numbers, they lost their nerve and pulled back to the mountain called Tmolus. Then, at nightfall, they set off for their ships.

102. So Sardis was burned, and with it the temple of Cybebe, the goddess of that country. Later the Persians used this as their reason for burning the temples in Greece in return. At the time I am speaking of, though, the Persians who held provinces on this side of the river Halys had learned of the attack beforehand and were gathering to come to the Lydians' help. As it happened, when they got there they found the Ionians already gone from Sardis. But they followed hard on their trail and caught up with them at Ephesus. The Ionians lined up against them, but when the battle was joined they were badly beaten. The Persians cut down many men of note, and among those who fell was Eualcides, the Eretrian commander, a man who had won garlands at the games and whom Simonides of Ceos praised again and again in his poems. The Ionians who survived the battle scattered to their various cities.

103. That was how they fought that time. After the battle the Athenians abandoned the Ionians altogether. Aristagoras sent messenger after messenger begging for their help, but they said they would not help them. Even without their Athenian allies, though, the Ionians went on preparing for war with the king, since they had already done Darius so much harm. They also sailed to the Hellespont and brought Byzantium and all the other cities in that region under their control. Then they sailed out of the Hellespont and won most of Caria over to their alliance as well. Even Caunus, which had refused to become their ally before, joined them too after they had burned Sardis.

104. The Cyprians also joined their alliance of their own accord, all except the people of Amathus. They too had revolted from the Persians, and this is how. There was a man named Onesilus, the younger brother of Gorgus king of Salamis in Cyprus. He was the son of Chersis, son of Siromus, son of Euelthon. Even in earlier times this man had often urged Gorgus to revolt from the king, and now, when he heard that the Ionians had revolted, he pressed him very hard and tried to drive him to it. But since he could not persuade Gorgus, Onesilus waited for a time when Gorgus had gone out of the city of Salamis, and then, with the men of his own faction, he shut him out of the gates. Robbed of his city, Gorgus fled to the Persians for refuge. Onesilus became ruler of Salamis and tried to persuade all the Cyprians to revolt with him. He persuaded the rest. But the people of Amathus refused to do what he wanted, so he camped before their city and laid siege to it.

105. So Onesilus was besieging Amathus. Meanwhile King Darius was told that Sardis had been taken and burned by the Athenians and the Ionians together, and that the leader of the alliance that had brought all this about was Aristagoras of Miletus. It is said that when he first heard the news, he paid no attention to the Ionians, because he knew that they at any rate would not get away with their revolt unpunished. But he asked who the Athenians were. When he had been told, he asked for his bow. He took it, fitted an arrow to the string, and shot it upward toward the sky, and as he shot it into the air he said: "Zeus, grant that I may take revenge on the Athenians!" After saying this he ordered one of his servants to say to him three times, every time his dinner was served: "Master, remember the Athenians."

106. After giving this order, he called in Histiaeus of Miletus, whom he had now kept with him for a long time, and said: "I am told, Histiaeus, that your deputy, the man you left in charge of Miletus, has rebelled against me. He brought men over against me from the other continent, and he persuaded the Ionians too (who will pay me the penalty for what they have done) to join them, and so he robbed me of Sardis. Now, how do you think this looks? And how could anything like this have been done without your advice? Be careful you don't find yourself to blame for this later." Histiaeus answered: "My king, what are you saying? That I advised something that was likely to cause you trouble, great or small? What more could I want than what I have, that I would do such things? What do I lack? I have everything you have, and you think me worthy to hear all your plans. No: if my deputy really is doing anything like what you say, you can be sure he has done it entirely on his own. For my part I don't even accept the report as true, that the Milesians and my deputy are doing anything rebellious against your rule. But if it turns out that they really are doing something of the kind, and what you have heard, my king, is the truth, then see what you did when you took me away from the coast. It seems that once I was out of the Ionians' sight, they did what they had long wanted to do. If I had been in Ionia, not one city would have stirred. So let me set out for Ionia as quickly as possible, so that I can put everything back as it was for you and hand over to you this deputy of Miletus who has plotted all this. And when I have done this to your satisfaction, I swear by the gods of the royal house that I will not take off the tunic I am wearing when I go down to Ionia until I have made Sardinia, the largest island in the world, pay you tribute."

107. That was what Histiaeus said, trying to deceive the king. Darius was persuaded and let him go, ordering him to come back to him at Susa when he had done what he had promised.

108. Meanwhile, while the news about Sardis was going up to the king, and while Darius, after what he did with the bow, was talking with Histiaeus, and while Histiaeus, released by Darius, was making his way down to the coast, all through that time the following events were taking place. As Onesilus of Salamis was besieging the people of Amathus, he received word that a Persian named Artybius was expected to arrive in Cyprus shortly, bringing a large Persian army with him by ship. When he heard this, Onesilus sent heralds to various places in Ionia to call the Ionians to his help. They talked it over among themselves and came at once with a large force. The Ionians arrived in Cyprus just when the Persians had crossed over by ship from Cilicia and were marching overland to attack Salamis, while the Phoenicians with the fleet were sailing around the headland called the "Keys of Cyprus."

109. Things being so, the tyrants of Cyprus called the Ionian commanders together and said: "Ionians, we Cyprians give you the choice of which enemy you would rather fight, the Persians or the Phoenicians. If you would rather line up on land and try your strength against the Persians, then it is time for you to get off your ships and form up on land, and for us to board your ships and fight the Phoenicians. But if you would rather take on the Phoenicians, then whichever of the two you choose, you must do your best, as far as it lies with you, to see that both Ionia and Cyprus are free." The Ionians answered: "We were sent out by the common council of the Ionians to guard the sea, not to hand our ships over to the Cyprians and fight the Persians on land ourselves. So we will try to serve well at the post we were given. And you must remember all you suffered when you were slaves of the Persians, and prove yourselves brave men."

110. That was the Ionians' answer. Afterward, when the Persians had come into the plain of Salamis, the Cyprian kings drew up their battle line. They chose the best of the troops from Salamis and from Soli to face the Persians, and set the other Cyprians against the rest of the enemy's troops. And Onesilus, by his own choice, took his place in the line facing Artybius, the Persian commander.

111. Now Artybius rode a horse that had been trained to rear up against a hoplite. Onesilus heard about this. He had a shield-bearer, a Carian by birth, who had a fine reputation as a soldier and was full of courage besides, and he said to him: "I am told that Artybius' horse rears straight up and attacks with both its hooves and its teeth anyone it is driven against. So think it over, and tell me right away which of the two you would rather watch for and strike, the horse or Artybius himself." His attendant answered: "My king, I am ready to do both, or either one, and in every case to do whatever you order. But I will tell you the way I think best suits your position. I say it is right for a king and commander to fight a king and commander. If you kill the enemy commander, it brings you great glory; and on the other hand, if he kills you, which heaven forbid, even death at the hands of a worthy enemy is only half a misfortune. For us who serve under you, it is right to fight the others who serve under him, and his horse. And don't be afraid of the horse's tricks at all. I promise you that after this, at least, it will never stand up against any man again." That is what he said, and soon afterward the two sides joined battle, on land and with their ships.

112. On that day the Ionians fought outstandingly and beat the Phoenicians, and of the Ionians the Samians fought best. Meanwhile on land the armies met, came to close quarters, and fought. As for the two commanders, this is what happened. When Artybius rode at Onesilus on his horse, Onesilus, as he had arranged with his shield-bearer, struck at Artybius himself as he came at him. And when the horse planted its hooves on Onesilus' shield, the Carian struck with a curved sword and cut off the horse's feet.

113. So Artybius, the Persian commander, fell there on the spot, together with his horse. While the rest were still fighting, Stesenor, the tyrant of Curium, deserted them with a large body of men (these men of Curium are said to be settlers from Argos), and as soon as the men of Curium deserted, the war chariots of the men of Salamis did the same as they had. When this happened, the Persians got the upper hand over the Cyprians. Their army was routed, and many fell. Among them were Onesilus son of Chersis, the man who had brought about the Cyprians' revolt, and also the king of Soli, Aristocyprus son of Philocyprus: that same Philocyprus whom Solon the Athenian, when he came to Cyprus, praised in verse above all other tyrants.

114. The people of Amathus cut off Onesilus' head, because he had besieged them. They took it to Amathus and hung it over the city gate. As the head hung there, once it had become hollow, a swarm of bees got into it and filled it with honeycomb. When this happened, the people of Amathus consulted an oracle about the head. The answer told them to take it down and bury it, and to sacrifice to Onesilus every year as a hero; if they did this, things would go better for them.

115. The people of Amathus went on doing this down to my own time. As for the Ionians who had fought the sea battle off Cyprus, they saw that Onesilus' cause was lost and that the Cyprian cities were under siege, all except Salamis, and that the Salaminians had handed Salamis over to Gorgus, their former king. As soon as they saw this, the Ionians sailed back to Ionia. Of the cities in Cyprus, Soli held out against the siege the longest. The Persians took it in the fifth month by digging a tunnel under the wall all around.

116. So the Cyprians, after being free for one year, had been made slaves once again. Meanwhile Daurises, who was married to a daughter of Darius, and Hymaees and Otanes, who were also Persian commanders and also married to daughters of Darius, had chased the Ionians who had marched on Sardis, beaten them in battle and driven them back to their ships by force. After this they divided the cities among themselves and set about sacking them.

117. Daurises headed for the cities on the Hellespont. He took Dardanus, Abydos, Percote, Lampsacus and Paesus, one each day. As he was marching from Paesus against the city of Parium, word came that the Carians had made common cause with the Ionians and revolted from the Persians. So he turned back from the Hellespont and marched his army against Caria.

118. As it happened, news of this reached the Carians before Daurises arrived. When they heard it, the Carians gathered at the place called the "White Pillars," on the river Marsyas, which flows from the region of Idrias and empties into the Maeander. When the Carians had gathered there, many plans were put forward. The best of them, I think, was that of Pixodarus son of Mausolus, a man from Cindya, who was married to the daughter of Syennesis, king of the Cilicians. His advice was that the Carians should cross the Maeander and fight the Persians with the river at their backs. That way the Carians, unable to run away and forced to stand where they were, would prove even braver fighters than they naturally were. This advice did not carry the day. Instead they decided that the Persians, and not they themselves, should have the Maeander at their backs. Their idea, clearly, was that if the Persians were routed and beaten in the battle, they would never get home but would end up in the river.

119. After this the Persians arrived and crossed the Maeander, and the Carians met them on the river Marsyas. They fought a hard battle that went on a long time, but in the end the Carians were beaten by sheer numbers. As many as two thousand Persians fell, but ten thousand Carians. Those of them who escaped were penned in at Labraunda, in the sanctuary of Zeus Stratius, Zeus of Armies, which is a large sacred grove of plane trees. The Carians are the only people we know of who sacrifice to Zeus Stratius. Penned in there, they were discussing how to save themselves: whether they would do better to surrender to the Persians or to leave Asia altogether.

120. While they were discussing this, the Milesians and their allies came to their aid. Then the Carians dropped the plans they had been considering and got ready to start the war all over again. When the Persians came to attack them, they met them and fought a battle, and were beaten even worse than before. Many fell on every side, but the Milesians suffered most.

121. Later on, though, the Carians made up for this loss and recovered from their defeat. They learned that the Persians had set out to march against their cities, and they laid an ambush on the road near Pedasus. The Persians fell into it by night and were wiped out, they and their commanders, Daurises, Amorges and Sisimaces; and Myrsus son of Gyges died with them too. The leader of this ambush was Heraclides son of Ibanollis, a man from Mylasa.

122. That was how these Persians were destroyed. Meanwhile Hymaees, another of those who had pursued the Ionians who marched on Sardis, headed for the Propontis and took Cius in Mysia. After conquering this city, when he heard that Daurises had left the Hellespont and was marching toward Caria, he left the Propontis and led his army to the Hellespont. There he conquered all the Aeolians who live in the region of Troy, and also the Gergithes, who are what is left of the ancient Teucrians. While he was conquering these peoples, Hymaees himself died of an illness in the Troad.

123. So he came to his end. Then Artaphernes, the governor of the province of Sardis, was appointed, together with Otanes, the third of the commanders, to march against Ionia and the part of Aeolis next to it. In Ionia they took the city of Clazomenae, and in Aeolis, Cyme.

124. While the cities were being taken, Aristagoras of Miletus showed, in this case, that he was not a man of any great courage. He had stirred up Ionia and set great things going, and when he saw how matters stood, he thought of running away. Besides, it had become clear to him that there was no way to beat King Darius. With all this in mind, he called the men of his own party together and talked it over with them. He told them it would be better to have a refuge ready for themselves, in case they were driven out of Miletus after all. And he put the question to them whether he should lead them from there to Sardinia to found a colony, or to Myrcinus in the land of the Edonians, which Histiaeus had been fortifying after receiving it as a gift from Darius. That was the question Aristagoras put to them.

125. Now the advice of Hecataeus son of Hegesander, the historian, was that he should not lead a colony to either of these places. Instead, if he were forced out of Miletus, he should build himself a fortress on the island of Leros and lie low. Later, using this as his base, he would be able to return to Miletus.

126. That was Hecataeus' advice. But Aristagoras leaned most toward going to Myrcinus. So he handed the government of Miletus over to Pythagoras, a respected man among the citizens, and he himself sailed off to Thrace, taking with him everyone who wanted to go, and took possession of the region he had set out for. But setting out from there to make war, he was killed by the Thracians, both Aristagoras himself and his army, while he was camped around a certain city whose Thracian defenders wanted to leave it under a truce.
