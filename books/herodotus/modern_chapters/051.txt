Chapter 51: The Battle of Mycale, the Wife of Masistes, and the Fall of Sestos

90. Now on the same day that the Persians were defeated at Plataea, as luck would have it, another defeat took place at Mycale in Ionia. This is how it came about. The Greeks who had come with the fleet under Leotychides the Spartan were lying at Delos, and envoys came to them there from Samos: Lampon son of Thrasycles, Athenagoras son of Archestratides and Hegesistratus son of Aristagoras. The people of Samos had sent them without the knowledge of the Persians or of the tyrant Theomestor son of Androdamas, whom the Persians had set up as tyrant of Samos. When they had been brought before the commanders, Hegesistratus spoke at great length and used every kind of argument. He said that as soon as the Ionians saw the Greeks they would revolt from the Persians at once, and that the Persians would not stay to meet their attack; and if after all they did stay, the Greeks would take a prize such as they would never find again. He appealed to the gods the two peoples worshiped in common and urged them to rescue men who were Greeks from slavery and to drive the foreigner away. This, he said, would be easy for them to do, because the enemy's ships sailed badly and were no match for theirs in a fight. And if the Greeks suspected that the envoys were trying to lead them on by a trick, the envoys were ready to be carried off in the Greek ships as hostages.

91. As the man from Samos pressed his request so hard, Leotychides asked him a question, either because he wanted to hear the answer for the sake of the omen, or perhaps by some chance that heaven brought about. "My friend from Samos," he said, "what is your name?" He said, "Hegesistratus," which means "Leader of the Army." Leotychides cut short the rest of his speech and stopped everything Hegesistratus meant to say next. "I accept the omen in your name, Hegesistratus, my friend from Samos," he said. "Now, for your part, see that you give us a pledge, you and the men with you, that the Samians will be our eager allies without fail. Then sail away home."

92. So he spoke, and he followed the words with the deed. The Samians at once gave their pledge and swore oaths of alliance with the Greeks. When this was done the other envoys sailed away home, but Leotychides told Hegesistratus to sail with the Greeks, since he took the man's name as a sign of success. The Greeks waited where they were that day, and on the next they made sacrifices to see whether the signs were good. Their diviner was Deiphonus son of Evenius, a man of Apollonia, the Apollonia that lies on the Ionian Gulf.

93. Here is what happened to this man's father, Evenius. At this Apollonia there are sheep sacred to the Sun. By day they graze along a river that runs from Mount Lacmon through the land of Apollonia down to the sea by the harbor of Oricus. By night they are guarded by men chosen for the job, the citizens most highly regarded for wealth and noble birth, each man taking charge of them for a year, because the people of Apollonia set great store by these sheep on account of an oracle. They are penned at night in a cave some distance from the city. At the time I am speaking of, this Evenius had been chosen and was keeping watch over them. One night he fell asleep on watch, and wolves got into the cave and killed about sixty of the sheep. When he saw what had happened he kept it secret and told no one, meaning to buy other sheep and put them in place of the ones that had been killed. But the people of Apollonia found out what had happened. When they learned of it they brought him to trial and sentenced him to be blinded for falling asleep on watch. But as soon as they had blinded Evenius, their flocks stopped bearing young and their land stopped yielding crops as it had before. They asked the oracles at Dodona and at Delphi the cause of the trouble they were suffering, and the prophets at both places told them that they had done wrong in blinding Evenius, the guardian of the sacred sheep. The gods they were asking had sent the wolves against the sheep themselves, the prophets said, and the gods would not stop taking vengeance for him until the people of Apollonia had paid Evenius whatever compensation he himself chose and thought was enough. Once that was done, the gods would give Evenius a gift of a kind that would make many people call him happy to have it.

94. These were the oracles the people of Apollonia received. They kept them secret and gave some of their citizens the job of handling the matter, and this is how those men handled it. Once when Evenius was sitting on a bench in public, they came and sat down beside him and talked about other things. At last they came around to sympathizing with him over his misfortune, and leading him on in this way, they asked what compensation he would choose if the people of Apollonia were willing to make up to him for what they had done. He had not heard the oracle, so he made his choice. He named certain citizens whom he knew to own the two best farms in Apollonia, and said that if he were given their land, and with it a house he knew to be the finest in the city, if he became the owner of these, he would bear the people no grudge from then on, and that compensation would be enough for him if he got it. While he was saying this, the men sitting beside him broke in: "Evenius, the people of Apollonia pay you this compensation for your blinding, in obedience to the oracles they have been given." At this he was angry, since now he learned the whole story and saw that he had been tricked. But they bought the property from its owners and gave him what he had chosen. And immediately after this he had the gift of prophecy born in him, so that he became very famous.

95. This Evenius, then, was the father of Deiphonus, who had been brought by the Corinthians and was serving as diviner for the army. But I have also heard that Deiphonus made false use of Evenius' name and took work of this kind all over Greece when he was not really Evenius' son at all.

96. When the sacrifices came out favorable for the Greeks, they put to sea from Delos and headed for Samos. They arrived off Calamisa in Samian territory, anchored there opposite the temple of Hera that stands at that place, and got ready for a sea battle. But the Persians, learning that the Greeks were sailing against them, put to sea as well and crossed over to the mainland with the ships they had left, since the Phoenician ships had already been sent home. They had talked the matter over and decided not to fight at sea, because they did not think they were a match for the enemy. They sailed to the mainland so as to be under the protection of their army at Mycale. This army had stayed behind from the rest of the forces on Xerxes' orders and was keeping watch over Ionia. It numbered 60,000 men, and its commander was Tigranes, who was the handsomest and tallest of all the Persians. The commanders of the fleet, then, had decided to take refuge under the protection of this army, to haul their ships up on shore, and to build a wall around them that would protect the ships and give themselves a place of refuge.

97. Having decided this, they put to sea. They sailed along past the temple of the Revered Goddesses to Gaeson and Scolopoeis in Mycale, where there is a temple of Eleusinian Demeter, built by Philistus son of Pasicles when he went with Neleus son of Codrus to found Miletus. There they hauled their ships up on shore and built a wall of stones and timber around them, cutting down orchard trees for the purpose. They drove stakes in around the wall and got ready either to stand a siege or to win a victory, since in their preparations they planned for both outcomes.

98. When the Greeks learned that the Persians had gone off to the mainland, they were annoyed, because they thought the enemy had escaped them, and they were at a loss what to do: whether to go back home or to sail down toward the Hellespont. In the end they decided to do neither, but to sail on to the mainland. So they got ready the boarding-bridges and everything else needed for a sea battle and sailed toward Mycale. When they came near the camp, no one put out to meet them. Instead they saw ships hauled up inside the wall and a large army drawn up along the shore. Then Leotychides first sailed along the coast in his ship, coming as close to the shore as he could, and had a herald make this proclamation to the Ionians: "Ionians, those of you who happen to be within hearing, listen to what I say, because the Persians will not understand a word of my instructions to you. When we join battle, each of you must remember first the freedom of everyone, and then the watchword 'Hebe.' And anyone who has not heard this, let him learn it from someone who has." The idea behind this was the same as Themistocles' at Artemisium: either the words would get past the Persians and persuade the Ionians, or they would be reported to the Persians and make them distrust the Greeks.

99. After Leotychides had made this suggestion, the Greeks next brought their ships in to land and went ashore on the beach, and there they began forming up for battle. When the Persians saw the Greeks preparing to fight, and saw too that they had appealed to the Ionians, they first took away the Samians' weapons, because they suspected them of leaning to the Greek side. This is why. Some Athenian prisoners, men whom Xerxes' army had found left behind in Attica, had been brought over in the Persian ships, and the Samians had ransomed them all and sent them back to Athens, providing them with what they needed for the journey. This was the chief reason they were under suspicion: they had ransomed five hundred of Xerxes' enemies. Second, the Persians assigned the Milesians to guard the paths that lead up to the peaks of Mycale, on the pretext that they knew the country best, but in fact to get them out of the camp. In this way the Persians protected themselves against those of the Ionians who, they suspected, would turn against them if they got the chance. Then they set up their wicker shields side by side to make a barricade for themselves.

100. When the Greeks had finished all their preparations, they advanced against the Persians. As they went, a rumor suddenly spread through the whole army, and at the same moment a herald's staff was found lying on the beach. The rumor that went through the army was this: that the Greeks were fighting in Boeotia and beating Mardonius' army. There are many signs that the divine has a hand in human affairs, and this is one of them. On the day when the defeat at Plataea and the one about to take place at Mycale happened to fall together, a rumor reached the Greeks at Mycale, so that the army took far more heart and was far more eager to face the danger.

101. And there was another coincidence too: next to both battlefields there was a sacred precinct of Eleusinian Demeter. At Plataea, as I said before, the fighting took place right beside the temple of Demeter, and at Mycale it was going to be the same. And the rumor that reached them, that the Greeks under Pausanias had already won a victory, turned out to be true. The battle at Plataea was fought while it was still early in the morning, and the fighting at Mycale took place in the afternoon. That the two happened on the same day of the same month became clear to them not long afterward, when they looked into it. Before the rumor arrived they had been afraid, not so much for themselves as for the Greeks in general, that Greece might stumble and fall over Mardonius. But when this news came to them so suddenly, they went at the enemy much more boldly and swiftly than before. So the Greeks and the Persians were both going eagerly into battle, since the islands and the Hellespont were set before them as the prizes of the fight.

102. For the Athenians and those posted next to them, who made up perhaps half the army, the way lay along the beach and over level ground. But the Spartans and those posted with them had to go around by a ravine and along the mountainside. So while the Spartans were still on their way around, the men on the other wing were already starting the fight. As long as the Persians' wicker shields were still standing, the Persians held their ground and even had rather the better of it. But then the Athenians and those next to them, wanting the victory to be theirs and not the Spartans', urged each other on and threw themselves into the work harder, and from that moment the fortunes of the battle changed. They pushed through the wicker shields and charged the Persians all together in one body. The Persians held off their first attack and went on fighting for a long time, but at last they fled to the wall. The Athenians, Corinthians, Sicyonians and Troezenians, which was the order they were drawn up in, followed close on their heels and burst in with them into the space inside the wall. Once the wall had been taken too, the rest of the king's army gave up resisting and turned at once to flight, all except the Persians themselves. They formed into small groups and kept on fighting the Greeks as they poured in through the wall. Of the Persian commanders, two escaped and two were killed. Artayntes and Ithamitres, the commanders of the fleet, escaped; Mardontes and the commander of the land army, Tigranes, were killed.

103. While the Persians were still fighting, the Spartans and those with them arrived and helped finish off the rest of the work. Many of the Greeks themselves fell there, and especially many of the Sicyonians, together with their commander Perilaus. As for the Samians who were serving with the enemy in the Persian camp and had been disarmed, when they saw that the battle was in doubt from the very start, they did all they could to help the Greeks. And when the other Ionians saw that the Samians had shown the way, they too revolted from the Persians and attacked the enemy.

104. The Milesians, too, had been assigned to guard the paths for the Persians in order to keep them safe: if what actually happened should happen after all, the Persians would have guides and could get away safely to the peaks of Mycale. The Milesians had been given this job for that reason, and also for fear that, if they stayed in the camp, they might turn against the Persians. But they did the exact opposite of what they had been assigned to do. They led the fleeing Persians down the wrong paths, paths that actually led toward the enemy, and in the end they themselves became the Persians' worst enemies and began killing them. And so, for the second time, Ionia revolted from the Persians.

105. In this battle the Athenians were the best of the Greeks, and the best of the Athenians was Hermolycus son of Euthoenus, a man who had trained for the pancration, the all-in contest of boxing and wrestling. Later, after these events, when the Athenians were at war with the Carystians, this Hermolycus was killed in battle at Cyrnus, in Carystian territory near Geraestus, and was buried there. After the Athenians, the Corinthians, Troezenians and Sicyonians fought best.

106. When the Greeks had killed most of the enemy, some in the battle and others as they fled, they first carried the plunder out onto the beach and then set fire to the ships and the whole wall. Among the rest they found some chests of money. After burning the wall and the ships they sailed away. When they reached Samos, the Greeks discussed moving the people of Ionia out. They considered where in the parts of Greece under their control they ought to settle them, leaving Ionia to the Persians. It was clear to them that they could not stand guard over the Ionians forever, and that if they did not, they had no hope that the Ionians would get off unpunished by the Persians. So the Peloponnesians in command decided that they should clear out the trading towns belonging to those Greek peoples who had gone over to the Persians, and give that land to the Ionians to live in. But the Athenians did not think the Ionians should be moved at all, and they did not think it was for the Peloponnesians to make decisions about Athenian colonies. Since the Athenians opposed the plan so strongly, the Peloponnesians gave way. So in the end they took into their alliance the Samians, Chians and Lesbians, and the other islanders who happened to be serving with the Greeks, binding them by pledges and oaths to stay loyal and never desert the alliance. After binding them by oath, they sailed off to break up the bridges, since they supposed they would find them still stretched across the strait.

So they were sailing for the Hellespont.

107. Meanwhile those of the enemy who had escaped and been driven up to the heights of Mycale, not many in number, were making their way to Sardis. On the road, Masistes son of Darius, who had been there for the disaster, kept insulting the commander Artayntes. Among other things, he said that the way Artayntes had led the army made him worse than a woman, and that he deserved every kind of punishment for bringing such harm on the king's house. Now among the Persians there is no worse insult than to be called worse than a woman. After Artayntes had taken a great deal of abuse, he finally lost his temper and drew his sword on Masistes, meaning to kill him. But as he was rushing at him, Xenagoras son of Prexilaus, a man of Halicarnassus, who was standing just behind Artayntes, saw what was happening. He grabbed Artayntes around the waist, lifted him up and threw him to the ground, and in the meantime Masistes' spearmen stepped in front of their master to protect him. That is what Xenagoras did, and by it he earned the gratitude of Masistes and also of Xerxes, for saving his brother's life. For this deed the king gave Xenagoras the rule of all Cilicia. Nothing else happened on the road beyond this, and they reached Sardis.

As it happened, King Xerxes had been at Sardis ever since he came there in flight from Athens, after his defeat in the sea battle.

108. While he was at Sardis this time, it seems, he fell passionately in love with the wife of Masistes, who was also there. But she would not give in to the messages he sent her, and he did not want to use force, out of respect for his brother Masistes. The same thing kept the woman firm, since she knew very well that force would not be used on her. So Xerxes gave up every other approach and arranged a marriage between his own son Darius and the daughter of this woman and Masistes, supposing that by doing this he would get the mother more easily. He made the betrothal, carried out all the customary ceremonies and then went off to Susa. But when he had arrived there and brought the girl into his own household as Darius' wife, he stopped pursuing Masistes' wife. His desire turned instead to Darius' wife, the daughter of Masistes, and he got her. This woman's name was Artaynte.

109. As time went on, though, the affair came to light, and this is how. Xerxes' wife Amestris had woven a large cloak, richly embroidered and well worth seeing, and she gave it to Xerxes. He was delighted with it, put it on and went to Artaynte. He was delighted with her too, and he told her to ask for anything she wanted in return for the favors she had given him, because whatever she asked for, he said, she would get. And she, since she and her whole house were fated to come to a miserable end, said to Xerxes: "Will you give me whatever I ask you for?" He supposed she would ask for anything but what she did ask for, so he promised and swore to it. Once he had sworn, she boldly asked for the cloak. Xerxes tried every way he could to talk her out of it, because he did not want to give it to her, for no other reason than that he was afraid of Amestris: she already had some suspicion of what was going on, and this would give him away to her. He offered Artaynte cities instead, and as much gold as she liked, and an army that no one but she would command. An army, by the way, is a thoroughly Persian gift. But since he could not persuade her, he gave her the cloak. She was overjoyed with the gift, and she wore it and showed it off.

110. Amestris was told that Artaynte had the cloak. When she learned what was going on, she was not angry with the girl. Instead she supposed that the mother was to blame and was behind the whole thing, and she plotted the destruction of Masistes' wife. She waited until her husband Xerxes was holding the royal banquet. This banquet is served once a year, on the king's birthday, and its name in Persian is tycta, which in Greek means "complete." On this occasion alone the king washes his head, and he gives gifts to the Persians. Amestris waited for this day and then asked Xerxes to give her Masistes' wife. Xerxes thought it a strange and terrible thing to hand his brother's wife over to her, especially since the woman was innocent in this business; for he understood why Amestris was asking.

111. But she kept pleading, and he was bound by the custom that at the royal banquet no one who makes a request of the king may be refused. So at last, very much against his will, he agreed. He handed the woman over and told Amestris to do as she liked. Meanwhile he sent for his brother and said: "Masistes, you are the son of Darius and my brother, and besides that you are a good man. I tell you, do not live any longer with the wife you live with now. I will give you my own daughter in her place. Live with her as your wife, and do not keep the wife you have now, because I do not think it right that you should keep her." Masistes was astonished at this, and said: "Master, what a useless thing you are saying to me! You tell me to send away a wife by whom I have sons who are grown young men, and daughters, one of whom you yourself took as a wife for your son; and she herself, as it happens, is very dear to me. You tell me to send her away and marry your daughter! My king, I count it a very great honor to be thought worthy of your daughter, but all the same I will do neither of these things. Do not force me to do such a thing. Another husband will be found for your daughter who is every bit as good as I am. Let me, I beg you, go on living with my own wife." That was more or less his answer, and Xerxes, stirred to anger, said: "Then this is how things stand for you, Masistes. I will not give you my daughter as your wife, and you will not live any longer with that one either, so that you may learn to take what you are offered." When Masistes heard this he went out, saying only: "Master, you have not destroyed me, surely?"

112. All this time, while Xerxes was talking with his brother, Amestris had sent for Xerxes' spearmen to bring Masistes' wife, and she was mutilating her horribly. She cut off her breasts and threw them to the dogs, and she cut off her nose and ears and lips and tongue, and sent her home in that condition.

113. Masistes had not yet heard any of this, but he suspected that some disaster had fallen on him, and he came running to his house. When he saw his wife mutilated like this, he at once talked it over with his sons and set out for Bactria with them, and no doubt with some others too, meaning to make the province of Bactria revolt and do the king all the harm he could. And this, I imagine, is what would have happened, if he had reached the country of the Bactrians and the Sacae before he was caught; for they were very devoted to him, and he was governor of the Bactrians. But Xerxes learned what he was doing and sent an army after him while he was on the road, and it killed him, and his sons, and his troops. So much for the story of Xerxes' passion and the death of Masistes.

114. Meanwhile the Greeks who had set out from Mycale for the Hellespont first anchored off Lectum, held up on their voyage by the winds. From there they came to Abydos and found that the bridges had been broken up. They had expected to find them still stretched across, and that was the main reason they had come to the Hellespont. So the Peloponnesians under Leotychides decided to sail back to Greece, while the Athenians and their commander Xanthippus decided to stay there and make an attempt on the Chersonese. So the Peloponnesians sailed away, and the Athenians crossed over from Abydos to the Chersonese and laid siege to Sestos.

115. Sestos was the strongest fortress in that region, so when the people of the surrounding towns heard that the Greeks had arrived at the Hellespont, they gathered there. In particular there came from the city of Cardia a Persian named Oeobazus, who had brought the cables of the bridges to Sestos. The people of the town were Aeolians, natives of the country, but a large number of Persians and of their allies were living with them.

116. The tyrant of the province, ruling as governor under Xerxes, was Artayctes, a Persian but a desperate and reckless man. On the king's march against Athens he had also played a trick on him, by carrying off from Elaeus the treasures belonging to Protesilaus son of Iphiclus. At Elaeus in the Chersonese there is the tomb of Protesilaus, with a sacred precinct around it. There were many treasures there, gold and silver cups, bronze, clothing and other offerings, and Artayctes carried them off as plunder, with the king's permission. He tricked Xerxes by saying something like this to him: "Master, there is a house here belonging to a Greek who made war on your land, got what he deserved and was killed. Give me this man's house, so that everyone may learn not to make war on your land." By saying this he could expect to persuade Xerxes easily enough to give him a man's house, since Xerxes had no suspicion of what he had in mind. When he said that Protesilaus had made war on the king's land, you have to understand that the Persians consider all Asia to be theirs and to belong to whichever king is reigning. So when the treasures had been given to him, he brought them from Elaeus to Sestos, and he planted the sacred precinct with crops and farmed it as his own. And whenever he came to Elaeus, he himself had sex with women in the inner shrine of the temple. Now he was being besieged by the Athenians, though he had made no preparations for a siege and had never expected the Greeks to come; they fell on him, you might say, with no way for him to escape.

117. But when autumn came and the siege was still going on, the Athenians grew restless at being away from their own country and at not being able to take the fortress. They asked their commanders to lead them home. The commanders said they would not, until either they had taken the town or the Athenian people officially recalled them. So the men put up with things as they were.

118. Inside the walls, meanwhile, the people had come to the depths of misery, so that they boiled the leather straps of their beds and ate them. When they no longer had even these, the Persians, and with them Artayctes and Oeobazus, ran away in the night. They climbed down at the back of the wall, where the enemy had left the place least guarded. When day came, the people of the Chersonese signaled to the Athenians from the towers what had happened and opened the gates to them. Most of the Athenians went in pursuit, and the rest took over the town.

119. Oeobazus escaped into Thrace, but there the Apsinthian Thracians caught him and sacrificed him to their local god Pleistorus with their own rites. The men who were with him they killed in another way. Artayctes and his companions had set out later in their flight, and they were overtaken a little above Aegospotami. They defended themselves for a good while, and some of them were killed and others taken alive. The Greeks tied them up and were bringing them to Sestos, and among them, in chains, were Artayctes and his son.

120. Then, the people of the Chersonese say, one of the guards was frying dried fish, and a portent happened. The dried fish, as they lay on the fire, began to jump and flop about just like fish freshly caught. The others gathered around and stared in amazement at the portent. But when Artayctes saw it, he called to the man who was frying the fish and said: "My Athenian friend, don't be afraid of this portent at all, because it has not appeared for you, but for me. Protesilaus of Elaeus is telling me by it that even though he is dead and dried up like these fish, he has power from the gods to take vengeance on the man who wrongs him. So now I am willing to pay this penalty to him: in place of the treasures I took from the temple, I will pay a hundred talents to the god, and as ransom for myself and my son I will pay two hundred talents to the Athenians, if my life is spared." That was his offer, but he did not win over the commander, Xanthippus. The people of Elaeus wanted vengeance for Protesilaus and asked for Artayctes to be put to death, and the commander himself leaned the same way. So they took him to the headland where Xerxes had made his crossing, or, as some say, to the hill above the town of Madytus, and there they nailed him to planks and hung him up. And they stoned his son to death before the eyes of Artayctes himself.

121. After doing this they sailed away to Greece, taking with them, among other things, the cables of the bridges, to dedicate them as offerings in the temples. And nothing more happened that year.

122. Now one of the ancestors of this Artayctes who was hung up was that Artembares who put a proposal to the Persians, which they took up and brought before Cyrus. It went like this: "Since Zeus has given the Persians the leadership, and you above all men, Cyrus, by overthrowing Astyages, come: since the land we hold is small, and rugged too, let us move out of it and settle in another that is better. There are many close at hand, and many farther off. If we take one of them, we shall be held in greater awe, and by more people. And it is only natural for men who rule to do this; for when will there ever be a better chance than now, when we rule many nations and the whole of Asia?" Cyrus heard this and was not surprised by the proposal. He told them to go ahead if they wanted, but he warned them that if they did, they had better be ready to stop being rulers and become subjects instead. "Soft lands breed soft men," he said. "The same soil does not grow both wonderful crops and men who are good at war." So the Persians admitted that he was right and went away, beaten by Cyrus' judgment. They chose to live in a poor land and rule, rather than to sow crops on a level plain and be slaves to others.
