Chapter 44: The Sea Fights off Artemisium, and the Attack on Delphi

1. These were the Greeks who had been assigned to serve in the fleet. The Athenians supplied 127 ships. The Plataeans, moved by courage and eagerness for the cause, joined the Athenians in manning those ships, even though they had no experience of the sea at all. The Corinthians supplied 40 ships and the Megarians 20. The Chalcidians manned 20 ships, which the Athenians provided for them. The Aeginetans supplied 18 ships, the Sicyonians 12, the Spartans 10, the Epidaurians 8, the Eretrians 7, the Troezenians 5, and the Styrians 2. The Ceans supplied 2 ships and 2 fifty-oared galleys. And the Locrians of Opus also came to help the rest with 7 fifty-oared galleys.

2. These were the peoples who joined the expedition to Artemisium, and I have listed them by the number of ships each supplied. So the number of ships gathered at Artemisium, not counting the fifty-oared galleys, was 271. The commander with supreme authority was supplied by the Spartans: Eurybiades son of Eurycleides. This was because the allies said they would not follow the Athenians' lead, and that unless a Spartan was in command they would break up the expedition that was being planned.

3. The reason was that, from the very beginning, even before they sent to Sicily to look for allies, there had been talk that the fleet ought to be put under the Athenians' command. But since the allies opposed this, the Athenians gave way. They cared deeply that Greece should be saved, and they saw that if they quarreled with one another over who should lead, Greece would be lost. And in this they judged rightly: civil strife among people of the same race is worse than a war fought with one mind, by as much as war is worse than peace. Since they understood this, they did not press their claim but gave way, for as long as they badly needed the allies. Their later conduct proved it. For once they had driven the Persians out of their own country and were fighting for the Persians' land rather than their own, they used Pausanias' arrogance as an excuse and took the leadership away from the Spartans. But that happened later.

4. At this point, though, the Greeks who had come to Artemisium saw that a great many ships had put in at Aphetae and that the whole place was full of the enemy's forces. They were struck with fear, because things had turned out for the Persians very differently from what they had expected, and they began to discuss retreating from Artemisium into the interior of Greece. When the Euboeans saw them discussing this, they asked Eurybiades to stay with them for a short while, until they could get their children and their households out of the country. They could not persuade him, so they went elsewhere and persuaded Themistocles, the Athenian commander, with a payment of thirty talents, on condition that the fleet would stay and fight the sea battle in front of Euboea.

5. This is how Themistocles got the Greeks to stay. He gave Eurybiades five talents of the money, pretending that it came from his own pocket. Once Eurybiades had been talked round, Adeimantus son of Ocytus, the Corinthian commander, was the only one of all the rest who still held out. He said he would sail away from Artemisium and would not stay with the others. So Themistocles said to him, with an oath: "You at least will not abandon us. I will give you bigger presents than the king of the Persians would send you for deserting your allies." As he said this, he sent three talents of silver over to Adeimantus' ship. So all of them were won over by gifts and changed their minds; at the same time the Euboeans got what they had asked for, and Themistocles himself made a profit. No one knew that he had kept the rest of the money, and those who got a share of it were fully convinced that it had come from the Athenian state for this purpose.

6. So they stayed at Euboea and fought a sea battle, and this is how it happened. The Persians arrived at Aphetae early in the afternoon. They had already been told before they came that a few Greek ships were stationed around Artemisium, and now they could see them for themselves, so they were eager to attack and see if they could capture them. But they did not think it wise to sail straight at them yet, for fear that the Greeks would see them coming and take to flight, and that darkness would come down on them as they fled. That way, the Persians thought, they would probably get away; whereas by the Persians' reckoning not even the fire-bearer should escape with his life. (The fire-bearer carried the sacred fire for an army's sacrifices and was spared by custom, so "not even the fire-bearer" meant not a single man.)

7. With this in mind they worked out the following plan. From their whole fleet they detached two hundred ships and sent them around Euboea, past Caphereus and around Geraestus to the Euripus. These ships went outside Sciathus so that the enemy would not see them sailing around Euboea. The idea was that this squadron would come up that way and block the Greeks' retreat, while the main fleet advanced on them from the front, and so they would surround them on every side. Having made this plan, they sent off the ships assigned to it. They themselves had no intention of attacking the Greeks that day, or until the ships sailing around showed them the agreed signal that they had arrived. So they were sending these ships around, and meanwhile they were counting the rest of the fleet at Aphetae.

8. While they were counting their ships, this happened. There was in that camp a man from Scione named Scyllias, the best diver of anyone in his time. In the shipwreck off Pelion he had saved a great deal of property for the Persians, and he had also taken a great deal for himself. This Scyllias, it seems, had intended for some time to desert to the Greeks, but until then he had had no chance to do it. How he actually reached the Greeks after this, I can't say for certain, but I am amazed if the story people tell is true. For it is said that he dived into the sea at Aphetae and did not come up until he reached Artemisium, covering a distance of about nine miles underwater. There are several other stories told about this man that look like lies, but some that are true. On this matter, though, let me give it as my opinion that he came to Artemisium in a boat. As soon as he arrived, he told the commanders about the shipwreck and how it had happened, and about the ships that had been sent around Euboea.

9. When the Greeks heard this, they talked it over among themselves. After a good deal of argument, the view that won was that they should stay where they were that day and camp on shore, and then, after midnight had passed, put to sea and go to meet the ships sailing around Euboea. But after this, since no one sailed out to attack them, they waited until late in the afternoon and then sailed out themselves to attack the Persians. They wanted to test the enemy's way of fighting and their maneuver of breaking through the line.

10. When the men in Xerxes' army, and their commanders too, saw the Greeks sailing against them like this with so few ships, they thought they must be out of their minds. They put out to sea themselves, expecting to capture them easily. And it was a reasonable expectation, since they could see that the Greek ships were few, while their own were many times as numerous and sailed better. With this confidence they came around and closed the Greeks in, in the middle. Now some of the Ionians were friendly to the Greeks and were serving in the expedition against their will. When they saw the Greeks being surrounded, they were deeply grieved, convinced that not one of them would get home again, so weak did they think the Greek force was. But those Ionians who were pleased by what was happening competed with one another, each trying to be the first to take an Athenian ship and win gifts from the king. For in the Persian camps there was more talk of the Athenians than of anyone else.

11. When the first signal was given, the Greeks drew up with their prows facing the enemy and their sterns pulled close together in the middle. When the second signal was given, they set to work hard, even though they were penned into a small space and fighting prow to prow. They captured thirty enemy ships, and also Philaon son of Chersis, the brother of Gorgus, king of Salamis in Cyprus, a man of great reputation in the army. The first Greek to capture an enemy ship was an Athenian, Lycomedes son of Aeschraeus, and he received the prize for valor. So they fought this sea battle with neither side winning, until nightfall parted them. The Greeks sailed back to Artemisium and the Persians to Aphetae, after a contest very different from what they had expected. In this sea fight Antidorus of Lemnos was the only one of the Greeks serving with the king who deserted to the Greek side, and for this the Athenians gave him a piece of land on Salamis.

12. When darkness had fallen, even though it was the middle of summer, there came a tremendous downpour of rain that lasted all night, with crashing thunder from Mount Pelion. The bodies of the dead and pieces of wreckage were washed up at Aphetae, where they got tangled around the prows of the ships and knocked against the blades of the oars. The men of the fleet there heard these things and grew afraid. They expected that they were certainly going to die, so many troubles had come upon them. For before they had even had a chance to catch their breath after the shipwreck and the storm off Mount Pelion, they had been hit by a hard sea battle, and after the sea battle by a violent rainstorm, with torrents rushing down to the sea and crashing thunder.

13. That was the kind of night these men had. Meanwhile the ships that had been assigned to sail around Euboea went through that very same night, but it raged much more fiercely against them, because it caught them out on the open sea. And it ended badly for them. The storm and the rain came on them as they sailed, when they were off the "Hollows" of Euboea. They were driven by the wind without knowing where they were being carried, and they were wrecked on the rocks. All this was being done by heaven, so that the Persian force would be brought nearer to the size of the Greek one and would not be so very much larger.

14. So these men were dying around the Hollows of Euboea. Meanwhile, at Aphetae, the Persians were glad to see the day dawn. They kept their ships quiet, content in their sorry state to stay still for the time being. But fifty-three Athenian ships came to reinforce the Greeks. Their arrival put new heart into the Greeks, and at the same time they were encouraged by news that the Persian ships sailing around Euboea had all been destroyed by the storm. So they waited until the same time of day as before, then sailed out and fell on some Cilician ships. After destroying these, they sailed away when darkness came on and went back to Artemisium.

15. On the third day the Persian commanders were furious that such a small number of ships should do them so much damage, and they were afraid of what Xerxes might do. So this time they did not wait for the Greeks to start the battle. They passed the word to one another and put out to sea around midday. Now it so happened that these battles at sea and the battles on land at Thermopylae were fought on the same days. For the men fighting at sea, the whole aim of the struggle was the channel of the Euripus, just as the aim of Leonidas and his men was to guard the pass. So the Greeks urged one another not to let the invaders through into Greece, while the Persians cheered one another on to destroy the Greek fleet and take control of the straits.

16. While Xerxes' fleet was sailing toward them in formation, the Greeks stayed quietly at Artemisium. The Persians arranged their ships in a crescent so as to close them in, and were trying to surround them. Then the Greeks put out to sea and engaged them. In this battle the two sides were about evenly matched. Xerxes' fleet did itself damage because of its sheer size and numbers, since its ships were thrown into confusion and kept running into one another. Even so it held its ground and did not give way, because they were too proud to be put to flight by so few ships. So many Greek ships were destroyed and many men were killed, but many more ships and men on the Persian side. After fighting in this way, they broke off and each went back to its own station.

17. In this sea battle the Egyptians did best of all those fighting for Xerxes. Besides other great feats they performed, they captured five Greek ships together with their crews. On the Greek side, those who did best that day were the Athenians, and of the Athenians Cleinias son of Alcibiades. He was serving with two hundred men and a ship of his own, paying the whole cost out of his own pocket.

18. After they broke off, both sides were glad to hurry back to their anchorages. Once they had separated and come out of the sea battle, the Greeks were in possession of the bodies of the dead and the wrecks of the ships. But they had been badly mauled, and especially the Athenians, half of whose ships were damaged. So they began to discuss retreating into the interior of Greece.

19. Themistocles, however, had come to the conclusion that if the Ionian and Carian peoples could be pried away from the Persian force, the Greeks would be able to beat the rest. The Euboeans were driving their flocks down to the shore at that point, and Themistocles called the generals together. He told them he thought he had a scheme by which he hoped to make the best of the king's allies abandon him. He revealed this much and no more. As for their present situation, he said, this is what they must do. Every one of them should slaughter as many of the Euboeans' sheep and goats as he wanted, since it was better for their own army to have them than the enemy. He also advised each of them to order his men to light a fire. As for the time of their departure, he would see to it that they got back to Greece safely. They were happy to do this, and as soon as they had lit their fires they turned their attention to the flocks.

20. For in fact the Euboeans had ignored the oracle of Bacis as if it meant nothing at all. They had not moved anything out of their country, nor had they laid in any store of food, as people do when they expect a war to come upon them; and so by their own behavior they had brought trouble on themselves. This is how the oracle Bacis gave about these things runs:

	"Watch: when a man of foreign speech yokes the sea with papyrus,
	then make your plans to move the loud-bleating goats out of Euboea."

In the troubles that were on them at this time, or soon to be expected, they had good reason to be sorry that they had paid no attention to these lines.

21. While they were busy with this, the lookout arrived from Trachis. There was a lookout at Artemisium named Polyas, a native of Anticyra, whose job was to report to the men at Thermopylae if the fleet was defeated, and he had a boat fitted out and ready for the purpose. In the same way there was an Athenian with Leonidas, Abronichus son of Lysicles, ready to carry the news to the men at Artemisium in a thirty-oared galley if anything went wrong for the land army. It was this Abronichus who now arrived, and he told them what had happened to Leonidas and his army. When they heard it, they put off their retreat no longer. They set out in the order in which each had been posted, the Corinthians first and the Athenians last.

22. Themistocles, however, picked out the fastest Athenian ships and went around to the places where there was drinking water, cutting messages into the stones there. The Ionians read them when they came to Artemisium the next day. This is what the messages said: "Men of Ionia, you are doing wrong in making war on your forefathers and trying to enslave Greece. The best thing would be for you to come over to our side. But if you cannot do that, then even now stand aside from the fight against us, and ask the Carians to do the same as you. If neither of these is possible, and you are held down by a compulsion too strong to let you revolt, then when we meet in battle hold back on purpose. Remember that you are descended from us, and that our quarrel with the foreigner began in the first place because of you." Themistocles wrote this, I suppose, with two things in mind at once. Either the messages would escape the king's notice and make the Ionians change sides and come over to the Greeks, or, if they were reported to Xerxes and the Ionians accused, they would make him distrust the Ionians, and so he would keep them out of the sea battles.

23. So Themistocles set up these inscriptions. Immediately after this, a man from Histiaea came to the Persians in a boat with the news that the Greeks had pulled out of Artemisium. The Persians did not believe it. They kept the messenger under guard and sent fast ships ahead to look. When these reported that it was true, the whole fleet sailed together to Artemisium, just as daylight was spreading across the sky. They stayed there until midday, and then sailed on to Histiaea. When they arrived, they took the city of Histiaea and overran all the villages along the coast in the district of Ellopia, which is Histiaean territory.

24. While they were there, Xerxes sent a herald to the fleet, after first making arrangements about the bodies of the dead. The arrangements he made beforehand were these. Of all the men of his army who lay dead at Thermopylae, and there were as many as twenty thousand in all, he left about a thousand where they were. The rest he buried in trenches he had dug, covering them with leaves and heaping earth over them, so that the men of the fleet would not see them. When the herald had crossed over to Histiaea, he called an assembly of the whole force and said: "Allies, King Xerxes gives permission to any of you who wish to leave his post and come and see how he fights against those utterly senseless men who expected to defeat the power of the king."

25. When the herald had made this announcement, boats became the scarcest thing of all, so many men wanted to see the sight. They crossed over and walked among the dead bodies, looking at them. Everyone supposed that the men lying there were all Spartans or Thespians, though there were helots too among the bodies they saw. But the men who had crossed over could not fail to see what Xerxes had done, as I mentioned, with the bodies of his own dead. In fact it was enough to make a person laugh: on one side a thousand bodies could be seen lying, while the others lay all gathered together in one place, four thousand of them. They spent that day looking, and the next day they sailed back to the ships at Histiaea, while Xerxes and his army set out on their march.

26. A few deserters from Arcadia had also come over to them, men with no means of living who wanted to be taken into service. The Persians brought these men before the king and asked them about the Greeks and what they were doing; one man asked the questions on behalf of all the rest. The Arcadians told them that the Greeks were holding the Olympic festival and watching contests of athletics and horse racing. The Persian then asked what prize was offered, for which they competed, and they told him about the wreath of olive that is given. At this Tigranes son of Artabanus spoke out a very noble thought, though it got him called a coward by the king. When he heard that the prize was a wreath and not money, he could not keep quiet, but said in front of everyone: "Mardonius, what kind of men are these you have brought us to fight, who compete not for money but for honor!" That is what this man said.

27. Meanwhile, as soon as the disaster at Thermopylae had happened, the Thessalians at once sent a herald to the Phocians. They had always had a grudge against the Phocians, and especially because of the latest defeat they had suffered at their hands. Not many years before the king's expedition, the Thessalians and their allies had invaded Phocis in full force, and the Phocians had beaten them and handled them roughly. The Phocians had been shut up on Mount Parnassus, and with them was a seer, Tellias of Elis. This Tellias came up with a trick for them. He took six hundred men, the best of the Phocians, and whitened them all over with chalk, both the men and their armor. Then he attacked the Thessalians by night, telling the Phocians beforehand to kill every man they saw who was not painted white. The Thessalian sentries saw them first and were terrified, thinking it was some supernatural thing and not what it really was; and after the sentries, the main body of the army panicked too. As a result the Phocians were left in possession of four thousand dead men and their shields. They dedicated half of the shields at Abae and half at Delphi. And from a tenth of the plunder taken in that battle were made the large statues that stand in front of the temple at Delphi, showing the struggle for the tripod; others like them are dedicated as an offering at Abae.

28. That is what the Phocians had done to the Thessalian infantry when it was besieging them. And they had done damage to their cavalry too, past all repair, when it invaded their land. In the pass near Hyampolis they dug a great trench and laid empty wine jars in it. Then they carried earth, spread it on top, and made the place look like the rest of the ground, and waited for the Thessalians to invade. The Thessalians, thinking they would make short work of the Phocians, came charging at full speed and rode straight onto the wine jars, and there their horses' legs were smashed.

29. Since they held a grudge for both of these things, the Thessalians sent a herald with this message: "Phocians, we advise you to start thinking better of it now and to admit that you are not our equals. In the past, among the Greeks, for as long as we chose to be on that side, we were always ranked above you. And now we have so much influence with the Persian that it is in our power to have you stripped of your land and sold into slavery as well. But although we hold all the power, we bear no malice. Pay us fifty talents of silver for it, and we promise to turn aside the dangers that threaten your land."

30. That was the Thessalians' proposal. For the Phocians were the only people in that whole region who were not siding with the Persians, and, as I judge, for no other reason than their feud with the Thessalians. If the Thessalians had supported the Greek cause, it is my opinion that the Phocians would have been on the Persian side. When the Thessalians made this proposal, the Phocians said they would not give the money. They said it was open to them to go over to the Persians just as much as it was to the Thessalians, if they wanted to for other reasons; but they would never of their own free will be traitors to Greece.

31. When this answer was reported, the Thessalians were angry with the Phocians and became guides to the Persian army, showing it the way. From the territory of Trachis they entered Doris. A narrow strip of Dorian land stretches in that direction, a little over three miles wide, lying between Malis and Phocis; in ancient times it was called Dryopis, and this land is the mother country of the Dorians of the Peloponnese. The Persians did not ravage this land of Doris when they entered it, because its people were siding with the Persians, and also because the Thessalians did not want it ravaged.

32. When they passed from Doris into Phocis, though, they did not catch the Phocians themselves. Some of the Phocians had gone up onto the heights of Parnassus. There is a peak of Parnassus, called Tithorea, which stands on its own near the town of Neon and has plenty of room for a great many people, and some of them had carried their goods up there and gone up themselves. But most of them had moved their goods out to the Ozolian Locrians, to the town of Amphissa, which lies above the Crissaean plain. The Persians, however, overran the whole of Phocis, since that is where the Thessalians led the army. Everything they came to on their march they burned or cut down, and they set fire to towns and temples alike.

33. They laid everything waste, advancing this way along the river Cephisus. They burned the town of Drymus, and also these: Charadra, Erochus, Tethronium, Amphicaea, Neon, Pedieis, Triteis, Elatea, Hyampolis, Parapotamii and Abae. At Abae there was a rich temple of Apollo, well stocked with treasuries and offerings, and there was an oracle there then, as there still is now. They plundered this temple and burned it. They also chased down some of the Phocians in the mountains and caught them, and some women died from being raped by many men, one after another.

34. Passing Parapotamii, the Persians came to Panopeus, and from there their army split and went different ways. The largest and strongest part of the army went with Xerxes himself against Athens and entered Boeotia, coming into the territory of Orchomenus. Now the Boeotians as a whole were siding with the Persians, and their towns were being held by Macedonians sent by Alexander, one group assigned to each town. They were holding them with this purpose: to make it plain to Xerxes that the Boeotians were on the Persian side.

35. That was the direction these Persians took. But others, with guides, had set out for the temple at Delphi, keeping Parnassus on their right. All the parts of Phocis they marched through they ravaged; they set fire to the towns of Panopeus, Daulis and Aeolis. The reason they marched that way, split off from the rest of the army, was to plunder the temple at Delphi and hand its treasures over to King Xerxes. Xerxes knew everything of any value that was in it better, I am told, than he knew the things he had left in his own palace at home, since many people kept talking about them, and especially about the offerings of Croesus son of Alyattes.

36. Meanwhile the Delphians had heard of this and were thrown into the utmost fear. In their terror they consulted the oracle about the sacred treasures, whether they should bury them in the ground or carry them away to another country. But the god told them not to touch them, saying that he was able to look after his own property himself. When they heard this, they began to think about themselves. They sent their children and women across the gulf to Achaea, while most of the men climbed up toward the peaks of Parnassus and carried their belongings into the Corycian cave, and others went off for refuge to Amphissa in Locris. In short, all the Delphians had left the town except sixty men and the prophet of the oracle.

37. When the Persians had come close and could see the temple, the prophet, whose name was Aceratus, saw weapons lying in front of the inner shrine. They had been carried out from the sanctuary, and they were sacred arms that no human being was allowed to touch. So he went to announce this omen to the Delphians who were still there. But when the Persians, pressing forward, came level with the temple of Athena Pronaea, still greater omens happened to them than the one before. That one was certainly a marvel, that weapons of war should appear of their own accord, laid out in front of the shrine; but what happened right after it deserves to be marveled at above all other wonders. Just as the Persians came level with the temple of Athena Pronaea, thunderbolts fell on them from the sky, and two crags broke off from Parnassus and came crashing down on them with a tremendous roar, falling on many of them. And from the temple of Pronaea came a shout, and a war cry was raised.

38. All these things together threw the Persians into terror. The Delphians, seeing them in flight, came down after them and killed a great many of them, and the survivors fled straight to Boeotia. Those of the Persians who got back reported, so I am told, that besides what I have described they saw other miraculous things too. Two armed men, they said, taller than any human being, followed them, killing and chasing them.

39. The Delphians say these two were the local heroes Phylacus and Autonous, whose sacred precincts are near the temple: Phylacus' right beside the road above the temple of Pronaea, and Autonous' near the Castalian spring, under the peak called Hyampea. What is more, the rocks that fell from Parnassus were still there in my own time, lying in the precinct of Athena Pronaea, where they came to rest after crashing through the Persian ranks. That is how these men left the temple.
