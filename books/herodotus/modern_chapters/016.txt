Chapter 16: The Madness of Cambyses, the Ring of Polycrates, and the Son of Periander

26. That is how the expedition against the Ethiopians turned out. Meanwhile the Persians who had been sent to march against the Ammonians set out from Thebes and went on their way with guides. It is known that they reached the city of Oasis, which is inhabited by Samians said to belong to the Aeschrionian tribe, and which lies seven days' journey from Thebes across sandy desert. In Greek this place is called the "Isle of the Blessed." The army, they say, got that far. But from there on, nobody can say anything about them, except the Ammonians themselves and those who have heard the story from them; for they never reached the Ammonians, and they never came back. The Ammonians themselves add this to the story. They say that as the army was marching from this Oasis across the sandy desert to attack them, and had reached a point about halfway between them and the Oasis, a violent south wind blew down on them while they were eating their morning meal. It carried heaps of desert sand along with it and buried them, and so they vanished and were never seen again. That, according to the Ammonians, is what happened to this army.

27. When Cambyses arrived at Memphis, Apis appeared to the Egyptians; this is the god the Greeks call Epaphus. As soon as he appeared, the Egyptians began to put on their finest clothes and to hold celebrations. Cambyses saw the Egyptians doing this and was sure they were rejoicing because things had gone badly for him. So he called for the officials in charge of Memphis. When they came before him, he asked them why the Egyptians had done nothing of the kind the last time he was at Memphis, but were doing it only now, when he had come back after losing a large part of his army. They told him that a god had appeared to them, one who usually appeared only at long intervals, and that whenever he appeared, all the Egyptians rejoiced and held a festival. When Cambyses heard this, he said they were lying, and he condemned them to death as liars.

28. After putting them to death, he next called the priests before him. When the priests gave him the same answer, he said he would find out whether some tame god had come to live among the Egyptians. With that he told the priests to bring Apis to him, and they went to fetch him. Now this Apis, or Epaphus, is a calf born of a cow that is never afterward allowed to conceive another calf. The Egyptians say that a flash of light comes down from the sky upon this cow, and from it she gives birth to Apis. This calf called Apis has the following markings: he is black, with a white square on his forehead and the likeness of an eagle on his back; the hairs of his tail are double; and on his tongue there is a mark like a beetle.

29. When the priests brought Apis in, Cambyses, who was already half mad, drew his dagger. He aimed at Apis' belly but struck his thigh. Then he laughed and said to the priests: "You miserable creatures, is this what gods are like, made of flesh and blood and able to feel an iron blade? This is certainly a god worthy of the Egyptians. But you, at least, will not get away with making a fool of me." With that he ordered the men whose job it was to whip the priests without mercy, and to put to death any other Egyptian they found celebrating the festival. So the Egyptians' festival was brought to an end, the priests were punished, and Apis lay dying in the temple from the wound in his thigh.

30. When he died of the wound, the priests buried him without Cambyses' knowledge. But Cambyses, as the Egyptians say, went completely mad immediately after this crime, though he had not really been in his right mind even before. The first of his crimes was to put to death his brother Smerdis, who had the same father and the same mother as he did. He had already sent this brother away from Egypt back to Persia out of envy, because Smerdis alone of all the Persians had been able to draw the bow that the Fish-Eaters had brought from the Ethiopian king, by about two finger-widths, an inch and a half; not one of the other Persians had managed it. Then, after Smerdis had gone back to Persia, Cambyses had a dream. It seemed to him that a messenger came from Persia and reported that Smerdis was sitting on the royal throne and that his head touched the sky. So he was afraid that his brother might kill him and reign in his place. He sent Prexaspes to Persia, the man he trusted most of all the Persians, with orders to kill him. Prexaspes went up to Susa and killed Smerdis. Some say he took him out hunting and killed him there; others say he took him to the Persian Gulf and drowned him.

31. This, they say, was the first of Cambyses' crimes. Next after this he put to death his sister, who had come with him to Egypt. He was also married to her, and she was his sister on both sides, by the same father and the same mother. This is how he came to marry her, for before this the Persians had never been in the habit of marrying their sisters at all. Cambyses fell in love with one of his sisters and wanted to marry her. Since what he had in mind was against custom, he called the Royal Judges and asked them whether there was any law that allowed a man who wished it to marry his sister. Now the Royal Judges are men chosen from among the Persians, and they hold office until they die or until they are found guilty of some injustice, and no longer. They give judgments for the Persians and interpret the laws handed down from their fathers, and every question is referred to them. So when Cambyses asked them, they gave him an answer that was both honest and safe. They said they could find no law that allowed a brother to marry his sister, but that they had found another law, which said that the king of the Persians could do whatever he wanted. So on the one hand they did not bend the law out of fear of Cambyses; and at the same time, so as not to be killed themselves for upholding the law, they found another law besides the one he had asked about, which suited a man who wanted to marry his sisters. So Cambyses then married the sister he was in love with, and not long afterward he married another sister as well. It was the younger of these, the one who had come with him to Egypt, whom he put to death.

32. About her death, as about the death of Smerdis, two different stories are told. The Greeks say that Cambyses had set a lion cub to fight a puppy, and this wife of his was watching too. When the puppy was losing, another puppy, its brother, broke its chain and came to help it, and once there were two of them instead of one, the puppies got the better of the cub. Cambyses was delighted to watch this, but she, sitting beside him, began to cry. Cambyses noticed and asked her why she was crying. She said she had cried when she saw the puppy come to help its brother, because she remembered Smerdis and realized that there was no one to come to his help. The Greeks say it was for saying this that Cambyses killed her. But the Egyptians say that as they were sitting at dinner, the wife took a head of lettuce and pulled off the leaves all around it. Then she asked her husband whether the lettuce looked better stripped like this or with all its leaves, and he said, "With all its leaves." Then she said: "And yet you once made something just like this lettuce, when you stripped bare the house of Cyrus." Cambyses was enraged and leaped on her. She was pregnant, and she miscarried and died.

33. These were the mad things Cambyses did to his own family, whether his madness really came about because of Apis or from some other cause, since many ills tend to fall upon men. For it is also said that Cambyses had suffered from birth from a serious illness, the one some people call the "sacred disease," epilepsy. And it was certainly nothing strange that when his body was suffering from a serious illness, his mind should not be sound either.

34. And here are the mad things he did to the other Persians. There was Prexaspes, the man he honored most, who used to carry his messages; Prexaspes' son was also Cambyses' cupbearer, and this too was no small honor. To him, it is said, Cambyses spoke as follows: "Prexaspes, what kind of man do the Persians think I am, and what do they say about me?" And Prexaspes said: "Master, in every other way they praise you highly, but they say you are too fond of wine." That is what he said about the Persians. At this Cambyses grew angry and answered: "So the Persians now say that I am given to wine, and that this has driven me out of my senses and out of my mind. Then what they said before was not sincere." For it seems that some time before this, when the Persians and Croesus were sitting with him in council, Cambyses had asked them what kind of man they thought he was compared with his father Cyrus. They answered that he was better than his father, because he not only held everything his father had held, but had also won Egypt and the sea besides. That is what the Persians said. But Croesus, who was there and was not satisfied with their judgment, said to Cambyses: "To me, son of Cyrus, you do not seem equal to your father, because you do not yet have a son like the one he left behind him in you." Cambyses was pleased to hear this and praised Croesus' judgment.

35. Remembering this, then, he said angrily to Prexaspes: "Now see for yourself whether the Persians are telling the truth, or whether when they say this it is they who are out of their senses. If I shoot at your son, standing there by the doorway, and hit him right in the middle of the heart, that will prove the Persians are talking nonsense. But if I miss, then you can say that the Persians are telling the truth and that I am not in my right mind." With that he drew his bow and hit the boy. When the boy fell, it is said that Cambyses ordered his body cut open and the wound examined. When the arrow was found lodged in the heart, he laughed with delight and said to the boy's father: "Prexaspes, you can see that it has now been proved that I am not mad and that it is the Persians who are out of their senses. Now tell me, have you ever in your life seen anyone shoot so straight?" Prexaspes saw that the man was not in his right mind, and he was afraid for himself. So he said: "Master, I don't think even a god could have hit the mark so well." That is what he did that time. On another occasion he condemned twelve Persians, men as good as the best of them, on some trivial charge, and buried them alive head downward.

36. While he was doing these things, Croesus the Lydian thought it right to warn him, in these words: "My king, do not give in to the heat of your youth and your temper in everything. Hold yourself back and keep yourself in check. It is a good thing to be prudent, and forethought is wisdom. But you are putting to death men of your own people, condemning them on trivial charges, and you are putting men's sons to death too. If you do many things like this, be careful that the Persians do not rise up against you. As for me, your father Cyrus charged me, and earnestly urged me, to warn you and to suggest to you whatever I found to be good." That was his advice, given out of goodwill. But Cambyses answered: "You dare to advise me? You, who governed your own country so very well, and gave my father such good advice when you urged him to cross the river Araxes and go against the Massagetae, when they were willing to cross over into our land! You utterly ruined yourself by misruling your own country, and you utterly ruined Cyrus, who followed your advice. But you will not escape punishment now, for I want you to know that I have long been looking for an excuse to deal with you." With that he took his bow, meaning to shoot him, but Croesus jumped up and ran out. Since Cambyses could not shoot him, he ordered his attendants to seize him and kill him. But the attendants knew his moods, so they hid Croesus. Their plan was this: if Cambyses changed his mind and wanted Croesus back, they could produce him and be rewarded for saving his life; but if he did not change his mind and did not want him back, then they would kill him. Not long afterward Cambyses did in fact want Croesus back, and the attendants, seeing this, told him that he was still alive. Cambyses said he was glad that Croesus was still alive, but that the men who had saved him would not get off: he would put them to death. And he did.

37. He did many mad things like this, both to Persians and to his allies, while he stayed at Memphis. He opened ancient tombs and examined the bodies of the dead. He also went into the temple of Hephaestus and made great fun of the god's image. For the image of Hephaestus looks very much like the Pataeci, the little figures the Phoenicians carry on the prows of their triremes; for anyone who has not seen these, I will describe it: it is the figure of a dwarf. He also went into the temple of the Cabeiri, which no one is permitted to enter except the priest alone. After mocking the images there at length, he even set them on fire. These too are like the images of Hephaestus, and they are said to be his children.

38. So it is clear to me, by every kind of proof, that Cambyses was utterly mad; otherwise he would never have set out to mock religious rites and established customs. For if you offered all the people in the world a choice, and told them to pick the best customs out of all the customs there are, every people, after looking them all over, would choose their own. That is how strongly everyone believes that their own customs are by far the best. So it is not likely that anyone but a madman would make fun of such things. There are many other proofs that all people feel this way about their customs, and this one in particular. During his reign Darius summoned the Greeks who were at his court and asked them what price would persuade them to eat their fathers' bodies when they died. They answered that no price in the world would make them do it. After this Darius summoned some of the Indians called Callatians, who eat their parents, and asked them, in front of the Greeks, who followed what was said through an interpreter, what payment would persuade them to burn their fathers' bodies when they died. They cried out loud and told him not to say such things. That is how firmly custom is fixed, and I think Pindar was right when he said that custom is king of all.

39. Now while Cambyses was marching on Egypt, the Spartans were also making an expedition against Samos and against Polycrates son of Aeaces, who had risen up and made himself ruler of Samos. At first he had divided the state into three parts and given a share to each of his brothers, Pantagnotus and Syloson. But afterward he killed one of them and drove out the younger, Syloson, and so took possession of all of Samos. Once it was his, he made a pact of guest-friendship with Amasis the king of Egypt, sending him gifts and receiving gifts from him in return. Right after this, in a short space of time, the power of Polycrates grew quickly, and he became famous not only in Ionia but across the rest of Greece too. For wherever he sent his forces, everything went his way. He had acquired a hundred fifty-oared ships and a thousand archers, and he plundered everyone, making no distinction between anyone. He used to say that he would win more gratitude from a friend by giving back what he had taken than by never taking it at all. So he had conquered many of the islands and many cities on the mainland as well. Among other things, he defeated the Lesbians in a sea battle as they were coming with all their forces to help the Milesians. He took them prisoner, and it was these men who dug the whole moat around the city wall of Samos, working in chains.

40. Now Amasis, as you might expect, did not fail to notice that Polycrates was extraordinarily lucky, and it worried him. And as more and more good fortune kept coming to Polycrates, he wrote this letter and sent it to Samos: "Amasis says this to Polycrates. It is pleasant to hear that a friend and guest-friend is doing well. But your great good fortune does not please me, because I know that heaven is jealous. For myself, and for those I care about, I think I would rather have some of our affairs go well and others fail, and so pass through life faring well and badly by turns, than be successful in everything. For I have never yet heard of anyone who was successful in everything and did not come to a thoroughly bad end at last. So take my advice and do as I say about your good fortune. Think it over, and whatever you find you value most, and would be most heartsick to lose, take that and throw it away, so that it will never again be seen by human eyes. And if from then on your good fortune still does not come mixed with disasters, use the remedy I have suggested again."

41. Polycrates read this and, thinking it over, saw that Amasis' advice was good. So he looked for which of his treasures he would be most heartsick to lose, and in looking he found the one I am about to name. He had a signet ring that he used to wear, an emerald set in gold, the work of Theodorus son of Telecles, of Samos. Since he decided this was the thing to throw away, here is what he did. He manned a fifty-oared ship with sailors, went on board himself, and told them to head out into the open sea. When he was well away from the island, he took off the signet ring and, in full view of everyone on board, threw it into the sea. After doing this he sailed home, and when he reached his house he grieved for his loss.

42. But on the fifth or sixth day after this, here is what happened to him. A fisherman caught a large and beautiful fish and thought it right to give it to Polycrates as a gift. So he carried it to the palace door and said he wanted to see Polycrates. When he was let in, he gave him the fish and said: "My king, when I caught this fish I didn't think I should take it to the market, even though I am a man who lives by the work of his hands. It seemed to me worthy of you and of your rule, so I have brought it to give to you." Polycrates was pleased with what he said and answered: "You have done very well, and you deserve double thanks, for your words and for your gift. We invite you to dinner." The fisherman went home thinking this a great honor. Then, as the servants were cutting up the fish, they found Polycrates' signet ring in its belly. As soon as they saw it and picked it up, they carried it to Polycrates, overjoyed. They gave him the ring and told him how it had been found. He realized that the hand of heaven was in this, so he wrote down everything he had done and everything that had happened to him, and when he had written it he sent the letter to Egypt.

43. When Amasis read the letter that had come from Polycrates, he understood that it is impossible for one man to rescue another from what is going to happen, and that Polycrates, who was successful in everything and even found again what he had thrown away, was not going to have a good end. So he sent a herald to him in Samos and said that he was breaking off their guest-friendship. He did this so that when some great and terrible disaster struck Polycrates, he would not himself have to grieve over him as a friend and guest.

44. It was against this Polycrates, then, successful in everything, that the Spartans were making their expedition. They had been called in to help by the Samians who later founded Cydonia in Crete. Now Polycrates, without the Samians knowing, had sent a messenger to Cambyses son of Cyrus while he was gathering an army to march against Egypt, asking him to send to Samos and request military help from him. Cambyses was very glad to hear this and sent to Samos to ask Polycrates to send a fleet with him against Egypt. So Polycrates picked out those citizens he most suspected of wanting to rise against him and sent them off in forty triremes, telling Cambyses not to send them back.

45. Now some say that these Samians sent away by Polycrates never reached Egypt. When they got as far as Carpathus on their voyage, they talked it over among themselves and decided not to sail any farther. Others say they did reach Egypt, were kept under guard there, and escaped. Then, as they were sailing back to Samos, Polycrates met them with his ships and fought them. The returning men won and landed on the island, but then they fought a battle on land there and were beaten, and so they sailed off to Sparta. Some, though, say that the men from Egypt defeated Polycrates. In my opinion that is not right. They would have had no need to call in the Spartans if they had been able to bring Polycrates to terms by themselves. Besides, it is not reasonable to think that a man with a great many foreign mercenaries and native archers was beaten by the returning Samians, who were only a few. Then Polycrates gathered up the children and wives of his citizens and shut them up in the ship-sheds. He kept them there ready, so that if his citizens went over to the returning exiles, he could burn them, sheds and all.

46. When the Samians who had been driven out by Polycrates reached Sparta, they were brought before the magistrates and made a long speech, pressing their request urgently. But at this first hearing the magistrates replied that they had forgotten what had been said at the beginning and could not understand what had been said at the end. After this they were brought in a second time. This time they brought a bag with them and said nothing but this: that the bag needed barley-meal. The Spartans replied that they had overdone it with the bag: even the word "bag" was one word too many. All the same, they decided to help them.

47. Then the Spartans got a force ready and sailed against Samos. According to the Samians, they did it to repay an old favor, because the Samians had once helped them with ships against the Messenians. But the Spartans say they made the expedition not so much because they wanted to help the Samians who asked them, as to take revenge on their own account for the theft of the mixing bowl they had been taking as a gift to Croesus, and of the linen breastplate that Amasis king of Egypt had sent them as a gift. For the Samians had carried off the breastplate too, the year before they took the bowl. It was made of linen, with many figures woven into it, and decorated with gold and with cotton. Every thread of this breastplate is a thing to marvel at: fine as each thread is, it is made of three hundred and sixty strands, every one of them visible. There is another just like it, which Amasis dedicated as an offering to Athena at Lindus.

48. The Corinthians also took part eagerly in this expedition against Samos, to make sure it happened. For the Samians had committed an outrage against them too, a generation before this expedition and at about the same time as the theft of the bowl. Periander son of Cypselus had sent three hundred sons of the leading men of Corcyra, the island now called Corfu, to Alyattes at Sardis to be castrated. The Corinthians who were taking the boys put in at Samos. When the Samians heard the story and learned why the boys were being taken to Sardis, they first told the boys to take hold of the temple of Artemis as suppliants, and then they would not let the Corinthians drag the suppliants away from the temple. And when the Corinthians cut the boys off from food, the Samians started a festival, which they still celebrate in the same way today. As long as the boys were suppliants, whenever night fell they held dances of girls and young men, and in arranging the dances they made it a rule of the festival that cakes of sesame and honey should be carried, so that the Corcyrean boys could snatch them and live on them. This went on until at last the Corinthians who were in charge of the boys gave up and left. As for the boys, the Samians took them back to Corcyra.

49. Now if the Corinthians had been on friendly terms with the Corcyreans after Periander's death, they would not have joined the expedition against Samos for the reason I have given. But as it is, the two have always been at odds with each other, ever since the Corinthians first colonized the island. This, then, was why the Corinthians bore a grudge against the Samians.

50. Periander had picked out the sons of the leading men of Corcyra and was sending them to Sardis to be castrated in order to take revenge. For the Corcyreans had started it, by doing him an outrageous wrong. After Periander had killed his wife Melissa, another misfortune happened to him on top of the one he had already suffered. This is how it went. He had two sons by Melissa, one seventeen years old and the other eighteen. Their mother's father Procles, who was tyrant of Epidaurus, sent for them and treated them kindly, as you would expect, since they were his own daughter's sons. When he was sending them home, he said to them as he said goodbye: "Do you know, boys, who killed your mother?" The elder paid no attention to this remark. But the younger, whose name was Lycophron, was so deeply hurt to hear it that when he got back to Corinth he would not speak to his father. When his father tried to talk with him, he would not talk back, and when his father asked him questions, he gave no answer, because he regarded him as his mother's murderer. In the end Periander grew furious with his son and drove him out of the house.

51. After driving him out, he asked the elder son what their grandfather had talked about with them. The elder son told him how kindly Procles had received them, but he did not remember the remark Procles had made as they left, since he had paid no attention to it. Periander said that Procles must surely have put some idea into their heads, and he kept pressing him with questions. At last the young man remembered and told him that too. Periander took note of it, and, determined to show no softness, he sent a messenger to the people his banished son was living with at the time and forbade them to take him into their houses. And whenever the boy was driven from one house and went to another, he was driven from that one too, because Periander threatened anyone who took him in and ordered them to shut him out. So, driven away again, he would go on to another house, where friends of his lived, and they took him in, perhaps because he was Periander's son, even though they were afraid.

52. At last Periander made a proclamation that anyone who took him into their house or even spoke with him would have to pay a sacred fine to Apollo, and he named the amount. Because of this proclamation, no one was willing either to speak to him or to take him into their house. And he himself did not think it right to try, since it had been forbidden. Instead he lay about in the colonnades, exposed to the weather. On the fourth day of this, Periander saw him filthy, wretched and starving, and he felt pity for him. His anger softened, and he went up to him and said: "Son, which is better: the life you are living now, or to inherit the power and wealth I have now, by doing as your father wishes? You are my son, the prince of rich Corinth, and yet you have chosen the life of a beggar by defying and raging against the one man you should least have treated that way. If any disaster has happened in the matter that makes you suspect me, it has happened to me first, and I bear more of it than anyone, since I am the one who did the deed. Now that you have learned how much better it is to be envied than to be pitied, and also what a hard thing it is to be angry with your parents and with those stronger than you, come back home." With these words Periander tried to win him back. But the son gave his father no other answer than this: that now his father owed the god a fine for having spoken with him. Then Periander saw that his son's sickness was hopeless and could not be overcome, so he sent a ship to Corcyra and sent him away out of his sight, for he was ruler of that island too. After sending him away, Periander made war on his father-in-law Procles, whom he blamed most for the state he was in. He took Epidaurus, and took Procles himself prisoner too.

53. But as time went on, Periander grew past his prime and realized that he was no longer able to oversee and manage the government of the state. So he sent to Corcyra and summoned Lycophron to come back and take power. For he did not see the ability needed in his elder son, but saw plainly that he was too slow-witted. Lycophron did not even bother to give the messenger an answer. Then Periander, who still clung to the young man out of love, sent him his own daughter, Lycophron's sister, thinking that he would listen to her more than to anyone else. She arrived and said to him: "Boy, would you rather see the tyranny fall to others, and your father's wealth carried off as plunder, than come back and have them yourself? Come home, and stop tormenting yourself. Pride is a dangerous thing to own. Do not cure one evil with another. Many people put what is fair ahead of what is strictly just, and many before now, in seeking their mother's due, have lost their father's. A tyranny is a slippery thing, and many people want it; and besides, he is an old man now and past his prime. Do not give away what is yours to others." She said the most persuasive things she could, for her father had coached her beforehand. But he answered that he would never come to Corinth as long as he heard his father was still alive. When she reported this, Periander sent a messenger for the third time. He said that he wanted to come to Corcyra himself, and he urged Lycophron at the same time to come back to Corinth and succeed him on the throne. The son agreed to return on these terms, and Periander was getting ready to sail to Corcyra and his son to Corinth. But the Corcyreans learned everything that had happened, and they killed the young man so that Periander would not come to their land. That is why Periander took his revenge on the people of Corcyra.
