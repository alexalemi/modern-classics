Chapter 17: The Siege of Samos, the False Smerdis, and the Plot of the Seven

54. So the Spartans had come with a great force and were besieging Samos. They attacked the wall and took the tower that stands by the sea in the suburb of the city, but later, when Polycrates came up to the rescue with a large body of men, they were driven out of it. Meanwhile, by the upper tower, which stands on the ridge of the mountain, the foreign mercenaries and many of the Samians themselves had come out to fight. They held their ground against the Spartans for a short while and then began to fall back, and the Spartans followed them and were cutting them down.

55. Now if all the Spartans there that day had been the equals of Archias and Lycopas, Samos would have been taken. For Archias and Lycopas alone rushed inside the wall along with the fleeing Samians, and since they were cut off from retreat, they were killed inside the city of the Samians. I myself, moreover, talked in Pitane (that was the deme he belonged to) with the third in descent from this Archias: another Archias, the son of Samius son of Archias. He honored the Samians above all other foreigners. Not only that, but he said that his own father had been named Samius because his father, Archias, had died a glorious death on Samos; and he said that he honored the Samians because the Samians had given his grandfather a public funeral.

56. When the Spartans had been besieging Samos for forty days and were getting nowhere, they set off back to the Peloponnese. But there is a less believable account that has gone around about these events: that Polycrates struck a large number of a certain local coin in lead, had them gilded over, and gave them to the Spartans, and that they took them and then left. This was the first expedition the Spartans (who are Dorians) ever made into Asia.

57. The Samians who had led the expedition against Polycrates also sailed away when the Spartans were about to abandon them, and they went to Siphnos. They needed money, and the people of Siphnos were then at the height of their prosperity and richer than all the other islanders. They had gold and silver mines on their island, so rich that there is a treasury at Delphi dedicated from a tenth of the money that came in from those mines, and furnished as splendidly as the wealthiest of the treasuries there. The people used to divide among themselves the money that came in from the mines each year. So when they were setting up the treasury, they asked the oracle whether their present prosperity could stay with them for a long time, and the Pythia gave them this reply:

	"But when the town hall in Siphnos shines out white,
	and white-browed is the marketplace, then a careful man is needed
	to beware of an army of wood and a herald in red."

Now at just that time the marketplace and town hall of the Siphnians had been decorated with Parian marble.

58. They could not understand this oracle, either then at first or when the Samians arrived. As soon as the Samians were putting in at Siphnos, they sent one of their ships to carry envoys to the city. Now in the old days all ships were painted red, and this was what the Pythia was warning the Siphnians about in advance, when she told them to guard against the "army of wood" and the "herald in red." So the messengers came and asked the Siphnians to lend them ten talents. When the Siphnians refused, the Samians began to ravage their land. As soon as the Siphnians heard of it they came out to the rescue, engaged the Samians in battle, and were defeated. Many of them were cut off from the city by the Samians and shut out of it, and after this the Samians made them pay a hundred talents.

59. Then the Samians bought the island of Hydrea, which lies near the coast of the Peloponnese, from the people of Hermione, and they handed it over to the Troezenians to look after. They themselves settled at Cydonia in Crete, though they had not sailed there for that purpose but to drive the Zacynthians out of the island. They stayed there and prospered for five years, so much so that they were the builders of the temples that stand in Cydonia today, and of the temple of Dictyna too. But in the sixth year the Aeginetans, together with the Cretans, beat them in a sea battle and made them slaves. They cut off the prows of their ships, which were shaped like boars' heads, and dedicated them in the temple of Athena on Aegina. The Aeginetans did this because they had a grudge against the Samians. The Samians had made the first attack, on Aegina, back when Amphicrates was king of Samos, and they had done the Aeginetans a great deal of harm and suffered a great deal of harm from them in return. That was the cause of it.

60. I have spoken about the Samians at greater length because they have three works that are greater than any others the Greeks have made. The first is a tunnel, started from the bottom and open at both ends, dug through a mountain that rises no less than 900 feet. The tunnel is 4,200 feet long, about four-fifths of a mile, and eight feet high and eight feet wide. All along its length a second channel has been dug, thirty feet deep and three feet wide, and through it water from a plentiful spring is carried in pipes to the city. The designer of this work was a man from Megara, Eupalinus son of Naustrophus. That is one of the three. The second is a breakwater in the sea around the harbor, built down to a depth of as much as 120 feet, and more than 1,200 feet long. The third work they have made is a temple, the largest of all the temples we know of. Its first architect was Rhoecus son of Philes, a native of Samos. That is why I have spoken at greater length about the Samians.

61. Now while Cambyses son of Cyrus was lingering in Egypt and had gone out of his mind, two brothers, both Magi, rose up against him. One of them had been left behind by Cambyses as steward of his household. This man rose against him because he saw that the death of Smerdis was being kept secret, that only a few of the Persians knew of it, and that most of them firmly believed he was still alive. So he set out to seize the kingdom, and this was his plan. He had a brother (the one who, as I said, rose up with him against Cambyses), and this brother looked very much like Smerdis son of Cyrus, whom Cambyses had killed, though Smerdis was his own brother. He looked like Smerdis, and not only that, he had the same name, Smerdis. The Magus Patizeithes persuaded this man that he would manage everything for him, and then brought him and seated him on the royal throne. After that he sent heralds around to all the provinces, and one in particular to the army in Egypt, to proclaim that from now on they must obey Smerdis son of Cyrus instead of Cambyses.

62. So the other heralds made this proclamation, and so did the one appointed to go to Egypt. He found Cambyses and his army at Ecbatana in Syria, stood up in the middle of them, and began to proclaim what the Magus had ordered him to. When Cambyses heard this from the herald, he believed the herald was telling the truth and that Prexaspes had betrayed him: that when Prexaspes had been sent to kill Smerdis, he had not done it. So Cambyses looked at Prexaspes and said: "Prexaspes, is this how you carried out the job I gave you?"

Prexaspes answered: "Master, it is not true that your brother Smerdis has risen against you, or that you will have any trouble from him, great or small. I myself did what you ordered me to do, and I buried him with my own hands. If the dead have come back to life, then you can expect Astyages the Mede to rise up against you too. But if things are as they always were, there is no fear that any trouble will spring up for you, at least not from him. So now I think we should send men after the herald and question him, and ask who sent him to proclaim to us that we are to obey Smerdis as king."

63. When Prexaspes had said this, Cambyses liked the advice, and so the herald was chased down at once and brought back. When he had come back, Prexaspes questioned him like this: "You say, man, that you have come as a messenger from Smerdis son of Cyrus. Now tell the truth and go away unharmed. I am asking you: did Smerdis himself appear before your eyes and give you this order, or was it one of his servants?"

He answered: "I have never yet seen Smerdis son of Cyrus, not since the day King Cambyses marched off to Egypt. It was the Magus whom Cambyses appointed steward of his household who gave me this order, and he said that it was Smerdis son of Cyrus who had commanded me to tell you these things."

That is what he told them, adding no lies to the truth. Then Cambyses said: "Prexaspes, you did what you were ordered like an honest man, and you are free of blame. But who among the Persians can this be who has risen up against me and taken the name of Smerdis?"

Prexaspes said: "My king, I think I understand what has happened. The Magi have risen against you: Patizeithes, whom you left as steward of your household, and his brother Smerdis."

64. When Cambyses heard the name Smerdis, he understood at once the true meaning of this news and of his dream. In his sleep he had dreamed that someone reported to him that Smerdis was sitting on the royal throne and that his head touched the sky. Seeing now that he had killed his brother for no reason, he began to weep for Smerdis. When he had wept for him and grieved bitterly over the whole disaster, he leaped onto his horse, meaning to march his army to Susa as fast as he could against the Magus. But as he leaped onto his horse, the cap of his sword's scabbard fell off, and the bare blade struck him in the thigh. He was wounded in the very place where he had once struck Apis, the god of the Egyptians. Believing the blow was a fatal one, Cambyses asked what the name of that town was, and they told him "Ecbatana." Even before this, the oracle at the city of Buto had told him that he would end his life in Ecbatana. He had supposed that he would die of old age in Ecbatana in Media, the seat of all his power; but the oracle, as it turned out, meant Ecbatana in Syria. So when he asked and learned the name of the town, the shock of the disaster the Magus had caused, and of the wound at the same time, brought him back to his senses. He understood the meaning of the oracle and said: "Here is where Cambyses son of Cyrus is fated to end his life."

65. That was all he said at the time. But about twenty days later he sent for the most distinguished of the Persians who were with him and said to them: "Persians, I am now forced to reveal to you the thing I used to keep more hidden than anything else. When I was in Egypt I saw a vision in my sleep, which I wish I had never seen. I dreamed that a messenger came from home and reported to me that Smerdis was sitting on the royal throne and that his head touched the sky. I was afraid my brother would take my power from me, so I acted quickly rather than wisely. For it seems that no human being can turn aside what is fated to happen. So I, fool that I was, sent Prexaspes off to Susa to kill Smerdis. When that great wrong had been done, I lived without fear, and never thought that some other man might one day rise up against me, now that Smerdis was out of the way. I missed the mark entirely about what was going to happen. I have made myself my brother's murderer, when there was no need, and I have lost the kingdom all the same. For it was really Smerdis the Magus that heaven was warning me about in the vision, saying he would rise against me.

"So that deed has been done by me, and you must understand that Smerdis son of Cyrus is no longer alive for you. It is the Magi who are really the masters of your kingdom: the one I left as steward of my household, and his brother Smerdis. The man who above all others should have avenged the insult I have suffered from the Magi has ended his life by an unholy death, at the hands of his own nearest kin. Since he is gone, the next best thing left to me, and the most necessary, is to charge you, Persians, with what I want done for me as I die. This, then, I lay on you, calling the gods of the royal house to witness: on all of you, and most of all on those of the Achaemenids who are here. Do not let the supreme power go back to the Medes. If they have taken it by trickery, take it from them by trickery; if they have won it by force of any kind, win it back by force and a strong hand. If you do this, may the earth bear its crops for you, and may your wives and your herds be fruitful, and may you stay free forever. But if you do not win back the power, or do not even try, I pray that the opposite of these blessings will fall on you, and on top of that, that every Persian will meet an end like the one that has come on me."

As soon as he had finished saying this, Cambyses began to weep and mourn over everything that had happened to him.

66. When the Persians saw the king weeping, they all tore the clothes they were wearing and mourned without restraint. After this the bone became infected and the thigh turned gangrenous, and the wound carried off Cambyses son of Cyrus. He had reigned seven years and five months in all, and he left no children at all, neither son nor daughter. The Persians who were there meanwhile were very little inclined to believe that the power was in the hands of the Magi. On the contrary, they were quite sure that Cambyses had said what he said about the death of Smerdis to deceive them, so that all the Persians would be stirred up to make war on Smerdis. So they were quite sure that it was Smerdis son of Cyrus who had been made king. For Prexaspes too denied very firmly that he had killed Smerdis, since now that Cambyses was dead it was not safe for him to say that he had killed the son of Cyrus with his own hand.

67. So when Cambyses had ended his life, the Magus took the throne without any trouble, posing as his namesake Smerdis son of Cyrus. He reigned for the seven months that were still lacking to make up Cambyses' eight years. During that time he did great good to all his subjects, so much so that when he died, everyone in Asia except the Persians themselves mourned for him. For the Magus sent messengers out to every nation he ruled and proclaimed that they would be free from military service and from tribute for three years.

68. He made this proclamation as soon as he was established on the throne. But in the eighth month it was found out who he was, in the following way. There was a man called Otanes son of Pharnaspes, second to none of the Persians in birth and in wealth. This Otanes was the first to suspect that the Magus was not Smerdis son of Cyrus but the man he really was. He drew this conclusion from two facts: the man never went out of the citadel, and he never summoned any of the distinguished Persians into his presence. Having become suspicious, he did this. Cambyses had married Otanes' daughter, whose name was Phaedyme, and the Magus was now keeping this same daughter as his wife and living with her, as he did with all the rest of Cambyses' wives. So Otanes sent a message to his daughter and asked her who the man was she slept beside: was it Smerdis son of Cyrus, or someone else? She sent word back that she did not know, because she had never seen Smerdis son of Cyrus, and she did not know in any other way who the man living with her was. Otanes then sent a second time and said: "If you do not know Smerdis son of Cyrus yourself, then ask Atossa who this man is, the man both she and you are living with as wives. She will surely know her own brother."

69. To this his daughter sent back word: "I can't speak with Atossa or see any of the other women who live here with me. As soon as this man, whoever he is, came to the throne, he split us up and put each of us in separate rooms of our own."

When Otanes heard this, the matter became clearer and clearer to him, and he sent another message in to her, which said: "Daughter, you are nobly born, and it is right for you to take on any risk your father tells you to take. For if this really is not Smerdis son of Cyrus but the man I suspect, he must not get away unpunished, either for taking you to his bed or for ruling over the Persians; he must pay the price. So now do as I say. When he is sleeping beside you and you can tell he is sound asleep, feel his ears. If it turns out that he has ears, then believe you are living with Smerdis son of Cyrus. But if not, believe it is Smerdis the Magus."

Phaedyme sent an answer saying that if she did this she would be running a great risk. If it turned out that he had no ears and she were caught feeling for them, she was quite sure he would put her to death. Even so, she said, she would do it. So she promised her father she would do this. Now this Smerdis the Magus had had his ears cut off by Cyrus son of Cambyses, while Cyrus was king, for some serious crime. So Phaedyme, the daughter of Otanes, set about doing everything she had promised her father. When her turn came to go to the Magus (for among the Persians the wives go to their husband in turn, each one regularly), she came and lay down beside him. When the Magus was deep asleep, she felt for his ears. She found, not with difficulty but easily, that her husband had no ears, and as soon as it was day she sent and told her father what had happened.

70. Then Otanes took Aspathines and Gobryas aside, who were leading men among the Persians and his own most trusted friends, and told them the whole story. They, as it turned out, already suspected the same thing themselves, and when Otanes reported this to them, they readily accepted his proposals. They decided that each of them should bring in the one Persian he trusted most. So Otanes brought in Intaphrenes, Gobryas brought in Megabyzus, and Aspathines brought in Hydarnes. When there were six of them, Darius son of Hystaspes arrived at Susa from the land of Persia, where his father was governor. So when he came, the six Persians decided to bring Darius in with them too.

71. So these seven came together, gave each other pledges of loyalty, and talked it over. When it was Darius' turn to give his opinion, he said to them: "I thought I was the only one who knew this: that it was the Magus who was ruling as king, and that Smerdis son of Cyrus was dead. And for that very reason I have come here, in earnest, to plan the Magus' death. But since it turns out that you know too, and not just me, I think we should act at once and not put it off. Delay is not the better way."

Otanes answered: "Son of Hystaspes, you come of a noble family, and you seem to be showing yourself in no way worse than your father. But do not rush this undertaking so hastily without thinking it through. Take it up more carefully. We must first become more in number, and then go ahead with it."

Darius said in reply: "You men here, if you follow the course Otanes suggests, you can be sure you will die miserably. For someone will carry word to the Magus, to get something for himself out of it. Your best course would have been to do this on your own risk alone. But since you decided to bring more people into it, and you told me, either we do the deed today, or you can be sure of this: if today goes by, no one will get in ahead of me as your accuser. I will tell the Magus all of it myself."

72. When Otanes saw Darius in such violent haste, he answered: "Since you are forcing us to hurry and won't let us wait, come, explain to us yourself how we are going to get into the palace and lay hands on them. There are guards posted in various places; you probably know that as well as we do, if not from seeing them then from hearing about them. How are we going to get past them?"

Darius replied: "Otanes, there are many things that cannot be explained in words, only in action. And there are other things that can be explained in words, but no famous deed comes of them. You can be sure, though, that the guards posted there are not hard to get past. In the first place, being who we are, there is no one who will not let us go by, partly, you may suppose, out of respect for us, and partly perhaps out of fear. In the second place, I have a perfectly convincing excuse to get us through. I will say that I have just come from Persia and want to give the king a message from my father. Where a lie has to be told, let it be told. We are all after the same thing, both those who lie and those who always tell the truth. The liars lie whenever they expect to gain something by getting people to believe their lies. The truth-tellers tell the truth so that they can win some gain for themselves by it, and so that people will trust them more readily. So we take different roads, but we are all after the same thing. If there were nothing to be gained by it, the truth-teller would lie and the liar would tell the truth, and neither would care. So any of the doorkeepers who lets us through willingly will be better off for it later. But any who tries to stand in our way, let him be marked as our enemy then and there. And after that, let us push our way in and get to work."

73. Then Gobryas said: "Friends, when will there ever be a better chance for us either to win back our rule, or, if we cannot get it back, to die? We are Persians, and we are being ruled by a Mede, a Magus, and a man whose ears have been cut off, at that. Besides, all of you who stood by Cambyses when he was sick surely remember what he laid on the Persians as he was dying, if they did not try to win back the power. We did not accept it then; we thought Cambyses had said it to deceive us. So now I vote that we follow Darius' advice, and that we do not leave this meeting to go anywhere except straight to attack the Magus." That is what Gobryas said, and they all approved the proposal.

74. While they were planning all this, something else was happening at the same time, by coincidence. The Magi had talked it over and decided to make Prexaspes their friend. They had three reasons. He had suffered a terrible wrong from Cambyses, who had shot his son dead with an arrow. He was the only one who knew for certain that Smerdis son of Cyrus was dead, since he had killed him with his own hands. And finally, Prexaspes was very highly respected among the Persians. For these reasons they summoned him and tried to win him over as a friend. They bound him with pledges and oaths that he would keep to himself, and reveal to no one, the trick they had played on the Persians, and they promised to give him countless things in return. When Prexaspes had promised to do this, the Magi, having persuaded him that far, proposed a second thing. They said they would call all the Persians together at the palace wall, and they told him to climb up a tower and speak to them, and tell them that they were living under the rule of Smerdis son of Cyrus and no one else. They gave him this task because they believed he was the most trusted man among the Persians, and because he had often declared that Smerdis son of Cyrus was still alive and had denied that he had killed him.

75. When Prexaspes said he was ready to do this too, the Magi called the Persians together, sent him up a tower, and told him to speak to them. But he deliberately forgot what they had asked of him. Starting from Achaemenes, he traced the ancestry of Cyrus on his father's side, and when he had come down to Cyrus himself, he went on at last to tell what great good Cyrus had done the Persians. When he had gone through all this, he went on to declare the truth. He said that he had kept it secret before, since it was not safe for him to tell what had been done, but that now he was forced to make it known. He told them how he himself had killed Smerdis son of Cyrus, because Cambyses forced him to, and that it was the Magi who were ruling now. Then he called down many curses on the Persians if they did not win back the power and take vengeance on the Magi, and with that he threw himself headfirst off the tower. So Prexaspes ended his life, a man who had been respected all his days.

76. Now the seven Persians, having decided to attack the Magi at once and not delay, prayed to the gods and set off, knowing nothing of what had happened with Prexaspes. As they were on their way, halfway there, they heard what had happened with Prexaspes. At that they stepped aside from the road and talked it over again. Otanes and his side strongly urged them to wait and not to go ahead while things were in such turmoil. Darius and his side urged them to go at once and do what they had decided, and not to delay. While they were arguing, seven pairs of hawks appeared, chasing two pairs of vultures, plucking out their feathers and tearing them. When they saw this, all seven of them approved Darius' view, and they went on to the king's palace, encouraged by the sight of the birds.

77. When they appeared at the gates, things happened very much as Darius had expected. The guards, out of respect for men who were among the first of the Persians, and not suspecting that they would do anything of the kind they intended, let them pass in, with heaven guiding them, and nobody asked them any questions. When they had passed into the courtyard, they met the eunuchs who carried messages in to the king. The eunuchs asked them what they had come for, and at the same time they threatened the gatekeepers with punishment for letting them in, and tried to stop the seven when they tried to go further. Then the seven gave each other the word, drew their daggers, and stabbed the men who were trying to stop them right there on the spot. Then they ran on toward the men's hall.

78. Now both of the Magi happened to be inside, talking over what Prexaspes had done. When they saw that the eunuchs had been attacked and heard them shouting, they both ran back, and when they saw what was happening they turned to defend themselves. One of them got down his bow and arrows before he was attacked, and the other took up his spear. Then the two sides came to grips. The one who had taken up his bow and arrows found them useless, since his enemies were close by and pressing hard on him. But the other defended himself with his spear. First he struck Aspathines in the thigh, and then Intaphrenes in the eye; Intaphrenes lost his eye from the wound, but he did not lose his life. So these two were wounded by one of the Magi. The other, when his bow and arrows turned out to be useless, ran into a bedroom that opened off the men's hall, meaning to shut the door, and two of the seven, Darius and Gobryas, rushed in with him. Gobryas was locked together with the Magus, wrestling, while Darius stood over them, not knowing what to do, because it was dark and he was afraid he would hit Gobryas. Seeing him standing there doing nothing, Gobryas asked him why he was not using his hands. Darius said: "Because I'm afraid I may stab you." And Gobryas answered: "Thrust with your sword, even if it goes through both of us." So Darius did as he said, thrust with his dagger, and happened to hit the Magus.

79. When they had killed the Magi and cut off their heads, they left their wounded behind, both because they were not able to go and so that they could hold the citadel. The other five took the heads of the Magi and ran out, shouting and clashing their weapons. They called on the other Persians to join them, told them what had been done, and showed them the heads, and at the same time they began killing every Magus who crossed their path. When the Persians heard what the seven had done and how the Magi had tricked them, they decided to do the same themselves. They drew their daggers and killed the Magi wherever they found one, so that if night had not come on and stopped them, they would not have left a single Magus alive. The Persians celebrate this day together more than any other day. They keep a great festival on it, which the Persians call the Slaughter of the Magi, and on it no Magus is allowed to appear in public; the Magi stay shut in their houses all that day.
