Chapter 35: Eretria, and the Battle of Marathon

94. So the Athenians were at war with the Aeginetans. Meanwhile the Persian king was pushing ahead with his plan. His servant kept reminding him, as he had ordered, to remember the Athenians; the sons of Pisistratus were close at hand and kept bringing charges against the Athenians; and at the same time Darius himself wanted to seize on this pretext to conquer those peoples of Greece that had not given him earth and water. Since Mardonius had fared so badly on his expedition, Darius removed him from his command. He appointed other generals and sent them out against Eretria and Athens. They were Datis, a Mede by birth, and Artaphernes son of Artaphernes, the king's own nephew. Darius sent them off with orders to enslave Athens and Eretria and to bring the slaves back into his presence.

95. These newly appointed commanders marched from the king's court to the Aleian plain in Cilicia, bringing with them a large and well-equipped land army. While they were camped there, the whole fleet came up to join them, the ships that the various subject peoples had been ordered to supply. With it came the horse transports, which Darius had ordered his tributaries to get ready the year before. They put their horses on board these, embarked the land army on the ships, and sailed for Ionia with six hundred triremes. From there they did not keep their ships along the mainland coast toward the Hellespont and Thrace. Instead they set out from Samos and made their way across the Icarian Sea and through the islands. The reason, I think, is that they feared the voyage around Athos more than anything else, since the year before they had met with a great disaster when they went that way. Naxos also drew them this way, since it had not been conquered the time before.

96. They came at Naxos from the Icarian Sea, for Naxos was the first place the Persians meant to attack, because they remembered what had happened there before. But the Naxians did not wait for them. They fled at once to the mountains. The Persians enslaved all of them they could catch and set fire to both the temples and the town. After this they put out to sea to attack the other islands.

97. While they were doing this, the Delians too had left Delos and fled to Tenos. As the fleet sailed toward Delos, Datis went ahead and would not let the ships anchor at the island of Delos. He had them anchor instead at Rhenaea, across the channel. He found out by asking where the Delians had gone, and he sent a herald to them with this message: "Holy men, why have you run away, thinking so badly of me? I have sense enough of my own, and besides, the king has ordered me not to do any harm to the land where the two gods were born, neither to the land itself nor to the people who live there. So now go back to your homes and live on your island." That was the message he sent the Delians by his herald. After this he heaped up three hundred talents' weight of frankincense on the altar, about 17,000 pounds, and burned it.

98. When he had done this, Datis sailed away with his army to attack Eretria first, taking Ionians and Aeolians along with him. After he had put out to sea from there, Delos was shaken by an earthquake. The Delians told me that the island had never been shaken before that time, or since, down to my own day. And surely this was a sign from heaven, showing men the troubles that were coming. For in the time of Darius son of Hystaspes, of Xerxes son of Darius, and of Artaxerxes son of Xerxes, three generations one after another, more troubles came upon Greece than in all the twenty generations before Darius. Some of these troubles came from the Persians, and others from the leading Greek states themselves, fighting one another for supremacy. So it was not unreasonable that Delos should be shaken, though it had never been shaken before. And there was an oracle written about it, which said:

	"I will shake even Delos, though it has never been shaken."

In Greek, these names mean the following: Darius means "Doer," Xerxes "Warrior," and Artaxerxes "Great Warrior." That is what the Greeks would rightly call these kings in their own language.

99. After they left Delos, the Persians put in at the islands as they went. From each they picked up more troops and took the sons of the islanders as hostages. Sailing around among the islands, they put in at Carystus too. But the Carystians would neither give them hostages nor agree to join an expedition against their neighbors, by which they meant Eretria and Athens. So the Persians laid siege to them and ravaged their land, until in the end the Carystians also came around to doing what the Persians wanted.

100. Meanwhile the Eretrians learned that the Persian fleet was sailing to attack them, and they asked the Athenians for help. The Athenians did not refuse. As helpers they gave them the four thousand settlers who had been given the land of the wealthy Chalcidians. But it turned out that the Eretrians had no sound plan. They had sent for the Athenians, but they were of two minds. Some of them proposed to leave the city and take to the heights of Euboea, while others, expecting to profit personally from the Persians, were getting ready to betray the place. Aeschines son of Nothon, one of the leading men of Eretria, found out about both these plans. He told the Athenians who had come the whole state of affairs and begged them to leave and go home, so that they would not be destroyed along with everyone else. The Athenians took Aeschines' advice.

101. So they crossed over to Oropus and saved themselves. The Persians, meanwhile, sailed on and brought their ships to land at Temenus, Choereae and Aegilea, in the territory of Eretria. When they had taken these places, they began at once to unload their horses and to prepare to advance against the enemy. The Eretrians did not mean to come out and fight. Their aim was to hold out behind their walls if they could, since the proposal to abandon the city had been voted down. Then the Persians made a violent assault on the wall, and for six days many men fell on both sides. But on the seventh day two respected citizens, Euphorbus son of Alcimachus and Philagrus son of Cyneus, betrayed the city to the Persians. The Persians entered the city, plundered the temples and set them on fire, in revenge for the temples that had been burned at Sardis. They also enslaved the people, as Darius had commanded.

102. Once they had Eretria in their power, they stayed a few days and then sailed for Attica. They pressed on hard, expecting that the Athenians would do just what the Eretrians had done. Marathon was the best place in Attica for cavalry to operate, and it was also very close to Eretria, so it was to Marathon that Hippias son of Pisistratus guided them.

103. When the Athenians learned of this, they too marched out to Marathon to defend their land. They were led by ten generals. The tenth was Miltiades, whose father Cimon son of Stesagoras had been forced into exile from Athens by Pisistratus son of Hippocrates. While he was in exile, Cimon happened to win a victory at the Olympic games with a four-horse chariot. In winning it he did the same thing his half-brother Miltiades had done, who had the same mother as he did. Then at the next Olympic games he won again with the same mares, and let Pisistratus be proclaimed the winner. Having handed the victory over to him, he came back to his own country under a peace agreement. Then, after he had won with the same mares at yet another Olympic festival, it was his fate to be killed by the sons of Pisistratus, Pisistratus himself being no longer alive. They killed him near the town hall, after setting men to lie in wait for him at night. Cimon is buried outside the city, beyond the road called Through Coele (the Hollow), and directly opposite him are buried the mares that won at three Olympic games. The mares of Evagoras the Laconian did the same thing, but no others ever have. Cimon's elder son, Stesagoras, was at that time being raised in the Chersonese by his father's brother Miltiades. The younger son was being raised in Athens with Cimon himself, and he had been named Miltiades after Miltiades who founded the settlement in the Chersonese.

104. This Miltiades, then, had come from the Chersonese and was now a general of the Athenians, after escaping death twice over. First, the Phoenicians had chased him as far as Imbros, very eager to catch him and bring him up to the king. Then, after he had escaped them and reached his own country and seemed to be safe from then on, his enemies were lying in wait for him there. They brought him to court and prosecuted him for his rule as tyrant in the Chersonese. He escaped them too, and had then been made a general of the Athenians, elected by the people.

105. First of all, while they were still in the city, the generals sent a herald off to Sparta. This was Pheidippides, an Athenian, who was also a long-distance runner who did this for a living. As Pheidippides himself said, and as he reported to the Athenians, Pan met him on Mount Parthenium, above Tegea. Pan called out Pheidippides' name and told him to take a message to the Athenians: why did they pay no attention to him, when he was friendly to the Athenians, had been useful to them many times before, and would be again? The Athenians believed this story was true. So when their affairs had come out well, they built a shrine of Pan below the Acropolis, and because of this message they have honored him ever since with yearly sacrifices and a torch race.

106. But at that time, the time when he said Pan appeared to him, this Pheidippides, sent by the generals, was in Sparta on the day after he left Athens. He came before the magistrates and said: "Men of Sparta, the Athenians ask you to come to their help, and not to let the most ancient city in Greece fall into slavery at the hands of foreigners. Eretria has already been enslaved, and Greece is the weaker by one famous city." He delivered the message he had been given, and the Spartans resolved to help the Athenians. But they could not do it at once, because they did not want to break their law. It was the ninth day of the month, and they said they would not march out on the ninth, or until the moon was full.

107. So the Spartans waited for the full moon. Meanwhile Hippias son of Pisistratus was guiding the Persians in to Marathon. The night before, he had seen a vision in his sleep: it seemed to Hippias that he slept with his own mother. From this dream he concluded that he would return to Athens, get back his power, and end his life as an old man in his own country. That is what he concluded from the dream. Then, as he guided the Persians in, he first landed the slaves from Eretria on an island that belongs to the Styrians, called Aegleia. Next, as the ships came to shore at Marathon, he anchored them there, and when the troops had come ashore from the ships, he set about posting them in their places. While he was arranging all this, he happened to sneeze and cough more violently than usual. He was an old man, and most of his teeth were loose, so the cough shook them, and one of them flew out with the force of it. The tooth fell into the sand, and he tried hard to find it. But when it could not be found, he groaned aloud and said to the men around him: "This land is not ours, and we will not be able to conquer it. Whatever share of it was mine, the tooth has."

108. Hippias decided that this was how his vision had come true. Meanwhile, when the Athenians had taken up their position in the sacred precinct of Heracles, the Plataeans came to help them with their whole force. The Plataeans had given themselves into the Athenians' keeping, and the Athenians had already gone through a great deal of trouble on their behalf. This is how the Plataeans had given themselves to them. When the Thebans were pressing them hard, the Plataeans first tried to give themselves to Cleomenes son of Anaxandrides and the Spartans, who happened to be in the area. But the Spartans would not accept them. They said: "We live too far away, and help like ours would be cold comfort to you. You could be enslaved many times over before any of us even heard about it. We advise you to give yourselves to the Athenians instead. They are your neighbors, and they are not bad at helping." The Spartans gave this advice not so much out of goodwill toward the Plataeans as because they wanted the Athenians to have trouble on their hands, tangled in a conflict with the Boeotians. That was the Spartans' advice to the men of Plataea, and the Plataeans took it. When the Athenians were sacrificing to the Twelve Gods, the Plataeans sat down at the altar as suppliants, and in that way gave themselves to Athens. When the Thebans heard of this, they marched against the Plataeans, and the Athenians came to their aid. They were about to join battle when the Corinthians, who happened to be there, stopped them and settled the quarrel. Both sides left the matter to them, and the Corinthians fixed boundaries for the land, on condition that the Thebans should leave alone any Boeotians who did not want to be counted as Boeotians. After giving this decision the Corinthians left. But as the Athenians were going home, the Boeotians attacked them, and were beaten in the fight. At that the Athenians went beyond the boundaries the Corinthians had set for the Plataeans, and made the river Asopus itself the boundary between Thebes and the lands of Plataea and Hysiae. That, then, is how the Plataeans had given themselves to the Athenians, and now they came to Marathon to help them.

109. The Athenian generals were divided in their opinions. One side urged them not to fight, because they were too few to take on the Persian army. The others, Miltiades among them, urged them to fight. They were split, and the worse opinion was about to win. But there was an eleventh vote, belonging to the man chosen by lot to be polemarch of the Athenians, the war-archon (in the old days the Athenians gave the polemarch an equal vote with the generals). At that time the polemarch was Callimachus of the deme of Aphidnae. Miltiades went to him and said: "Callimachus, it is up to you now. You can bring Athens into slavery, or you can make her free and leave behind you, for as long as men live, a memorial greater than even Harmodius and Aristogeiton left. The Athenians have now come to the greatest danger they have faced since they first became a people. If they give in to the Persians, it is already settled what they will suffer, once they are handed over to Hippias. But if this city wins, it may become the first of the cities of Greece. How this can happen, and how the decision comes to rest with you of all people, I will now explain. We generals, ten of us, are divided: some say we should fight, and some say we should not. Now, if we do not fight, I expect some great spirit of discord will fall on the Athenians and shake their resolve until they go over to the Persians. But if we fight before any rot sets in among any part of the Athenian people, then, if the gods give us a fair chance, we can win the battle. All of this now belongs to you and depends on you. If you side with my view, you will have a country that is free and a city that will be the first in Greece. But if you choose the view of those who are dead set against fighting, you will have the opposite of all the good things I have just described."

110. With these words Miltiades won Callimachus over. With the polemarch's vote added, it was decided to fight. After this, the generals who had voted to fight each handed over his command to Miltiades when his day to command came around. Miltiades accepted, but he still would not start the battle until his own day to command had come.

111. When his day came round, the Athenians were drawn up for battle in this order. The polemarch Callimachus led the right wing, since it was the Athenian custom at that time for the polemarch to hold the right wing. After him, with him in the lead, came the tribes in the order of their numbers, one after another, and last of all the Plataeans were drawn up on the left wing. Ever since this battle, when the Athenians offer sacrifices at the great festivals held every four years, the Athenian herald prays "that blessings may come to the Athenians and the Plataeans both." At this time, though, as the Athenians were being drawn up at Marathon, something like this happened. Their line was made as long as the Persian line, and so the center was only a few ranks deep, and there the army was weakest, while each wing was strong in numbers.

112. When they had taken their places and the sacrifices came out favorable, the Athenians were let loose, and they charged the enemy at a run. The distance between the two armies was at least 4,800 feet, nearly a mile. When the Persians saw them coming at a run, they got ready to meet them, and they thought the Athenians had gone mad, a madness that would destroy them, since there were so few of them and yet they were rushing forward at a run, with no cavalry and no archers. That is what the Persians thought. But once the Athenians closed with them all together, they fought in a way that deserves to be remembered. They were the first Greeks we know of who charged the enemy at a run, and the first who stood their ground at the sight of Median dress and the men who wore it. Until then, the Greeks had been terrified just to hear the name of the Medes.

113. The fighting at Marathon went on for a long time. In the center of the line, where the Persians themselves and the Sacae were posted, the Persians were winning. There they broke through and chased the Athenians inland. But on both wings the Athenians and the Plataeans each won. Having won, they let the beaten part of the enemy run away unchallenged, brought their two wings together, and fought the troops who had broken through their center. And the Athenians won. Then they followed the fleeing Persians, cutting them down, until they reached the sea. There they called for fire and began to lay hold of the ships.

114. In this part of the fighting the polemarch Callimachus was killed, after proving himself a brave man. One of the generals, Stesilaus son of Thrasylaus, was also killed. Besides these, Cynegirus son of Euphorion took hold of the ornament on the stern of a ship, had his hand cut off with an axe, and fell. And many other well-known Athenians were killed as well.

115. In this way the Athenians captured seven of the ships. With the rest, the Persians pushed off from shore. They picked up the Eretrian captives from the island where they had left them and sailed around Sunium, meaning to reach the city before the Athenians. A charge went around among the Athenians that the Alcmaeonids had put them up to this plan. The Alcmaeonids, it was said, had an understanding with the Persians and held up a shield as a signal to them when they were already back on their ships.

116. So the Persians were sailing around Sunium. Meanwhile the Athenians hurried back as fast as they could to defend the city, and they got there before the Persians arrived. They had come from the precinct of Heracles at Marathon, and now they camped at another precinct of Heracles, the one at Cynosarges. The Persians came and lay at anchor off Phalerum, which was the harbor of Athens at that time. They anchored their ships off this place for a while, and then sailed back to Asia.

117. In this battle at Marathon about 6,400 of the Persians were killed, and 192 of the Athenians. That was the number who fell on each side. A strange thing also happened there. An Athenian, Epizelus son of Cuphagoras, was fighting in the thick of it and proving himself a brave man when he lost the sight of his eyes, though he had not been struck anywhere on his body or hit by anything thrown. He stayed blind for the rest of his life. I was told that he used to tell this story about what happened to him: it seemed to him that a tall man in full armor stood against him, whose beard overshadowed his whole shield; this figure passed him by and killed the man standing next to him. That, as I was told, is the story Epizelus told.

118. Datis, though, on his way back to Asia with his army, saw a vision in his sleep when he reached Myconos. What the vision was is not reported. But as soon as day dawned he had the ships searched, and in a Phoenician ship he found a gilded statue of Apollo. He asked where it had been taken from, and when he learned which temple it came from, he sailed in his own ship to Delos. The Delians had returned to their island by then. Datis set the statue in the temple and told the Delians to take it back to Delium in Theban territory, which lies on the coast directly opposite Chalcis. After giving this order, Datis sailed away. The Delians, however, never took the statue back. Twenty years later the Thebans themselves brought it to Delium, because of an oracle.

119. As for the Eretrians who had been enslaved, Datis and Artaphernes took them up to Susa when they reached Asia. King Darius had been very angry with the Eretrians before they were captured, because they had wronged him first, without provocation. But when he saw them brought before him and in his power, he did them no further harm. Instead he settled them in the land of Cissia, on one of his own estates, called Ardericca. This is about twenty-three miles from Susa, and about four and a half miles from the well that yields three different things: they draw asphalt, salt and oil from it. This is how it is done. The liquid is drawn up with a swing-pole, which has half a skin tied to it instead of a bucket. A man dips this down into the well, draws it up, and pours it into a cistern. From there it runs into another vessel and separates three ways. The asphalt and the salt harden at once. The oil, which the Persians call rhadinace, is black and has an unpleasant smell. That is where King Darius settled the Eretrians, and they were still living in that land in my time, keeping their old language. So much for what happened to the Eretrians.

120. Two thousand Spartans came to Athens after the full moon. They were in such a hurry to be in time that they reached Attica on the third day after leaving Sparta. Although they had come too late for the battle, they still wanted to see the Persians. So they went out to Marathon and looked at the bodies of the dead. Then they went home, praising the Athenians and what they had done.
