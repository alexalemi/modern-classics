Chapter 46: The Battle of Salamis, and Xerxes Turns for Home

83. So the Greeks, believing what the Tenians had told them, got ready for a sea battle. As dawn broke they called an assembly of the men who fought on board the ships, and speeches were made. Themistocles' speech was the finest of all. The heart of it was a setting-out of everything that is better against everything that is worse, among all the things that arise in human nature and the human condition. He urged them to choose the better, and with that he wound up his speech and ordered them to board their ships. So they began to board. Meanwhile the trireme arrived from Aegina, the one that had gone off to bring the sons of Aeacus.

84. Then the Greeks put out with all their ships, and while they were still putting out from shore the enemy attacked them at once. Most of the Greeks began backing water and were about to run their ships aground. But Ameinias of Pallene, an Athenian, put out with his ship and rammed one of the enemy's. His ship got locked together with it and the men could not break free, so the others joined the fight to help Ameinias. That is how the Athenians say the battle began. But the Aeginetans say that the ship that had gone to Aegina to bring the sons of Aeacus was the one that started the fight. There is also a story that a phantom woman appeared to them and urged them to fight, so loudly that the whole Greek force heard her. But first she scolded them: "Madmen, how much farther are you going to back water?"

85. Facing the Athenians were the Phoenicians, who held the wing toward Eleusis and the west. Facing the Spartans were the Ionians, who held the wing that stretched east toward Piraeus. A few of the Ionians fought badly on purpose, as Themistocles had urged them to, but most did not. I could give the names of many ship captains who destroyed Greek ships, but I will use none of them except Theomestor son of Androdamas and Phylacus son of Histiaeus, both of them Samians. I mention these two and not the rest for this reason: because of this deed the Persians made Theomestor tyrant of Samos, and Phylacus was entered on the list of the king's benefactors and given a great deal of land as a reward. In the Persian language the king's benefactors are called orosangae.

86. So it went with these two. But most of the king's ships at Salamis were disabled, destroyed some by the Athenians and some by the Aeginetans. For the Greeks fought in good order, each ship in its place, while the enemy had lost all order and did nothing with any plan, so it was likely that things would turn out much as in fact they did. And yet on this day the king's men were far braver than they had been in the fighting off Euboea. Every one of them fought hard out of fear of Xerxes, each man thinking that the king was watching him in particular.

87. As for the rest, I cannot speak of each one separately or say exactly how each man fought, whether foreigner or Greek. But in Artemisia's case, this is what happened, and it raised her even higher in the king's esteem than before. When the king's side had fallen into great confusion, at that moment Artemisia's ship was being chased by an Athenian ship. She could not escape, because ahead of her were other ships of her own side, and her ship, as it happened, was the one nearest the enemy. So she decided what to do, and it turned out very much to her advantage that she did it. With the Athenian ship hard after her, she charged at full speed into a ship of her own side, crewed by Calyndians and carrying Damasithymus, the king of the Calyndians himself. Now it may be true that she had quarreled with him earlier, while they were still at the Hellespont. Even so, I cannot say whether she did this on purpose, or whether the Calyndian ship just happened to fall in her path. But she rammed it and sank it, and her luck brought her a double gain. First, the captain of the Athenian ship, seeing her ram a ship of the king's fleet, turned away and went after others. He supposed that Artemisia's ship was either Greek or had deserted from the enemy and was fighting for the Greeks.

88. That, then, was her first piece of luck: she escaped and was not destroyed. The second was that, though she had done harm to her own side, the very same act won her a great reputation with Xerxes. For it is said that the king, watching the battle, noticed her ship ramming the other one, and one of the men beside him said: "Master, do you see how well Artemisia is fighting? She has just sunk an enemy ship!" He asked whether it was really Artemisia who had done it, and they said it was, since they knew her ship's ensign very well. And they were sure that the ship she had destroyed was one of the enemy's. Among the other things that went her way, as I have said, there was this too: not one man of the Calyndian ship's crew survived to accuse her. And Xerxes, they say, answered what had been said to him with these words: "My men have become women, and my women men." That, they say, is what Xerxes said.

89. Meanwhile, in this struggle, the commander Ariabignes was killed, the son of Darius and brother of Xerxes, and many other notable men of the Persians and Medes and of the allies were killed too. Of the Greeks only a few died. Since the Greeks knew how to swim, those whose ships were destroyed and who were not killed in hand-to-hand fighting swam across to Salamis. But most of the king's men could not swim, and they died in the sea. The greatest number died when the front ships turned to run. The ships stationed behind were trying to push through to the front so that they too could do some brave deed for the king to see, and they ran into their own side's ships as those fled.

90. It also happened, in all this confusion, that some of the Phoenicians whose ships had been destroyed came to the king and accused the Ionians. They said their ships had been lost because of the Ionians, who had betrayed the cause. But as it turned out, not only did the Ionian commanders not lose their lives, but the Phoenicians who accused them got the reward I will now tell. While they were still speaking, a Samothracian ship rammed an Athenian ship. As the Athenian ship was sinking, an Aeginetan ship came at the Samothracian and ran it down. Then the Samothracians, who were skilled javelin-throwers, hurled their javelins and cleared the fighting men off the ship that had wrecked theirs, then boarded it and took it over. This saved the Ionians from punishment. For when Xerxes saw them do so great a deed, he turned on the Phoenicians, since he was bitterly upset and ready to find fault with everyone, and ordered their heads cut off, so that men who had been cowards themselves would not accuse their betters. Xerxes was sitting at the foot of the mountain opposite Salamis called Aegaleos, and whenever he saw any man of his own side do a brave deed in the battle, he asked who had done it, and his scribes wrote down the captain's name, with his father's name and his city. Besides this, Ariaramnes, a Persian who was there and a friend of the Phoenicians, shared their fate. So they went off to deal with the Phoenicians.

91. Meanwhile, as the king's ships turned to run and were sailing out toward Phalerum, the Aeginetans lay in wait for them in the strait and did deeds worth remembering. While the Athenians, in the confusion and uproar, were disabling both the ships that fought back and the ones that fled, the Aeginetans were destroying the ones trying to sail away. Whenever any ship escaped the Athenians, it ran at full speed straight into the Aeginetans.

92. Then two ships met: Themistocles' ship, which was chasing an enemy ship, and that of Polycritus son of Crius, the Aeginetan. Polycritus had just rammed a ship of Sidon. It was the same ship that had captured the Aeginetan guard ship stationed ahead at Sciathus, the one that carried Pytheas son of Ischenous. The Persians had kept Pytheas on board their ship, all cut to pieces as he was, because they marveled at his courage. So now the Sidonian ship was captured with Pytheas on it, along with the Persians I mentioned, and that is how Pytheas came home safe to Aegina. When Polycritus looked at the Athenian ship, he recognized the ensign of the admiral's ship. He shouted across at Themistocles, mocking him about the charge that the Aeginetans had gone over to the Persians, and taunting him with it. Polycritus flung this taunt at Themistocles right after he had rammed the Sidonian ship. Meanwhile the enemy ships that had escaped destruction fled to Phalerum, to be under the protection of the land army.

93. In this sea battle, of all the Greeks the Aeginetans won the highest praise, and after them the Athenians. Of the individual men, the most praised were the Aeginetan Polycritus and two Athenians, Eumenes of Anagyrus and Ameinias of Pallene, the one who had chased Artemisia. If Ameinias had known that Artemisia was sailing in that ship, he would not have stopped until he had either taken her or been taken himself. For the Athenian captains had been given orders about her, and a prize of 10,000 drachmas was offered besides to whoever took her alive, since they thought it an outrage that a woman should make war on Athens. But she, as I said before, had escaped. And the others whose ships had escaped destruction were at Phalerum too.

94. As for Adeimantus, the Corinthian commander, the Athenians say that right at the start, when the ships were closing for battle, he was seized with panic and terror, hoisted his sails and ran away. When the Corinthians saw their admiral's ship running away, they left too. After this, as the story goes, when they had fled as far as the temple of Athena Sciras on Salamis, a light boat met them, sent by the hand of heaven. No one was ever found who had sent it, and it came up to the Corinthians at a time when they knew nothing of how things stood with the fleet. This is why people guess the hand of heaven was in it. For when the boat came near the ships, the men in it said: "Adeimantus, you have turned your ships around and taken to flight, deserting the Greeks. But they are winning, and getting the better of their enemies just as they hoped." When they said this, Adeimantus did not believe it, so they spoke a second time and said that he could take them as hostages and kill them if the Greeks turned out not to be winning. So he turned his ship back, he and the others with him, and they reached the fleet when the work was already done. That is the story the Athenians spread about them. The Corinthians, however, do not accept it. They hold that they were among the first in the fighting, and the rest of Greece backs them up.

95. Aristides son of Lysimachus, the Athenian, whom I mentioned a little earlier as a very good man, did this in the uproar around Salamis. He took a number of the Athenian-born hoplites who had been posted along the shore of Salamis, landed with them on the island of Psyttalea, and they killed all the Persians on that islet.

96. When the sea battle was over, the Greeks towed back to Salamis all the wrecks that happened to be still in the area, and held themselves ready for another sea battle, since they expected the king to use the ships he had left that were undamaged. But the west wind caught many of the wrecks and carried them to the beach in Attica called Colias. So it fulfilled not only all the rest of the oracle spoken about this sea battle by Bacis and Musaeus, but also, and especially as to the wrecks washed up there, a line spoken many years before in an oracle by Lysistratus, an Athenian prophet, which none of the Greeks had understood:

	The women of Colias will roast their barley over fires of oars.

This was to come true after the king had marched away.

97. When Xerxes understood the disaster that had happened to him, he was afraid that one of the Ionians might suggest to the Greeks, or that they might think of it themselves, to sail to the Hellespont and break up the bridges. Then he would be cut off in Europe and in danger of being destroyed completely. So he began to think about running away. But he did not want his plan to be noticed by the Greeks or by his own side. So he tried to build a causeway across to Salamis, and he lashed together Phoenician merchant ships to serve him as both a bridge and a wall, and he made preparations for war as if he meant to fight another battle at sea. Everyone else who saw him doing this was sure he was in earnest and meant to stay and fight. But Mardonius was not fooled: he saw what all this really meant, since long experience had taught him exactly how the king thought.

98. While Xerxes was doing this, he sent a messenger to Persia to announce the disaster that had come on them. Nothing mortal travels faster than these messengers, so cleverly have the Persians devised the system. For they say that however many days the whole journey takes, that many horses and men are posted at intervals along the road, one man and one horse for each day's journey. Neither snow nor rain nor heat nor the dark of night keeps them from finishing their appointed stage at top speed. The first rider delivers the message he carries to the second, and the second to the third, and from there it passes from hand to hand down the line, like the torch race the Greeks run in honor of Hephaestus. The Persians call this relay of horses angareion.

99. The first message that reached Susa, announcing that Xerxes had taken Athens, delighted the Persians who had been left at home so much that they strewed all the roads with myrtle branches, kept burning incense without end, and gave themselves up to sacrifices and feasting. But the second message, which came after it, upset them so badly that they all tore their clothes and gave themselves up to endless weeping and wailing, laying the blame on Mardonius. And the Persians did this not so much out of grief for the ships as out of fear for Xerxes himself.

100. This went on among the Persians the whole time until Xerxes himself came home and put a stop to it. Meanwhile Mardonius saw that Xerxes was badly shaken by the sea battle, and he suspected that the king meant to run away from Athens. He thought about his own position. He would be punished for having persuaded the king to march against Greece, and it was better for him to take the risk of either conquering Greece or dying with honor, staking his life on a great goal. Even so, he leaned toward thinking that he would conquer Greece. He weighed all this, and then he made the king this speech: "Master, don't grieve and don't be so troubled over what has happened. Our whole fate does not hang on a contest of planks and timbers, but on men and horses. None of these Greeks who now think they have finished the job will get off their ships and try to stand against you, and neither will anyone here on the mainland. The ones who did stand against us have paid for it. So if you think it best, let us attack the Peloponnese at once; or if you think it best to wait, we can do that. But don't lose heart. There is no way for the Greeks to escape being your slaves, once they have first paid the price for what they have done to you, now and in the past. That would be the best course. But if you have really decided to go back yourself and take your army with you, I have other advice for that case too. My king, do not let the Persians become a laughingstock to the Greeks. None of your affairs has suffered because of the Persians, and you cannot name a single place where we acted like cowards. If Phoenicians or Egyptians or Cyprians or Cilicians acted like cowards, the disaster that followed has nothing to do with the Persians. So now, since it is not the Persians who have wronged you, take my advice. If you have decided not to stay here, then go back to your own home and take the main body of the army with you. And it will be my job to hand Greece over to you, conquered, if I can choose 300,000 men from the army to do it."

101. When Xerxes heard this he was glad and pleased, as far as he could be after his misfortunes, and he told Mardonius that he would think it over and then answer him and say which of the two courses he would take. So when he was consulting with the Persians who had been called to advise him, he decided to send for Artemisia to advise him as well, because before the battle she alone had shown that she saw what ought to be done. When Artemisia came, Xerxes sent everyone else away, both his Persian counselors and his spearmen of the guard, and said to her: "Mardonius urges me to stay here and attack the Peloponnese. He says the Persians and the land army have had no share in my disaster, and that they would gladly prove it to me. So he urges me either to do this, or else he wants to choose 300,000 men from the army himself and hand Greece over to me, conquered, while I go back home with the rest of the army. Now, you gave me good advice about the sea battle that has been fought, when you urged me not to fight it. So advise me now too, which of these things I should do, so that I may decide well."

102. That was his question, and she answered: "My king, it is hard to hit on the best answer when someone asks for advice. But in the present situation I think you should go back home and leave Mardonius here, if he wants to and promises to do this, with the men he wants to have. For on the one hand, if he conquers the people he says he wants to conquer, and the plans he has in mind when he talks this way succeed, then the deed is yours after all, Master, since it was your slaves who did it. On the other hand, if things turn out the opposite of what Mardonius intends, it will be no great misfortune, since you yourself will be safe, and so will the power of your house in that part of the world. For as long as you and your house are safe, the Greeks will have to fight many battles, many times over, for their own survival. As for Mardonius, if anything bad happens to him, no one will care much. And if the Greeks win, it is a victory that is no victory, since they will only have destroyed one of your slaves. And you will go home having done what you made your march to do: you will have burned Athens to the ground."

103. Xerxes was delighted with this advice, since she had managed to say exactly what he himself meant to do. In my opinion, not even if every man and every woman in the world had been advising him to stay would he have stayed, he was so terrified. So he praised Artemisia and sent her off to take his sons to Ephesus, for some of his illegitimate sons had come along with him.

104. To look after these sons he sent with them Hermotimus, a native of Pedasa, who in the king's eyes was second to none of his eunuchs. The Pedasians live inland from Halicarnassus, and at Pedasa something like this happens. Whenever some trouble is about to come, within a certain time, on all the people who live around this city, the priestess of Athena there grows a long beard. This has already happened to them twice.

105. Hermotimus was one of these Pedasians. And of all the men we know of up to now, he took the greatest revenge for a wrong done to him. He had been captured by enemies and was being sold as a slave, and he was bought by Panionius, a man of Chios, who had set himself to make his living by the most unholy trade there is. Whenever he got hold of boys who had any good looks, he castrated them and took them to Sardis or Ephesus and sold them for large sums of money. For among non-Greeks, eunuchs are thought more valuable than men who are not eunuchs for every kind of position of trust. Panionius castrated many boys, since that was how he made his living, and among them was this man I am speaking of. But Hermotimus was not unlucky in everything. He was sent from Sardis to the king along with other gifts, and as time went on he came to be honored by Xerxes above all his other eunuchs.

106. When the king was at Sardis, setting the Persian army in motion to march against Athens, Hermotimus went down on some business to the part of Mysia called Atarneus, which the Chians occupy, and there he found Panionius. He recognized him and spoke to him in a very friendly way. First he went over all the good things he had because of him, and then he promised to do him many good things in return, if Panionius would bring his household and come to live there. So Panionius gladly accepted the offer and brought his children and his wife. Then, when Hermotimus had caught him with his whole household, he said: "You have made your living by the most unholy deeds of any man who ever lived. What harm did I, or any of my ancestors, ever do to you or to anyone of yours, that you made me nothing instead of a man? Did you think the gods would never notice the things you did then? But the gods, following the law of justice, have delivered you into my hands for your unholy deeds, so you will have no cause to complain about the punishment I am going to give you." When he had finished reproaching him, he had Panionius' sons brought in, four of them, and Panionius was forced to castrate his own sons. He was forced, and he did it. And when he had done it, the sons were forced to do the same to him. So vengeance, by the hand of Hermotimus, caught up with Panionius.

107. When Xerxes had entrusted his sons to Artemisia to take back to Ephesus, he called Mardonius and told him to choose whatever men he wanted from the army, and to make his deeds match his words, if he could. That is as far as things went that day. Then in the night, on the king's orders, the commanders of the fleet began withdrawing their ships from Phalerum toward the Hellespont, each one as fast as he could, to guard the bridges for the king to cross. When the king's ships were near Zoster on their voyage, they saw the little points of rock that jut out into the sea from that part of the mainland, took them for ships, and fled a good distance. In time, though, they realized these were not ships but rocks, gathered together again, and went on with their voyage.

108. When day came, the Greeks saw that the land army was still in its place, and they supposed that the ships were still around Phalerum too. They expected another sea battle and got ready to fight it off. But when they learned that the ships had gone, they decided at once to chase them. So they chased them as far as Andros, but never caught sight of Xerxes' fleet. When they reached Andros, they held a council about what to do. Themistocles' view was that they should make their way through the islands chasing the ships, and then sail straight to the Hellespont and break up the bridges. But Eurybiades argued the opposite. He said that if they broke up the floating bridges, they would be doing Greece the greatest harm possible. For if the Persian king were cut off and forced to stay in Europe, he would try not to sit still. If he sat still, none of his affairs could move forward, no way home would open up for him, and his army would starve to death. But if he took action and kept at it, all Europe might come over to him, city by city and nation by nation, either conquered or surrendering on terms before they could be conquered. And they would have the Greeks' harvest, year after year, to live on. But Eurybiades thought that, beaten at sea as he was, the Persian would not stay in Europe. So they should let him run away until his flight brought him home to his own country. After that, he said, they could start fighting for the Persian king's land. The commanders of the other Peloponnesians also held to this view.

109. When Themistocles saw that he could not persuade them, or at least most of them, to sail to the Hellespont, he changed his tune and turned to the Athenians. They were the angriest at the enemy's escape, and were eager to sail to the Hellespont even on their own if the others would not. To them he said: "I have been there myself many times before now, and I have heard of many more cases, when something like this has happened: men driven into a tight corner have fought again after a defeat and made good their earlier disaster. As for us, we have had the luck to save ourselves and Greece by driving off so huge a cloud of men. So let us not chase enemies who are running from us. For we are not the ones who did this, but the gods and heroes. They grudged that one man should be king of both Asia and Europe, and a man at that who was wicked and arrogant, who made no difference between things sacred and things profane, who burned and threw down the statues of the gods, and who even had the sea whipped and lowered chains into it. But as things stand now, it is best for us to stay in Greece and take care of ourselves and our families. Let each man rebuild his house and see to the sowing of his fields, now that he has driven the invader out completely. Then at the start of spring let us sail for the Hellespont and Ionia." He said this to build up a store of gratitude with the Persian king, so that if any trouble ever came to him at the hands of the Athenians, he would have somewhere to take refuge. And that is in fact what happened.

110. So Themistocles said this to deceive them, and the Athenians did as he said. He had long had a reputation as a clever man, and now he had shown that he really was both clever and wise, so they were ready to follow his advice in everything. Once they were persuaded, Themistocles at once sent off some men in a boat, men he could trust to keep silent no matter what test they were put to, with a message he ordered them to give the king. One of them, again, was his servant Sicinnus. When they reached Attica, the others stayed behind in the boat while Sicinnus went up to Xerxes and said: "Themistocles son of Neocles, the commander of the Athenians and the best and ablest man of all the allies, has sent me to tell you this. Themistocles the Athenian, wanting to do you a service, has held back the Greeks when they were eager to chase your ships and break up the bridges on the Hellespont. So now you can go home in peace." When they had delivered this message, they sailed back.

111. The Greeks, meanwhile, having decided not to chase the king's ships any farther or sail to the Hellespont to break up the crossing, were besieging Andros, meaning to take it. For the Andrians were the first of the islanders to refuse when Themistocles asked them for money. Themistocles put it to them that the Athenians had come with two great gods on their side, Persuasion and Compulsion, so the Andrians absolutely had to give them money. The Andrians answered that it was no wonder, then, that Athens was great and prosperous, since the Athenians were so well supplied with useful gods. But the Andrians, they said, were poor, as poor as anyone could be, and they had two useless gods who never left their island but always stayed fondly there, namely Poverty and Helplessness. Since the Andrians had these gods, they would not give money; for the power of the Athenians could never be stronger than their inability.

112. So, having given this answer and refused the money, the Andrians were under siege. And Themistocles, who never stopped wanting money, sent threatening messages to the other islands asking them for money, using the same messengers he had sent to the king. He said that if they did not give what was asked of them, he would bring the Greek fleet against them and besiege them and take them. By saying this he collected great sums of money from the Carystians and the Parians. They heard that Andros was under siege because it had gone over to the Persians, and that Themistocles was more highly regarded than any of the other commanders, and out of fear of this they sent money. Whether any other islanders also gave money I can't say for certain, but I think some others did, and not these alone. Even so, the Carystians at least got no relief from trouble because of it; but the Parians escaped attack, because they won Themistocles over with money. So Themistocles, with Andros as his base, was collecting money for himself from the islanders without the other commanders knowing.
