EULER, LETTERS TO A GERMAN PRINCESS -- TEXT ANALYSIS

Source: Cournot's 1842 Hachette reprint, 2 vols, which prints the 1768-72
St Petersburg text unretouched (Condorcet's 1787 edition cut and rewrote,
especially the religious letters; Cournot restored the princeps) but
numbers the letters in three thematic series. We restore Euler's single
numbering 1-234 and keep Cournot's three series as Parts, since that is the
structure a reader can use: I physics and cosmology (magnitude, speed,
sound, music, light, the eye, gravity, the planets, tides), II metaphysics,
logic and morals (the soul, monads, free will, prayer, the syllogism with
the famous circle diagrams, language), III particular physics (optics
again, electricity, magnetism, the compass, longitude, lenses and
telescopes, refraction of the air).

Voice: a teacher of genius writing to a clever teenager. Short steps,
homely examples, courteous address. The difficulty is not archaism (his
French is plain) but OCR noise, page-break paragraph splits, Cournot's
footnotes interleaved with the text, and six plates of figures cited by
number (numbering restarts in the second volume, letter 115 on).

Sensitive content: Euler is a devout Calvinist arguing against the
freethinkers and Leibniz-Wolff determinism; render as his argument in
measured register. War: the letters are written during the Seven Years'
War (Berlin occupied October 1760); render references plainly.

Famous passages: the Euler circles (Letters 102-105 in the continuous
numbering, Figures 39-89 of the first volume); why the sky is blue and
bodies coloured (vibration theory); the objection to monads.
