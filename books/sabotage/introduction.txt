The Office of Strategic Services was the United States' intelligence and special-operations agency in the Second World War. It was created in 1942 under William J. Donovan, a New York lawyer and decorated veteran of the First World War, and it ran spies, propaganda and resistance operations behind enemy lines until it was dissolved in 1945. The Central Intelligence Agency, founded in 1947, is its successor. This manual is one of its field manuals, dated 17 January 1944 and issued with a short covering memorandum signed by Donovan.

It was not written for agents. Its readers were meant to be ordinary people in enemy-occupied countries: factory hands, railwaymen, clerks, telephone operators. The idea, set out in its opening sections, is that an occupied population can hurt an enemy war economy without explosives, training or great risk, through countless small acts that look like carelessness: a tool left dull, a wrong switch, a misfiled order, a meeting that never reaches a decision. The manual explains what such sabotage can achieve, how to persuade people to do it, and how to keep them safe from detection, and then lists suggestions by kind of target, from buildings and factories to railways, telephones and power lines.

The section most often quoted today is the eleventh, "General Interference with Organizations and Production". Its advice to insist on doing everything "through channels", to refer every question to a committee, to haggle over the wording of communications and to reopen decisions already made reads, eighty years later, as a description of ordinary office life. Management writers have cited it for that reason, and it is the part of the manual most people have heard of.

It should be read as a document of its war: instructions for undermining an occupying army in 1944, written in the confident, practical voice of military doctrine, by an agency that measured success by enemy production lost.

This is a restored edition of the manual as issued, with Donovan's memorandum and the five numbered sections, keeping the manual's own lettered and numbered outline. The text is that of Project Gutenberg, checked word by word against a scan of a 1944 copy, which bears a later "declassified" stamp; one heading is corrected from it. As a work of the United States government, the manual is in the public domain.
