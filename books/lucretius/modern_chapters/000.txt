Book One: Matter and Void
(Part 1 of 2)

Mother of the sons of Aeneas, delight of men and gods, Venus who
feeds us all: under the gliding signs of heaven you fill the
ship-carrying sea and the crop-bearing lands with life. Through you
every kind of living thing is conceived, and rises up, and looks at
the light of the sun. From you, goddess, the winds run away. From you
and from your coming the clouds of heaven run. For you the crafty
earth sends up sweet flowers, for you the level water of the sea
laughs, and the sky goes calm and shines with light poured out
everywhere.

The moment the look of a spring day is uncovered, the moment the
breeding breath of the west wind is unbarred and blowing, the birds of
the air are the first to signal you and your arrival, goddess, with
their hearts struck through by your power. Then the wild herds go
bounding over the fat pastures and swim the fast rivers. Every one of
them, caught by your charm, follows you eagerly wherever you lead.
Then, all through the seas and the mountains and the tearing rivers,
through the leafy houses of the birds and over the green plains,
striking soft love into the breast of every one of them, you make them
breed, kind after kind, and want to.

Since you alone govern the nature of things, and nothing without you
comes up into the shining shores of light, and nothing is made glad or
lovable without you, I want you with me while I write these verses. I
am trying to set down the nature of things for our friend Memmius,
whom you, goddess, have always wanted to be outstanding, and dressed
in every excellence. All the more, then, give my words a charm that
lasts.

And while I write, make the savage business of soldiering fall asleep
and lie quiet over every sea and every land. You alone can help
mortals to a quiet peace, because the savage business of war is
governed by Mars, who is strong in arms, and Mars throws himself into
your lap again and again, beaten by the wound of love that never
heals. He looks up at you with his smooth neck bent back and feeds his
hungry eyes on love, gaping at you, goddess, with his breath hanging
on your mouth as he lies there. Pour your sweet talk over him,
glorious one, while he lies back against your holy body and you bend
around him, and ask for a quiet peace for the Romans. In this bad time
for our country I cannot do this work with an untroubled mind, and the
famous son of the house of Memmius cannot fail the common good at a
moment like this.

For it must be that the whole nature of the gods, in itself, enjoys
undying life in the deepest peace, cut off from anything to do with
us, far away and separate. It is free of all pain, free of all danger,
strong in its own resources, in need of nothing from us. It is not won
over by our good behaviour and it is not touched by anger.

Now for the rest. Empty your ears, and bring me a sharp mind cleared
of its worries, and listen to true reasoning. Do not throw away my
gifts, laid out for you with faithful care, before you have understood
them. I am going to begin explaining to you the highest law of heaven
and of the gods. I am going to unfold the first-beginnings of things:
the atoms, out of which nature makes everything, and feeds it, and
makes it grow, and into which the same nature breaks it all down again
when it is destroyed. In setting out the argument I am used to calling
them the material of things, the bodies that do the begetting, the
seeds of things, and, because everything comes first from them, the
first bodies.

While human life lay on the ground in front of everyone's eyes,
disgusting to look at, crushed under the weight of religion, which
kept showing its head from the regions of the sky and standing over
mortals with a horrible face, one Greek was the first man who dared to
lift his mortal eyes against it, the first to stand up to it. Stories
about gods did not stop him; neither did lightning, or the sky with
its threatening rumble. If anything, they sharpened the keen courage
of his mind, and made him want to be the first to break the tight bars
on nature's gates. So the living force of his mind won through, and he
went far outside the flaming walls of the world and travelled the
whole immensity in mind and spirit; and out of that he brings us back,
in victory, an account of what can come to be and what cannot, and on
what principle each thing has its power limited, and a boundary stone
driven deep. So now religion is under our feet in its turn, and
trodden down, and that victory lifts us level with the sky.

The thing I am afraid of here is that you may think you are being
started on the elements of an ungodly argument, and set on the road to
crime. It is the other way round. It was that religion which again and
again gave birth to criminal and ungodly acts.

Think of Aulis, and how the chosen leaders of the Greeks, the first
men of the army, fouled the altar of the virgin goddess of the
crossroads with the blood of Iphigenia. The woollen band was tied
round her girl's hair and came streaming down evenly on both sides of
her cheeks. She saw her father standing sadly in front of the altar,
and beside him the attendants hiding the knife, and the people of her
own country in tears at the sight of her. She was dumb with fear and
sank to the ground on her knees. It did not help the poor girl at that
moment that she had been the first to give the king the name of
father. She was lifted by men's hands and carried shaking to the
altar, not so that she could be escorted by a ringing wedding song
when the solemn ritual was over, but so that at the very moment for
marriage she could fall, clean and unclean, a victim cut down by the
hand of her father, and give the fleet a lucky and favourable send-off.

That is how much evil religion has been able to talk people into.

You yourself, sooner or later, will want to break away from me,
beaten by the terrifying words of the prophets. Of course you will.
Think how many dreams they can invent for you, dreams that can turn
your whole plan of life upside down and stir all your fortunes about
with fear. And there is good reason for it. If people could see that
there is a fixed end to their troubles they would have some grounds
for standing up to the religions and the threats of the prophets. As
it is, there is no way of standing up to them, no chance of it,
because they have to be afraid of everlasting punishments after death.
And that is because they do not know what the nature of the soul is:
whether it is born with us, or slips into us instead when we are being
born, and whether it is torn away by death and dies along with us, or
goes to see the dark places and the huge pits of the underworld, or
whether by some divine arrangement it slides into other animals, as
our own Ennius sang. He was the first to bring down from lovely
Helicon a crown of evergreen leaves, to be famous through the peoples
of Italy. And yet Ennius also sets out, in verses that will last, that
there are regions of the dead where neither our souls nor our bodies
stay, but a sort of image of us, strangely pale. It was from there, he
says, that the ghost of Homer, who is always in flower, rose up in
front of him and began to pour out salt tears and to unfold the nature
of things in words.

So we have to work out an account of the things above us: on what
principle the sun and the moon make their journeys, and by what force
everything happens down here on earth. And first of all we have to see
with sharp reasoning where the soul and the nature of the mind come
from, and what it is that meets us when we are awake and terrifies our
minds when we are sick or buried in sleep, so that we seem to see, and
to hear standing in front of us, people whose bones the earth is
holding, who have met their death.

I know perfectly well that it is hard to throw light on the dark
discoveries of the Greeks in Latin verse, above all because so much
has to be done with new words, given how poor our language is and how
new the subject matter. Still, your excellence and the pleasure I hope
for from our friendship talk me into taking on any amount of work, and
make me stay awake through quiet nights, hunting for the words and the
poetry that will let me hold a bright light in front of your mind, so
that you can see all the way into hidden things.

So this terror of the mind and this darkness have to be scattered.
Not by the rays of the sun and the bright arrows of daylight, but by
looking at nature and working out how it runs.

Here is the first principle we shall take as our starting point:
nothing is ever produced out of nothing by any divine power. The
reason all mortals are gripped by fear like this is that they see many
things happening on earth and in the sky, and cannot in any way work
out what causes them, and so they think it is done by a god's will.
That is why, once we have seen that nothing can be created out of
nothing, we shall then see more clearly what we are looking for: what
each thing can be created from, and how each thing comes about,
without any work of gods.

Take it that things could come out of nothing. Then anything could be
born from anything; nothing would need a seed. Men could come up out
of the sea, and the scaly kind out of the earth, and birds could burst
out of the sky. Cattle and other herd animals, every kind of wild
animal, would hold the farmland and the wilderness alike, with no rule
about what gave birth to what. The same fruit would not stay on the
same trees. It would change about, and any tree could carry anything.
If each thing had no bodies of its own to be begotten from, how could
anything have a mother that stayed the same? As it is, each thing is
created out of fixed seeds, and grows up out of them, and comes out
into the shores of light where its own material and first bodies are.
That is why everything cannot be produced out of everything: each
particular thing holds a power that is its own and kept separate.

Then again, why do we see the rose in spring, the corn in the heat,
the vines pouring out their fruit when autumn calls them, if it is not
that fixed seeds of things have flowed together at their own time, and
whatever is being created is uncovered then, while the seasons are
there and the living earth brings tender things out into the shores of
light in safety? If they came out of nothing they would spring up
suddenly at unpredictable intervals in the wrong parts of the year,
because there would be no atoms to be kept apart from a breeding
partnership at the wrong season.

Nor, if things could grow out of nothing, would there be any need of
time for the seed to come together in as they grow. Small babies would
turn into young men on the spot, and trees would leap up suddenly out
of the earth, fully grown. Plainly none of that happens. Everything
grows little by little, as you would expect from a fixed seed, and
keeps its own kind as it grows. So you can be sure that every thing
gets larger and is fed out of its own material.

Add to this that the earth cannot bring up its cheerful crops without
the fixed rains of the year, and that the nature of living things,
kept apart from food, cannot pass on its kind or keep its own life
going. It is better to think of it this way: many things have many
bodies in common, as we see letters shared between words, than that
anything could exist without first-beginnings.

Finally, why could nature not have produced men on a scale that let
them cross the sea on foot at the shallows and tear great mountains
apart with their hands and outlive many generations of life, if not
because a fixed material has been assigned to things for their
begetting, and that fixes what can come out of it?

So we have to admit that nothing can be made out of nothing, since
things need a seed, and it is from the seed that each of them can be
brought out into the soft air.

Lastly, since we see that cultivated ground does better than
uncultivated and gives back better fruit for the work of our hands, it
follows that there are atoms in the earth which we stir into rising up
when we turn the fertile clods with the ploughshare and work the soil
of the land. If there were none, you would see everything getting a
great deal better on its own, without any labour of ours.

Add to this that nature breaks each thing down again into its own
bodies, and does not destroy things into nothing. If anything were
mortal in all its parts, everything would be snatched from our eyes
and perish on the spot. There would be no need of any force to break
its parts apart and untie its knots. As it is, everything consists of
everlasting seed, and so, until some force comes along to knock a
thing apart with a blow, or to get inside through the empty spaces and
break it up, nature does not let the destruction of anything be seen.

Besides, if time completely destroys the things it takes away with
age, and eats up all their material, where does Venus bring the
animals back into the light of life from, kind after kind? And where
does the crafty earth find what feeds them and makes them grow, kind
after kind, once they are back? Where do the springs that rise in the
ground and the far-off rivers keep the sea supplied from? Where does
the upper air feed the stars from? Endless time and all the days that
have gone by ought to have used up everything that has a mortal body.
But if the things out of which this whole sum of things is remade have
existed through all that stretch of time gone by, then plainly they
are furnished with an undying nature. So things cannot fall back into
nothing.

And one last point. The same force and the same cause would finish
everything off, all together, if there were no everlasting material,
tangled up more or with less. A touch would be enough to bring death,
since there would be nothing made of everlasting body whose weave a
force would have to break. As it is, the knots that tie the atoms
together are of different kinds, and the material is everlasting, and
so things stay unhurt in their bodies until a force is found sharp
enough to match the weave of each. So nothing goes back to nothing.
Everything goes back at the break-up into the bodies of matter.

And in the end the rains die, when father sky has thrown them down
into the lap of mother earth. But the bright crops rise up and the
branches go green on the trees, the trees themselves grow and are
weighed down with fruit; and from that our own kind and the animals
are fed; from that we see happy cities in flower with children and the
leafy woods singing everywhere with new birds; from that the tired
cattle lay their fat bodies down through the glad pastures and the
white milky liquid runs out of their swollen udders; from that the new
young play about frisking on their weak legs through the soft grass,
with their new little minds struck through by neat milk. So the things
we see do not utterly perish, since nature makes one thing out of
another and does not let anything be born unless death has helped it,
somewhere else.

Now then. I have shown you that things cannot be created out of
nothing, and that once born they cannot be called back to nothing.
Still, in case you begin to distrust what I say because atoms cannot
be made out by the eye, take in some further bodies which you must
admit exist among things and cannot be seen.

To begin with, the driving force of the wind whips the sea, wrecks
huge ships and scatters the clouds; sometimes it sweeps across the
plains in a tearing whirlwind and strews them with great trees, and
batters the mountain tops with forest-breaking gusts. That is how it
rages, with a sharp roaring, and how the sea storms with a threatening
rumble. So there is no doubt: winds are invisible bodies, which sweep
the sea, sweep the lands, sweep the clouds of the sky, and snatch them
about with a sudden whirl and knock them to pieces. They flow and
spread their wreckage exactly the way the soft nature of water does
when it is suddenly carried along in an overflowing river, when a
great rush of water off the high mountains swells it with heavy rains
and throws in broken branches and whole trees. Strong bridges cannot
stand the sudden force of the water coming at them: that is how
violently the river, made muddy by the great rain, drives at the
piles with all its strength. It wrecks them with a huge noise, and
rolls great rocks under its waves, and tears down whatever stands in
the way of its flood. So the blasts of the wind must travel in the
same way. When they have come down like a strong river in whatever
direction, they push things in front of them and knock them over with
repeated blows, and sometimes they catch them up in a twisting eddy
and carry them off fast in a spinning whirl. So again and again: winds
are invisible bodies, since in their actions and their behaviour they
turn out to be rivals of great rivers, which have bodies you can see.

Then, again, we smell the different smells of things and yet we never
see them coming to our nostrils. We do not look at heat, we cannot
take hold of cold with our eyes, and we are not used to seeing voices.
But all of these must be made of bodily stuff, since they can strike
the senses. Nothing can touch or be touched except body.

Then again, clothes hung up on a shore where the waves break get damp,
and the same clothes spread out in the sun dry off. But it was never
seen how the moisture of the water settled into them, or how it ran
away again in the heat. So the moisture is scattered into small parts
which the eyes cannot see in any way at all.

More than that: with the sun coming back over many years, a ring on
the finger is worn thin underneath by being worn. The dripping of
water hollows out a stone. The curved iron ploughshare secretly grows
smaller in the fields. We see stone paving worn away by the feet of
the crowd. And the bronze statues by the gates show their right hands
worn thin by the touch of people greeting them and passing by. So we
see that these things get smaller, since they have been rubbed down.
But nature, keeping it to herself, has shut off our view of the bodies
that leave at any particular moment.

Finally, whatever the days and nature add to things little by little,
forcing them to grow at their measured rate, no sharpness of the eye
can make out however hard it strains; and the same with whatever
shrinks with age and wasting, and with the rocks that hang over the
sea and are eaten away by the gnawing salt: you cannot see what each
of them loses at any given time. So nature carries on her business
with invisible bodies.

And yet things are not held packed tight with body on every side. There
is void in things. Knowing this will be useful to you in many places.
It will stop you wandering about in doubt and asking forever about the
sum of things, and distrusting what I say.

There is, then, a place that cannot be touched: empty, void. If there
were not, things could not move in any way at all. The job that
belongs to body is to get in the way and to block, and it would be
doing that to everything all the time. So nothing could go forward,
because nothing would ever start by giving way. As it is, all through
the seas and the lands and the heights of the sky we see many things
moving in many ways for many reasons in front of our eyes, and if
there were no void they would not so much lack their restless motion
as never have been produced at all, since matter would be jammed tight
on every side and lying still.

Besides, however solid things are thought to be, you can see from this
that they are made of thin body. In rocks and caves the liquid wet of
water works its way through and everything weeps with fat drops. Food
spreads itself out into the whole body of every animal. Trees grow and
pour out their fruit in season, because the food is spread through the
whole of them, from the deepest roots up through the trunk and along
every branch. Voices go through walls and fly across the shut rooms of
houses; stiff cold works its way in to the bones. If there were no
empty spaces for these bodies to pass through, you would see none of
it happening.

And finally, why do we see some things heavier than others when their
size is no bigger? If there is as much body in a ball of wool as there
is in a lump of lead, they ought to weigh the same, since it is the
job of body to press everything downwards, while the nature of the
void stays weightless. So a thing which is just as big and yet turns
out lighter plainly declares that it has more void in it; and the
heavier thing announces that it has more body in it and much less
emptiness inside. So there is, beyond doubt, the thing we are hunting
for with careful reasoning, mixed in with things: what we call the
void.

There is one point here that I am forced to get in first, in case
something certain people invent leads you away from the truth. They
say that the waters give way to the shining scaly kind and open liquid
roads for them, because the fish leave places behind them for the
water to flow together into as it gives way; and that in the same
manner other things can move about among themselves and change places,
even though everything is full. That whole idea has been taken up on a
false argument. Where, in the end, will the fish be able to go forward
to, unless the water has given them room? And where will the waves be
able to give way to, when the fish cannot go? So either we have to
take motion away from every body, or we have to say that void is mixed
in with things, and that this is where each thing gets its first start
of moving from.

Lastly, if two broad bodies quickly bounce apart after coming
together, all the air has to take over the void that is made between
the bodies. However fast the air rushes together from all round it,
still it cannot fill the whole space at one moment. It must occupy the
first place, then the next, and then take possession of the whole. If
someone happens to think that this happens because the air packs
itself tighter when the bodies have sprung apart, he is wrong. In that
case a vacuum is being made where there was none before, and a vacuum
that was there before is being filled up again; and air cannot be
packed tighter on that argument, and even if it could, it could not, I
think, without void draw itself into itself and bring its parts
together into one.

So, however long you hold things up by finding objections, you have to
admit in the end that there is void in things. And I can scrape
together a great many more arguments to give you confidence in what I
say. But for a sharp mind these small tracks are enough, and you can
work out the rest by yourself. Just as dogs very often find, by their
noses, the leaf-covered resting places of a mountain-wandering wild
animal, once they have got onto its sure tracks, so you will be able
to see one thing after another for yourself in matters like these, and
work your way into all the hidden dens and drag the truth out of them.

And if you are lazy about it, or back off from the subject even a
little, here is what I can promise you plainly, Memmius: my sweet
tongue will pour you out such generous draughts from great springs,
out of a rich heart, that I am afraid slow old age may creep through
my limbs and undo the bars of life in me before the whole store of
arguments on any single question has been sent through your ears in
verse.

But now, to take up the thread and go on weaving the argument: the
whole of nature, taken by itself, consists of two things. There are
bodies, and there is void, in which those bodies are placed and
through which they move about in different directions. That there is
body is declared by the common feeling of everyone; and unless there
is a first belief founded firmly on that, we shall have nothing to
refer back to in matters that are hidden, and no way of confirming
anything by the reasoning of the mind. Then again, if there were no
place and space, which we call void, bodies could not be situated
anywhere at all, and could not travel about in any direction
whatsoever. I showed you that a little earlier.

Besides, there is nothing you could name which is separate from all
body and cut off from void, and which might be found as a sort of
third nature. Whatever exists will have to be something in itself,
whether its size is great or in the end small, so long as it exists.
If it can be touched, however lightly and however little, it will
increase the total of body and add itself to the sum. If it cannot be
touched, and cannot in any part stop anything travelling through it,
then plainly it will be the empty thing we call void.

Besides, whatever exists in itself will either do something, or have
to be acted on by other things acting, or it will be such that things
can exist and happen in it. But nothing can do anything or be acted on
without body, and nothing can provide a place except empty void. So
beyond void and bodies no third nature can be left over in the total
of things, not one that ever falls under our senses at any time, and
not one that anybody could grasp by the reasoning of the mind.

Everything else you can name will turn out to be either attached to
these two, or something that happens to them. Attached means whatever
can nowhere be separated and taken away without a fatal breaking up:
weight for rocks, heat for fire, wetness for water, touch for all
bodies, untouchableness for void. On the other hand slavery, poverty
and riches, freedom, war, peace, and the rest, whose coming and going
leaves the nature of a thing unhurt: these we are used to calling, as
is right, events.

Time, likewise, does not exist by itself; but from things themselves
there follows a sense of what has been done in the past, and then what
is upon us now, and then what will come after. Nobody, we have to
admit, feels time by itself, apart from the motion of things and their
quiet rest.

And in the end, when people speak of the rape of the daughter of
Tyndareus, and of the peoples born in Troy being beaten down in war,
we have to be careful not to be forced into saying that these things
exist by themselves, since the generations of men whose events they
were have already been carried off, past recall, by time gone by. Any
action whatsoever could be called an event, of the lands, or of the
regions themselves. And in the end, if there had been no material of
things and no place and space in which things happen, the fire lit by
love of the beauty of Tyndareus's daughter, growing hot under the
Phrygian breast of Paris, would never have set alight the famous
struggles of savage war; and the wooden horse would never have set
Troy on fire without the Trojans knowing, giving birth in the night to
Greeks. So you can see all the way through this: things that were done
do not exist in themselves, as body does, and cannot be said to exist
in the way void does. It is more correct to call them events of body,
and of the place in which things happen.

Now, bodies are partly atoms and partly the things that are made out
of atoms coming together. As for the atoms, no force can put them out:
they win in the end by being solid body.

It does seem hard to believe that anything can be found among things
with a solid body. The lightning of the sky goes through the walls of
houses, as shouting and voices do. Iron glows white in the fire, and
rocks split apart in the fierce boiling heat. The stiffness of gold is
undone and melts away in the heat, and the ice of bronze is beaten by
the flame and turns to liquid. Heat works its way through silver, and
so does penetrating cold, since we feel both when we are holding cups
in our hands in the usual way and the dew of water is poured in from
above. So it looks as if there is nothing solid among things at all.
But because true reasoning and the nature of things force it, stay
with me for a few verses while I show that there are things which
consist of solid and everlasting body: what we teach are the seeds of
things and the atoms, out of which the whole sum of things has now
been created.

To begin with, since a double nature of two things has been found,
utterly unlike each other, body and place, in which things happen,
each of them has to exist by itself and pure. Wherever there is space,
what we call void, there is no body; and wherever body holds itself,
there is certainly no empty void. So the first bodies are solid and
without void.

Besides, since there is void in the things that are born, there has to
be solid matter round it. And nothing can be proved by true reasoning
to hide void inside its own body and hold it there, unless you allow
that what confines it is solid. And that can be nothing except a
gathering of matter, which is able to hold in the void of things. So
matter, which consists of solid body, can be everlasting, while the
rest is broken down.

Then again, if there were nothing that could be called void, everything
would be solid; and unless on the other hand there were fixed bodies
to fill whatever places they held, all that exists as space would be
empty void. So plainly body and void alternate, marked off from each
other, since the total is neither properly full nor properly empty.
There are, then, fixed bodies which can mark off empty space from
full. These cannot be broken up when they are struck by blows from
outside, nor unravelled by anything penetrating right into them, nor
made to give way by any other attack, as I showed you a little earlier.
For it seems that nothing can be knocked to pieces, or broken, or cut
in two by splitting, without void; and nothing can take in wet, or
seeping cold, or penetrating fire, which is what finishes everything
off. And the more void a thing holds inside it, the more it gives way
when these things get right into it. So if the first bodies are solid
and without void, as I have shown, they must be everlasting.

Besides, if matter had not been everlasting, everything would before
now have gone back completely into nothing, and everything we see
would have been born again out of nothing. But since I showed above
that nothing can be created out of nothing and that what has been born
cannot be called back to nothing, the atoms must have an undying body,
into which each thing can be broken down at its last hour, so that
there is material available for things to be made again. So the atoms
are of solid simplicity, and there is no other way they could have
been kept through the ages and made things again out of an infinite
time gone by.
