Book Four: The Senses, Sight, and Love
(Part 2 of 3)

Then there is the puddle. A collected splash of water no deeper than
one finger, standing among the paving stones along the street, offers
you a view down under the earth as deep as the high gaping of the sky
opens upward away from the earth, so that you seem to be looking down
on clouds and to be seeing the sky, and bodies wonderfully hidden away
under the earth in a sky of their own.

Then again, when our spirited horse has stopped dead in the middle of
a river and we have looked down into the fast water of the stream, a
force seems to be carrying the body of the horse sideways, though he
is standing still, and shoving him quickly against the current; and
wherever we throw our eyes, everything seems to be carried along and
flowing in the same way we are.

A colonnade, however even the line it is built on, standing unbroken
on a row of matching columns, still, when the whole long thing is
looked at from one end, gradually draws itself into the top of a
narrow cone, joining roof to floor and everything on the right to
everything on the left, until it has pulled them together into the dim
point of the cone.

Out at sea, to sailors, the sun seems to come up out of the waves and
to go down and bury his light in the waves, because they have nothing
in view but water and sky. Do not be quick to conclude from any of
this that the senses are being undermined on every side.

But to people who do not know the sea, ships in harbour look crippled,
straining against the water with their sterns smashed. Whatever part
of the oars is raised above the salt spray is straight, and the
steering paddles are straight above the water; but the parts that are
sunk and pass under the water look broken, look as if the whole of
them were turned back and bent upwards and reversed, and almost
floating flat on the surface of the water.

And when the winds carry thin clouds across the sky at night, then the
bright signs seem to slide along against the storm clouds and
to travel up there in an altogether different direction from the one
they are truly carried in.

Now suppose a hand is put under one eye from below and presses on it.
Then, by a certain sensation, everything we look at seems to become
double as we look at it: two lamp flames flowering with fire, two sets
of furniture all through the house, two faces on every man and two
bodies.

Or take this. When sleep has bound the limbs in sweet drowsiness and
the whole body is lying in the deepest quiet, we still seem to
ourselves to be awake and to be moving our limbs, and in the blind
darkness of night we suppose we are looking at the sun and the light
of day. Shut up in one room, we seem to be changing our sky, our sea,
our rivers, our mountains, and to be crossing the plains on foot, and
to be hearing sounds when the stern silences of night stand all round
us, and to be answering out loud when we are not making a sound.

There are a great many other things of this kind, all of them
astonishing, and all of them, so to speak, trying to break down our
trust in the senses. They fail, because the largest part of them
cheats us by way of the opinions of the mind, which we add ourselves,
so that things not seen by the senses at all pass for things seen. For
nothing is harder than to separate the facts that are plain from the
doubtful things that the mind adds to them straight away, out of its
own resources.

And now, if somebody thinks that nothing can be known, he does not
know whether that itself can be known, since he admits that he knows
nothing. So I will not bother to argue the case against a man who has
planted his own head where his feet should be. And yet, even
supposing I grant that he knows this much, I would still ask him
this: since he has never before seen anything true among things, where
does he get his knowledge of what knowing is, and what not knowing is
in its turn? What produced his idea of the true and the false? What
proved to him that the doubtful is different from the certain?

You will find that the idea of what is true was created in the first
place out of the senses, and that the senses cannot be refuted. What
we are looking for is something that deserves greater trust, something
that can beat the false with the true on its own authority. Well, what
ought to be held in greater trust than the senses? Will reasoning that
takes its rise from a false sense be able to speak against those
senses, when the whole of it takes its rise from the senses? If they
are not true, all reasoning turns false along with them.

Or will the ears be able to convict the eyes, or touch the ears? Will
the taste in the mouth bring a charge against touch? Will the nostrils
prove it wrong, or the eyes catch it out? That is not how it is, I
think. Each of them has had its own power marked off and handed to it
separately; each has its own force. And so it must be that we see
separately what is soft and what is cold and what is burning hot, and
separately perceive the various colours of things and whatever goes
along with colours. In the same way the taste of the mouth has its
power separately, smells arise separately, and sounds separately. And
so it must be that one sense cannot convict another. Nor will they be
able to convict themselves, either, since equal trust will always have
to be placed in them. So whatever has appeared to them at any given
time is true.

And if reasoning cannot untie the knot and say why a thing that was
square close up looked round from far away, it is still better, when
you are short of the explanation, to give a faulty account of the
cause of both shapes than to let the plain facts slip anywhere out of
your own hands, to break the trust that comes first, and to tear up
the whole foundation that life and safety rest on. Because it is not
only that all reasoning would fall down. Life itself would collapse on
the spot, unless you dared to trust the senses and to keep away from
sheer drops and from everything else of that kind that has to be
avoided, and to go after their opposites. So the whole stock of words
drawn up and marshalled against the senses is empty.

Look at it this way. In a building, if the first rule is crooked, if
the square is untrue and comes away from the straight lines, if the
level limps by a hair anywhere at all, then everything is bound to
come out faulty and lopsided: crooked, sagging, leaning forward,
leaning back, out of true, roofs that already look as though they mean
to fall in, and do fall in, all of them betrayed by the first
misleading measurements. In just that way, your reasoning about the
world is bound to be crooked and false, whenever it has taken its rise
from senses that are false.

Now for the way each of the other senses feels the thing that belongs
to it. It is not a rough road at all.

To begin with, a sound and every voice is heard when they have worked
their way into the ears and struck the sense with their own body. For
we must admit that voice and sound are bodily too, since they are able
to strike the senses.

Besides, the voice often scrapes the throat, and shouting on its way
out makes the windpipe rougher. Naturally enough: when the atoms of
voice have started to go out through a narrow passage in a bigger
crowd than usual, the doorway of the mouth, filled to bursting, gets
scraped as well. So there is no doubt about it: voices and words
consist of bodily atoms, since they are able to hurt.

And it does not escape you how much bodily substance is carried off,
how much is drawn out of a man's sinews and out of his strength
itself, by talking without a break from the rising brightness of dawn
right through to the shadow of black night, and above all if it has
been poured out at the top of his voice. So the voice must be bodily,
since a man who talks a great deal loses part of his body.

Roughness in the voice comes from roughness in the atoms, and
smoothness in the same way is created out of smooth ones. The atoms do
not enter the ears with the same shape when the trumpet bellows deep
with a low rumble and the quick-strung lyre throws back a hoarse
booming, and when the daughters of Daulis, out of the gardens of
Helicon, lift up their clear complaint in a mourning voice.

So when we press these voices out from deep inside our body and send
them out straight through the mouth, the quick tongue, that skilled
craftsman, breaks them into joints, and the shaping of the lips does
its share of giving them shape. When the distance is not long from
where a voice sets out to where it arrives, the words themselves are
bound to be heard plainly and made out joint by joint, because the
voice keeps the making and keeps the shape it was given. But if the
space in between is longer than it should be, the words are bound to
be muddled together in all that air, and the voice thrown into
confusion while it flies across the breezes. So it turns out that you
can feel the sound and yet not make out what the meaning of the words
is: the voice comes to you as confused and hobbled as that.

Besides, one word sent out from the mouth of a herald often sets all
the ears in a crowd going. So one voice suddenly scatters into many
voices, since it divides itself up among individual ears, stamping the
shape of the word and its clear ring on each of them. Whatever part of
the voices does not fall on the ears themselves is carried past and
perishes, spread uselessly through the air. Part of it, dashed against
solid places, is thrown back and gives a sound in return, and
sometimes cheats you with the ghost of a word.

When you see this properly, you can give an account, to yourself and
to other people, of how in lonely places the rocks give back matching
shapes of words in the right order, when we are looking for companions
who have straggled off among the shadowy mountains and are calling to
them, scattered as they are, at the top of our voices. I have seen
places give back six or seven cries when you threw out one: the hills
themselves drove the words back against hills and kept repeating what
had been said.

The people who live nearby make out that these places are held by
goat-footed Satyrs and by Nymphs, and they say there are Fauns there,
by whose night-wandering racket and joking games, they generally
insist, the silences that hold their tongue are broken, and the sounds
of strings are made, and the sweet complaints that the pipe pours out
when the fingers of the players beat on it. The farming people hear it
far and wide, they say, when Pan, shaking the pine leaves that cover
his half-animal head, runs his curved lip again and again over the
open reeds, so that the pipes never stop pouring out the woodland
music. All the other monsters and marvels of this kind they talk about
for the same reason: so that they will not be thought to live in
deserted places that even the gods have left alone. That is why they
throw out miracles when they talk. Or else some other reason leads
them on, since the whole human race is far too greedy for something to
pour into its ears.

For the rest, there is nothing to wonder at in the way voices come
through places where the eyes cannot make out plain objects, and go on
to trouble the ears. We often see people talking with the doors shut,
and no wonder, since a voice can get through the bent channels of
things unharmed, while the images refuse: they are torn to pieces
unless they swim through straight channels, like the channels in
glass, which every appearance flies across.

Besides, a voice divides into every direction, since one voice gives
birth to others, once a single voice, having risen, has burst apart
into many, the way a spark of fire will often scatter itself into
fires of its own. So places hidden away round the back get filled with
voices, everything about them seething and stirred up with sound. But
images all make straight for where they were sent when they were once
sent off; and that is why nobody can often see over a wall, though he
can take in voices from outside. And even so, this voice itself, while
it comes through the shut-up parts of a house, is blunted, and gets
into the ears confused, and we seem to be hearing a sound rather than
words.

In this business, the tongue and the palate, which are how we feel
flavour, have rather more reasoning in them and more work to do. To
begin with, we feel the flavour in the mouth when we squeeze it out of
the food by chewing, the way somebody might start pressing and drying
out a sponge full of water in his hand. Then everything we squeeze out
is spread through all the little openings of the palate and through
the winding channels of the loose-woven tongue. When the bodies of the
running juice are smooth, they touch pleasantly and pleasantly handle
everything around the moist, sweating precincts of the tongue. When
they are not, they prick the sense and tear it as they rise up, and
the more so the more each of them is packed with roughness.

Then again, the pleasure that comes from the flavour reaches only as
far as the palate. Once it has plunged down through the throat there
is no pleasure in it, while it is all being spread out into the limbs.
And it makes no difference at all what food the body is fed on,
provided you can digest what you take and spread it through the limbs
and keep the stomach running moist and even.

Now I will set out how it is that different creatures have different
food, and why what is harsh and bitter to some can still seem very
sweet to others, and how great the distance and the difference is in
these matters, so great that what is food to one is fierce poison to
another. There is a snake, for instance, which, touched by a man's
spit, wastes away and finishes itself off by chewing itself to pieces.
Besides, hellebore is fierce poison to us, but it puts fat on goats
and quails.

So that you can find out by what means this comes about, the first
thing you should remember is what we said earlier: that the atoms are
held mixed together in things in many different ways. Then, all
living things that take food, as they are unlike each other on the
outside and unlike kind by kind, and as the outer outline of their
limbs fences each of them in, so in the same way they are made of
atoms and vary in shape. And since the atoms differ, it must be that
the intervals and the paths, which we call the pores, differ too, in
all the limbs and in the mouth and in the palate itself. So some must
be smaller and some larger, some three-cornered for one creature,
square for another, many of them round, and some many-angled in many
different ways. For as the arrangement and the motions of their shapes
demand, so the shapes of the pores must differ, and the paths must
vary according to the weave that hems them in.

So when what is sweet to some turns bitter to others, then for the one
it is sweet to, the smoothest bodies must be entering the openings of
the palate caressingly; while for those in whom the same thing is
harsh inside, no doubt rough and hooked ones are getting into the
throat.

Now it is easy to know each of these cases from all this. When a fever
has come up in a man because the bile has got the upper hand, or the
force of some disease has been stirred up in another way, then the
whole body is thrown into disorder and all the positions of the atoms
are changed. What happens is that the bodies which used to suit the
sense do not suit it now, and others fit better, the ones which can
produce a harsh feeling when they get in. Both sorts are mixed
together in the flavour of honey, as I have often shown you above.

Now then, let me deal with the way the impact of a smell touches the
nostrils. First of all, there must be many things from which a varied
tide of smells rolls out flowing, and we must suppose that they flow
and are sent out and scattered everywhere alike. But one is better
suited to some living things and another to others, because of the
differences in their shapes. And so bees are led through the air by
the smell of honey however far off it is, and vultures by dead bodies.
The loosed power of dogs leads the hunter wherever the split hoof of a
wild animal has taken its steps, and the white goose, guardian of the
citadel of the sons of Romulus, senses the smell of a human being a
long way off. So a different scent given to different creatures leads
each of them to its own food and makes it spring back from foul
poison, and in that way the generations of wild animals are kept
going.

This smell itself, then, whatever it is that troubles the nostrils,
can be sent further in one case than another. Still, none of them is
carried anything like as far as a sound, as a voice, to say nothing
of the things that strike the sharp edge of the eyes and trouble the
sight. A smell comes slowly and wandering, and it perishes first,
scattered easily, little by little, into the breezes of the air. In
the first place it is only with difficulty sent out from deep inside a
thing: that smells do flow and come away from deep within things is
shown by the fact that everything seems to smell more strongly when it
is broken, when it is ground down, when it is undone by fire. Then you
can see that a smell is created of larger atoms than a voice is, since
it does not get through stone walls, where a voice and a sound
commonly go. That is also why you will find it is not so easy to track
down what region the thing that smells is sitting in: the blow cools
off by dawdling through the air, and the news of things does not come
running down to the sense while it is still hot. That is why dogs so
often go wrong and cast about for the trail.

This does not happen in smells alone, or in the class of flavours. In
the same way the appearances of things and their colours do not all
suit all senses so well that there are not some which are sharper than
others to look at. In fact the lions, for all their rush, cannot stand
up to the cock, the bird that is used to clapping the night away with
his wings and calling the dawn with his clear voice; they cannot look
him in the face, and think at once about running. No doubt because
there are certain atoms in the bodies of cocks which, when they are
let into the eyes of lions, bore into the pupils and give such sharp
pain that the fierce animals cannot hold out against them. And yet
these same atoms cannot hurt our own sight at all, either because they
do not get in, or because, when they do get in, they are given a free
way out of the eyes, so that by hanging about they cannot injure the
light of the eyes in any part.

Now then, take in what things move the mind, and learn in a few words
where the things that come into the mind come from. The first thing I
say is this: many images of things are wandering about in many ways in
every direction, thin ones, which join easily with each other in the
air when they meet, like a spider's web or gold leaf. They are far
thinner in their weave than the ones that take hold of the eyes and
trouble the sight, since they get in through the loose parts of the
body and stir the thin nature of the mind inside and trouble the sense
there.

And so we see Centaurs, and the limbs of Scyllas, and dogs with
Cerberus faces, and the images of people whose bones the earth is
holding in her arms, who have met their death. Images of every kind
are carried about everywhere: partly those that come to be of their
own accord in the air itself, partly those that come away from all
sorts of things, and those that are put together out of the shapes of
these. The image of a Centaur certainly is not made from a living one,
since no such living nature ever existed; but when the image of a
horse and the image of a man have come together by chance, they stick
easily and at once, as I said before, because of their fine nature and
thin weave. Everything else of this kind is created in the same way.

And since they are carried nimbly and with the greatest lightness, as
I showed you earlier, any one of these fine images easily moves the
mind with a single blow; for the mind is thin and itself wonderfully
quick to move. That these things happen as I am telling you, you can
easily learn from this: in so far as the one is like the other, what
we see with the mind and what we see with the eyes must come about in
the same way.

So now, since I have taught you that I perceive a lion, say, through
the images that trouble the eyes, you may take it that the mind is
moved in a like way, through the images of lions and of everything
else that it sees, just as much as the eyes do and no less, except
that what it perceives is thinner.

And it is for no other reason, when sleep has poured out the limbs,
that the intelligence of the mind stays awake: it is because these
same images trouble our minds that trouble them when we are awake, and
so much so that we seem for certain to be seeing the man who has left
life behind and whom death and the earth now hold. Nature forces this
to happen because all the senses of the body are blocked and are
resting throughout the limbs, and cannot convict the false with true
things. Besides, memory is lying down and is faint with sleep, and
does not object that the man the mind believes it is seeing alive was
taken long ago by death and destruction.

For the rest, it is nothing to wonder at that the images move and
throw their arms and their other limbs about in time, since an image
does seem to do this in dreams. What happens is that when the first
image perishes and another is born after it in a different posture,
the earlier one seems to have changed its gesture. Of course we must
suppose that this happens fast. There is so much mobility, and so
great a store of things, and in any single moment we can feel there is
so great a store of little parts, that the supply can keep up.

There is a great deal to ask about in all this, and a great deal we
have to make clear, if we really want to lay the facts out plainly.
The first question is why, when the fancy for something has come over
a man, his mind thinks of that very thing on the spot. Do the images
keep watch on what we want, so that as soon as we want one the image
turns up in front of us, whether it is the sea we have set our heart
on, or the land, or the sky itself? Gatherings of people, a
procession, banquets, battles: does nature create and get all these
ready at a word? And this when the mind of another man in the same
region and the same place is thinking of things utterly unlike them.

And what about this, when we see the images step forward in time in
our dreams and move their soft limbs, when they nimbly throw out first
one soft arm and then the other and repeat the gesture with their eyes
while the foot keeps time? Of course the images are soaked in art and
have wandered off well trained, so that they can put on shows for us
in the night.

Or will this be nearer the truth? In a single stretch of time, that
is, in the time it takes us to feel one thing, the time it takes for a
single word to be uttered, there are many times lying hidden, which
reasoning finds out to be there. And that is why, at any moment you
please, the various images are on hand and ready, each in its place.
So great is the mobility, so great the store of things. And when the
first image perishes and another is born after it in a different
posture, the earlier one seems to have changed its gesture.

And because they are thin, the mind cannot see them sharply unless it
strains at them. So all the others that are there besides perish,
except the ones the mind has got itself ready for. It gets itself
ready, and expects to see what follows in each case; and so that is
what happens. Do you not see that the eyes do the same, when they set
about seeing things that are fine, and strain and make themselves
ready, and that without that we cannot see sharply? And yet you can
find, even with plain things, that if you do not turn your mind to
them it is exactly as if they had been kept away from you the whole
time and put far off. So why is it a wonder if the mind loses
everything except the things it has given itself up to? Then we build
great opinions out of small signs, and dress ourselves up in a cheat
of our own making.

It also happens now and then that an image of the same kind is not
supplied, and what was a woman before seems to have turned into a man
and to be there in your arms, or one face and one age follows another.
Sleep and forgetting see to it that we do not wonder at that.

In all this there is one fault you must run away from with all your
strength, one error you must be on your guard against and afraid of
beforehand: do not suppose that the bright lights of the eyes were
created so that we might see ahead of us, or that the tops of the
thighs and the calves were able to be bent, set on the feet, so that
we could take long strides, or again that the forearms were fitted to
strong upper arms, and hands given as servants on either side, so that
we could do whatever life required. Every other explanation of this
kind is back to front, with the reasoning turned round the wrong way.
Nothing at all was born in the body so that we might use it. What is
born creates the use.

Seeing did not exist before the lights of the eyes were born, nor
pleading in words before the tongue was created. On the contrary, the
origin of the tongue came a long way before speech, and the ears were
created much earlier than any sound was heard; and all the limbs, I
think, were there before there was any use for them. So they cannot
have grown for the sake of being used.

On the other hand, closing hand to hand in the struggle of a fight,
and tearing men's joints apart, and fouling their limbs with gore,
came long before shining spears ever flew; and nature forced men to
dodge a wound before the left arm ever held up a shield made by art.
Clearly, giving the tired body over to rest is far older than the soft
bedding of a bed, and quenching thirst was born before cups were.

These things, then, which have been found out from use and from life,
can be believed to have been discovered for the sake of use. But quite
separate from them are all the things which were born first, and only
afterwards gave us the knowledge of what they were good for. First
among that class we see the senses and the limbs. So again and again:
you are a very long way from being able to believe that these could
have been created to do a useful job.
