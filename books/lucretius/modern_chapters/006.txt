Book Four: The Senses, Sight, and Love
(Part 1 of 3)

I am travelling over the pathless places of the Muses, where nobody's
foot has trodden before. I love coming on springs nobody has touched,
and drinking them dry; I love picking new flowers, and hunting from
there a glorious crown for my head, from ground where the Muses have
never yet veiled anyone's temples. First, because I am teaching about
great matters, and going on to loose the mind from the tight knots of
religion; and then because on so dark a subject I am making verses so
clear, touching everything with the charm of the Muses. That too seems
to have a reason behind it. It is like doctors trying to give children
foul wormwood: first they brush the rim of the cup all round with the
sweet yellow liquid of honey, so that the unthinking years of childhood
are fooled as far as the lips, and meanwhile the bitter draught of
wormwood is drunk right down. The children are tricked, but they are not
trapped; instead they are put back on their feet that way and grow
strong again. In the same way now, since this reasoning generally seems
rather grim to those who have not handled it, and the crowd shrinks back
from it, I have wanted to set our reasoning out for you in the
sweet-speaking song of the Muses, and to touch it, so to speak, with
their sweet honey, in the hope of holding your mind on my verses that
way, until you take in the whole nature of things and feel all the way
through how much good it does you.

But I have already taught what the atoms of all things are like, and
how they differ in all sorts of shapes, and how they fly about of their
own accord, driven by a motion that never stops, and in what way each
thing can be created out of them. And I have taught what the nature of
the mind is, and out of what things it is made and keeps its strength
while it is fitted together with the body, and in what way it is torn
apart and goes back to its atoms. So now I shall begin on a matter
that bears very heavily on all this: that there exist the things we
call the images of things. They have to be named something like skins,
or films, or a rind, because each one carries the same look and the
same shape as the body it is said to have streamed off and gone
wandering from. Like skins stripped off the outermost surface of
things, they fly this way and that through the air. And it is these
same images that meet us when we are awake and terrify our minds, and
meet us in sleep, when we are often looking at strange shapes and at
the images of people who have lost the light — images that have often
startled us horribly out of a slack sleep. We must not decide, on the
strength of that, that souls get out of Acheron, or that ghosts flit
about among the living, or that anything of us can be left behind
after death, once body and mind alike have been destroyed and each has
gone off into its own atoms. So what I say is that things send off
likenesses of themselves, thin shapes, off the outermost rind of their
bodies. And you can learn this from what follows, however dull your
wits may be.

To begin with, among the things that are out in the open we see many
that send off bodies. Some send them loosely and scattered, as logs
send off smoke and fires send off heat. Some send them more woven
together and packed close, as the cicadas do in summer when they lay
down their smooth tunics, and calves do when they are born and drop
the membranes off their outermost surface, and the slippery snake does
when it strips its clothing off among the thorns — for we often see
the brambles made richer with those flying spoils. Since these things
happen, a thin image must be sent off things as well, off the
outermost surface of things. For there is no way of opening your mouth
to say why those thicker coverings should drop off and leave things,
rather than the ones that are thin. Especially since there are a great
many tiny bodies on the surfaces of things, which can be thrown off in
the same order they were in before and keep the outline of their
shape, and thrown a great deal faster, since being few, and placed
right at the front, they have that much less to get in their way.

For we certainly see many things throwing off and handing out a great
deal, not only from deep inside them, as I said before, but often the
very colour off their surfaces. The awnings do this everywhere,
yellow and red and rust-coloured, when they are stretched over the
great theatres and spread out along the poles and the beams and shiver
and ripple. There they dye the seated crowd below in the hollow of the
theatre, and the whole show of the stage, and the fathers and mothers
in their places, and force it all to move in their own colour. And the
more the walls of the theatre shut them in all round, the more
everything inside laughs, soaked in that loveliness, with the daylight
caught and held off. So, since the cloths send off a dye from their
outermost body, everything must send off thin likenesses as well,
since in both cases they are shot off the surface. There are, then,
sure tracks of shapes flying about everywhere, furnished with a fine
thread, which cannot be seen one at a time on their own.

Besides, every smell, and smoke, and heat, and other things like them
stream out of things and spread, because while they are coming from
deep inside and working their way out they get torn apart along a bent
road, and there are no straight gateways for them to force their way
out through in a body. But when the thin film of surface colour is
thrown off, there is nothing that can tear it, since it is right there
at the front, ready to hand.

Lastly, whatever images appear to us in mirrors, in water, in any
shining surface at all, must be made of images sent off from things,
since they are furnished with the same look as the things. So there
are thin likenesses of shapes, resembling them, which nobody can pick
out one at a time, and which all the same, being thrown back by a
steady and repeated bouncing, give back a sight from the flat surface
of a mirror. There seems to be no other way they could be kept so well
that shapes so like each thing are given back.

Now then. Take in how thin the nature of an image is. And first of
all, since the atoms are so far below our senses, and so much smaller
than the objects that our eyes are first beginning to be unable to
make out, let me confirm that point too. Take in, in a few words, how
fine the atoms of all things are.

To begin with, some living creatures are already so tiny that a third
of one of them cannot be seen in any way at all. What are we to think
any inward part of one of these is like? The little ball of its heart,
or its eyes? Its limbs? Its joints? How very small they are! And what
about each of the atoms out of which its soul and the nature of its
mind must be made — do you not see how fine and how minute they have
to be?

Besides, take anything that breathes a sharp smell out of its body:
all-heal, foul wormwood, strong-smelling southernwood, bitter
centaury. If you happen to rub any one of them lightly between two
fingers, the smell will hang about your hand for a long while, and yet
you will not see anything at all leaving it. So why not learn instead
that images of things go wandering about, many of them, in many ways,
with no force in them and nothing for the senses to take hold of?

But do not go thinking that the only images wandering about are the
ones that come away from things. There are others as well, which are
born of their own accord and put themselves together in this sky of
ours, the thing we call the air. Formed in many ways, they are carried
up high; and, being liquid, they never stop changing their look and
turning into the outlines of shapes of every kind. That is how we
sometimes see clouds thickening easily up there and spoiling the calm
face of the world, stroking the air with their movement. Often the
faces of giants seem to fly along and draw a wide shadow after them;
sometimes great mountains and rocks torn off mountains go on ahead and
pass in front of the sun; and after them some beast drags along and
brings on other stormclouds.

Now for how easily and how quickly these images are produced, and how
they flow off things without a break and slip away as they fall. For
there is always a surface streaming off things, more than enough for
them to throw away. And when this surface reaches other things, it
goes through them, as it does above all with cloth. But where it
reaches rough rocks or the material of wood, it is torn on the spot,
so that it can give back no image. But when shining, dense things have
been set in the way — a mirror above all — nothing of the kind
happens. It cannot go through, the way it goes through cloth, and it
cannot be torn either, since the smoothness remembers to keep it safe.
That is why images come streaming back to us from a mirror. And
however suddenly, at whatever moment you like, you put anything in
front of a mirror, the image appears. From that you can learn that
thin textures of things and thin shapes are flowing continuously off
their outermost body.

So a great many images are produced in a short space of time, and
their origin is rightly called a fast one. And just as the sun has to
send out a great many lights in a short space of time, so that
everything is full of light without a break, in the same way, and for
the same reason, a great many images of things must be carried off
things in a point of time, in many ways, in every direction all round.
For whatever face of things we turn a mirror to, things answer back
there with the same shape and the same colour.

Besides, the weather of the sky may be perfectly clear one moment and
turn foully thick and stormy the next, so that you would think all the
darkness everywhere had left Acheron and filled up the great hollows
of the sky. That is how, once the filthy night of the stormclouds has
gathered, the faces of black Terror hang over us from above. And of
those there is nobody who could say, or give any account of in words,
how small a part an image is.

Now then. How fast the images travel, and what mobility is given them
for swimming across the air, so that a short hour is worn away over a
long stretch of space, whichever place each of them is making for
under its own impulse — I shall set that out in sweet-speaking verses
rather than in many of them, the way the swan's small song is better
than the clamour of the cranes scattered high up among the south
wind's clouds.

To begin with, you can very often see that light things, made of tiny
bodies, are fast. In that class are the sun's light and its heat,
because they are made out of small atoms, which are, so to speak,
hammered along and do not hesitate to cross the stretch of air, driven
on by the blow that follows behind. Light is supplied by light on the
instant, and gleam is spurred on by gleam, like an ox in a team. So on
the same principle the images must be able to run across an
unspeakable stretch of space in a point of time. First, because there
is a very small cause a long way behind them, carrying them forward
and pushing them on, and because after that they are carried along
with such a flying lightness. Second, because they are sent off with a
texture so loosely woven that they can easily get into anything at all
and, so to speak, seep through the stretch of air.

Besides, if the little bodies of things that are sent out from deep
inside — the light and the heat of the sun — are seen to slip through
the whole space of the sky in a point of the day, spreading themselves
out, flying across sea and lands and drenching the sky, then what
about the ones that are already there ready at the very front, when
they are thrown off and nothing holds up their departure? Do you not
see that they must go faster, and further, and run across many times
the stretch of space in the same time that the sun's light takes to
spread over the sky?

And here is what seems the best and truest proof of all of how fast
the images of things travel. The moment a shining sheet of water is
set down under the open sky, if the sky is clear and full of stars,
the shining stars of the world answer back at once in the water. Now
do you see in what a point of time the image falls from the shores of
the upper air to the shores of the earth? So again and again you have
to admit that things send off bodies that strike the eyes and rouse
sight.

And smells flow continuously off certain things, as cold flows off
rivers, heat off the sun, and spray off the waves of the sea, which
eats away the walls along the coast. And voices of all kinds never
stop flying about through the air. Then again, a damp salt taste often
comes into our mouth when we are walking about beside the sea; and
when we watch wormwood being thinned and mixed in front of us, the
bitterness touches us. So thoroughly does every thing stream off
everything and get sent out in every direction all round, with no
delay and no rest allowed in the flowing — since we are feeling
without a break, and are always able to see anything, and smell it,
and hear it make a sound.

Besides, a shape handled with the hands in the dark is known to be the
same one that is seen in the light and the bright daylight, and so
touch and sight must be set going by a cause of the same kind. So if
we test a square and it moves us in the dark, what thing in the light
could fall on our sight and look square, unless it were the image of
it? So the cause of seeing seems to lie in images, and nothing can be
seen without them.

Now, the images of things I am speaking of are carried in every
direction and thrown out, spread into every quarter. But because we
can see with our eyes alone, it comes about that wherever we turn our
sight, all things strike against it there with their shape and their
colour.

And it is the image that makes us see how far off each thing is from
us, and takes care that we can tell. For when it is sent off it shoves
ahead at once and drives along whatever air lies between itself and
our eyes, and all that air glides through our sight and, so to speak,
wipes across our pupils, and passes on. That is why we see how far off
each thing is. And the more air is driven along in front, and the
longer the draught that wipes across our eyes, the further off each
thing is seen to be. Of course all this is done at an extremely fast
rate, so that we see at one and the same time what kind of thing it is
and how far away it is.

And there is nothing in all this that ought to be held any sort of
wonder: that although the images which strike the eyes cannot be seen
one at a time, the things themselves are made out clearly. For with
the wind too, when it beats on us little by little and when the sharp
cold streams over us, we are not in the habit of feeling one private
particle of that wind and that cold, but rather the whole of it at
once; and we see the blows made on our body just as if some thing were
beating on it and giving us the feel of a body outside us. Besides,
when we knock a stone with a finger, we touch the very outside of the
rock and its surface colour, and we do not feel that colour by touch:
what we feel instead is the hardness of the stone itself, deep down
inside.

Now then. Take in why the image is seen beyond the mirror, since it
certainly does seem to be a long way back inside it. It is the same
sort of thing as the objects out of doors that are truly seen through
an opening, when a doorway offers a clear view through itself and lets
many things outside be seen from the house. That sight too is made by
a double, twinned air. First the air on this side of the doorposts is
made out; then the doors themselves follow, on the right and the left;
then the light from outside wipes across the eyes, and a second lot of
air, and then the things out of doors that are truly seen through the
opening.

In the same way, as soon as the image of the mirror has thrown itself
forward, then, while it is coming to our sight, it shoves ahead and
drives along whatever air lies between itself and our eyes, and brings
it about that we can feel all this air before we feel the mirror. But
once we have felt the mirror itself as well, the image that is carried
from us to it gets there straight away and, thrown back, comes back to
our eyes, rolling a second lot of air along in front of itself as it
drives it, and brings it about that we see this air before we see the
image; and that is why the image seems to stand off at just that
distance behind the mirror. So again and again it is not in the least
surprising that this is what happens with the images that give back a
sight from the flat surface of a mirror, since both of these things
are brought about by two lots of air.

Now, the part of our limbs that is on our right is seen in mirrors to
be on the left, because when the image comes to the flat of the mirror
and hits it, it is not turned round unhurt, but is knocked straight
backwards, the way it would be if somebody dashed a plaster mask
against a post or a beam before it was dry, and it kept the shape at
its front straight away and moulded itself back again from being
knocked into reverse. What happens is that the eye that was the right
one is now the left, and the other way about, the left becomes the
right.

It also happens that an image is handed on from mirror to mirror, so
that five or even six images have been produced. For whatever things
are hidden away back in an inner room can still be brought out from
there, however twisted the way and however deeply set back, through
the bending approaches, and can be seen to be in the house by means of
several mirrors. That is how far an image shines through from mirror
into mirror; and when it has been given as a left, it becomes a right
again, and then goes back again and turns round to the side it was on
before.

More than that: little curved side-mirrors, whose bend matches the
bend of our own flank, send images back to us with the right on the
right — either because the image is carried over from mirror to mirror
and then, after being knocked off twice, flies across to us; or else
because the image is spun round as it comes, since the bent shape of
the mirror teaches it to turn towards us.

Again, you would believe that the images walk along beside us and put
their feet down with ours and copy our movements; and the reason is
that whatever part of the mirror you step away from, the images cannot
come back from there any longer, since nature forces everything to be
carried back and to spring off things at matching angles.

Then again, the eyes run away from bright things and avoid looking at
them. The sun even blinds you, if you go on trying to strain towards
it, because its own force is great and its images are carried down
from high up through the pure air and strike the eyes and disturb
their arrangement. Besides, any keen brightness often burns the eyes,
because it holds many seeds of fire in it, which work their way in and
give birth to pain in the eyes.

Besides, everything people with jaundice look at turns yellow, because
many seeds of yellowness flow off their bodies to meet the images of
things, and many more are mixed in their eyes as well, and these paint
everything with paleness by their contact.

And we see, out of the dark, the things that stand in the light, and
this is why. The nearer dark, murky air enters the open eyes first and
takes possession of them, and then the shining bright air follows
immediately after and, so to speak, cleans them out and scatters the
black shadows of that other air, since it is many times more mobile,
many times finer, and far more powerful. And the moment it has filled
the pathways of the eyes with light and opened up the ways that the
black air had blocked before, the images of the things that stand in
the light follow straight after and provoke us into seeing. We cannot
do the opposite, and see things in the dark from out of the light,
because the murky air comes along behind, thicker, and fills every
opening and blocks up the pathways of the eyes, so that no images of
anything can be thrown in and set them moving.

And when we make out the square towers of a city a long way off, this
is why they often look round. Every angle is seen blunt from far away,
or rather it is not seen at all, and its blow perishes, and its stroke
does not glide through to our sight, because while the images are
being carried through a great deal of air, the air forces the blow to
go blunt with its repeated knocks. Once every angle has slipped away
from the sense in that manner, it comes about that the stonework is
looked at as though it had been turned on a lathe — not the way things
close at hand and truly round are looked at, but a little like them,
and as if sketched in shadow.

Our shadow in the sun seems to us to move as well, and to follow our
footsteps and copy our movements — if you can believe that air robbed
of its light is able to walk along, following the motions and the
movements of men. For what we are used to calling a shadow can be
nothing else but air with its light taken away. Naturally enough: the
earth loses the sun's light in one place after another in a fixed
order, wherever we get in the way as we go along, and the part of it
we have left behind is filled up again. That is why it comes about
that what was the body's shadow seems to have followed us all the way,
straight along the same line. For new lights from the rays are always
pouring themselves out, and the first ones perish, the way wool is
drawn into a fire. That is why the earth is easily robbed of light and
easily filled up again, and washes the black shadows off itself.

And yet, for all that, we do not concede that the eyes are cheated in
this by the smallest amount. It is their job to notice in what place
there is light and in what place shadow. But whether the lights are
the same lights or not, and whether the shadow that was here is the
same one now passing over there, or whether instead what happens is
what I said a little earlier — that is a thing which in the end the
reasoning of the mind has to sort out, and the eyes cannot get to know
the nature of things. So do not fasten this fault of the mind onto the
eyes.

The ship we are sailing in is moving, at the moment when it seems to
be standing still; and the one that is staying at its mooring is
believed to be going past. The hills and the plains that we drive the
ship past, flying along under sail, seem to be running away astern.

The stars all seem to stay still, fixed in the caverns of the upper
air, and yet every one of them is in constant motion, since they rise
and then go back to their far-off settings when they have measured out
the sky with their bright bodies. And in the same way the sun and the
moon seem to stay at their moorings, though the plain fact of the
matter shows that they are being carried along.

And mountains standing up out of the middle of the water a long way
off, with a huge free way out between them, wide enough for a fleet,
look all the same like one single island joined together out of the
two of them.

Halls seem to spin round and columns to run in a circle when children
have stopped whirling themselves about — so completely that they can
hardly believe the whole roof is not threatening to come down on top
of them.

And now, when nature begins to lift the red blaze high up with its
shaking fires and to raise it above the mountains: those mountains,
which the sun then seems to you to be right on top of, touching them
close up with his own burning fire — they are scarcely two thousand
bowshots away from us, often scarcely five hundred casts of a javelin.
But between them and the sun lie the vast level plains of the sea,
spread out beneath the huge shores of the upper air, and there are
many thousands of lands set in between, which all sorts of peoples and
generations of wild animals hold.
