Book Two: The Movements and Shapes of Atoms
(Part 2 of 2)

There is one more thing in all this that you should keep sealed up and
stored away in a mind that does not forget. Among the things whose
nature lies open in front of us, there is nothing that is made out of
one kind of atom only, and nothing that is not put together out of mixed
seed. And the more powers and properties a thing has in it, the more it
shows you that there are many kinds of atoms inside it, and many
different shapes.

Take the earth first. It has atoms in it out of which the springs,
rolling their cold water, keep the measureless sea renewed without a
break. It has atoms out of which fires can rise, since the ground is
alight and burning in many places, and the rush of Etna rages up out of
the deepest fires of all. It has atoms out of which it can raise shining
crops and cheerful orchards for the human race, and out of which it can
supply rivers and leaves and glad pastures to the mountain-roaming kinds
of wild animals. That is why she alone has been called the great mother
of the gods, and the mother of the wild animals, and the one who gave
birth to our own bodies.

The old learned poets of the Greeks sang of her sitting in her chariot
and driving a team of two lions, and by that they were teaching that the
great earth hangs in the space of the air and that earth cannot rest on
earth. They yoked wild animals to her car, because however savage the
offspring, it has to be beaten down and made gentle by the services of
its parents. They ringed the top of her head with a crown of city walls,
because she carries cities on her, fortified in their choice positions.
Decorated with that badge, the image of the divine mother is carried now
through great countries, and people shudder as it goes by.

Many different nations, following the old custom of the rites, call her
the Mother of Ida and give her Phrygian bands for company, because they
say that it was from those lands that grain first began to be produced
through the whole circle of the world. They assign to her the Galli, the
castrated priests, because they want to show that men who have violated
the divine power of the Mother and been found ungrateful to their
parents ought to be judged unfit to bring a living child out into the
shores of light. Tight drums thunder under their palms, hollow cymbals
crash all round, horns threaten with a harsh-sounding song, and the
hollow pipe goads their minds with a Phrygian beat. They carry weapons
out in front of them, the signs of a violent madness, weapons that can
terrify the ungrateful minds and ungodly hearts of the crowd with dread
of the goddess and her power.

So, the moment she is driven into the great cities and, saying nothing,
blesses mortals with a silent greeting, they strew the whole road she
takes with bronze and silver, making her rich with lavish offerings, and
they make rose petals fall like snow, shadowing the Mother and the band
of her attendants. Here an armed troop, whom the Greeks call the
Curetes, if it happens so, plays among the Phrygian companies and leaps
in time, glad with blood, shaking the terrifying crests on their heads
with a nod. They are meant to recall the Curetes of Dicte, who, so the
story runs, once drowned out that crying of the infant Jupiter in Crete,
when the boys, in arms, danced their quick dance around the boy and beat
bronze on bronze in time, so that Saturn should not catch him and put
him in his jaws and give the mother an everlasting wound deep in her
heart. That is why armed men escort the great Mother. Or else it is
because they mean by it that the goddess is telling men to be willing to
defend the land of their fathers with weapons and with courage, and to
make themselves a protection and a credit to their parents.

However well and however finely all this is arranged as it is handed
down, it is a long way off from true reasoning. For it must be that the
whole nature of the gods, in itself, enjoys undying life in the deepest
peace, cut off from anything to do with us, far away and separate. It is
free of all pain, free of all danger, strong in its own resources, in
need of nothing from us. It is not won over by our good behaviour and it
is not touched by anger. As for the earth, it has no sensation at any
time; it is only because it holds the atoms of many things that it
brings many of them out into the light of the sun in many ways. And
here, if somebody decides to call the sea Neptune and the crops Ceres,
and prefers to misuse the name of Bacchus rather than say the proper
word for the liquid, let us allow him to go on calling the round earth
the mother of the gods, as long as in real fact he keeps himself from
touching his mind with a foul religion.

So it often happens that woolly sheep cropping the grass in one field,
and the warlike offspring of horses, and the herds of horned cattle,
under the same covering of sky, quenching their thirst out of the water
of one river, live with unlike looks and keep their parents' nature and
copy their parents' habits, kind by kind. So great is the difference in
the material in any sort of grass you like, so great in any river too.

Then again, take any one living creature you like out of them all:
bones, blood, veins, heat, moisture, flesh and sinews make it up, and
these are things that differ from each other by a long way, and they are
finished out of atoms of unlike shape.

Then again, whatever burns and is consumed by fire lays up in its body,
if nothing else at all, at least the atoms out of which it can throw
fire and send up light and drive out sparks and scatter ash far and
wide. If you travel over everything else with the same reasoning of the
mind, you will find that things hide the seeds of many things in their
bodies and hold different shapes.

Finally, you see many things that have colour and taste given to them
together with smell, and above all most of the fruits of the trees. So
these must be made of different shapes. The scent gets in where the dye
does not travel into our limbs, and the dye works its way into the
senses one way and the taste another, so that you can know that they
differ in the shapes of their atoms. So unlike forms come together into
one lump, and things are made of mixed seed.

Yes, and everywhere in these very verses of mine you see many letters
shared between many words, and yet you have to admit that the verses and
the words are made out of different letters, each from each. It is not
that few letters run through them in common, or that no two words at all
are made of the same ones throughout, but that as a general rule they
are not all the same as all. So it is with other things too. While many
atoms are shared between many things, the things can still be made of a
whole that is unlike from one to another. So it is fair to say that the
human race, and the crops, and the cheerful trees are made of different
atoms.

Still, you must not think that all things can be joined together in
every way. If they could, you would see monsters born on every side:
half-beast shapes of men coming into being, tall branches sometimes
growing out of a living body, limbs of sea creatures joined to land
creatures, and nature feeding Chimaeras, breathing flame out of a foul
mouth, all over the earth that gives birth to everything. Plainly none
of this happens. We see that everything is created out of fixed seeds
and from a fixed mother, and can keep to its own kind as it grows. Of
course this has to happen on a fixed principle. Out of all the food, the
bodies that belong to each thing pass inside into its limbs, and there
they are joined and produce the motions that suit it; while on the other
side we see nature throwing back onto the ground what is foreign to it,
and many bodies flee out of the body, driven by invisible blows, because
they could not be joined to any part of it, or, once inside, agree with
the motions of life and copy them.

And in case you happen to think that living things alone are held by
these laws, the same principle sets a limit to everything. Just as all
created things are unlike each other in their whole nature, so each of
them must be made of atoms of an unlike shape. Not that few of them are
furnished with a similar form, but that as a general rule they are not
all the same as all. And since the seeds differ, there must be
differences in their intervals, their paths, their connections, their
weights, their blows, their meetings and their motions, which do not
only keep the bodies of living creatures apart, but separate the lands
and the whole sea and hold the whole sky away from the lands.

Now come and take in this teaching, hunted out by my sweet labour, in
case you happen to think that the white things you see shining in front
of your eyes are made of white atoms, or that the things that look black
are born of black seed, or that anything steeped in any colour at all
carries that colour because the bodies of its material are dyed a colour
like it. For the bodies of matter have no colour at all, neither like
the things nor unlike them. And if it happens to seem to you that the
mind can throw no grip onto bodies of that kind, you are wandering a
long way off the road. People born blind, who have never made out the
light of the sun, still recognise bodies by touch, bodies that from the
beginning of their lives have had no colour attached to them. So you can
be sure that our minds too can come to know bodies that have no dye
smeared round them. And besides, whatever we touch ourselves in blind
darkness, we do not feel it dyed with any colour.

Since I am winning the point that it can be so, I shall now teach you
that it is so. Every colour changes into every other colour, without
exception, and atoms must not do that in any way. Something unchangeable
has to survive, or everything would be reduced completely to nothing.
For whatever is changed and moves outside its own limits, that is at
once the death of what it was before. So take care not to touch the
seeds of things with colour, or everything will go back completely to
nothing for you.

Besides, if no nature of colour is given to atoms, and they are
furnished instead with various forms out of which they produce and vary
colours of every kind, since it makes a great difference which seeds are
held with which and in what arrangement, and what motions they give and
take from one another, then you could give the reason at once, and very
easily, why things that were black in colour a moment ago can suddenly
become as white as marble, as the sea does when great winds have stirred
up its level plains and it is turned into white waves of gleaming
marble. You could say that when the thing we often see as black has had
its material mixed up, and the order of its atoms changed, and some
added and some taken away, the immediate result is that it looks glowing
and white.

But if the level waters of the sea were made of blue seeds, they could
not turn white in any way. However you disturb blue things, they can
never pass over into the colour of marble. And if the seeds that make
the one pure sheen of the sea are dyed one colour and another, in the
way that something square, one shape, is often made out of other forms
and various shapes, then it would follow that, just as in the square we
can see the unlike forms in it, so in the level water of the sea, or in
any other single pure sheen you like, we should see colours that were
far unlike one another and various. Besides, the unlike shapes do not
get in the way of the whole and stop it being square on the outside, but
the different colours of things do get in the way and stop the whole
thing having a single sheen.

Then again, the reason that leads us on and tempts us to attribute
colours to atoms sometimes falls to pieces, since white things are not
created out of white things, and the things that are called black are
not made out of black ones, but out of various colours. In fact white
will come up far more readily born of no colour at all than born of
black, or of any other colour that fights against it and stands in its
way.

Besides, since colours cannot exist without light, and atoms do not come
out into the light, you can be sure how little they are wrapped in any
colour. What sort of colour could there be in blind darkness? In the
light itself a colour changes, and for this reason: it flashes back
differently as it is struck by a straight or a slanting light. Look at
the feathers on a dove's neck in the sun, the ones that are set round
the back of the neck and crown the throat. Sometimes they turn red as
bright gold-bronze, and sometimes it happens that, to a certain feeling,
they seem to mix green emeralds in among the blue. And a peacock's tail,
when it is filled with plenty of light, changes its colours in the same
way as it turns. And since those colours are produced by a certain blow
of light, you can be sure that without that blow they cannot happen at
all.

And since the pupil of the eye takes into itself one kind of blow when
we say it feels the colour white, and another kind when it feels black
and the rest, and since it does not matter what colour the things you
touch may happen to be furnished with, but rather what shape they have,
you can be sure that atoms have no need of colour: they deliver varying
touches by their varying forms.

Besides, since a particular colour does not belong to a particular
shape, and all the shapings of atoms can be found in any sheen you like,
why are the things made out of them not equally soaked with colours of
every kind in every kind of thing? It would follow that ravens, flying,
should often throw off a white colour from white feathers, and that
swans should turn black from black seed, or be of any other colour you
like, single or various.

Yes, and the more anything is torn apart into small pieces, the more you
can see the colour fading away little by little and going out. That is
what happens when a piece of bright cloth is picked apart into small
parts: the purple, and the scarlet, much the brightest of colours, is
completely ruined when it is pulled to pieces thread by thread. From
that you can know that the little parts breathe out all their colour
before they break down into the atoms of things.

Lastly, since you admit that not all bodies send out a voice or a smell,
it follows that you do not assign sounds and smells to all of them. In
the same way, since we cannot make out everything with our eyes, you can
be sure that some things are as bereft of colour as others are without
any smell and far from any sound, and that a keen mind can grasp these
things no less than it can note the things that lack some other quality.

But in case you happen to think that atoms are left stripped of colour
alone: they are also cut off from warmth, and from cold, and from hot
steam altogether, and they travel sterile of sound and starved of taste,
and they throw no smell of their own off their bodies. It is like this.
When you set about making the sweet liquid of marjoram and myrrh, and
the flower of nard that breathes nectar to the nostrils, the first thing
you have to look for is the nature of an oil of olives with no smell in
it, as far as you may and can find one, which sends no breath at all to
the nostrils, so that it may do as little as possible to spoil the
scents mixed and cooked in its body by handling them with a strength of
its own. For the same reason, atoms must not bring their own smell to
the things they beget, or their own sound, since they can send nothing
off themselves, nor in the same way any taste at all, nor cold either,
nor heat or warm steam, nor any of the rest. All those qualities are
such that they belong to mortal things: the limp with a soft body, the
crumbling with a brittle one, the loose-textured with a hollow one. They
must all be kept separate from atoms, if we want to lay undying
foundations under things for the whole of their safety to rest on, or
everything will go back completely to nothing for you.

Now, whatever we see having sensation, you must all the same admit is
made out of atoms that have no sensation. The plain facts, the things
known and lying open in front of us, do not refute this and do not fight
against it. Instead they lead us by the hand and force us to believe
that living things are born out of things without sensation, which is
what I am saying. You can see living worms coming up out of foul dung
when the soaked earth has caught rot from unseasonable rains.

Besides, all things in the same way turn themselves into other things.
Rivers, leaves and glad pastures turn into cattle, and cattle turn their
nature into our bodies, and often out of our bodies the strength of wild
animals grows, and the bodies of the strong-winged birds. So nature
turns all foods into living bodies and produces out of them all the
sensations of living creatures, in much the same way as she unfolds dry
logs into flames and turns everything into fire. Do you see now, then,
that it matters a great deal in what order the atoms of things are each
of them set, and mixed with which others they give and take their
motions?

Then again, what is it that strikes your mind itself, and moves it and
forces it to bring out all these different arguments against believing
that a thing with sensation is born out of things without it? No doubt
it is this: that stones and wood and earth, even when they are mixed
together, cannot deliver a living sensation. So it will be right to
remember this much in these matters. I do not say that sensations are
produced straight away out of absolutely everything that creates things
which have sensation. It matters a great deal, first, how small the
atoms are that make the sensitive thing, and what shape they are
furnished with, and finally what their motions, arrangements and
positions are. We see none of this in logs and clods, and yet, when
these have been rotted, so to speak, by the rains, they give birth to
little worms, because the bodies of matter, stirred out of their old
arrangements by a new circumstance, are brought together in the way that
living things have to be produced.

Next there are those who lay it down that a thing with sensation can be
created out of things that have sensation, and those in turn out of
others that are accustomed to feel. They make their atoms soft, since
all sensation is tied to flesh and sinews and veins, and we see that all
such things are soft and made of a mortal body. But let us grant, for
now, that these can stay everlasting. Even so, they must either have the
sensation of a part, or be reckoned to be like whole living creatures.
But parts cannot possibly feel by themselves, since the mind refuses all
sensation to the limbs on their own, and a hand cut off from us, or any
other part of the body at all, cannot hold sensation on its own. What is
left is that they are like whole living creatures, so that they can
share, all through, in a living sensation.

How, then, can they be called the atoms of things, and dodge the roads
of death, when they are animals, and animals are one and the same thing
as mortals? And even suppose they could dodge them: by meeting and
combining they would make nothing but a mob and a crowd of living
creatures, exactly as men and cattle and wild animals could not produce
anything at all among themselves by coming together. And if by chance
they let go of their own sensation inside a body and take on another,
what was the use of assigning them the thing that is taken away again?
Then besides, to go back to the argument we poured out before, since we
see the eggs of birds turn into chicks, and worms boil up out of the
earth when it has caught rot from unseasonable rains, you can be sure
that sensations can be produced out of things that have no sensation.

And if somebody happens to say that sensation can arise out of what has
no sensation to this extent only, by a change, or because it is brought
forth by something like a birth, it will be enough to make it plain to
him and prove to him that there is no birth unless a combination has
been formed first, and that nothing is changed without a joining
together.

To begin with, no body can have sensations before the nature of the
living creature itself has been produced. Obviously that is because its
material is held scattered about through air and rivers and lands and
the things the land creates, and has not yet come together and brought
into one the motions of life that suit it, the motions that light up the
all-seeing senses which watch over every living creature.

Besides, a blow greater than its nature can stand strikes any living
creature down on the spot and goes on to throw all the sensations of its
body and its mind into confusion. The positions of the atoms are
dissolved and the motions of life are blocked deep inside, until the
material, shaken all through the limbs, unties the living knots of the
soul from the body and throws the soul out, scattered, through all the
passages. For what else can we suppose a blow delivered on a thing can
do, except shake it apart and dissolve every bit of it?

It also often happens that, when the blow delivered is less sharp, the
motions of life that are left win through. They win, and they quiet the
huge uproar of the blow, and call each thing back into its own channels,
and shake apart the motion of death that is already taking control in
the body, and light up the senses that were almost lost. For otherwise,
by what means could things gather their minds and come back to life from
the very doorstep of death, rather than go on to where they have nearly
finished their run, and go away?

Besides, pain is when the bodies of matter, stirred up by some force
through the living flesh and through the limbs, shake in their places
inside; and a soothing pleasure comes when they move back into place
again. So you can be sure that atoms cannot be assaulted by any pain and
cannot take any pleasure out of themselves, since they are not made of
any atoms whose new motions could hurt them, or from which they could
pick some fruit of kindly sweetness. So they must not be furnished with
any sensation at all.

Finally, if in order for every living creature to be able to feel we now
have to assign sensation to its atoms, what about the ones that the
human race in particular is built out of? Naturally they laugh out loud,
shaken by a shivering laughter, and sprinkle their faces and their
cheeks with dewy tears, and they are clever enough to say a great deal
about the mixture of things, and to go on and ask what atoms they
themselves are made of. For since they are like whole mortal creatures,
they too must be made out of other elements, and those out of others
again, so that you would never dare to make a stop anywhere. I shall
follow you wherever you go: whatever you say talks and laughs and
thinks, I shall say is made of other things that do the same. But if we
see that all this is raving nonsense, and that a man can laugh without
being built out of laughing things, and can think and give his reasons
in learned words without being made out of wise and eloquent seeds, why
can the things we see having sensation not be mixed out of seeds that
lack sensation altogether?

Finally, we all rise from heavenly seed. There is one father of us all,
and when the kindly mother earth has taken in from him the drops of
flowing moisture, she is pregnant and gives birth to the shining crops
and the cheerful trees, and to the human race, and to all the
generations of wild animals, since she supplies the food on which they
all feed their bodies and lead a sweet life and pass their offspring on.
That is why she has fairly won the name of mother. In the same way, what
was earth before goes back into the earth, and what was sent down from
the shores of the upper air is carried back again and taken in by the
regions of the sky.

And death does not destroy things in such a way as to finish off the
bodies of matter. It only scatters their combination, and then joins one
thing to another and brings it about that all things change their forms
and alter their colours and take on sensations and give them back in a
moment of time. So you can know that it matters, for the atoms of
things, which others they are held with and in what arrangement, and
what motions they give and take from one another; and that you must not
think that what we see floating on the surface of things, and sometimes
born and suddenly dying, belongs for ever to the atoms. Why, even in
these very verses of mine it matters which letters each of them is set
with and in what order. The same letters mean sky, sea, lands, rivers,
sun, and the same letters mean crops, trees, living creatures. If they
are not all of them the same, at least much the greatest part is, and it
is by their arrangement that the things they mean are told apart. It is
the same in things themselves: when the intervals of the material, and
the paths, the connections, the weights, the blows, the meetings, the
motions, the order, the arrangement and the shapes are all changed
about, then the things too must be changed.

Now bring your mind to bear on true reasoning. A new thing is struggling
hard to reach your ears, and a new face of things to show itself to you.
But there is nothing so easy that it is not harder to believe at first
than it is afterwards, and equally there is nothing so great and nothing
so wonderful that everyone does not gradually stop being amazed by it.
Take the clear pure colour of the sky first, and everything it holds in
it, the stars wandering all over, the moon, and the brilliance of the
sun with its splendid light. If all of these were now there for mortals
for the first time, if they were suddenly thrown in front of them with
no warning, what could have been called more wonderful than these
things, or what would the nations have dared less to believe could
exist? Nothing, I think: so amazing would that sight have been. And now
nobody, worn out and full up with the sight of it, thinks it worth
lifting his eyes to the shining regions of the sky. So stop spitting out
this reasoning from your mind, terrified by its sheer newness. Weigh it
instead with a sharp judgement, and if it seems true to you, give in;
and if it is false, arm yourself against it. For the mind asks for an
account. Since the sum of space out there beyond the walls of this world
is infinite, it asks what there is out in it, out where the mind wants
to look on and on, and where the free flight of the spirit flies away by
itself.

To begin with, in every direction all round us, on both flanks, above
and below, through the whole, there is no end. I have shown it, and the
thing itself cries it out loud of its own accord, and the nature of the
deep shines out with it. Now that empty space stretches without limit in
every direction, and the seeds are numberless in number and fly about in
every way through the unfathomable whole, driven by an everlasting
motion, there is no way at all that it can be thought likely that this
one circle of earth and this one sky have been created, and that all
those bodies of matter out there are doing nothing. Especially since
this world was made by nature, and the seeds of things, of their own
accord, by chance, knocking against each other, driven together in many
ways at random, in vain and with no result, at last fell into the
combinations which, once they had been suddenly thrown together, could
become the beginnings of great things again and again: of earth and sea
and sky and the race of living creatures. So again and again you have to
admit that there are other gatherings of matter elsewhere, like this one
that the upper air holds in a greedy embrace.

Besides, when there is plenty of material ready, and space to hand, and
no thing and no cause holds it back, then things must certainly be
carried through and completed. And now, if there is such a supply of
seeds that all the lifetimes of living creatures could not count them,
and if the same nature stays there, able to throw the seeds of things
together into each place in the same way as they have been thrown
together here, then you have to admit that there are other worlds in
other parts of space, and other races of men and other generations of
wild animals.

Add to this that in the sum of things there is nothing that is one
alone, nothing born unique that grows up single and by itself without
belonging to some kind and without there being a great many others of
the same sort. Look at living creatures first, with your mind as your
guide. You will find that the mountain-roaming kinds of wild animals are
like this, and so is the twin offspring of men, and so, in the end, are
the silent flocks of scaly fish and all the bodies of flying things. On
the same principle, then, you must admit that the sky and the earth and
the sun, the moon, the sea and everything else that exists are not
unique, but rather beyond counting in number, since the boundary stone
of life is driven just as deep and waits for them just as surely, and
they are made of a body that was born just as much, as every kind here
on earth that is so crowded with members of its own sort.

If you hold all this well understood, nature is seen at once to be free,
rid of her proud masters, doing everything of her own accord, herself,
by herself, with no gods involved. For, by the holy hearts of the gods,
which pass a calm age and a serene life in a quiet peace, who has the
power to rule the sum of the immeasurable? Who has the power to hold the
strong reins of the deep in his hand and drive with them? Who can turn
all the skies at once, and warm all the fruitful lands with the fires of
the upper air, and be present in all places at all times, so as to make
darkness with clouds and shake the calm parts of the sky with noise, and
then throw down thunderbolts, and often knock his own temples about, and
go off into the deserts and rage there, practising with the weapon that
so often passes the guilty by and takes the life of people who do not
deserve it and have done nothing wrong?

And long after the world's birth-time, and after the first day of the
sea and the earth and the rising of the sun, many bodies have been added
from outside, many seeds added all round, which the great whole brought
together in its hurling, so that the sea and the lands could grow bigger
from them, and the house of the sky could get more room and lift its
high roofs far off the earth, and the air could rise up. For from all
places the blows distribute to each thing the bodies that belong to it,
and they all retire to their own kinds: moisture goes to moisture, earth
grows with an earthy body, fires forge fire and the upper air forges
upper air, until nature, the creator who finishes things off, has
brought everything to the final limit of growing. That is what happens
when there is no more being put inside the veins of life than what flows
away and retires. This is the point where the age of every thing has to
stop; this is the point where nature curbs the increase with her own
forces.

For whatever you see growing bigger with a glad increase, climbing step
by step to the stage of full growth, takes more bodies into itself than
it sends out of itself, as long as all its food is easily put into its
veins, and as long as it is not so widely spread out that it sends much
back and spends more than its age feeds on. Certainly one must give in
and admit that many bodies flow away and retire from things; but more
must come in, until they have touched the highest peak of growing. From
then on age breaks their strength and their full-grown vigour little by
little, and melts away into the worse part. For the bigger a thing is,
once its growth is taken away, and the broader it is, the more bodies it
scatters in every direction all round and sends off itself. Its food is
not easily distributed through all its veins, and there is not enough of
it, compared with the lavish exhalations it steams off, to let it be
resupplied and kept going. So things fairly perish, when they have been
thinned out by all that flowing away, and when they go down under blows
from outside, since at a great age the food fails at last, and bodies
never stop hammering at a thing from outside and finishing it off and
mastering it with hostile blows.

So in the same way the walls of the great world all round will be
stormed and will give way and fall into rotting ruins. Food is what has
to restore everything by making it new, food that has to prop it up,
food that has to hold everything up, and it will be no use, since the
veins cannot take in what is enough, and nature does not supply as much
as is needed.

Even now the earth's age is broken, and the earth, worn out with
childbearing, scarcely creates small animals, she who created all the
generations and gave birth to the huge bodies of wild animals. It was
not, I think, a golden rope that let the generations of mortals down
from the sky above into the fields, and it was not the sea or the waves
beating on the rocks that created them: it was the same earth that gave
birth to them that now feeds them out of herself. Besides, of her own
accord she first created the shining crops and the glad vineyards for
mortals, and of herself she gave the sweet fruits and the glad pastures,
which now scarcely grow big even when our labour is added to them. We
wear out the oxen and the strength of the farmers, we use up the iron,
and we are barely fed by the fields: that is how much they hold their
yield back and multiply the work.

And now the old ploughman shakes his head and sighs, more and more
often, that the great labours of his hands have come to nothing; and
when he sets the present times beside the times gone by, he keeps
praising his father's luck. The planter of a shrivelled old vine is
gloomy in the same way, and blames the way the times are moving, and
wears the age out with complaining, and grumbles on about how the old
generation, full of respect for the gods, got through life very easily
on a narrow plot, when each man's measure of land was far smaller than
it is now. He does not grasp that everything is little by little wasting
away and going to the grave, worn out by the long stretch of its years.
