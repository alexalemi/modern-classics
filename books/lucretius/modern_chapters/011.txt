Book Five: The World, Its Making, and the Rise of Mankind
(Part 3 of 3)

And yet in those days people were not letting go of the sweet light of
life with cries of pain much more often than now. It is only that back
then a man, caught out on his own, would more often be swallowed down
by teeth and give the wild animals a living meal, and would fill the
groves and the mountains and the woods with his groaning, watching his
own live flesh buried in a living grave. And the ones whom flight had
saved, with their bodies eaten into, would afterwards hold their
shaking palms over the foul sores and call on Death with horrifying
cries, until the savage twisting pains took their life away, with no
help to be had and no idea what their wounds wanted. But in those days
a single day did not hand thousands of men over to destruction,
marched out under the standards, and the churning flats of the sea did
not smash ships and crews against rocks. The sea would often get up
and rage, wildly, pointlessly, to no purpose, and would lay its empty
threats aside as lightly as it had made them, and the sly coaxing of a
calm sea, with its laughing waves, could not lure anybody into
disaster. The reckless science of sailing lay in the dark. In those
days it was shortage of food that gave failing limbs over to death;
now it is having too much of everything that drowns them. They used to
pour out poison for themselves, not knowing what they were doing; now
they hand it to other people, and they do it with more skill.

Afterwards, once they had got themselves huts and skins and fire, and
once the woman was joined to the man and moved with him into one
household, and the laws of marriage were learned, and they saw
children born from themselves, then for the first time the human race
began to grow soft. Fire saw to it that their chilled bodies could no
longer bear the cold so well under the roof of the sky. Venus took the
edge off their strength, and children easily broke down their parents'
proud tempers with their coaxing. Then, too, neighbours began to want
to bind themselves together in friendship, so as not to do harm and
not to be harmed, and they asked protection for the children and the
womenfolk, making it clear with cries and gestures, in their
stammering way, that it is only right for everyone to have pity on the
weak. All the same, harmony could not be produced in every case; but a
good and large part of them kept faith honestly, or else the whole
human race would have been finished off then and there and no breeding
could have carried the generations down as far as us.

As for the various sounds of speech, it was nature that drove people
to utter them, and usefulness that pressed out the names of things —
in very much the same way that not being able to talk yet seems to
push small children into gesturing, so that they point with a finger
at whatever is in front of them. Every creature feels what powers it
has and what it can put them to. Before a calf's horns are out and
showing on his forehead, he gets angry and butts with them and shoves
at you with them. Panther cubs and lion cubs fight back with claws and
paws and biting when their teeth and claws have hardly been made yet.
And we see every kind of winged thing trusting to its wings and asking
a shaky sort of help from its feathers.

So the idea that somebody in those days handed out names to things,
and that people learned their first words from him, is silly. Why
should this one man have been able to mark everything with sounds and
utter the various noises of the tongue, and the rest be supposed
unable to do it at the same time? Besides, if the others had not
already been using words among themselves, where did he get the
implanted notion of what words were good for, and where was he given
this first power of knowing and seeing in his mind what he wanted to
do? One man could not force a crowd, or beat them down, into being
willing to learn the names of things by heart. And it is not easy to
teach the deaf what needs doing, or to talk them into it by any
argument: they would not put up with it, and would not go on lending
their ears, on any terms at all, to have sounds they had never heard
hammered at them for nothing.

And after all, what is so amazing about this business — that the human
race, with a voice and a tongue in full working order, should mark
different things with different sounds as it felt different feelings?
Dumb cattle, and even the wild kinds of animal, are in the habit of
rousing unlike and varied sounds when there is fear or pain in them,
and when joy is swelling up. You can see this in perfectly plain
cases. When the great Molossian hounds are first provoked, and their
soft lips growl and pull back off the hard teeth, the rage held in
behind them threatens in a sound quite different from the sound they
make once they are barking outright and filling everything with their
voices. And when they set about licking their puppies fondly with the
tongue, or tossing them about with their paws, and, snapping with the
teeth held back, pretend to swallow them whole, they coax them with a
whimper of the voice that is nothing like the way they howl when they
are left alone in the house, or the way they shrink whining from a
beating with their bodies low.

Again, does the neighing of a horse not plainly differ, when a young
stallion in the flower of his years rages among the mares, struck by
the spurs of winged Love, and blows a snort out of his wide nostrils
calling to arms — and when he neighs at some other time with his limbs
shaking?

Lastly, the winged kinds and the various birds — hawks and ospreys and
gulls, looking for their food and their living in the salt water among
the sea waves — throw out cries at one time quite unlike the cries
they throw out at another, and unlike again when they are fighting
over food and their prey is struggling back. And some of them change
their hoarse songs along with the weather, like the long-lived
families of crows and the flocks of ravens, when they are said to be
asking for water and rain, and sometimes to be calling up the winds
and the breezes.

So if different feelings force animals to send out different sounds,
dumb as they are, how much more reasonable it is that mortals should
have been able in those days to mark unlike things with one sound and
another!

In case you are quietly wondering about it in the middle of all this:
it was lightning that first brought fire down to earth for mortals,
and it is from there that all the burning of flame is spread. We see
plenty of things blaze up after they have had fire from the sky
planted in them, when a stroke out of heaven has made them a present
of heat. But also, when a branching tree is beaten by the winds and
sways and staggers and leans on the branches of another tree, fire is
squeezed and rubbed out of them by the strong force, and sometimes the
burning heat of flame flashes out while the branches and the trunks
are rubbing against each other. Either of the two could have given
fire to mortals. Then the sun taught them to cook food and soften it
in the heat of the flame, since they could see plenty of things all
over the fields growing tender, beaten by the blows of its rays and
overcome by the heat.

And day by day the men who stood out in ability and were strong in
their wits showed the rest how to change their earlier food and way of
living, with fire and with new inventions. Kings began to found cities
and to plant a citadel as a defence and a bolt-hole for themselves,
and they divided up the cattle and the land and handed them out
according to each man's looks and strength and brains; for good looks
counted for a great deal in those days, and strength was worth having.
Later on property was invented and gold was discovered, and it easily
stripped the honour from the strong and the handsome, because as a
rule, however brave a man is and however beautiful the body he was
born with, he follows the richer man's party.

And yet if a man steered his life by true reasoning, great riches for
a human being consist in living simply, with a calm mind; for there is
never a shortage of a little. But men wanted to be famous and
powerful, so that their fortune could rest on a firm foundation and
they could pass a quiet life on their wealth. All for nothing: in
fighting their way up to the highest honours they made the road
dangerous to travel, and even so, when they are at the top, envy
sometimes strikes them like a lightning bolt and throws them down in
contempt into the foul pit of Tartarus — since it is generally the
highest places, and whatever stands out above the rest, that envy
scorches as a thunderbolt does. So it is far better to obey quietly
than to want to govern the world with power and hold kingdoms of your
own. Let them go on sweating blood, worn out for nothing, struggling
along the narrow path of ambition. They get their wisdom out of other
people's mouths and go after things by hearsay rather than by their
own senses, and that is no more true now, and will be no more true
soon, than it was before.

So the kings were killed, and the old grandeur of thrones and the
proud sceptres lay overturned, and the splendid crown of the highest
head, bloodied under the feet of the crowd, mourned its lost
greatness; for what has been feared too much beforehand gets trampled
with real appetite. So things came back down to the utter dregs and to
mob violence, with every man out to get command and supremacy for
himself. Then some of them taught the rest to appoint magistrates and
to set up rights, so that people would be willing to make use of laws.
For the human race, worn out with living by force, was sick from its
own feuds, and that made it all the readier to come down under laws
and tight legal rights of its own accord. Because each man in his
anger was setting about a fiercer revenge than fair laws now allow,
people got sick of a life lived by force.

That is how fear of punishment comes to spoil the prizes of life.
Violence and injustice catch a man in their own net and generally come
back on the head of the man they started from, and it is not easy for
someone who breaks the common agreements of peace by what he does to
pass a calm and peaceful life. Even supposing he gets past the notice
of gods and men alike, he still has to distrust the idea that it will
stay hidden forever — since so many people, they say, have talked in
their sleep, again and again, or raved in a fever, and have given
themselves away and brought their hidden crimes and their sins out
into the open.

Now, what it was that spread the powers of the gods abroad through
great nations, and filled the cities with altars, and led to the
taking up of solemn rites — rites that flourish now at great occasions
and in great places — and where the dread comes from that is planted
in mortals even now, the dread that raises new shrines to the gods all
over the world and drives people to crowd into them on festival days:
it is not so hard to give the reason for that in words.

The fact is that even in those days mortals used to see the splendid
faces of gods with their minds awake, and in sleep to see them with
bodies of still more astonishing size. So they credited these figures
with sensation, because they seemed to move their limbs and to speak
proud words that suited their glorious faces and their great strength.
And they gave them everlasting life, because the faces kept being
supplied and the shape stayed the same — and above all because they
thought that beings built up with such great powers could not easily
be overcome by any force. They thought them far better off than
anyone else, because the fear of death did not trouble any of them;
and at the same time because in dreams they saw them doing many
wonderful things and taking no trouble over it at all.

Besides, people could see the workings of the sky going round in a
fixed order, and the various seasons of the year coming round, and
they could not find out what causes made it happen. So their refuge
was to hand everything over to the gods and to make everything turn at
their nod. They put the homes and the temples of the gods in the sky,
because it is through the sky that night and the moon are seen to
roll — moon, day and night, and the grave signs of the night, and the
night-wandering torches of the sky, and the flying flames, the clouds,
the sun, the rain, snow, winds, lightning, hail, and the sudden roars
and the great mutterings of threat.

What an unlucky race we are, to have credited the gods with doings
like that and to have thrown in bitter anger with them! What groans
they made for themselves then, what wounds for us, what tears for our
children after us! It is no piety to be seen turning again and again
to a stone with your head veiled, or to go up to every altar, or to
lie stretched face down on the ground with your palms spread in front
of the shrines of the gods, or to spatter the altars with the blood of
four-footed animals in quantity, or to tie vow on to vow. It is piety,
rather, to be able to look at everything with a mind at peace.

For when we look up at the sky-regions of the great world, at the
upper air fixed above with its glittering stars, and the journeys of
the sun and the moon come into our minds, then in our hearts, already
weighed down with other troubles, this worry too begins to wake up and
lift its head: whether there may not, after all, be some measureless
power of the gods above us, wheeling the white stars round in all
their different motions. Not having any explanation is what troubles
the doubting mind: whether there ever was a birth-time and a beginning
of the world, and at the same time whether there is an end, and how
long the walls of the world can stand this strain of silent motion; or
whether they have been given everlasting safety by a god and can slide
on through the endless stretch of time and laugh at the powerful
forces of measureless age.

Besides, whose mind is not squeezed tight with fear of the gods, whose
limbs do not crawl with terror, when the scorched earth shudders under
the horrible stroke of the thunderbolt and the rumblings run right
across the great sky? Do peoples and nations not shake? Do proud kings
not draw their limbs in, struck through with fear of the gods, in case
the heavy time has come round for paying for something foully done or
proudly said? And when the whole violence of a raging wind sweeps the
admiral of a fleet across the sea with his powerful legions and his
elephants, does he not go to the gods with vows for peace, does he not
beg in his fear for the winds to be quiet and for a favourable breeze?
For nothing: he is caught up in the violent whirlwind all the same and
carried down to the shallows of death just as much. So thoroughly does
some hidden force grind human affairs down, and seem to trample the
beautiful rods and the cruel axes of office underfoot and to keep them
about as a joke. And lastly, when the whole earth wobbles under our
feet, and shaken cities fall or stand tottering and threatening to
fall, is it any wonder that mortals think little of themselves and
leave room in the world for enormous and marvellous powers and forces
of the gods to govern everything?

For the rest: copper and gold and iron were discovered, and with them
the weight of silver and the usefulness of lead, when fire had burned
up huge forests with its heat in the great mountains. Either lightning
was sent down from the sky, or people fighting a war among the woods
had carried fire against the enemy to frighten them, or, drawn on by
the goodness of the soil, they wanted to open up rich fields and turn
the country into pasture, or to kill wild animals and get rich on the
spoils. For hunting with a pit and with fire arose before fencing a
clearing round with nets and driving it with dogs.

Whatever the truth of that, and from whatever cause, when the flaming
heat had eaten out the forests down to their deep roots with a
horrible noise and had baked the earth with fire, a stream of silver
and gold, and of copper and lead too, ran out of the boiling veins and
gathered in the hollow places of the ground. And when they saw these
afterwards set hard and shining in the earth with a bright colour,
they picked them up, taken by the smooth and glossy charm of them, and
they saw that each piece had been shaped into the same figure as the
marks of the hollow it had lain in. Then it got through to them that
these things could be melted with heat and made to run into any shape
and appearance of a thing they liked, and could be hammered out and
drawn to points as sharp and as fine as they wanted, so that they
could get themselves weapons, and be able to cut down forests and hew
timber and plane beams smooth, and to bore and punch and drill holes
right through things.

And they set about doing this with silver and gold no less than with
the tough violent strength of copper. For nothing: their power gave
way, beaten, and they could not stand up to the hard work equally
well. In those days copper was what was valued and gold lay about
useless, with its blunt edge turned back; now copper lies about and
gold has gone up to the highest honour. That is how the rolling years
change the values of things. What was worth a great deal comes in the
end to be worth no honour at all; then something else takes its place
and comes up out of contempt, and is wanted more every day, and once
it is found it flowers in praise and is held in wonderful honour among
mortals.

Now, Memmius, how the nature of iron was discovered is easy for you to
work out for yourself. The earliest weapons were hands, nails and
teeth, and stones, and the broken-off branches of forest trees, and
flame and fire, once these had come to be known. Afterwards the
strength of iron and copper was discovered — and the use of copper was
known before iron's, because its nature is easier to work and there is
more of it. With copper they worked the soil of the earth; with copper
they stirred up the waves of war and sowed enormous wounds and took
away cattle and lands. For everything naked and unarmed gave way
easily to men who were armed. Then, little by little, the iron sword
came forward and the shape of the bronze sickle turned into a thing to
be ashamed of, and they began to break up the soil of the earth with
iron, and the contests of doubtful war were made equal.

And mounting armed on a horse's ribs, and steering him with the reins
and keeping the right hand free to work, came before trying the
dangers of war in a two-horse chariot. And harnessing a pair came
before harnessing two pairs, and before mounting armed into chariots
with scythes on them. After that the Carthaginians taught the
Lucanian oxen — the hideous ones with the tower on their bodies and
the snake for a hand — to bear the wounds of war and to throw the
great troops of Mars into confusion. So grim discord gave birth to one
thing after another, to be a horror to the nations under arms, and day
after day it added to the terrors of war.

They tried bulls, too, in the business of war, and they tried sending
savage boars against the enemy. Some sent powerful lions on in front
of them, with armed trainers and fierce handlers who could manage them
and hold them on chains. For nothing: hot with the mixed slaughter,
they savagely threw the squadrons into confusion without telling one
side from the other, shaking the terrifying manes on their heads in
every direction, and the horsemen could not calm their horses' hearts,
which were terrified by the roaring, or turn them with the reins
against the enemy. The lionesses hurled their bodies in a leap on
every side, and gaped in the faces of the men coming at them, and tore
men down from behind who were not expecting it, and got their grip and
brought them wounded to the ground, fastened on with strong bites and
hooked claws. The bulls tossed their own side and trampled them
underfoot, and gored the flanks and bellies of horses from below with
their horns, and dug up the ground with a threatening look. The boars
cut their own allies down with their strong tusks, savagely staining
with their own blood the spears that had broken off in them, and
brought horse and foot down in one ruin together. The animals swerved
aside to escape the fierce thrust of the tusk, or reared up on end and
pawed at the air. For nothing: you could see them fall with their
sinews cut and cover the ground with a heavy fall. If men had thought
that any of them were tame enough after being broken in at home, they
saw them boil over when it came to the doing of it, from the wounds,
the shouting, the running, the terror, the uproar, and they could not
get any part of them back. Every different kind of wild animal
scattered — just as now the Lucanian oxen, badly cut about by the
steel, often scatter after they have done so much savage damage to
their own side.

But they did not choose to do it so much in the hope of winning as to
give the enemy something to groan about, and to die themselves,
because they had no confidence in their numbers and were short of
weapons — if it ever happened at all. Though I can hardly bring myself
to believe that they could not foresee it and see in their minds,
before it happened, what a foul and general disaster it would be. You
could better argue that it happened somewhere in the universe as a
whole, among the various worlds made in various ways, than on any one
particular globe of earth.

Clothing knotted together came before woven covering. Woven cloth
comes after iron, because a loom is made with iron, and there is no
other way of making things as smooth as this: the treadles, the
spindles, the shuttles, the sounding beams. And nature forced men to
work wool before womankind did, since the whole male sex is far better
at a craft and much cleverer at it — until the hard-headed farmers
made a reproach of it, so that the men were willing to hand it over to
women's hands and to take their equal share of hard labour, and to
toughen their limbs and hands with hard work.

As for a pattern for sowing, and the origin of grafting, the first was
nature herself, who makes things: berries and acorns falling from the
trees produced swarms of shoots underneath them in due season. From
that they got the idea of putting slips into branches and planting new
sets in the ground about the fields. Then they tried one kind of
cultivation after another on their beloved plot of land, and they saw
wild fruits growing tame in the earth under kind treatment and gentle
care. And day by day they forced the woods to go further up the
mountain and to give up the ground below to what was cultivated, so
that they could have meadows and pools and streams and crops and glad
vineyards over the hills and the plains, and so that the blue-green
strip of olives could run between and mark them off, spreading out
over the mounds and the valleys and the fields — just as you see
everything now, picked out with varied charm, set out with sweet fruit
trees planted between and hedged all round with fruitful plantations.

But imitating the liquid voices of birds with the mouth came long
before men could join in smooth songs with singing and please the ear.
And the whistling of the west wind through the hollows of reeds first
taught country people to blow into hollow stalks. Then, little by
little, they learned the sweet complaints that the pipe pours out when
the fingers of the players strike at it — the pipe, found out among
trackless groves and woods and glades, in the deserted places of
shepherds and in the god-touched quiet. So time draws each thing out
little by little into the middle, and reasoning lifts it up into the
shores of light.

These things soothed their minds and gave them pleasure once they had
had enough food; for then everything appeals. So they would often lie
about together on the soft grass beside a stream of water, under the
branches of a tall tree, and give their bodies a pleasant time at no
great cost — especially when the weather was smiling and the season of
the year was painting the green grass with flowers. Then there would
be jokes, then talk, then sweet laughing; for then the country muse
was at her best. Then a happy playfulness moved them to wreathe head
and shoulders with plaited garlands of flowers and leaves, and to move
their limbs out of time and stamp with a clumsy foot on mother earth —
and out of that came laughter and sweet peals of it, because all this
was newer then, and being new it was wonderful.

And for the wakeful there were consolations for lost sleep: to run the
voice up and down in all sorts of ways and bend the notes, and to run
a curved lip over the reeds — which is why watchmen even now keep up
these things as they received them. And people have learned to keep
time as a group; and yet they get not one bit more sweetness out of it
than the woodland race born of the earth used to get. For whatever we
have to hand, if we have known nothing sweeter before, is what pleases
us most and seems to be the strongest; and then, generally, some later
and better thing gets discovered and ruins it and changes how we feel
about everything that came before.

So the acorn came to be hated; so those beds strewn with grass and
piled with leaves were abandoned. In the same way clothing made of
animal skin fell into contempt — though I imagine that when it was
first found it stirred up so much envy that the man who first wore it
was killed from ambush, and even then the skin was torn to pieces
among them and ruined with all that blood, and could not be turned to
any use. So in those days it was skins, and now it is gold and purple,
that trouble men's lives with cares and wear them out with war — and
the greater blame there, I think, has settled on us. Cold used to
torture the earth-born people when they had no skins and were naked;
but it does us no harm at all to go without a purple robe, embroidered
with gold and with enormous figures on it, so long as we have a common
one that will keep the weather off.

So the human race labours for nothing and to no purpose, always, and
eats up its life in empty cares — plainly because it has not learned
where the limit of getting is, or how far real pleasure grows. And it
is that, little by little, that has carried life out into the deep and
stirred the great tides of war up from the bottom.

But the sun and the moon, the watchmen of the world, going round the
great turning temple of the sky and lighting it with their own light,
taught people thoroughly that the seasons of the year come round again
and that things go on by a fixed plan and in a fixed order.

By now they were passing their lives fenced in behind strong towers,
and the land was divided and marked off and worked; by now the sea was
in flower with sail-flying ships; by now they had auxiliaries and
allies under a formal treaty, when poets began to hand down famous
deeds in songs — and letters had not been found out much before that.
That is why our own age cannot look back at what was done earlier,
except where reasoning shows us the tracks.

Ships and the cultivation of fields, walls, laws, weapons, roads,
clothes and everything else of that kind, all the prizes and all the
luxuries of life without exception, songs, pictures, cunningly
polished statues: practice, and at the same time the experience of a
mind that would not rest, taught these things to people as they went
forward, one careful step at a time. So time draws each thing out
little by little into the middle, and reasoning lifts it up into the
shores of light. For they saw one thing become clear out of another in
their minds, until they came to the highest peak of the arts.
