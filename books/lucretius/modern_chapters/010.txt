Book Five: The World, Its Making, and the Rise of Mankind
(Part 2 of 3)

So then the weight of the earth settled and stood firm, its body
packed close, and all the heavy sludge of the world, if you like, ran
together into the bottom and sank right down like the dregs in a jar.
After that the sea, and after that the air, and after that the
fire-carrying ether itself were left pure, with nothing in them but
their own flowing bodies, and each one lighter than the one under it.
Ether, the most flowing and the lightest of them all, floats along
above the airy currents and does not mix its own flowing body in with
the brawling gusts of the air. It lets everything down below be turned
over by violent whirlwinds, lets it be knocked about by squalls that
come without warning, while it carries its own fires along, gliding
with one steady drive. That the ether can flow like that, quietly, on
one unbroken push, is shown by the Pontus, a sea that flows with a
fixed current and keeps one single set of its course the whole way.

Now let me sing what causes the movement of the stars. To begin with,
if the great sphere of heaven turns round, then we have to say that a
body of air presses on the axis at either end, and holds it from
outside, and shuts it in at both ends; and that another current of air
flows above it and drives in the same direction as the rolling stars
of the everlasting world flash along; or else that another flows
underneath, carrying the sphere the other way, up and on, in the way
we see rivers turn wheels and water-scoops. Then again, it may also be
that the whole sky stays where it is and does not move, while the
bright signs are carried along inside it. That could be because rapid
tides of ether are shut up in there and go whirling round looking for
a way out, and roll the fires this way and that through the high
precincts of the sky; or because some air flowing in from somewhere
outside drives the fires and turns them; or because the fires can
creep along by themselves, going wherever their food calls them and
invites them as they go, grazing their flame-bodies all over the sky.
Which of these is the cause in this world of ours is hard to lay down
for certain. But what can happen, and does happen, right through the
whole of things, in the various worlds made on various plans — that
is what I am teaching, and I go on to set out several causes that
might account for the motions of the stars anywhere in the whole; and
one of them has to be the cause here as well, the one that puts
movement into our signs. But to say in advance which of them it is, is
no business at all of a man who advances one careful step at a time.

Now, for the earth to stay at rest in the middle region of the world,
its weight has to fade away little by little and get less; and it has
to have another kind of thing underneath it, joined to it from the
very start of its life and fitted into a single whole with the airy
parts of the world, in which it lives, rooted. That is why the earth
is not a load and does not press down on the winds beneath. It is like
the way a man's own limbs are no weight to him: the head is no load to
the neck, and we do not feel the whole weight of the body sitting on
the feet. But any weights that come to us from outside, weights laid
on us, do hurt us, and often they are far lighter. It matters that
much what each thing is able to do in itself. So the earth is not some
foreign substance suddenly brought in and thrown down out of somewhere
else onto air that has nothing to do with it. It was conceived
alongside the air at the world's first beginning, and it is a fixed
part of the world, in the way our limbs are plainly a part of us.
Besides, when the earth is shaken all at once by a great clap of
thunder, its movement shakes everything above it — which it could not
possibly do unless it were bound fast to the airy parts of the world
and to the sky. They hold together by roots they share, joined from
the very start of their life and grown into a single whole. Do you not
see, as well, how the extremely fine force of the soul holds up our
body for us, heavy weight that it is, and does it because the two are
so joined and fitted into one? And in the end, what can lift the body
in a quick jump but the force of the soul, which steers the limbs? Now
do you see how much a thin, fine kind of thing can do, once it is
joined to a heavy body — as the air is joined to the earth, and the
force of the mind to us?

The sun's wheel cannot be much bigger, and its blaze cannot be much
smaller, than it looks to our senses. Whatever the distance from which
fires can throw their light at us and breathe their hot vapour over
our limbs, they lose nothing of their body of flame across those
stretches, and the fire is not made the least bit narrower to look at.
So, since the sun's heat and its poured-out light reach our senses and
make our places bright, the sun's shape and its outline must be seen
from here just as they really are, with nothing whatever that you
could truly add or take away. And the moon, whether she travels
lighting up her regions with borrowed light, or throws out a light of
her own from her own body — whichever it is, she travels with a shape
no bigger than the one she seems to have to the eyes we look at her
with. For everything that we look at from a long way off, through a
great deal of air, goes blurred in its look before it goes smaller in
its outline. So, since the moon presents a clear look and a definite
shape, marked all round by the exact edge she has, she must be seen up
there from here as big as she is and no bigger. And finally, take any
of the fires of the ether you can see from down here: you may take it
that they can be a very tiny bit smaller, or bigger by a small short
part, and no more. Because any fires we can make out here on earth are
seen to change their outline only the least little bit one way or the
other according to how far off they are, as long as their flicker and
their bright burning can still be made out.

There is nothing to wonder at, either, in how so very small a sun can
send out so much light — enough to fill the seas and all the lands
and the sky, drenching them, and to pour warm vapour over everything.
It may be that from this one point a single spring, running wide, has
been opened up for the whole world, and gushes and bursts out with
light, because the atoms of heat come together from every part of the
world from all sides, and their gathering flows together in such a way
that the burning streams out from one head. Do you not see, as well,
how widely a small spring of water sometimes waters the meadows and
floods the fields? It may also be that the burning heat takes hold of
the air with hot fevers, even from a fire in the sun that is not
large, if the air happens to be in the right condition and ready for
it, so that it can be set alight when small heats strike it. That is
the sort of thing we sometimes see happen with standing corn and
stubble, where a single spark sets fires going everywhere. And perhaps
the sun, shining high up with a rosy lamp, has a great deal of fire
round about him in invisible heats, marked by no brightness at all,
and that is what makes him a bringer of heat and multiplies the strike
of his rays.

Nor is there one straightforward account, plain to see, of how the sun
gets from his summer quarters to the winter turning-point in Capricorn
and then, coming back from there, swings round to the solstice mark in
Cancer; or of how the moon is seen to cross in a month the same
distance the sun uses up a whole year of time in travelling. No, I
say, no single cause has been given for these things. What seems most
likely of all is the doctrine the holy judgement of Democritus lays
down: the nearer any of the heavenly bodies are to the earth, the less
they can be carried along by the whirl of the sky, because that
whirl's swift, sharp forces die away and are cut down lower down, and
so the sun is left behind by degrees among the signs that follow after
him, since he is a great deal lower than the blazing signs. And this
goes for the moon still more: the further down her course runs, away
from the sky and close to the lands, the less she can keep pace with
the signs. And the more slack the whirl she is carried by, being lower
than the sun, the more all the signs catch her up as they circle round
and go past her. That is why she seems to come back to any given sign
more quickly than the sun — because the signs are coming back round
to her. It can also be that two currents of air blow out at fixed
times, in turn, from opposite quarters of the world: one able to shove
the sun out of the summer signs all the way down to the winter turn
and the freezing cold, and the other to throw him back out of the icy
shadows of the cold into the heat-bearing quarters and the blazing
signs. And we have to suppose that the moon and the stars work the
same way — the stars that roll their great years round in great
orbits — and that they can be moved by currents of air from
alternating quarters. Do you not see, as well, how clouds are carried
in opposite directions by opposite winds, the low ones one way and the
high ones another? Why should those stars up there be any less able to
be carried along their great circles of ether by tides running
opposite to each other?

As for night, it buries the lands in enormous darkness either when the
sun, at the end of his long course, has struck the far edge of the sky
and, worn out, has breathed out his fires, shaken by the journey and
weakened by all that air; or because the same force that drove his
wheel over the lands makes him turn his course beneath them. And in
the same way, at a fixed time, Matuta spreads the rosy dawn along the
coasts of the ether and opens out the light — either because the same
sun, coming back round under the lands, gets ahead of himself and
tries to set the sky alight with his rays; or because fires come
together, and many seeds of burning are in the habit of flowing
together at a fixed time, and these make the sun's light be born new
every day. In the same way, the story goes, from the high mountains of
Ida scattered fires can be seen at first light, and then gather into
something like a single ball and make up a disc. And there is nothing
in all this that ought to seem strange: that these seeds of fire can
flow together at so exact a time and make the sun's brightness over
again. We see many things that happen at a fixed time in every part of
nature. Trees come into flower at a fixed time, and at a fixed time
they let their flowers go. Age orders the teeth to fall out at a time
no less fixed, and orders the boy who is not yet a man to grow into
manhood with a soft covering, and to let a soft beard down from both
cheeks at the same time. And lastly, lightning, snow, rain, clouds,
winds all come about at parts of the year that are not too
unpredictable. Once the first beginnings of causes have been like
that, and once things fell out that way from the world's first origin,
they come round again, one after another, in a fixed order.

In the same way, days can grow and nights shrink, and daylight get
less while the nights take on their increases. That could be because
the same sun, running under the lands and above them on unequal arcs,
cuts the coasts of the ether apart and divides his circle into
unequal parts; and whatever he has taken off one part he puts back
onto the opposite part as he is carried round, that much more, until
he arrives at the sign of heaven where the year's knot makes the
shadows of night equal to the daylight. For when he is halfway
through his course, between the blast of the north wind and the south
wind, the sky holds his two goals apart at an equal distance, because
of the fixed position of the whole sign-bearing circle, the one the
sun winds through as he closes up a year of time, lighting the earth
and the sky with a slanting light — as the account of it is set out
by the men who have mapped all the regions of the sky and marked them
out with the signs laid down in order. Or it could be because the air
is thicker in certain quarters, so that the trembling ray of his fire
sticks under the lands and cannot easily get through and come up to
the sunrise. That is why the nights hang about, long, in winter time,
until the many-rayed badge of day comes. Or it could also be, as I
said, that in alternating parts of the year the fires that make the
sun rise from a fixed quarter are in the habit of flowing together
more slowly and more quickly, and that is why the men who say a new
sun is born each morning seem to be telling the truth.

The moon may shine because she is struck by the sun's rays, and turn
that light towards us more and more every day for us to see, the
further she moves away from the sun's disc, until, facing him, she has
shone out with a full light, and, rising, has looked down on his
setting from up above; and then she must hide her light back away
again, bit by bit, as she slides nearer to the sun's fire round the
circle from the other side of the signs. That is the account given by
the men who imagine the moon is like a ball and holds her course on a
path below the sun. But there is also a reason why she could roll
along with a light of her own and give back the different shapes of
her brightness. There may be another body carried along with her,
sliding beside her, getting in her way and blocking her in every
possible manner, and it cannot be seen because it travels with no
light in it. Or she can turn about like a ball, if that is what she
is, with half of her dyed in glowing light, and by turning that globe
put out shapes that change, until she turns towards our sight and our
open eyes whatever part of her has been given fire; and then, bit by
bit, she twists back round and takes the light-bearing part of that
sphere and ball away. That is the doctrine of the Babylonians, the
teaching of the Chaldeans, refuting the art of the astronomers of
Greece and setting out to prove the opposite — as if the thing each
side is fighting for could not both be true, or as if there were any
reason why you should dare to take up the one rather than the other.
And finally, why a new moon could not be created every time, in a
fixed order of shapes and with fixed forms, and why each one, once
made, could not die away on its own day, with another put in its
position and place, is hard to teach by argument and hard to win by
words — when you can see so many things created in an order just as
fixed. Spring comes, and Venus, and the winged herald of Venus goes
before her; and hard on the footsteps of Zephyr, Mother Flora scatters
the way ahead of them and fills it all with wonderful colours and
smells. Next in the line comes dry Heat, with dusty Ceres for a
companion, and the Etesian blasts of the north winds. Then Autumn
arrives, and Bacchus comes striding along with him. Then other
weathers and other winds follow — high-thundering Volturnus, and the
south wind with its lightning strength. In the end the shortest day of
the year brings the snows and gives back the numbing frost. Winter
comes after it, with its teeth chattering in the cold. So it is less
of a marvel if a moon is born at a fixed time and destroyed again at a
fixed time, when so many things can happen at times so exactly fixed.

You must take it, too, that the failures of the sun and the hidings
away of the moon come about from several causes. If the moon can shut
the earth off from the sun's light, and push her high head up between
the earth and him, putting her dark disc in the way of his burning
rays, why should it be thought that some other body, one that slides
along without any light in it for ever, could not do the same thing at
the same time? And why should the sun not be able, at a fixed time, to
grow weak and let his fires go, and then make his light again, once he
has passed the places in the air that are hostile to flames, the ones
that put fires out and kill them? And why should the earth be able, in
her turn, to rob the moon of light, and to hold the sun down out of
sight up above her, while the moon on her monthly journey glides
through the stiff shadows of the cone — and yet, at the same time,
some other body not be able to come up under the moon, or to slide
along above the sun's disc, and break the rays and the poured-out
light apart? And even if the moon does shine with a brightness of her
own, why should she not be able to grow faint in some fixed quarter of
the world, while she is passing through the regions that are
unfriendly to her own light?

Now for what is left. Since I have unravelled how everything can
happen through the blue reaches of the great world, so that we could
know what force and what cause sets going the sun's various courses
and the moon's journeys, and how the two of them can go dark when
their light is blocked and draw shadow over lands that were not
expecting it, and then, when they seem to blink and open the light
again, look out over everything and light up all those regions with
white brightness — now I go back to the newness of the world, and the
soft fields of earth, and what they first decided to lift up to the
shores of light in their new bearing, and to trust to the winds that
come without warning. To begin with, the earth put out the kinds of
grass and a green shining round the hills and over all the plains, and
the flowering meadows blazed with a greening colour; and after that
the various kinds of trees were given a great free-rein contest of
growing, up through the air. In the way that feathers and hairs and
bristles are the first things made on the limbs of four-footed
animals and on the bodies of the strong-winged ones, so the new earth
first raised up grasses and bushes, and then made the generations of
mortal creatures, springing up in many ways and in many different
fashions. Living things cannot have fallen out of the sky, and the
land-dwellers cannot have come out of the salt pools. So what remains
is that the earth has rightly won the name of mother, since everything
was created out of the earth. Even now many living things come up out
of the soil, packed together by the rains and the warm vapour of the
sun. So it is less of a marvel if in those days more of them came up,
and bigger ones, grown to their full size in a new earth and a new
ether. To begin with, the race of winged things and the birds of many
kinds left their eggs behind, hatched out in the spring, in the way
that the smooth-shelled crickets nowadays leave their husks of their
own accord in summer, going after food and a living. Then it was that
the earth first gave you the generations of mortal creatures. For a
great deal of heat and moisture was overflowing in the fields. And so,
wherever a suitable patch of ground offered itself, wombs would grow,
holding on to the earth by roots; and when, in the ripeness of time,
the age of the young inside had broken them open — the young running
away from the wet and making for the air — nature would turn the
pores of the earth towards them there and force it to pour out of its
opened veins a juice much like milk, in the way that any woman
nowadays, once she has given birth, is filled with sweet milk, because
that whole rush of nourishment is turned towards her breasts. The
earth gave the children food, the warmth gave them clothing, the grass
gave them a bed, thick and rich in soft down. And the newness of the
world stirred up no hard frosts and no excessive heats and no winds of
great force. Everything grows and takes on its strength at the same
rate.

So, again and again, the earth has rightly won and keeps the name of
mother, since she herself created the human race and poured out, at
just about a fixed time, every kind of animal that runs wild over the
great mountains, and the birds of the air at the same time, with all
their varying shapes. But because she has to have some end to her
child-bearing, she stopped, like a woman worn out by a long stretch of
years. For time changes the nature of the whole world, and one state
of things has to take over from another, and nothing stays like
itself: everything moves on, nature changes everything and forces it
to turn into something else. One thing rots and goes weak and slack
with age; another comes up in its place and comes out of being nothing
much. That, then, is how time changes the nature of the whole world:
one state of the earth takes over from another, and what she could do
once she cannot do now, and what she never bore before, she can bear
today. In those days the earth tried to create many monsters too,
which came up with strange faces and strange limbs: the man-woman,
between the two and neither, kept away from both sides; some robbed of
feet, some in turn deprived of hands; some found dumb without a mouth,
or blind without a face; some bound fast by their limbs clinging to
the whole body, so that they could not do anything, or go anywhere, or
keep out of harm's way, or take what they needed. She kept creating
the other monsters and freaks of this kind, and it came to nothing,
because nature frightened them off from growing, and they could not
reach the flower of life they wanted, or find food, or join together
in the business of Venus. For we see that a great many conditions have
to come together for a kind of creature to be able to hammer out
generation after generation by breeding. First, there must be food.
Then there must be a way for the seeds that do the begetting to run
out through the frame, once the limbs are relaxed. And the female must
have what she needs to be joined with the male, so that the two of
them can exchange the pleasures each has for the other.

And many kinds of living thing must have died out in those days,
unable to hammer out offspring by breeding. Any creature you see
feeding on the breath of life has had cunning, or courage, or at any
rate quickness, keeping its kind safe from the very start. And many of
them survive because they are useful to us, and have been recommended
to us that way, handed over to our care. The fierce breed of lions and
the savage kinds have been kept safe by courage, foxes by cunning,
deer by running away. But the light-sleeping hearts of dogs, with
their faithful chests, and every kind born from the seed of beasts of
burden, and the wool-bearing flocks along with them and the
horn-bearing kinds, have all been handed over to the care of human
beings, Memmius. They ran from the wild animals eagerly, and went
after peace and generous fodder got without any work of their own,
which we give them as a reward for being useful. But those to whom
nature granted none of these things — creatures unable to live on
their own account, and unable to give us any usefulness in return for
which we might put up with letting their kind feed and be safe under
our protection — those, plainly, lay there as prey and profit for
others, all of them tangled in the bonds of their own doom, until
nature reduced that kind to extinction.

But there never were any Centaurs, and there cannot ever be creatures
of a double nature and a twofold body, put together out of limbs of
different stock, so that the power on each side could be evenly
matched. You can work this out from what follows, however dull your
wits are. To begin with, a horse comes into its full vigour by the
time three years have gone round, but a boy is nowhere near it; often
enough he is still hunting in his sleep for the milky nipple of his
mother's breast. Later, when the strong powers of the horse and its
limbs give out, weak in old age, as life runs away from them, only
then does youth in its flowering years begin for a boy and cover his
cheeks with soft down. So do not imagine that Centaurs can be put
together out of a human being and the seed of horses, the beasts of
burden, or exist at all; and no more can there be Scyllas, half-sea
bodies with rabid dogs belted round them, or the rest of that sort, in
whom we can see the limbs at odds with each other. They do not come
into flower at the same time or take on their strength together in the
body, and they do not throw it off together in old age; they do not
burn with the same kind of desire, and their habits do not agree, and
the same foods are not pleasant to the two frames. In fact you can
often see bearded goats grow fat on hemlock, which for a human being
is a sharp poison. And since flame is in the habit of scorching and
burning the tawny bodies of lions just as much as every other kind of
flesh and blood that exists on earth, how could it have happened that
one creature with a triple body — a lion in front, a snake behind,
and in the middle the goat that gives her name — the Chimaera, could
breathe a sharp flame out of her body and out of her mouth? So anybody
who imagines that animals like that could have been born when the
earth was new and the sky was fresh, leaning on nothing but that one
empty word _new_, may as well blurt out a great many things on the
same principle. He may say that in those days rivers of gold ran
everywhere over the lands, that trees used to flower with jewels, or
that a human being was born with such a reach of limbs that he could
plant his feet across the deep seas and turn the whole sky round about
him with his hands. The fact that there were many seeds of things in
the earth at the time when the ground first poured out living
creatures is no sign at all that animals could have been created
mixed with one another, with the limbs of different creatures put
together. The kinds of grasses and the crops and the flourishing trees
that even now come up in plenty out of the ground cannot be created
tangled into one another. Each thing goes forward in its own way, and
they all keep their distinctions by the fixed laws of nature.

And the human race in those fields was far tougher, as you would
expect, since a tough earth had created it, and it was built on bigger
and more solid bones inside, and fitted with strong sinews through the
flesh, not easily got at by heat or cold or by strange food or by any
weakness of the body. Through many circuits of the sun rolling across
the sky, they dragged out their lives in the wandering manner of wild
animals. Nobody was the sturdy handler of the curved plough, and
nobody knew how to work the fields with iron, or to dig new shoots
into the ground, or to cut the old branches off tall trees with
pruning hooks. What the sun and the rains had given them, what the
earth had created of its own accord, was gift enough to satisfy their
hearts. They looked after their bodies among the acorn-bearing oaks,
for the most part; and the arbutus berries that you see now ripening
to a bright red in winter time, the earth then bore in far greater
numbers and far bigger. Besides that, the flowering newness of the
world in those days bore many coarse foods, plenty of them for
miserable mortals. And rivers and springs called them to put out their
thirst, in the way that now the water rushing down from the great
mountains calls the thirsty generations of wild animals from far off.
And in their wanderings they kept to the woodland haunts of the nymphs
that they had come to know, places from which they knew that gliding
streams of water washed the wet rocks with a generous overflow — the
wet rocks, dripping down over the green moss — and that in some
places the water welled up and burst out across the level plain. They
did not yet know how to handle things with fire, or to use skins and
clothe the body with the spoils of wild animals. They lived in the
groves and the hollows of the mountains and the woods, and hid their
filthy limbs among the bushes when they were forced to keep out of the
lashing of the winds and the rain. They could have no thought for the
common good, and they did not know how to use any customs or any laws
among themselves. Whatever plunder chance had put in a man's way, he
carried off, each of them taught to be strong and to live for himself
alone. And Venus joined the bodies of lovers in the woods, since a
woman was won over either by a desire the two of them shared, or by
the man's violent force and his heavy lust, or by a price — acorns
and arbutus berries or choice pears. And trusting to the astonishing
strength of their hands and feet, they hunted down the woodland
generations of wild animals with thrown stones and with the great
weight of the club. They beat many of them, and there were a few they
kept out of the way of, in hiding places; and when the night caught
them, they threw their wildmen's limbs down naked on the ground, like
bristly boars, rolling themselves up in leaves and branches. They did
not go looking for daylight and the sun over the fields with loud
wailing, frightened and straying about in the shadows of the night;
they waited in silence, buried in sleep, until the sun brought his
light into the sky with a rosy torch. Because from the time they were
small they had always been used to seeing darkness and light come
about in turn, there was never any way they could be astonished by it,
or be afraid that an everlasting night might hold the lands for ever
with the sun's light taken away. What worried them more than that was
that the generations of wild animals often made rest dangerous for
those miserable people. Driven out of house and home, they would run
from their shelters of rock at the arrival of a foaming boar or a
strong lion, and in the dead of night they would give up their bedding
of spread leaves, terrified, to those savage guests.
