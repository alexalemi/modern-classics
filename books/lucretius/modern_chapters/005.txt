Book Three: The Mind, the Soul, and the Fear of Death
(Part 2 of 2)

And here is another way of putting it. The mind is one part of a man,
and it stays fixed in one particular place, just as the ears are, and
the eyes, and all the other senses that steer a life. And just as a
hand, or an eye, or a nose, taken away and cut off from the rest of us,
cannot feel anything and cannot even go on existing, but in a very
short time rots away, so the mind cannot exist by itself, without the
body and without the man himself, who seems to be a sort of jar for it
— or anything else you like to imagine that is joined to it still
more closely than that, since the body clings to the mind by a real
connection.

Then again, the living powers of the body and of the mind are strong
when they are joined together, and they enjoy life together. The nature
of the mind cannot by itself, on its own, without a body, produce the
motions of life; and the body, emptied of soul, cannot last and go on
using the senses. Obviously an eye, torn out by the roots, cannot make
anything out by itself once it is separated from the whole body; and in
the same way soul and mind seem able to do nothing on their own. It is
no wonder. They are mixed in through the veins and the flesh, through
the sinews and the bones, and are held in by the whole body, and their
atoms cannot go bounding freely apart across wide gaps. So, shut in as
they are, they make the motions that give us feeling — motions they
cannot make outside the body, thrown out into the air after death,
because there they are not held in the same way. Air would have to be a
body, and alive, if the soul could hold itself together in air and shut
itself up in the motions it used to make a moment earlier in the sinews
and in the body itself. So again and again: when the whole wrapping of
the body has been undone and the breath of life has been forced out,
you have to admit that the sensations of the mind dissolve, and the
soul dissolves too, since the cause of life is one thing joined for the
two of them.

And finally, the body cannot bear to have the soul torn away from it
without rotting in a foul smell. So why do you doubt that the force of
the soul has risen up out of the depths and drained away, spreading
like smoke, and that the body has fallen in on itself, changed and
rotten, in so complete a collapse, because its foundations have been
shifted from deep inside as the soul drains out through the limbs and
through every winding passage in the body and out of every opening?
There are many ways, then, for you to learn that the nature of the soul
went out in pieces, distributed through the limbs, and that it was
already torn apart inside the body itself before it slipped out and
swam away into the air.

More than that. Even while a life is still turning inside its own
boundaries, the soul often seems, for one cause or another, to be
shaken loose and to be going, and to be coming free of the whole body,
all of it at once. The face goes slack, as if the last hour had come,
and all the limbs collapse softly on the bloodless body. That is the
sort of thing we mean when we say that a man has had a turn, or that
his soul has left him — the moment when everyone is in a panic and
wants to catch hold of the last link of life. At that moment the mind
and the whole power of the soul are shaken to pieces, and they are
tottering along with the body itself, so that a cause only a little
stronger could dissolve them altogether.

So why do you still doubt that, once it has been thrust outside the
body — a feeble thing, out in the open, with its covering stripped
off — the soul could not merely fail to last through all time, but
could not hold together for the shortest stretch of time you like to
name?

And nobody who is dying seems to feel his soul going out of the whole
body in one piece, or coming up first into his throat and the top of
his gullet. He feels it failing where it is, in one particular region,
just as he knows that the other senses dissolve, each in its own part
of the body. If our mind were immortal, then as it died it would not
complain so much of being dissolved; it would complain rather of going
out and leaving its clothes behind, the way a snake does.

And why is the mind, the thinking and deciding part, never born in the
head, or in the feet, or in the hands? Why does it cling in every one
of us to one seat and one fixed region? It must be because definite
places have been assigned for the birth of each thing, and definite
places where each created thing can last, so that the limbs can be
arranged in all their various ways without the order of the parts ever
coming out backwards. So thoroughly does one thing follow another.
Flame is not in the habit of being made in rivers, and cold is not
begotten in fire.

Besides, if the nature of the soul is immortal and can feel when it has
been separated from our body, then I suppose we shall have to fit it
out with the five senses. There is no other way for us to picture to
ourselves the souls below wandering along the Acheron. That is why
painters, and the writers of earlier generations, have brought souls on
stage furnished with senses. But the soul cannot have separate eyes, or
a separate nose, or a hand of its own, and it cannot have a separate
tongue or separate ears. So by themselves souls cannot feel, and cannot
even exist.

And since we feel that the sense of life is in the whole body, and we
see that the whole of it is alive, then if some force suddenly cuts it
through the middle with a quick blow, so as to sever the two halves
from one another, there is no doubt that the force of the soul will be
divided and split as well, and flung apart along with the body. And
whatever is split, and separates into parts of any kind, plainly denies
that it has an everlasting nature.

They tell how chariots armed with scythes lop off limbs so suddenly,
hot with the confusion of the slaughter, that the piece which falls,
cut away, is seen quivering on the ground, while the man's mind and his
own force cannot yet feel the pain, because the injury has been so
quick; and because his mind is at the same time given over to the
eagerness of the fight, he goes after the fight and the killing with
what is left of his body. Often he does not notice that the wheels and
the tearing scythes have dragged his left arm away among the horses,
shield and all. Another does not notice that his right hand has dropped
off, while he is climbing up and pressing on. Another tries to get to
his feet with his leg gone, while the dying foot on the ground nearby
is twitching its toes. And a head cut off from a warm, living trunk
keeps the look of life on the ground, and the eyes open, until it has
given up all the remnants of the soul.

Or take a snake, with its tongue flickering and its tail threatening
and its long body, and suppose you took it into your head to cut it
into a number of pieces with a blade. You would see every part of it
separately, chopped through by the fresh wound, twisting about and
spattering the ground with gore, and the front part turning back and
going for the hinder part with its jaws, to clamp down on the burning
bite of the wound in its pain. Shall we say, then, that there are whole
souls in all those little pieces? But on that reasoning it would follow
that one animal had many souls inside one body. So the soul that was
one has been divided along with the body; and that is why both have to
be counted mortal, since both alike are cut up into many parts.

Besides, if the nature of the soul is immortal, and slips into the body
when we are being born, why can we not remember the stretch of time
lived before that? Why do we hold no traces of the things we did? If
the power of the mind has been changed so completely that all its hold
on what it did has fallen away, then that, I think, is not so very far
from death. So you have to admit that the soul which was there before
has perished, and that the one which is here now has been created now.

Besides, if the living power of the mind is normally put into us when
the body is already finished, at the moment when we are born and cross
the threshold of life, then it would not fit for it to look as though
it grows along with the body and along with the limbs, in the very
blood. It ought rather to live alone, by itself, as if in a cage —
and yet the whole body is flooded with sensation. So again and again:
souls must not be thought to have no origin, or to be released from the
law of death. It must not be thought that they could have been fastened
so tightly to our bodies after slipping in from outside, since the
plain facts teach us that the whole thing works the other way round.
The soul is so interconnected through the veins, the flesh, the sinews
and the bones that even the teeth share in sensation, as we see from
toothache, and the shock of icy cold water, and a rough little stone
crunched out of a mouthful of bread. And since they are woven together
so closely, souls do not look as though they could go out unhurt, or
work themselves free undamaged from all the sinews and bones and joints.

And if you happen to think that the soul slips in from outside and then
normally seeps through our limbs, all the more will it perish, poured
through the body as it is. Whatever seeps through is dissolved, and
therefore dies, since it is distributed through all the little channels
of the body. Food is spread out into all the limbs and members, and
perishes, and supplies another nature out of itself; and in just the
same way soul and mind, however whole and new they are when they go
into a body, are still dissolved in the seeping through, while the
particles are distributed through all the channels into the limbs —
the particles out of which this nature of mind is created, the mind
which now rules in our body, born out of that other one which perished
when it was divided up through the limbs.

So the nature of the soul turns out to have both a birthday and a
funeral.

Besides, are seeds of soul left behind in the lifeless body or not? If
they are left behind and are still in it, the soul cannot fairly be
held immortal, since it has withdrawn diminished, with parts of it
lost. But if it has got away, carried off with its limbs so intact that
it has left no parts of itself behind in the body, then where do
corpses breathe out the worms from flesh that is already going rancid?
And where does such a great supply of living things, boneless and
bloodless, come surging up through the swollen limbs?

Perhaps you believe that souls slip in from outside to the worms, and
that a separate one can come into each body, and it does not occur to
you to ask why many thousands of souls should gather where one has
withdrawn. Even so, here is a point that seems worth asking and worth
putting to the test: do the souls hunt out the seeds of each little
worm and build themselves a place to live in, or do they slip into
bodies that are, so to speak, ready-made? There is no way of saying why
they should do the work themselves, or why they should take the
trouble. While they are without a body they do not go fluttering about
troubled by disease and cold and hunger; it is the body that suffers
more from these faults, by its kinship with them, and the mind that
takes on many evils by contact with it. But grant, if you like, that it
is useful for them to make a body to get into. There is no way visible
by which they could. So souls do not make bodies and limbs for
themselves. Nor is there any way for them to slip into ready-made
bodies, since they will not be able to be finely interwoven with them,
and no shared feeling will pass between them by contact.

Then again, why does fierce violence go with the grim breed of lions,
and cunning with foxes, and running away with deer? Why is the fear of
their fathers handed down and set going in their limbs? Why does
everything else of this kind grow up from the very beginning of life,
in the body and the character alike, if it is not because a fixed force
of mind, coming from its own seed and its own stock, grows equally with
the body? If the mind were immortal, and were in the habit of changing
bodies, animals would have all their characters mixed up. The hound of
Hyrcanian breed would often run from the charge of the antlered stag;
the hawk would tremble as it fled through the air at the coming of the
dove; men would be witless, and the wild breeds of wild animals would
be wise.

For it is a false argument when people say that an immortal soul is
altered by a change of body. Whatever is altered is dissolved, and
therefore dies. The parts are shifted across and move out of their
order; and so they must be able to be dissolved through the limbs as
well, and in the end to perish, all of them, along with the body.

And if they say that human souls always go into human bodies, I shall
still ask why a wise soul can turn into a stupid one, why no boy is
prudent, and why a mare's foal is not so well schooled as the strength
of a full-grown horse. Of course they will take refuge in saying that
the mind grows tender in a tender body. But if that is what happens,
they have to admit that the soul is mortal, since it is so completely
changed through the limbs that it loses the life and the sense that it
had before. And how will the force of the mind be able to grow strong
along with each body and reach the flower of life it wants, unless it
is a partner in that body from the first beginning? Or what does the
soul mean by wanting to go out from limbs that have grown old? Is it
afraid of staying shut up in a rotting body, and afraid that the house
it lives in, worn out by its long span of years, will come down on top
of it? But there are no dangers at all for an immortal.

Again, it seems ridiculous that souls should be standing ready for the
couplings of Venus and the births of wild animals — immortals waiting
for mortal limbs, in numbers past numbering, and racing each other in a
great hurry to see which shall slip in first and take the best place.
Unless perhaps the souls have made a treaty about it, so that whichever
one comes flying up first gets in first, and they never have any trial
of strength between them at all.

Then again, a tree cannot exist up in the sky, or clouds in the deep
sea, and fish cannot live in the fields, and there is no blood in wood
and no sap in stones. It is fixed and arranged where each thing may
grow and where it may belong. In the same way the nature of the mind
cannot arise on its own without a body, or be far away from the sinews
and the blood. If it could, then it could much sooner be in the head,
or in the shoulders, or right down in the heels; it would be in the
habit of being born in whatever part you like, and would at least stay
in the same man and the same jar. But since it stands fixed even in our
own body, and it appears to be arranged where the soul and the mind can
each of them separately exist and grow, we must all the more deny that
they can last or be born entirely outside the body. So when the body
has perished, you have to admit that the soul has perished too, torn
apart through the whole body.

And in fact, to join the mortal to the eternal and to imagine that they
feel together and can act on one another is foolishness. What could you
think of that is more unlike, more disconnected, more out of tune, than
a mortal thing yoked together with an immortal and everlasting one and
putting up with savage storms in that partnership?

Besides, whatever stays eternal must do it in one of three ways. Either
it repels blows because it is made of solid body, and lets nothing get
inside it that could break up the close-packed parts within — as the
bodies of matter are, whose nature I have already shown you. Or it can
last through all time because it is exempt from blows, as the void is,
which stays untouched and is never affected by a blow at all. Or else
there is no supply of space around it into which its parts could go off
and be dissolved — as the sum of all sums is eternal, with no place
outside it for anything to fly apart into, and no bodies that could
fall on it and break it up with a strong blow.

But perhaps the soul is to be counted immortal on this ground instead:
that it is kept protected by the forces of life; or because nothing
hostile to its well-being comes near it at all; or because whatever
does come is somehow driven off and retreats before we can feel any
harm from it. But this is very far from true. Quite apart from the fact
that the mind sickens when the body is diseased, there comes upon it
that thing which torments it about what is still to come, keeps it
wretched in fear, and wears it out with worries; and when the wrong is
already in the past, the sins of it gnaw at it still. Add the madness
that belongs to the mind alone, and its forgetting of things. Add that
it goes under into the black waves of lethargy.

So death is nothing to us, and does not matter in the least, since the
nature of the mind is now understood to be mortal. In the time gone by
we felt nothing wrong when the Carthaginians were coming from every
side to fight us, when everything was shaken by the trembling uproar of
war and shuddered horribly under the high shores of heaven, and it was
in doubt to whose empire all human beings must fall, on land and sea
alike. In the same way, when we shall not exist, when the split between
body and soul has come, out of which we are put together as one thing,
then plainly nothing whatever will be able to happen to us — who
shall not exist then — or to move our senses: not if the earth is
mixed with the sea and the sea with the sky.

And even if the nature of the mind and the power of the soul do have
feeling after they have been torn away from our body, it is still
nothing to us, who exist as one thing by the fitting together and the
marriage of body and soul. And if time should gather our matter
together again after our death, and put it back as it is placed now,
and the light of life should be given to us a second time, even that
would not matter to us in the least, once the thread of our memory of
ourselves has been snapped. As it is, nothing about the selves we were
before matters to us now, and no anguish about them touches us. When
you look back at the whole stretch of measureless time gone by, and
then at how many kinds of motion matter has, you can easily believe
this too: that these same seeds we are made of now have often been set
before in the same order they are in now. And yet we cannot get hold of
that with a remembering mind, because a pause of life has been thrown
in between, and all the motions have wandered away astray in every
direction from the senses.

For if there is going to be misery and suffering, then the person to
whom the harm can happen must also himself exist at the time. Death
takes this away, and stops the man existing to whom the trouble could
be given; and so we may be sure that there is nothing for us to fear in
death, that a man who does not exist cannot be made miserable, and that
it makes not the slightest difference whether he was ever born at any
earlier time, once immortal death has taken his mortal life away.

So when you see a man resenting the thought that after death his body
will be laid away and rot, or be finished off by flames or by the jaws
of wild animals, you may be sure he does not ring true, and that there
is some hidden sting deep in his heart, however much he denies
believing that he will have any sensation in death. He does not grant,
I think, what he claims to grant, or the ground it rests on. He does
not pull himself up out of life by the roots and throw himself away.
Without knowing it, he leaves something of himself over.

For when a man is alive and imagining that after death birds and wild
animals will tear his body, he is sorry for himself. He does not
separate himself from that thing, or take himself far enough away from
the body flung down there. He imagines that the body is himself, and he
stands beside it and infects it with his own power of feeling. That is
why he resents having been created mortal, and does not see that in
real death there will be no second self, alive, to mourn his own
destruction, and to stand there grieving that he lies there being torn
or burned. For if it is an evil in death to be pulled about by the jaws
and teeth of wild animals, I cannot see why it is not bitter to be laid
on hot fires and scorched in the flames, or to be laid in honey and
smothered, or to go stiff with cold when a man lies on the top surface
of a slab of icy stone, or to be pressed down and buried under the
weight of earth from above.

"Now, now, your happy house will not welcome you any more, or your best
of wives; no sweet children will run up to snatch the first kiss and
touch your heart with a silent sweetness. You will not be able to make
a success of your affairs, or be a protection to your own people. Poor
man, poor man," they say, "one hostile day has taken away from you all
these prizes of life."

What they do not add is this: "and no longing for any of those things
is left sitting inside you." If they saw that clearly in their minds,
and followed it up in what they say, they would set themselves free of
great anguish and fear.

"You, as you are, put to sleep in death, will be like this for the rest
of time, taken out of the reach of all painful suffering. But we stood
next to the horrible pyre while you were being turned to ashes, and
wept for you without ever having enough of it, and no day will take
that everlasting grief out of our hearts."

The question to put to a man who talks like that is what is so bitter
about it, if the thing comes down in the end to sleep and rest, that
anyone should waste away in unending mourning.

People do this too when they have taken their places at table and are
holding their cups, with their faces shaded by garlands, and they say,
out of their hearts, "This pleasure is a short one for us poor little
men; soon it will have been, and there will be no calling it back
afterwards." As if in death the chief of their troubles would be that
thirst would burn the poor things and scorch them dry, or that a
longing for something else would settle on them.

For nobody misses himself and his life at the time when mind and body
are both asleep and at rest. As far as we are concerned, that sleep
could go on forever, and no longing for ourselves affects us. And yet
at that moment those atoms of ours are not wandering at all far from
the motions that make our senses, all through our limbs, since a man
collects himself the moment he is snatched out of sleep. So death must
be thought to be much less to us than sleep is — if there can be
anything less than what we can already see to be nothing. A greater
scattering of the disturbed matter follows on death, and nobody wakes
up once the cold pause of life has caught him.

Finally, suppose the nature of things were suddenly to find a voice and
speak up herself, and tell one of us off like this: "What is the matter
with you, mortal, that is so serious that you give yourself over to
this sickly grieving? Why do you groan and weep over death? If the life
you have lived so far has been welcome to you, and all your good things
have not been poured into you as into a leaky jar and run straight out
and gone to waste, why do you not step away from the table like a guest
who has had his fill of life, and take your untroubled rest with an
easy mind, you fool? But if everything you have enjoyed has been poured
away and lost, and life is now an offence to you, why do you look to
add more, which will only perish badly again and fall away without
thanks, all of it? Why not rather make an end of your life and your
labour? For there is nothing further that I can contrive and discover
to please you: it is all the same forever. If your body is not yet
withering with the years, and your limbs are not yet worn out and
slack, still everything stays the same, even if you should go on to
outlive all the generations — and still more so if you were never
going to die at all."

What can we answer to that, except that nature is bringing a fair suit
and setting out a true case in what she says?

And if it were an older and more elderly man complaining, lamenting his
own death, poor wretch, more than is fair, would she not have all the
more right to shout at him, and to tell him off in a sharper voice?
"Take your tears away from here, you bottomless pit, and hold in your
complaints. You have used up all the prizes of life, and now you are
withering. But because you always want what is not there and think
nothing of what is in front of you, life has slipped out of your hands
unfinished and unwelcome, and death has stood by your head before you
could get up from the table full and satisfied with things. Now, come
along: let go of everything that does not suit your age, and do it with
an easy mind. Give way. You must."

She would be in the right, I think; she would be right to tell him off
and to scold him. For the old is always thrust out and gives way to the
new, and one thing has to be repaired out of another. Nobody is handed
down into the pit or into black Tartarus. There has to be material so
that the generations to come can grow — and all of them will follow
you when they have finished their lives; and just as much as you, the
generations before have fallen and the ones after will fall. So one
thing will never stop rising out of another. Life is given to nobody to
own outright; to everybody it is given to use.

And look back at how the past stretch of everlasting time before we
were born was nothing at all to us. So nature holds this up in front of
us as a mirror of the time to come after our own death. Is there
anything horrible in it? Does anything about it look sad? Is it not
more free of trouble than any sleep?

And of course all the things they say are down in the depths of Acheron
are here in our own lives. There is no poor Tantalus, numb with an
empty fear, afraid of the great rock hanging in the air over him, as
the story has it. It is in life that an empty dread of the gods presses
hard on mortals, and each of them fears whatever fall of fortune chance
may bring him.

There are no birds getting into Tityos as he lies in Acheron, and they
could not possibly find anything to search out under that great chest
of his through all of everlasting time. However huge the sprawl of body
he stretches out, even if the man covered with his spread limbs not
just nine acres but the whole circle of the earth, he still would not
be able to bear everlasting pain, or to go on supplying food out of his
own body forever. Our Tityos is here: the man lying in love, torn at by
birds, eaten out by anxious anguish, split apart by cares over some
other craving.

Sisyphus, too, is in front of our eyes in this life. He is the man who
has drunk in the wish to ask the people for the rods and the cruel axes
of office, and who always retires beaten and gloomy. To seek power,
which is empty and is never actually handed over, and to keep on
putting up with hard labour for it — this is what it is to shove a
stone with all your strength up an opposing hill, when the stone rolls
straight back down from the very top and makes headlong for the levels
of the flat plain.

And to be forever feeding an ungrateful nature of mind, filling it with
good things and never satisfying it — which is what the seasons of
the year do for us when they come round and bring their fruits and
their various charms, and we are never filled up with the fruits of
life — this, I think, is the story of the girls in the flower of
their years carrying water into a leaky jar, which cannot be filled by
any means at all.

Cerberus, and the Furies, and that lack of light, and Tartarus belching
horrible waves of heat out of his throat: they exist nowhere and
certainly cannot exist. But in life there is a fear of punishment for
bad deeds, a fear as remarkable as the deeds are, and a paying-off for
crime — prison, and the horrible throwing down from the rock, the
floggings, the executioners, the rack, the pitch, the hot metal plate,
the torches. And even where none of these are present, still the mind,
knowing what it has done, is frightened in advance and puts the goads
to itself and burns itself with whips, and it does not see meanwhile
what limit there can be to its troubles or what end there is to be to
its punishments, and it is afraid that the very same things will get
heavier in death. So this, in the end, is how the life of fools becomes
their Acheron here on earth.

Here is something else you can sometimes say to yourself. "Even good
Ancus let the light go from his eyes, and he was a better man than you
in a great many ways, you worthless creature. And since then many other
kings and rulers have gone under, men who gave orders to great nations.
Even the man who once paved a road across the great sea and gave his
legions a way to march over the deep, who taught them to cross the salt
lagoons on foot and treated the roaring of the sea with contempt,
prancing over it with his cavalry — even he had the light taken away
from him and poured his soul out of a dying body. Scipio, the
thunderbolt of war, the terror of Carthage, gave his bones to the earth
just as if he had been the lowest slave in the house. Add to them the
men who discovered the sciences and the graces; add the companions of
the Muses of Helicon, and among them Homer, who alone held the sceptre,
and who is now asleep in the same rest as all the rest of them. And
Democritus, when a ripe old age warned him that the remembering motions
of his mind were slackening, went to meet death of his own accord and
offered up his head to it. Even Epicurus himself died, when the light
of his life had run its course — the man who beat the whole human
race in genius and put all the others out, as the sun of heaven, when
it rises, puts out the stars.

"And you are going to hesitate and resent having to die? You, whose
life is as good as dead while you are still alive and looking at the
world, who wear away the greater part of your time in sleep, and snore
while you are awake, and never stop seeing dreams, and carry about a
mind troubled with an empty dread, and often cannot even find out what
is wrong with you, when you are pressed on every side, drunk and
miserable, by a crowd of worries, and go drifting and wandering about
with a mind that cannot hold still."

If people could learn the causes of it, in the same way that they seem
to feel that there is a weight lying on the mind which wears them out
with its heaviness — if they could learn where it comes from and
where so great a mass of trouble, as it were, in the breast, comes to
be piled up from — they would not live their lives the way we now
mostly see them do: each man not knowing what he wants for himself, and
looking always for a change of place, as if he could put the load down
that way.

Often the man who is sick of being at home goes out of his great house,
and comes straight back again, since he feels no better outside it. He
runs, driving his ponies headlong out to his country place, as if he
were in a hurry to bring help to a burning roof; he yawns the moment he
has touched the threshold of the villa, or drops off heavily to sleep
and looks for forgetfulness, or even hurries back to town and goes to
look at it again. This is how each man runs away from himself; and of
course, as happens, the self is the one thing he cannot escape from. He
clings to it against his will and hates it, because he is a sick man
who does not grasp the cause of his illness. If he saw that clearly,

learn the nature of things, since what is in question here is the state
of everlasting time and not of one single hour — the state in which
mortals must pass the whole of the time that is left after death.

And in the end, what is this bad craving for life, and why is it so
great, that it drives us to shake like this in doubtful dangers? A
fixed end of life certainly stands waiting for mortals, and death
cannot be dodged; we have to go and meet it. Besides, we turn about
among the same things, and we stay among them, and no new pleasure is
hammered out by living on. It is only while the thing we want is out of
reach that it seems to beat everything else; afterwards, once we have
touched it, we want something else, and one and the same thirst holds
us, with our mouths open for life forever. And it is in doubt what
fortune the coming years may bring, or what chance may carry to us, or
what end is standing over us. Nor do we take away one bit from the time
of death by drawing life out, or manage to nibble anything off it, so
that we may be dead for a shorter while. So live on, and bury as many
generations as you like: that everlasting death will still be waiting
for you all the same, and the man who made an end of his life with
today's light will be gone for no shorter a time than the man who died
many months and many years before him.
