Book Six: Storms, Earthquakes, Magnets, and the Plague at Athens
(Part 2 of 3)

For the rest, it is easy from all this to understand how the things the
Greeks named presters — "burners," a name they took straight from the
thing itself — come down out of the sky onto the sea. Sometimes what
looks like a lowered column comes down from the sky onto the sea, and
round it the water boils and seethes, whipped up by heavily blowing
gusts, and any ships caught in that uproar at the time are knocked about
and brought into the worst danger there is.

This happens when the roused force of the wind cannot break through the
cloud it has started on, and presses it down instead, so that it is like
a column let down from the sky onto the sea — gradually, as though
something were being shoved from above by a fist and the thrust of an
arm and stretched out into the waves. Then, when the wind has split the
cloud, its force bursts out from there into the sea and works up an
extraordinary seething among the waves. For the whirl comes down
spinning, and brings that cloud down along with it, pliant body and all;
and as soon as it has forced the loaded cloud right down to the level of
the sea, it suddenly throws its whole self into the water and rouses the
whole sea with an enormous noise, forcing it to boil.

It happens too that the whirl of the wind wraps itself in clouds on its
own account, scraping together the seeds of cloud out of the air, and so
imitates a prester let down from the sky. When this one has come down
onto the land and broken open, it vomits out an immense force of
whirlwind and squall. But since it happens rarely on the whole, and on
land the mountains are bound to get in its way, the same thing is seen
more often over the wide prospect of the sea, under an open sky.

Clouds thicken into being when many bodies, flying about in this space
of the sky up above, have suddenly come together — rougher ones, which
can be held caught in one another even though the couplings are slight.
These first make small clouds form. Then those catch hold of one another
and flock together, and grow by joining, and are carried along by the
winds, until at last a savage storm has got up.

It happens too that the nearer a mountain's peaks are to the sky, the
more steadily its heights smoke with the thick murk of a tawny cloud.
That is because when clouds first form, before the eyes can see them,
thin as they are, the winds are already carrying them and driving them
up to the topmost peaks of the mountain. There at last, once a bigger
crowd of them has gathered and packed close, they can be seen, and at
the same moment they seem to be rising into the upper air out of the
mountain's own summit. For the thing itself, and our own senses, declare
that windy places lie open up there, whenever we climb high mountains.

Besides, that nature lifts a great many bodies off the whole sea is
declared by clothes hung up on the shore, when they take up the clinging
damp. So it looks all the more as though many bodies could rise from the
salt heaving of the sea as well, to swell the clouds; for all moistures
are of one blood.

Besides, we see mists and steam rising from all the rivers and from the
earth itself, and these, squeezed up from there like a breath, are
carried upward and spread their murk under the sky and, gathering little
by little, keep the high clouds supplied. For the heat of the
star-carrying upper air presses down on them as well, and by a sort of
thickening weaves the dark storm clouds under the blue.

It happens too that the bodies which make the clouds and the flying
storm clouds come into this sky of ours from outside. For I have taught
you that their number is countless and that the sum of the deep is
infinite, and I have shown with what speed the atoms fly and how they
are used to crossing an unspeakable space in a moment. So it is no
wonder if, in a short time, storm and darkness often cover sea and land
with such great winds, hanging heavy overhead, since everywhere, through
all the pores of the upper air and, so to speak, through the breathing
holes all round this great world, an exit and an entrance stand provided
for the atoms.

Now then. I will unfold how the rainy moisture thickens in the clouds
high up, and how it is let down onto the lands and falls as a shower.
First I shall win the point that many seeds of water rise together with
the clouds themselves out of all things, and that the two grow in step,
the cloud and the water that is in the cloud, just as our body grows in
step with our blood, and with sweat, and with any other moisture there
is in our limbs.

Clouds often take in a great deal of sea-water besides, like hanging
fleeces of wool, when the winds carry them over the great sea. In the
same way moisture is lifted into the clouds out of all the rivers. And
when the seeds of water have come well together there, many of them, in
many ways, swollen from every side, the packed clouds compete to send
the moisture out in two ways at once: the force of the wind crowds them
together, and the sheer quantity of storm clouds, driven into a bigger
crowd, presses and bears down from above and makes the rain flow out.

Besides, when the clouds are also thinned out by the winds, or broken up
by being struck from above by the heat of the sun, they let their rainy
moisture go and drip, the way wax over a hot fire melts and runs freely.
But a violent downpour comes when the clouds are violently weighed down
by both together, by their own piled mass and by the charge of the wind.
And rains are used to holding on for a long time and hanging about, when
many seeds of water are stirred up, and cloud on cloud and soaking storm
cloud on storm cloud are carried along on top of one another and from
every quarter alike, and when the whole earth steams and breathes its
moisture back out.

At such a time, when the sun's rays have flashed out through the dark
weather facing the spray of the storm clouds, the colour of the rainbow
appears in the black clouds.

As for the other things that grow up above and are made up above, and
everything that thickens in the clouds — all of them, absolutely all:
snow, winds, hail, the freezing frosts, and the great power of ice, that
great hardener of water, that check which reins in the rivers everywhere
as they push on — all this is perfectly easy to find out and to see with
the mind, how each of them happens and why they come about, once you
have properly understood what powers have been given to the atoms.

Now then, take in what the explanation of earthquakes is. And first of
all make yourself think of the earth as full of windy caverns below,
just as it is above, everywhere; and that it carries in its lap many
lakes and many pools, and cliffs and broken rocks; and you must suppose
that many hidden rivers under the earth's back roll their waves with
force, and rocks sunk under them. For the thing itself demands that the
earth be like itself everywhere.

With these things joined and set underneath it, then, the earth trembles
up above, shaken by great collapses, when down below time has undermined
huge caverns. Whole mountains fall, in fact, and from that great sudden
shock the tremors creep out far and wide. And rightly so, since houses
along the road shake all through when a cart of no great weight jars
them, and the things in them jump just as much whenever some bit of the
road jolts the iron-shod rims of the wheels on either side.

It happens too, when a huge clod of earth loosened by age rolls down
into the great wide pools of water, that the wavering earth is tossed as
well by the wave of the water — the way a vessel cannot stand steady
among water unless the liquid inside it has stopped being tossed about
in an uncertain swell.

Besides, when the wind, gathered in the hollow places under the earth,
bears down from one quarter and pushes, straining against the deep
caverns with great strength, the earth leans over the way the force of
the wind presses it. And then the houses built on the earth above lean
and threaten in that same direction, thrust forward, and the more so the
higher each one is raised towards the sky; and the beams are dragged out
and hang over, ready to go. And men are afraid to believe that some
destroying time and disaster is waiting for the nature of the great
world, when they can see so great a mass of lands leaning over.

And if the winds did not draw breath, no force would rein things in or
catch them back from going to their ruin. As it is, because they draw
breath by turns and grow heavy again, and, as if rallying, come back and
then are driven off and give way, the earth threatens collapse more
often than it brings it about. It leans, and then rocks back, and after
tipping forward it takes up its own seat again under its weight. That is
why every building sways, the top more than the middle, the middle more
than the bottom, the bottom hardly at all.

There is this cause too for the same great shaking. When a wind, some
very great force of air, gathered either from outside or out of the
earth itself, has suddenly thrown itself into the hollow places of the
earth, it first roars there among the great caverns in an uproar and is
carried round and round; and afterwards, when its force has been driven
on and lashed up, it bursts out and at the same moment splits the deep
earth and works up a great gaping chasm. That is what happened at Sidon
in Syria, and at Aegium in the Peloponnese, cities which this outrush of
air, and the earthquake that came of it, threw down. And besides those,
many walled towns have fallen in great movements of the land, and many
cities have gone down into the sea and sunk together with their
citizens.

And if it does not burst out, still the charge of the air itself and the
wild force of the wind is dealt out through the earth's many pores like
a shivering fit, and shakes a tremor into it from there, just as when
cold has come right into our joints it shakes us against our will and
makes us tremble and move.

So people flutter in a double terror through the cities. They are afraid
of the roofs above them, and they dread the caverns underneath, in case
the nature of the earth suddenly breaks them open and, pulled apart,
opens its own gaping mouth wide, and, all confused, wants to fill it
with its own ruins. So let them go on thinking, if they like, that
heaven and earth will stay uncorrupted, entrusted to an everlasting
safety. All the same, from time to time the sheer present force of the
danger puts this goad of fear under them from one side or another: that
the earth may suddenly be pulled out from under their feet and carried
down into the pit, and the sum of things follow it, given up utterly,
and the world become nothing but a confused ruin.

To begin with, people wonder that nature does not make the sea bigger,
given that there is such a running-down of waters into it, and that all
the rivers come to it from every quarter. Add the wandering rains and
the flying storms, which sprinkle and soak every sea and every land; add
its own springs. And yet, against the sum of the sea, all of these will
barely amount to the addition of a single drop. So it is less of a
wonder that the great sea does not grow.

Besides, the sun draws off a great part of it with its heat. We see, in
fact, that the sun with its burning rays dries clothes soaked with wet;
and we see that the seas are many and are spread out wide underneath. So
however small a portion of the moisture the sun sips off the surface at
any one place, over so great a space it will still carry off plenty from
the waves.

Then again, the winds too can lift a great part of the moisture by
sweeping the surface of the water, since we very often see the roads
dried by the wind in a single night and the soft mud thickening into a
crust.

Besides, I have taught you that the clouds also lift a great deal of
moisture, taken up from the great level of the sea, and scatter it here
and there over the whole circle of the lands, when it rains on the earth
and the winds carry the clouds along.

Finally, since the earth's body is porous, and since it is joined to the
sea and rings its shores everywhere, water must seep from the salt level
into the lands, just as the moisture comes from the lands into the sea.
For the brine is filtered off, and the material of the water seeps back
and all runs together to the head of the rivers; and from there it comes
back over the lands in a fresh-water column, along the road once cut for
it, where its liquid foot carried the waves down.

Now I will unfold what the explanation is for the fires that breathe out
through the throat of Mount Etna, sometimes in such a whirlwind. For the
storm of flame that rose and took command through the fields of Sicily
was no ordinary disaster, and it turned the faces of the neighbouring
peoples towards it, when they saw all the shining high places of the sky
smoking and sparking and filled their hearts with frightened worry about
what new thing the nature of things was working at.

In matters like these you have to look wide and deep and peer far off in
every direction, so that you remember that the sum of things is
bottomless, and see how tiny a part of the whole sum this one sky of
ours is — how small a fraction, not even as large a part of it as one
man is of the whole earth. If you set that proposition plainly in front
of you and see it plainly, you will stop wondering at a great many
things.

Does any of us wonder if somebody takes a fever into his joints,
gathering with a hot burning, or any other pain of disease through his
limbs? A foot suddenly swells up; a sharp pain often grabs at the teeth,
or gets into the eyes themselves; the holy fire breaks out and burns and
crawls over the body, taking whatever part it has seized and creeping on
through the limbs. And no wonder, since there are seeds of many things,
and this earth and this sky carry enough of disease and harm for an
immense power of illness to grow out of. So we have to suppose that to
the whole sky and the whole earth everything is supplied in plenty out
of the infinite: enough for the earth to be shaken and suddenly moved,
for a fast whirlwind to run across the sea and the lands, for the fire
of Etna to overflow, for the sky to burst into flame. For that happens
too: the shining high places of the sky catch fire, and rainstorms come
with a heavier gathering, when the seeds of water happen to have brought
themselves together that way.

"But the roiling blaze of that burning is far too enormous." Of course
it is. And a river seems enormous to a man who has never seen a bigger
one before; a tree seems enormous, and a man; and of every kind whatever
a man has seen the largest of, that is what he imagines to be enormous —
when in fact all of them together, sky and earth and sea thrown in, are
nothing against the whole sum of the total.

Now, though, I will unfold the ways in which that flame is suddenly
stirred up and blows out of the vast furnaces of Etna. First, the nature
of the whole mountain is hollow underneath, propped up nearly everywhere
on caverns of stone. And in all the caves there is wind and air; for
wind is made when air has been roused up by being stirred about. When
this air has got hot through and, raging, has made all the rocks around
it hot wherever it touches them, and the earth as well, and has struck
out of them a hot fire with swift flames, it lifts itself up and hurls
itself high, straight out through the throat. So it carries its blaze a
long way, and scatters its ash a long way, and rolls out smoke in a
thick murk, and at the same time shoves out rocks of astonishing weight.
Leave yourself in no doubt that this is the turbulent force of the air.

Besides, over a great stretch the sea at the roots of that mountain
breaks its waves and sucks its surf back again. From this sea caverns
run in underneath, right up to the mountain's high throat. You have to
admit that the way in goes through here, and the plain fact forces us to
say that water and air get deep in from the open sea and blow out again,
and so heave up the flame and throw up rocks and raise clouds of sand.
For at the very summit there are what the people there call bowls; what
we call them is throats and mouths.

There are also a number of things for which it is not enough to state
one cause: you must state several, though only one of them is in fact
the true one. Suppose you were to catch sight of a man's lifeless body
lying some way off. It would be right to name all the causes of death,
so that the one cause of his death might be named among them. You could
not prove that he had died by the sword, or by cold, or by disease, or,
as it might be, by poison; but we know that what happened to him was
something of this kind. And we have to talk the same way in a great many
other matters.

The Nile rises in summer and floods the fields, the only river in the
world that does it, the river of the whole of Egypt. It waters Egypt
right through the middle of the heat, and it may be because in summer
the north winds are set against its mouths — the winds that at that
season of the year are called the Etesians — and, blowing against the
stream, hold it back, and by forcing its waters upstream fill it up and
make it stand. For there is no doubt that these blasts, driven as they
are from the cold stars of the pole, are carried against the current,
while the river itself comes up from the south, out of the quarter that
carries the heat, rising deep inland among the black generations of men,
whose colour is baked through, out of the very middle region of the day.

It may also be that a great heap of sand piled up by the waves against
it blocks its mouths, when the sea, stirred up by the winds, drives the
sand inland; and by that the river's way out is less free, and the
downhill charge of its waters is less as well.

It may happen too that the rains at its head are perhaps heavier at that
season, because the Etesian blasts of the north winds are then throwing
all the clouds into those parts. Naturally, once they have gathered
there, driven out towards the middle region of the day, the clouds are
at last crowded against the high mountains and packed together and
pressed hard. Or perhaps the river swells far inland from the high
mountains of the Ethiopians, when the sun, going over everything, drives
the white snows with its melting rays to come down into the plains.

Now then, I will unfold for you what those places and lakes are that go
by the name of Avernus, and what sort of nature they are furnished with.
First, the fact that they are called Avernian — birdless — was laid on
them from the thing itself, because they are set against all birds. For
when the birds have come in their flight to a spot straight over those
places, they forget the rowing of their wings, they let their sails go
slack, and they fall headlong, their soft necks poured out, down to the
earth if the nature of the ground happens to be like that, or into the
water if an Avernian lake happens to lie beneath.

There is such a place near Cumae, where the mountains are filled with
sharp sulphur and smoke, swollen with hot springs. There is another
inside the walls of Athens, on the very peak of the citadel, beside the
temple of Pallas Tritonis, the kindly one, where the hoarse crows never
bring their bodies on their wings, not even when the altars are smoking
with offerings. That is how completely they keep away — and not out of
fear of Pallas's sharp anger over the crow that once kept watch on her
secret and carried the tale to her, as the poets of the Greeks have sung
it, but because the nature of the place does the work, all by itself.

In Syria too there is said to be a place of the same kind to be seen,
where even four-footed animals, as soon as they have set their first
steps in it, are forced down by its sheer power and collapse, as if they
had suddenly been slaughtered as an offering to the gods below.

All these things are done by natural reasoning, and it is plain from
what causes they come about. So nobody should believe that this is where
the gate of Orcus is, or go on from there to suppose that the gods below
drag souls down from here to the shores of Acheron, the way that
swift-footed stags are so often thought to draw the crawling generations
of wild snakes out of their hiding places with their nostrils. Take in
how far all that is from a true account; for now I am going to try to
speak about the fact itself.

To begin with, I say this, which I have often said before as well: there
are shapes of things of every kind in the earth. Many of them are
life-giving and good for food, and many can strike disease into us and
hurry death on. And we have shown before that different things suit
different living creatures for the business of life, because the nature
of each is unlike the rest, and so are their textures and their basic
shapes.

Many hostile things travel in through the ears; many work their way in
through the nostrils themselves, dangerous and rough to the touch. There
are not a few to be avoided by touch, and not a few to be run from by
sight, and some that are bitter to the taste.

Then you can see how many things there are that are sharply hostile to a
man's senses, filthy and hard to bear. In the first place, certain trees
have been given a shade so heavy that they often bring on headaches, if
a man has lain down under them, stretched out on the grass. And there is
a tree on the great mountains of Helicon whose flower has a foul smell,
and it is used to killing a man with it.

All these things rise out of the earth for one reason: the earth carries
the seeds of many things mixed together in many ways, and hands them
over separately. And when a lamp that has just been put out at night
hits the nostrils with its sharp reek, it puts to sleep on the spot the
man who is subject to falling down and foaming at the mouth. A woman
drowses back in her chair at the heavy smell of castor, and the bright
work slips out of her soft hands, if she has smelled it at the time when
her monthly flow is released. And there are many things besides that
loosen the limbs all through the frame and make them faint, and unsettle
the soul in its seat inside.

Finally, if you linger in a hot bath when you are too full, and wash
there, how easily it happens that you go down in the middle of the tub
of boiling water! And how easily the heavy force and smell of charcoal
works its way into the brain, unless we have taken water beforehand! But
when a burning fever has got hold of a man's limbs and mastered them,
then the smell of wine is like a deadly blow.

Do you not see how sulphur is bred in the earth itself, and how bitumen
thickens with a foul smell? And where men follow the veins of silver and
gold, searching the earth's hidden places deep down with iron, what
smells does Scaptensula breathe out below? And what harm comes out of
the gold mines! What faces they give men, what colours! Do you not see,
or hear, how quickly those men are apt to die, and how the store of life
fails in them, whom the great force of necessity keeps at such work? So
the earth steams up all these vapours and breathes them out into the
open and into the parts of the sky that lie exposed.

In the same way the Avernian places must send up a death-carrying force
to birds, one that rises from the earth into the air and poisons a
certain part of the space of the sky. As soon as a bird has been carried
there on its wings, it is stopped, caught by a poison it cannot see, so
that it drops straight down at the place where the vapour rises. And
when it has fallen there, that same force of the vapour takes away what
is left of its life from all its limbs. For it first stirs up a sort of
dizziness in them; and afterwards, once they have fallen right into the
very springs of the poison, they have to vomit up their life there as
well, because there is so great a supply of harm all round them.

It also happens sometimes that this force and vapour of Avernus breaks
up the air lying between the birds and the ground, so that the place is
left very nearly empty. And when the birds have come in their flight
straight over that place, at once the beat of their wings goes lame in
the emptiness, and every effort of the wings on both sides is betrayed.
Here, when they can neither lean on the air nor hold themselves up on
their wings, of course nature forces them to slip down to the earth by
their weight; and lying there through what is by now almost empty void,
they scatter their souls out through all the pores of their bodies.
