Book Four: The Senses, Sight, and Love
(Part 3 of 3)

There is nothing to wonder at, either, in the fact that the very nature
of the body, in every living creature, goes looking for food. I have
shown you that bodies stream away and depart from things in many ways
and in great numbers; but the most of them must come away from living
creatures, because they are worked hard by motion. A great many are
breathed out through the mouth when a tired animal is panting, and a
great many are squeezed up from deep inside and carried off in sweat.
So by all this the body grows thin, and its whole nature is undermined
from below, and pain follows on that. That is why food is taken: to
prop up the tottering limbs, and, once it has been given out among
them, to make their strength again, and to stop up the craving to eat
which stands open all through the limbs and the veins. Liquid, in the
same way, goes off into all the places that call for liquid; and the
many packed bodies of heat, which set our stomachs on fire, are
scattered by the arriving drink and put out as you put out a fire, so
that dry heat can no longer burn the limbs. That is how the panting
thirst is washed out of our body, and that is how the starved craving
is filled up.

Now I will tell you how it comes about that we can take steps forward
when we want to, and why we have been given the power to move our
limbs, and what it is that has got into the habit of shoving this whole
great load of our body along. Take in what I say. I say that first the
images of walking fall on our mind and strike the mind, as I said
earlier. Then the will is formed; for nobody begins to do anything at
all before the mind has seen in advance what it wants, and what it sees
in advance is fixed by the image of that thing. So when the mind stirs
itself in such a way that it wants to go and step forward, it strikes
at once the force of the soul which is sown through the whole body,
through the limbs and joints; and that is easy to do, since the soul is
held joined with it. Then the soul in its turn strikes the body, and so
the whole mass, little by little, is shoved along and moved. Besides,
at such a time the body grows loose in its texture too, and air, being
of course what it always is, forever on the move, comes in through the
opened pores and works its way in in quantity and is sprinkled into
every one of the small parts of the body. So by these two things,
working from two directions, the body is carried along as a ship is
carried by sails and wind.

And there is nothing to marvel at in this either, that bodies so tiny
can swing so great a body round and turn our whole weight about; for
the wind, thin as it is, with its fine body, drives and pushes a great
ship of great bulk, and one hand steers it however hard it is going,
and one rudder swings it wherever you like; and a machine, by its
pulleys and drums, sets many things of great weight moving and lifts
them with a light effort.

Now, by what means sleep floods rest through the limbs and unties the
mind's worries out of the breast: I would rather set it out for you in
a few sweet-spoken verses than in many, as the small song of the swan
is better than the screaming of cranes scattered among the high clouds
of the south wind. Give me a fine ear and a sharp mind. Do not deny
that what I am going to say can happen, and do not walk off with your
heart pushing the true words back, when the fault is your own and you
cannot see it.

To begin with, sleep comes about when the force of the soul has been
pulled apart through the limbs, and part of it has been thrown out and
gone away, and part has been crowded back and gone deeper in. Only then
do the limbs come undone and go slack. There is no doubt that this
sense of ours is the work of the soul; so when sleep blocks that sense
from existing, we have to suppose that the soul has been thrown into
disorder and thrown out — not the whole of it, though, or the body
would lie soaked in the everlasting cold of death. If no part of the
soul were left lurking among the limbs, as fire lurks buried under a
great deal of ash, where could sense be suddenly lit again from, all
through the limbs, in the way flame rises out of a hidden fire?

But I will untangle for you by what means this strange new state is
brought about, and what the soul can be disturbed by and the body go
slack. See to it that I am not pouring my words out to the winds.

First, the body on its outer part must be beaten, and struck by the
repeated blows of the air, since it is touched along its border by the
gusts next to it; and that is why nearly all things are covered with
hide, or else with shells, or with hard skin, or with bark. This same
air beats the inner part too, in breathing creatures, when the breath
is drawn in and blown out again. So, since the body is thrashed on both
sides at once, and since the blows come through the small pores right
in to the first parts and first atoms of our body, a kind of collapse
comes about in us, little by little, all through the limbs.
For the arrangements of the atoms of the body and of the mind are
thrown into confusion. It comes about that part of the soul is then
drawn out of us, and part retreats and hides away inside, and part,
dragged apart through the limbs, cannot be joined together with itself
or take and give motion in return; for nature fences off the meetings
and the roads between them. So sense goes away deep inside as the
motions change. And since there is nothing left, so to speak, to prop
the limbs up, the body goes weak and all the limbs go slack; the arms
and the eyelids drop, and often, when you are lying down, even the
backs of your knees give way beneath you and let their strength go.

Then again, sleep follows food, because food does the very same thing
the air does, while it is being distributed into all the veins. And by
far the heaviest sleep is the one you take when you are full or tired,
because that is when the greatest number of bodies are thrown into
confusion, bruised by hard work. In the same way it comes about that
part of the soul is driven deeper, and more of it is thrown outside,
and what is left inside is more divided against itself and more pulled
apart.

Whatever pursuit a man is held tight by and sticks to, whatever we have
spent a long time over before, whatever it was that the mind was more
taken up with, that is mostly what we seem to be busy with in our
sleep. Lawyers plead their cases and put statutes together; generals
fight and go into battle; sailors carry on the running war they have
with the winds; and I myself am doing this work, forever hunting for
the nature of things and, when I have found it, setting it down in the
writing of my own country. That is how the other pursuits and skills
generally seem to hold men's minds in sleep and make fools of them. And
whenever people have given days on end of unbroken attention to the
games, we mostly see that, even when they have stopped taking those
games in with their senses, there are still roads left standing open in
the mind for the same images of things to come along by. So for many
days afterwards the same sights float in front of their eyes, so that
even wide awake they think they can see the dancers moving their supple
limbs, and can take in with their ears the clear song of the lyre and
its talking strings, and can see the same crowd on the benches and all
the varied splendours of the stage shining at once.

So much difference does it make what a man's enthusiasm and his will
are set on, and what things he has been in the habit of working at —
and not men only, but every kind of animal. You will see strong horses,
when their limbs are lying still, sweating in their sleep all the same,
and blowing hard again and again, and straining every muscle as though
they were going for the prize, or as though the starting gates had been
thrown open. And the dogs of hunting men, in their soft rest, often
toss their legs suddenly and suddenly let out cries and pull the air in
through their noses again and again, as though they were holding a wild
animal's tracks that they had found; and once woken they often go
chasing the empty images of stags, as though they saw them given over
to running away, until they shake the mistake off and come back to
themselves. But the fawning breed of puppies that are used to the house
are all urgency to shake themselves and snatch their bodies up off the
ground, exactly as if they were looking at faces and mouths they did
not know. And the rougher the breed a creature comes of, the more it is
bound to rage in the same way in its sleep.

But the various birds take flight, and trouble the sacred groves of the
gods suddenly with their wings in the night time, when in their gentle
sleep they have seen hawks flying and giving chase and offering them
battle and a fight.

Then again, the minds of men, which carry out great things by great
motions, often do and carry through the same things in sleep. They take
kings' towns by storm, they are captured themselves, they mix in
battle, they raise a shout as though their throats were being cut on
the spot. Many fight it out and let out groans at the pain, and, as if
they were being chewed by the bite of a panther or a savage lion, they
fill everything round them with great cries. Many talk in their sleep
about great matters, and have very often been the evidence that
convicted them of their own doing. Many meet their death. Many are
terrified as though they were falling headlong with their whole body
from high mountains to the ground, and come out of sleep as though out
of their minds, and hardly come back to themselves, shaken by the
heaving of their bodies. A thirsty man, in the same way, sits down
beside a river or a pleasant spring and all but takes the whole stream
down his throat. Clean children, tied fast in sleep beside a basin or a
cut-down jar, often think they are lifting their clothes, and pour out
the strained water of their whole body, and soak the Babylonian
coverings in all their magnificent brightness.

And there are those into whose channels the seed of manhood is first
working its way, when the day itself has ripened it in their limbs.
Images come to them from outside, out of some body or other, messengers
of a splendid face and a lovely colour, and stir and irritate the
places that are now swollen with a great deal of seed, so that often,
as though the whole thing had actually been carried through, they pour
out the great flood of the stream and stain their clothing.

That seed, as I said, is stirred in us when the years of full growth
first put strength into the limbs. Different things stir and provoke
different things, and it is the force of a human being, and only that,
which draws human seed out of a man. As soon as it is driven out of its
own places and comes away, it goes off through the limbs and joints out
of the whole body, gathering into fixed regions of the sinews, and it
stirs up at once the very parts of the body that do the begetting.
Those places, irritated, swell with the seed, and there comes the will
to throw it out towards whatever the terrible lust is straining after.
It goads him on, irritating the places that are swollen with a great
deal of seed; and the body makes for the body that the mind has its
wound of love from. For as a rule everyone falls towards their wound,
and the blood spurts out in the direction the blow struck us from, and
if the enemy is at close quarters the red liquid covers him. So a man
who takes a blow from Venus's weapons — whether it is a boy with
girlish limbs who throws the spear at him, or a woman shooting love out
of her whole body — strains towards the place he is struck from, and is
desperate to come together with it and to throw into that body the
liquid drawn out of his own. Because his speechless craving foretells
the pleasure.

This is Venus for us; this is where the name of Love comes from; this
is where that drop of Venus's sweetness first dripped into the heart,
with cold care coming in after it. For if the thing you love is away
from you, its images are there all the same, and the sweet name keeps
floating about in your ears. But the right thing is to run away from
those images, and to frighten off from yourself whatever feeds your
love, and to turn your mind elsewhere, and to throw the collected
liquid into any body at all, rather than to hold it back once it has
been turned towards the love of one person, and so store up care for
yourself and pain that is certain to come. The sore lives on and gets
old by being fed; every day the madness swells and the misery gets
heavier — unless you unsettle the first wounds with new blows, and
treat them while they are still fresh by wandering after a wandering
Venus, or unless you can lead the motions of your mind off somewhere
else.

And the man who keeps away from love is not going without the fruit of
Venus. He takes instead the advantages of it that come with no penalty.
For the pleasure of it is certainly purer for people in their right
minds than it is for the wretched. In the very moment of getting what
they want, the heat of lovers tosses about, uncertain and astray, and
they cannot make up their minds what to enjoy first with their eyes and
their hands. What they went for they squeeze hard, and they hurt the
body, and often they drive their teeth into the lips and press their
mouths on it — because the pleasure is not pure, and there are goads
underneath urging them to hurt the very thing, whatever it is, that
these seeds of madness rise up from. But Venus lightly breaks the
punishment in the middle of the love, and the coaxing pleasure mixed in
with it reins the biting back; because in this there is a hope, that
the flame can be put out by the very body it took fire from. Nature
protests that the whole opposite is the case. This is the one thing
where the more of it we have, the more the heart burns with the
terrible longing. Food and drink are taken up inside the limbs; and
since they can occupy fixed places, the craving for water and for bread
is easily filled. But out of a human face and a lovely colour nothing
is given to the body to enjoy except thin images — and a poor little
hope that is, and the wind often snatches it away.

It is like a thirsty man who tries to drink in his sleep and gets no
water that could put out the fire in his limbs; he goes for images of
liquid and works at it for nothing, and stays thirsty in the middle of
a rushing river even while he drinks. In the same way, in love, Venus
makes fools of lovers with images. They cannot fill themselves by
looking at the body in front of them, and they cannot rub anything off
those tender limbs with their hands, straying uncertainly all over the
body. And in the end, when they have brought their limbs together and
are enjoying the flower of youth, when the body is already promising
itself its delights and Venus is at the point of sowing the woman's
fields, they fasten on the body greedily and join the wet of their
mouths and breathe into each other, pressing teeth on mouths — and all
for nothing, since they cannot rub anything off it, and cannot get
inside and go away into that body with the whole of their own. For
sometimes they seem to want to do exactly that, and to be fighting to
do it. So greedily do they hang in the fastenings of Venus, while their
limbs go weak and melt under the force of the pleasure. At last, when
the gathered longing has burst out of the sinews, there is a short
pause for a little while in the violent heat. Then the same madness
comes back and the old frenzy returns, when they cast about for what it
is they want to reach themselves, and cannot find any device that will
beat that trouble down. So uncertain are they, wasting away with a
hidden wound.

Add to this that they use up their strength and are worn out by the
labour. Add to this that a man's life is lived under somebody else's
nod. His duties go slack, his good name falls sick and staggers.
Meanwhile his property slides away and turns into Babylonian perfumes;
lovely Sicyonian shoes laugh on her feet; and of course great emeralds
with their green light are set in gold, and the sea-coloured dress is
worn out by constant use and drinks up the sweat of Venus. The good
things his fathers earned turn into headbands and turbans, and
sometimes into a Greek gown and stuffs from Elis and Ceos. Banquets are
got ready, with rare coverings and rare food, and games, and cup after
cup, and perfumes and crowns and garlands — all for nothing, since out
of the middle of the fountain of charms something bitter rises up which
chokes him among the very flowers: either because his own mind, knowing
what it is doing, bites him for spending his life in idleness and going
to ruin in brothels, or because she has thrown out some word and left
it standing in doubt, and it sticks in his hungry heart and lives there
like a fire, or because he thinks she is casting her eyes about too
much and looking at somebody else, and sees the traces of a laugh in
her face.

And these troubles are found in a love that is his own and going
perfectly well. In love that is crossed and has nothing to hope for,
the troubles are ones you could take hold of with your eyes shut, and
there is no counting them. So it is better to keep watch beforehand, in
the way I have taught you, and to take care not to be drawn in. To
avoid being thrown into the snares of love is not so hard as it is to
get out again once you are caught in the nets themselves, and to break
through the strong knots of Venus. And yet, even tangled and tripped
up, you could still get away from the danger, unless you get in your
own way and stand in front of yourself, and unless you begin by passing
over all the faults of mind, and all the faults of body, that belong to
the woman you prefer and want. Because that is generally what men do,
blind with longing, and they hand her advantages that are not really
hers. That is how we see women who are crooked in a hundred ways, and
ugly, kept as darlings and flourishing in the highest honour. And one
man laughs at another and tells him to make his peace with Venus, since
he is being knocked about by a foul love — and the poor fool very often
does not look at his own troubles, which are the biggest of all.

The dark one is melichrus, honey-coloured. The dirty and rank one is
acosmos, careless of her looks. The grey-eyed one is a Palladion, a
little Athena. The stringy, wooden one is a dorcas, a gazelle. The tiny
one, the dwarf, is chariton mia, one of the Graces, all pure wit. The
huge and monstrous one is cataplexis, a knockout, and full of dignity.
She stammers and cannot get a word out: she traulizei, she has a sweet
lisp. The one who cannot speak at all is modest. The fiery, hateful
chatterbox becomes a Lampadion, a little torch. She turns into an
ischnon eromenion, a slender darling, when she is too thin to stay
alive; and when she is nearly dead of a cough she is rhadine, willowy.
The overgrown one with the great breasts is Ceres herself with Bacchus
at the breast. The snub-nosed one is a Silena, a she-satyr. The
thick-lipped one is philema, all kiss. It would take a long time if I
tried to go through the rest of this kind.

But let her face have all the glory you like, and let the force of
Venus rise out of every one of her limbs. There are others, all the
same. We lived without her before now, all the same. She does the same
things the ugly one does, and we know she does, all the same, and
smokes her own wretched self with foul perfumes, while her maids run a
long way off and giggle behind their hands. And the lover, shut out and
in tears, often covers her doorstep with flowers and garlands, and rubs
marjoram oil on her proud doorposts, and presses his kisses on the
doors, poor man. Let him be let in at last, and let one single breath
of air meet him as he comes; he would be looking for respectable
excuses to leave, and the complaint he had worked up for so long and
drawn from so deep would fall flat, and he would condemn himself for a
fool on the spot, seeing that he had allowed her more than it is right
to grant to any mortal woman. And this is not lost on our Venuses. All
the more, then, they take enormous trouble to hide everything that goes
on backstage of life from the men they want to keep hold of and to have
bound down in love — and for nothing, since you can still drag it all
into the light with your mind and look into all the laughing; and if
she has a fine mind and is not hateful, you can pass it over in your
turn and make an allowance for human things.

And a woman does not always sigh with a pretended love, when she has
her arms round a man and joins her body to his and holds him and makes
the kisses wet with sucking lips. Often she does it from the heart,
and, looking for delights that are shared, she urges him on to run the
course of love right through. There is no other way birds and cattle
and wild animals and flocks and mares could give in to the males,
unless it were that their own nature is on heat, and burning, and full
to overflowing, and glad to take on the Venus of the ones that mount
them. Do you not see, too, that those whom a shared pleasure has bound
are tortured in their common bonds? How often in the crossroads dogs
that are wild to get apart pull in opposite directions with all their
strength, while the whole time they are stuck fast in the strong
fastenings of Venus? They would never do that unless they knew the
shared delights that could throw them into the trap and hold them tied
there. So again and again, as I say, the pleasure belongs to both.

And when, in the mixing of the seed, the woman's force has beaten the
man's by a sudden effort and caught it, then the children come out like
their mothers, from the mother's seed, as from the father's seed they
come out like their fathers. But those you see with the shape of both,
blending the faces of their parents side by side, grow up out of the
father's body and the mother's blood, when the seeds have been stirred
up through the limbs by the goads of Venus and a heat breathing in both
of them at once has driven them together head-on, and neither of them
has won and neither has been beaten. It happens sometimes, too, that
children can come out like their grandparents, and often bring back the
shapes of great-grandparents, because the parents often carry many
atoms hidden in their bodies, mixed in many ways, handed down by father
to father from the root of the family. Out of that Venus brings out
shapes by a throw that goes different ways, and brings back the faces
and the voices and the hair of the ancestors; since these things come
from a fixed seed no more and no less than our own faces and bodies and
limbs do. And the female line rises out of the father's seed, and males
come into being created out of the mother's body; for a birth always
consists of a double seed, and whichever of the two the created thing
is more like, it has more than an equal share from that one — which you
can see for yourself, whether the offspring is male or female in
origin.

And it is not that any divine power keeps the seed of begetting from a
man, so that he is never called father by sweet children and has to see
his life out in a childless Venus. That is what most people think, and
in their gloom they sprinkle the altars with a great deal of blood and
burn gifts on the high platforms, to make their wives heavy with plenty
of seed. They wear out the power of the gods and the sacred lots for
nothing. Some men are childless because the seed is too thick, and
others, the other way about, because it is more liquid and thin than it
ought to be. Because the thin cannot fasten itself and hold on to the
places, it slips away at once and comes back and goes off without
result. And where a seed thicker than it should be is sent out, in too
solid a state, either it does not fly out with a long enough spurt, or
it cannot get into the places properly, or, once it has got in, it
mixes only with difficulty with the woman's seed. For the harmonies of
Venus turn out to make a great deal of difference. Some men fill some
women more easily, and some women take up the weight more readily from
some men and grow heavy with it. Many women have been childless before,
through several marriages, and afterwards have found men from whom they
could take up children all the same, and grow rich with a sweet birth.
And for men whose fertile wives at home have often been unable to bear
for them, a matching nature has been found for them too, so that they
could fortify their old age with children. It makes so much difference
that seeds should be able to be mixed with seeds in a way fit for
begetting, and that the thick should meet the liquid and the liquid the
thick.

And in this it matters what diet a life is kept up on; for some things
make the seed thicken in the limbs, and others, the other way about,
thin it out and waste it away. And the ways the coaxing pleasure itself
is handled — that matters enormously too. For it is generally thought
that wives conceive more readily in the manner of wild animals, after
the fashion of the four-footed kinds, because in that way the seeds can
take their places, with the breast laid down and the loins raised. And
wives have no need at all of soft movements. For a woman keeps herself
from conceiving and fights against it if, in her gladness, she takes on
the man's Venus with her own hips and stirs up waves with her whole
breast gone boneless; because that throws the furrow out of the
straight line and the track of the ploughshare, and turns the spurt of
the seed away from the places. And prostitutes have made a habit of
moving that way for their own reasons: not to be filled up over and
over and lie heavy with child, and at the same time to make Venus more
elegant for the men. Our wives plainly have no need of that.

And it is not by divine action or by the arrows of Venus, either, when
a woman of poorer looks is loved. Sometimes the woman herself brings it
about, by the things she does, by her obliging ways and her neat and
well-kept body, so that she easily gets you used to spending your life
with her. And beyond that, habit works love up. Whatever is struck by a
repeated blow, however lightly, is beaten in the long run all the same,
and gives way. Do you not see, too, that drops of water falling on
rocks bore through the rocks in the long run?
