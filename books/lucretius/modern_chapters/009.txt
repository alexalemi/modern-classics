Book Five: The World, Its Making, and the Rise of Mankind
(Part 1 of 3)

Who is strong enough in the chest to build a song worth the majesty
of the nature of things and worth these discoveries? Who is master
enough of words to frame the praise that man deserves, who left us
such prizes, won and hunted down by his own mind? Nobody, I think,
born of a mortal body. Because if I am to speak as the known majesty
of the subject itself demands, then he was a god. A god, famous
Memmius: the man who first found that plan of life which is now
called wisdom, and who by his skill lifted life out of such great
waves and such great darkness and set it down in water so calm and
light so clear.

Set the old divine discoveries of others beside that. Ceres, they
say, gave mortals grain, and Bacchus the juice of the liquid the vine
begets. And yet life could go on without those things, and there are
peoples said to be living without them even now. But life could not
be lived well without a mind that had been cleaned out. So he seems
to us, with better reason, to be our god: the man from whom the sweet
comforts of life, spread now through great nations, soothe men's
minds.

And if you think the deeds of Hercules beat that, you will be carried
much further from true reasoning. What harm would that great gaping
mouth of the Nemean lion do us now, or the bristling boar of Arcadia?
What real trouble could the birds that lived at Stymphalus give us?
Or, for that matter, the bull of Crete, or the plague of Lerna, the
hydra fenced round with poisonous snakes? Or the three-chested
strength of triple Geryon, or the horses of Diomedes breathing fire
out of their nostrils, off in Thrace by the Bistonian country and
Ismara? Or the snake that guards the golden shining apples of the
Hesperides, rough, with a bitter stare, huge in body, wound round the
tree trunk? What harm could that do us out by the Atlantic shore and
the grim stretches of sea, where none of our people goes and no
foreigner dares to? All the other monsters of that kind that were
killed off: if they had not been beaten, what damage could they be
doing alive? None, I think. The earth is crawling with wild animals
even now, up to the point of glut, and packed full of shivering
terror through the groves and the great mountains and the deep
woods. But it is generally in our own power to keep away from those
places.

Whereas if the chest is not cleaned out, what battles and dangers we
have to let into ourselves, whether we want them or not! How the
sharp cares of appetite tear a man apart and leave him raw, and the
fears with them! And pride, filth, insolence — what disasters they
bring off! What about extravagance and idleness? So the man who put
all of that down and drove it out of the mind with words rather than
weapons: will it not be fitting to think a man like that worth
counting among the gods? Especially since he was in the habit of
saying a great many good things, and saying them in a divine way,
about the immortal gods themselves, and of unfolding the whole nature
of things in what he said.

I have set my feet in his footprints, and while I follow his
reasoning through and teach in words the laws of nature under which
each thing has been created, and how it has to stay under them and
cannot undo the strong laws of time, I found first of all, in the
class of mortal things, that the nature of the mind consists of a
body that is born, and cannot last unhurt through a great stretch of
time, and that it is images which regularly deceive the mind in
sleep, when we seem to see somebody whom life has left. As for the
rest, the order of the argument has now brought me to this point: I
have to give an account of how the world consists of a mortal body
and was also born; and in what ways that meeting of matter founded
earth, sky, sea, stars, sun and the ball of the moon; then what
living creatures came up out of the earth, and which have never at
any time been born; and in what way the human race began to use names
for things among themselves, with speech that varied; and in what
ways that fear of the gods worked its way into people's chests, which
now, all round the circle of the lands, keeps holy the shrines, the
lakes, the groves, the altars and the images of the gods.

Besides that, I shall untangle by what force the steering hand of
nature bends the sun through its courses and the moon through her
journeys. We must not think, after all, that these run their yearly
rounds freely between sky and earth, of their own accord, obliging us
by making the crops and the living creatures grow, or that they are
rolled along by any plan of the gods. Because even people who have
learned properly that the gods pass their time free of care, if all
the same they wonder in the meantime by what principle things can be
carried on, above all in the things we see overhead in the regions of
the upper air, are carried back to the old religions and take on
harsh masters, whom the poor creatures believe can do anything,
because they do not know what can be and what cannot, and on what
principle each thing has its power limited, and a boundary stone
driven deep.

Now for the rest, and I shall not hold you up with more promises. To
begin with, look at the seas and the lands and the sky. Their triple
nature, Memmius, their three bodies, three kinds of thing so unlike
each other, three fabrics woven like that: one day will hand them all
over to destruction, and the mass and machinery of the world, held up
through so many years, will come down in ruins.

I am not blind to how new and strange it is for a mind to take in the
coming destruction of sky and earth, and how hard it will be for me
to win the argument in words. It always happens like that when you
bring an unfamiliar thing to somebody's ears and cannot put it in
front of their eyes or into their hands — and the road of sight and
touch is the paved road, the one that carries belief straight into
the human chest and the regions of the mind. Still, I shall speak.
The event itself may give my words credit, and you may see everything
shaken to pieces in a short space of time by earthquakes coming up
from below. May the steering hand of fortune turn that far away from
us, and may it be reasoning rather than the event itself that
persuades you that everything can be beaten and collapse with a
horrible crash.

Before I set out on this, and give you oracles about the matter in a
holier way and with far more certain reasoning than the Pythian
priestess who prophesies from Apollo's tripod and laurel, I shall
unfold a good many comforts for you in learned words. I do not want
you, held back by the bridle of religion, to think that the lands and
the sun and the sky, the sea, the stars, the moon, have to stay
everlasting because their body is divine — and then to think it right
that everyone who shakes the walls of this world by their reasoning
should pay the penalty for a monstrous crime, like the Giants,
because they want to put out the shining sun of heaven and are
branding immortal things with mortal talk. These things are so far
from any divine power, and so unworthy of being counted among the
gods, that they are better taken as giving you an idea of what a
thing is like when it has no living motion and no sense in it.

For it simply is not the case that the nature of the mind and its
judgment can be thought to exist with any body you please. A tree
cannot exist in the upper air, clouds cannot exist in the salt sea,
fish cannot live in the fields, there is no blood in wood and no sap
in stones: where each thing can grow and have its place is fixed and
laid out. In the same way the nature of the mind cannot come up on
its own without a body, and cannot be far away from sinews and blood.
If it could, then the force of the mind itself could much sooner be
in the head or the shoulders or the bottoms of the heels, and would
regularly be born in whatever part you like — but at least it would
stay in the same man and the same vessel. As it is, since even in our
own body it is fixed and plainly laid out where the soul and the mind
can each be and grow separately, we have to deny all the more that
they could last entirely outside a body and an animal shape, in the
crumbling clods of the earth, or in the fire of the sun, or in water,
or in the high shores of the upper air. So these things are not
furnished with a divine sense, since they cannot be alive in the way
a living thing is.

Here is something else you cannot believe: that there are holy seats
of the gods in any parts of the world. The nature of the gods is
thin, and so far removed from our senses that it is barely seen by
the mind's thinking. Since it slips away from the touch and the blow
of our hands, it cannot come in contact with anything that is
touchable to us at all, because a thing cannot touch which itself may
not be touched. So their dwellings too must be unlike our dwellings,
and thin, matching their bodies. I shall prove all that to you later
at length.

Then, to say that the gods wanted to prepare this splendid nature of
the world for the sake of human beings, and that it is therefore
right to praise their praiseworthy work and to think it everlasting
and immortal, and that it is a crime ever to shake with any force out
of its place what was founded for the peoples of mankind for all time
by the gods' ancient plan, or to harass it with words and turn it
upside down from the bottom — to invent all that, Memmius, and to
pile more of the same kind on top of it, is idiocy.

What benefit could our gratitude confer on beings who are immortal
and blessed, that they should undertake to manage anything for our
sake? What new thing, after so long, could tempt them, when they had
been at rest, into wanting to change their old way of life? A man
seems bound to enjoy new things if the old ones hurt him; but for one
who has met with nothing painful in the time gone by, while he was
passing his life beautifully, what could ever set alight a love of
novelty in a being like that? And what harm was it to us not to have
been created? Were our lives lying in darkness and grief, I suppose,
until the birth-dawn of things broke? Anyone who has once been born
must want to stay in life so long as coaxing pleasure keeps him
there. But for a man who has never tasted the love of life and never
been in the count of things, what harm is it that he was never
created?

Besides, where did the gods first get the model for making things,
and the very notion of human beings planted in them, so that they
knew what they wanted to do and saw it in their minds? How was the
power of the atoms ever learned, and what they could produce among
themselves by changing their order round, if nature herself had not
given the pattern for creating? For so many atoms, in so many ways,
struck by blows through infinite time gone by, and driven on by their
own weights, have been carried along and have met each other in every
possible way and tried out everything that they could create by
coming together, that it is no wonder if they also fell into
arrangements like these and came into motions like these, the ones by
which this sum of things is now kept going by renewal.

And even if I knew nothing about what the atoms of things are, I
would still dare to affirm this from the very workings of the sky,
and to make it out from a great many other things as well: that the
nature of things was in no way made ready for us by divine power — it
stands there with so much wrong with it.

To begin with, of all the space that the huge sweep of the sky
covers, a greedy share of it has been taken over by mountains and by
the forests of wild animals; crags hold it, and vast marshes, and the
sea, which keeps the shores of the lands far apart. Then, on top of
that, burning heat and the constant fall of frost take nearly
two-thirds of it away from mortals. What is left of the ploughland,
nature would still cover over with brambles by her own force, if
human force did not stand up to it — human force which for the sake
of staying alive is used to groaning over the strong two-pronged hoe
and cutting the earth open by leaning on the plough. If we did not
turn the fertile clods with the ploughshare and work the soil of the
ground and stir the crops into coming up, they would not be able to
rise into the clear air of their own accord. And even then, sometimes,
when things won by great labour are already in leaf across the land
and everything is in flower, either the sun of the upper air scorches
them with too much heat, or sudden rains and freezing frosts kill
them, or the blasts of the winds harry them with a violent whirl.

Besides that, why does nature feed and increase the horrible breed of
wild animals, an enemy to the human race by land and sea? Why do the
seasons of the year bring diseases with them? Why does death go
wandering about before its time? Then there is the child. Like a
sailor thrown up by savage waves, he lies naked on the ground, unable
to speak, in need of every kind of help for living, the moment nature
has poured him out of his mother's womb with hard struggles into the
shores of light. And he fills the place with a mournful wailing,
which is fair enough for somebody who has so much trouble left to get
through in life. But the various flocks and herds and wild animals
grow up without any of that. They need no rattles, nobody has to use
the coaxing broken talk of a kind nurse on them, they do not look for
different clothes to suit the season of the sky, and in the end they
need no weapons and no high walls to protect what is theirs, since
the earth herself and the crafty nature of things bring out
everything for all of them in plenty.

Now, first. Since the body of the earth and water, and the light
breaths of the airs, and the hot vapours — the things out of which
this whole sum of things is seen to consist — all consist of a body
that was born and is mortal, the whole nature of the world has to be
thought of in the same way. Where we see the parts and limbs of a
thing made of a body that was born, and of mortal shapes, we as a
rule see that those same things are mortal, and were born as well.
So, since I see that the greatest limbs and parts of the world are
used up and made again, I can be sure that there was some starting
time for the sky and the earth too, and that a disaster is coming for
them.

And do not think, over all this, that I have grabbed the point for
myself, because I have assumed that earth and fire are mortal, and
had no doubt that water and the airs perish, and said that these same
things are born and grow again. Take it in order. First, some part of
the earth, scorched by unrelenting suns, pounded by the force of many
feet, breathes out a mist of dust and flying clouds, which strong
winds scatter through the whole air. Part of its clods is called back
into the flood by rains, and rivers scrape their banks and gnaw them
away. Besides, whatever feeds a thing and makes it grow is paid back
again for its own share; and since the mother of everything is beyond
doubt also the common grave of things, then the earth, you see, is
drawn down and grows back after it has been increased.

For the rest, there is no need of words to show that the sea and the
rivers and the springs always overflow with new water and that the
liquid runs on for ever: the great downrush of waters on all sides
declares it. But the top of the water is taken off as it rises, and
so it comes about that on the whole there is no excess of water.
Partly this is because strong winds sweeping the level water cut it
down, and the sun of the upper air unweaves it with his rays; partly
because it is distributed underground through all the lands. The
brine is filtered out, and the material of the water seeps back and
all of it gathers at the river-heads, and from there it flows over
the lands in a sweet stream, along whatever channel was cut once and
has carried its waves down on liquid feet.

Now, then, I shall talk about air, which is changed in its whole body
countless times, hour by hour. Whatever flows off things is always
carried, all of it, into the great sea of the air; and unless the air
gave bodies back to things in turn and made them up again as they
stream away, everything would have been dissolved by now and turned
into air. So it never stops being born out of things and falling back
into things, since everything is in constant flow.

The generous spring of liquid light, too, the sun of the upper air,
waters the sky constantly with fresh brightness and supplies new
light on top of the light at once. Whatever is first of the brightness
is lost to it, wherever it happens to fall. You can learn this from
the following. The moment clouds have begun to pass under the sun and
to break the rays of light in half, as it were, all the lower part of
them is lost immediately, and the earth is put in shadow wherever the
storm clouds are carried. So you may know that things always need a
new splendour, and that each first throw of brightness perishes, and
that there is no other way for things to be seen in the sun unless
the fountain-head of the light keeps supplying it without a break.

In the same way, the lights we have on earth at night — hanging
lamps, and torches bright with flickering flames and fat with heavy
soot — hurry in exactly the same fashion, with the heat as their
servant, to supply new light. They press on to tremble with fires,
they press on, and the light does not leave gaps between, as though
it had been broken. That is how quickly the destruction of every one
of those fires is covered over by the swift birth of a new flame. So
we have to think that the sun and the moon and the stars throw out
their light from one rising after another, and always lose whatever
is first of their flames — so that you do not happen to believe that
these things flourish untouchable.

And after all, do you not see that stones too are beaten by time,
that high towers fall and their rocks rot away, that the shrines and
the images of the gods wear out and crack, and that a holy divine
power cannot push forward the boundary marks of fate or struggle
against the laws of nature? Do we not see the monuments of great men
fallen to pieces, asking us, in their turn, whether we believe they
grow old too? Do we not see rocks torn loose from the high mountains
and coming down in ruin, unable to bear and put up with the strong
forces of even a finite stretch of time? They would not be torn loose
and fall so suddenly if they had endured all the torments of age
through infinite time without ever breaking.

Then look at this thing which holds the whole earth in its embrace,
around it and above it. If it produces everything out of itself, as
some people report, and takes things back when they are destroyed,
then the whole of it consists of a body that was born and is mortal.
Whatever gives increase and food to other things out of itself has to
be diminished, and made up again when it takes those things back.

Besides, if there was no birth and beginning of the earth and the
sky, and they had always been everlasting, then why have other poets
not sung of other matters before the war at Thebes and the deaths at
Troy? Where have so many deeds of men fallen away to, so many times
over, without flowering anywhere, planted in the everlasting
monuments of fame? The truth, I think, is that the sum of things is
new, and the nature of the world is recent, and took its beginnings
not long ago. That is why certain skills are still being polished
even now, and are still growing now: many things have been added to
ships lately, and it was only the other day that instrument makers
gave birth to tuneful sounds. And in the end this nature of things,
this account of it, was discovered only recently, and I myself have
now been discovered, the very first among the first, as a man who can
turn it into the words of our own country.

And if you happen to believe that all these same things existed
before this, but that the generations of men perished in a burning
heat, or that cities fell in some great shaking of the world, or that
after unrelenting rains greedy rivers ran out over the lands and
covered the towns, then you are beaten all the more and have to
admit that a destruction of the lands and the sky is coming as well.
Because when things were being tried by such great diseases and such
great dangers, if a grimmer cause had leaned on them then, they would
have collapsed in wide disaster and great ruins. And there is no
other way we can be seen to be mortal ourselves, except that we
sicken among us with the same diseases as the people whom nature has
already removed from life.

Besides, whatever stays everlasting must either spit back blows
because it is made of solid body, and let nothing get inside it that
could break up the tight-packed parts within — the way the bodies of
matter are, whose nature I have set out earlier. Or it can last
through all time because it is exempt from blows, as the void is,
which stays untouched and is not affected by a blow in the least. Or
it lasts because there is no supply of room around it for the thing
to depart into, so to speak, and be dissolved — as the sum of all
sums is everlasting, with no place outside it to fly apart into and
no bodies that could fall on it and break it up with a strong blow.

But, as I have taught you, the nature of the world is not made of
solid body, since void is mixed into things. It is not like the void
either. And there is no lack of bodies which might rise up out of the
infinite by chance and bring this sum of things down in a violent
whirlwind, or bring some other disaster of danger on it. Nor does the
nature of place fail, or the space of the deep, into which the walls
of this world could be scattered — or they can be knocked to pieces
and perish by some other force. So the door of death is not shut and
barred against the sky, or against the sun and the earth and the deep
waves of the sea. It stands open, and it looks back at them with a
huge and gaping mouth. So you have to admit that these same things
were born, too; because things that are made of a mortal body could
not have gone on, from infinite time until now, treating the strong
forces of measureless time with contempt.

And finally, since the greatest limbs of the world fight each other
so hard, stirred up in a war that is in no way holy, do you not see
that some end can be given to their long struggle? It may come when
the sun and all the heat have won by drinking up all the moisture,
which is what they are trying to do, though so far their attempts
have not been brought off. The rivers supply so much, and threaten
beyond that to flood everything out of the deep whirlpool of the sea.
It is no use: winds sweeping the level water cut it down, and the sun
of the upper air unweaves it with his rays, and they are confident of
being able to dry everything up before the water can reach the end of
what it has set out to do. Breathing out such a war in an even
contest, they struggle to settle great matters between them — though
once in the meantime fire was the stronger, and once, as the story
goes, water was king in the fields.

Fire won, and burned much up as it went round, in the time when the
greedy strength of the sun's horses ran away with Phaethon off his
road, all through the upper air and over every land. But the almighty
father, stirred up then by sharp anger, knocked great-hearted
Phaethon off the horses to the earth with a sudden stroke of
lightning; and the Sun, meeting him as he fell, caught the everlasting
lamp of the world, and drove the scattered horses back together and
yoked them trembling, and then steered them along their own road and
brought everything back to life again. That is how the old poets of
the Greeks sang it, and it is far too far removed from true
reasoning. Fire can win when more bodies of matter have risen up out
of the infinite; and then its forces fall away, beaten back somehow,
or else things perish, burnt up by the scorching airs. Water, in the
same way, once rose up and began to win, as the story goes, when it
buried the lives of men under the waves; and then, when the force
that had risen up out of the infinite was somehow turned aside and
went back, the rains stopped and the rivers cut their violence down.

But I shall set out in order the ways in which that throwing together
of matter founded the earth and the sky and the depths of the sea,
and the courses of the sun and the moon. Certainly the atoms did not
place themselves each in its own order by design, with a shrewd mind,
and they certainly did not bargain over what motions each of them
would make. But so many atoms, in so many ways, struck by blows
through infinite time gone by, and driven on by their own weights,
have been carried along and have met each other in every possible way
and tried out everything that they could create by coming together;
and because of that it comes about that, scattered far and wide
through a great stretch of time, by trying out meetings and motions
of every kind, they at last fall together into the combinations
which, suddenly thrown together, often turn out to be the beginnings
of great things: of earth and sea and sky and the race of living
creatures.

At that time you could not have made out the wheel of the sun flying
high with its generous light, or the stars of the great world, or the
sea, or the sky, or the earth, or the air, or anything at all like
the things we have. There was only a kind of new storm, a mass that
had risen up. Then the parts of it began to fly apart in different
directions, and like began to be joined to like, and to shut a world
off, and divide up its limbs and lay out its great parts. This was
out of atoms of every kind, whose discord was throwing their
intervals, paths, connections, weights, blows, meetings, motions into
confusion and mixing up battles, because of their unlike forms and
their various shapes — because they could not all stay joined
together like that, or give each other motions that suited. That is
to say, the sorting out was this: to separate the high sky from the
lands, and the sea on its own, so that it could spread with its water
kept separate, and likewise, apart from both, the fires of the pure
and separated upper air.

To begin with, the bodies of earth, because they were heavy and
tangled, came together in the middle and all took up the lowest
places; and the more they came together tangled with each other, the
more they squeezed out the ones that were to make the sea, the stars,
the sun and the moon and the walls of the great world. For all of
these consist of smoother and rounder seeds, and of far smaller atoms
than the earth. So the fire-bearing upper air was the first to break
out through the loose openings of the earth and lift itself up, and
it carried off many light fires with it. It is not far different from
what we often see when the golden morning light of the rayed sun
first goes red across the grass gemmed with dew, and the lakes and
the rivers that never fail breathe out a mist, and sometimes the
earth herself seems to be smoking. When all of that is gathered
together up above, the clouds, with their body now packed solid,
weave a covering under the sky. So in the same way, then, the light
and outspreading upper air, with its body packed solid, was put round
everything and fenced it in on every side, and spreading itself far
and wide into all parts in every direction, fenced in everything else
in a greedy embrace.

The beginnings of the sun and the moon followed it, with their globes
turning in the air between the two. Neither the earth claimed them
for itself nor the greatest upper air, because they were not heavy
enough to be pressed down and settle, and not light enough to be able
to slip along the topmost shores. And yet they are between the two in
such a way that they turn living bodies round and stand out as parts
of the whole world — in the same way that in us certain limbs can
stay in position while there are others that move.

So when these things had been drawn out, the earth suddenly sank
where the huge blue stretch of the sea now spreads itself out, and
flooded the hollows with its salt whirlpool. And day by day, the more
the tides of the upper air and the rays of the sun forced the earth
together on all sides with repeated lashings, packed tight to its
outermost edges, so that under the driving it drew together and
condensed at its own centre, so much the more the salty sweat
squeezed out of its body kept increasing the sea and the swimming
plains by seeping out; and so much the more those many bodies of heat
and air slipped out and flew away and thickened the shining high
places of the sky, far from the lands. The plains sank down, and the
slopes of the high mountains rose; because the rocks could not settle
lower, and all the parts could not give way and sink equally.
