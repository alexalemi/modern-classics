Book Two: The Movements and Shapes of Atoms
(Part 1 of 2)

It is sweet, when the winds are stirring up the water on the great
sea, to watch from the shore another man's hard struggle. Not because
there is any pleasure in seeing somebody in trouble, but because it is
sweet to make out what troubles you are free of yourself. It is sweet,
too, to look at the great contests of war drawn up across the plains
when you have no share in the danger. But nothing is sweeter than to
hold the high calm places, well walled and fortified by the teaching
of the wise, and to be able to look down from there on other people
and see them wandering all over the place, off the road, hunting for a
way to live: competing in talent, struggling over rank, working nights
and days with enormous effort to climb to the top of wealth and take
possession of the world.

What miserable minds men have, what blind hearts! In what darkness, in
what dangers, this little stretch of life, whatever it comes to, is
spent! Can you not see that nature barks for nothing except this: that
pain should be kept away and separated from the body, and that the
mind should enjoy a pleasant feeling with worry and fear taken out of
the way?

So we see that the nature of the body needs very few things
altogether: only whatever takes the pain away, and whatever can also
spread a good many delights underneath. Sometimes that is the more
agreeable arrangement, and nature herself asks for nothing more, even
if there are no golden statues of young men through the halls holding
out fire-bearing lamps in their right hands to supply light for
feasts at night, and the house does not shine with silver or glitter
with gold, and no lyres go booming back off panelled and gilded
ceilings; and yet all the same, stretched out together on the soft
grass beside a stream of water under the branches of a tall tree,
people take care of their bodies pleasantly at no great expense,
especially when the weather smiles and the seasons of the year
sprinkle the green grass with flowers. And hot fevers do not leave
your body any sooner if you toss about on embroidered tapestries and
blushing purple than if you have to lie on a poor man's blanket.

So, since treasures do nothing for our body, and neither does noble
birth nor the glory of a kingdom, the next thing to think is that
they do nothing for the mind either. Unless perhaps, when you see your
legions boiling over the ground of the parade field, stirring up the
images of war, steadied with great reserves and a force of cavalry,
and your ships fitted out with weapons and all fired with the same
spirit, it is at moments like that that your religious terrors take
fright and run out of your mind in a panic, and the fears of death
leave your breast empty and untied from care.

But if we see that all this is ridiculous, a joke; if in real truth
men's fears and the cares that follow at their heels are not afraid of
the clash of weapons or savage spears, but move about boldly among
kings and the men who hold power, and are not overawed by the flash of
gold or the bright splendour of purple clothing — then why do you
doubt that the whole of this power belongs to reasoning? Especially
when the whole of life is labouring in the dark. For just as children
shake and are afraid of everything in the blind dark, so we are
sometimes afraid in the light of things that are no more to be feared
than the things children are frightened of in the dark and make up as
about to happen. So this terror of the mind and this darkness have to
be scattered. Not by the rays of the sun and the bright arrows of
daylight, but by looking at nature and working out how it runs.

Now then, let me set out by what motion the begetting bodies of matter
give birth to the different things there are, and dissolve them again
once they are born, and by what force they are made to do it, and what
mobility has been given them for travelling through the great void.
Remember to hand yourself over to what I say. For matter certainly
does not cohere packed tightly together, since we see everything
getting smaller, and we can make out that everything, so to speak,
flows away over the long stretch of time and that age withdraws it
from our eyes — and yet the sum total seems to stay unharmed. And the
reason is this: the bodies that leave any particular thing diminish
what they go away from and give increase to what they come to. They
force one thing to grow old and another to flower, and they do not
linger there either. So the sum of things is always being renewed, and
mortal creatures live by exchanging with one another. Some kinds grow
and others dwindle, and in a short space of time the generations of
living things are changed, and like runners they hand on the torch of
life.

If you think the atoms can come to a stop, and by stopping give birth
to new motions in things, you have wandered a long way off the road,
away from true reasoning. Since they travel about through the void,
all the atoms of things must be carried along either by their own
weight or sometimes by a blow from another. For when in their speed
they often meet and collide, the result is that they leap apart
suddenly in different directions. And that is no wonder: they are as
hard as anything can be, with solid weights, and nothing blocks them
from behind.

And so that you can see better how all the bodies of matter are
tossed about, remember that there is no bottom at all in the sum of
things, and that the first bodies have nowhere to settle, since space
is without end or limit. I have shown you in many places, and proved
by sure reasoning, that it stretches out immeasurably in every
direction on all sides. Since that is settled, then obviously no rest
is granted to the first bodies through the deep void. Instead, driven
by constant and varied motion, some of them are jammed together and
bounce back over great intervals, while others are shaken about by the
blow over short ones. And whatever atoms are carried together in a
denser union and bounce back over tiny intervals, tangled up by their
own interlocking shapes, these make the strong roots of rock and the
savage bodies of iron and everything else of that kind. The few others
that wander through the great void leap far apart and run far back
again over great intervals: these supply us with thin air and the
shining light of the sun. And besides these, many wander through the
great void which have been thrown out of the unions of things and have
never anywhere been taken in and got their motions to fit with the
rest.

Of the thing I am describing here, a likeness and an image is always
moving in front of our eyes and pressing on us. Look at what happens
whenever the sun's light and its rays are let in and pour through the
dark rooms of a house: you will see many tiny bodies mixing in many
ways through the void in the very light of the rays, and, as if in a
war that never ends, fighting battles and skirmishes, struggling
squadron against squadron and never giving it a rest, kept busy by
constant meetings and partings. From this you can work out what it is
like for the atoms of things to be tossed about forever in the great
void — at least so far as a small thing can give a model of great ones
and put you on the track of knowledge.

There is another reason why it is right for you to turn your mind to
those bodies that can be seen churning about in the sun's rays. Such
churning is a sign that there are hidden and invisible motions of
matter underneath it. For you will see many of them there, struck by
invisible blows, change course, and be driven back and return, now
this way, now that, in every direction everywhere. Plainly all this
wandering comes to them from the atoms. First the atoms of things move
by themselves. Then the bodies that are of small combination, and are,
so to speak, closest to the strength of the atoms, are set going,
driven by the atoms' invisible blows, and these in their turn provoke
bodies a little bigger. So motion climbs up from the atoms and comes
out little by little to our senses, until those bodies too are moved
which we can make out in the sun's light, though it is not obvious by
what blows they are doing it.

Now, Memmius, you can learn in a few words from this what mobility
has been given to the bodies of matter. When dawn first sprinkles the
lands with new light and the different birds flitting through the
trackless woods fill the soft air with their liquid voices, we all see
plainly and obviously how suddenly at such an hour the risen sun
covers everything and drenches it with its own light. But that heat
the sun sends out, and its clear light, do not travel through empty
void, and so they are forced to go more slowly, because they are
beating their way through waves of air, as it were. And the little
bodies of the heat do not travel one at a time but bound together and
balled up with each other, so that they are held back by each other
and blocked from outside at the same time, and that forces them to go
more slowly. But the atoms, which are of solid simplicity, when they
travel through the empty void with nothing outside to hold them back,
and are themselves one thing made out of their own parts, and are
carried, straining, to the one place they set out for — those must
beyond any doubt beat everything for mobility, and be carried much
faster than the light of the sun, and run across many times as much
space in the same time it takes the sun's flashes to spread over the
sky. People do not follow up the atoms one by one to see on what
principle each thing is carried on.

But there are some who set themselves against all this, knowing
nothing about matter, and say that nature cannot without the will of
the gods change the seasons of the years with such adjustment to human
needs, and produce the crops, and all the other things that divine
pleasure, the guide of life, persuades mortals to go for and leads
them to herself, coaxing them through the works of Venus to keep the
generations going so that the human race will not die out. When they
imagine that the gods set the whole thing up for the sake of men, they
seem in every way to have slipped a long way from true reasoning. For
even if I knew nothing about what the atoms of things are, I would
still dare to affirm this from the very workings of the sky, and to
make it out from a great many other things as well: that the nature of
this world was in no way created for us by divine power — it stands
there with so much wrong with it. That, Memmius, we shall make clear
for you later on; now let me unfold what is left about motions.

Now is the place, I think, to establish this too for you in this
business: nothing bodily can be carried upward or travel upward by its
own force. Do not let the bodies of flame play a trick on you here.
Flames are born growing upward and take their increase upward, and
shining crops and orchards grow upward, at a time when all weights, so
far as it lies in them, are carried downward. And when fires leap up
to the roofs of houses and taste the beams and rafters with a quick
flame, you must not think they do it of their own accord with no force
underneath them. It is the same as when blood is let out of our body:
it spurts up high, leaping, and scatters gore.

And do you not see, too, how much force water throws beams and rafters
back with? The harder we push them straight down, and the more of us
press them down with all our strength and with difficulty, the more
eagerly the water pushes them up and sends them back, so that more
than half their length comes out and shoots into the air. And still I
do not think we doubt that all of them, so far as it lies in
themselves, would be carried downward through the empty void. So in
the same way flames must be able, when they are squeezed out, to rise
up through the breezes of the air, although their weights, so far as
it lies in them, are fighting to draw them down. Do you not see the
torches of the night flying high in the sky, and the long trails of
flame they draw after them in whatever direction nature has given them
a way through? Do you not make out stars and constellations falling to
the earth? The sun too, from the top of heaven, scatters its heat in
every direction and sows the fields with light: so the sun's heat is
turned toward the earth as well. And you can make out lightning flying
sideways through the rain, when the fires break out of the clouds now
from here, now from there, and run together. The force of flame falls
to earth all the time.

There is something else in this business I am eager for you to learn.
When the atoms are being carried straight downward through the void by
their own weights, at quite uncertain times and in uncertain places
they push a little way out of their course — just enough that you can
call it a changed motion. If they were not in the habit of swerving,
they would all fall downward like drops of rain through the deep void;
no collision would ever come about, and no blow would be produced
among the atoms; and so nature would never have created anything at
all.

And if anyone happens to believe that heavier bodies, because they are
carried faster straight down through the void, can fall from above on
the lighter ones and so produce blows able to give rise to the motions
that beget things, he has backed a long way off the road, away from
true reasoning. Whatever falls through water or through thin air has
to speed up its fall in proportion to its weight, and for this reason:
the body of water and the thin nature of air cannot hold each thing
back equally, but give way faster when they are beaten by heavier
ones. The empty void, on the other hand, cannot support anything on
any side or at any time, but goes on giving way, as its own nature
demands. So everything must be carried at the same rate through the
still void, even though the weights are not the same. Therefore
heavier bodies will never be able to fall on the lighter ones from
above, and never able to produce by themselves the blows that vary
motions and let nature carry on her business.

So again and again: the atoms must swerve a little out of line. But no
more than the smallest amount, or we shall look as if we are inventing
sideways motions, and the facts will refute us. For this much is plain
and obvious to see: weights, so far as it lies in them, cannot travel
sideways when they fall headlong from above — you can make that out
for yourself. But that nothing at all swerves from the straight line
of its road: who is there who can make that out by his own senses?

Finally, if all motion is always linked together, and new motion
arises out of old in a fixed order, and the atoms do not by swerving
make some beginning of motion that breaks the laws of fate, so that
cause need not follow cause from infinity — then where does this free
will come from that living things all over the earth have? Where, I
say, does this will torn loose from the fates come from, by which we
go forward wherever pleasure leads each of us, and by which we swerve
our own motions in the same way, not at a fixed time or in a fixed
place, but wherever our own mind has taken us? For beyond doubt in
these matters it is each man's own will that gives the start, and it
is from there that motions are watered out through his limbs.

Do you not see too, when the starting gates are thrown open at a point
of time, that the eager strength of the horses still cannot burst out
as suddenly as the mind itself is longing to? Because the whole supply
of matter through the whole body has to be roused first, so that once
it is roused through every joint it can strain and follow the
eagerness of the mind. So you can see that the beginning of motion is
created out of the heart, and comes first from the will of the mind,
and from there is passed on through the whole body and the limbs.

It is not the same as when we go forward driven by a blow, by somebody
else's great strength and great compulsion. Then it is quite clear
that all the matter of our whole body goes and is carried off against
our will, until our will has reined it back in through the limbs. Now
do you see that although an outside force pushes many people and often
compels them to go forward against their will and be snatched along
headlong, there is still something in our breast that can fight back
and stand in the way? And at its decision the supply of matter is
sometimes forced to be bent about through the limbs and the joints,
and, after being thrown forward, is reined in and settles back.

So you have to admit the same thing in the seeds of things: that there
is another cause of motion besides blows and weights, and that this is
where this power we are born with comes from — since we see that
nothing can come out of nothing. For weight prevents everything from
happening through blows, by an outside force, as it were. But that the
mind itself should not have an inward necessity in everything it does,
and should not be forced, like a conquered thing, to bear and suffer —
that is done by the tiny swerve of the atoms, at no fixed place and no
fixed time.

The supply of matter was never packed more tightly than it is now, and
never separated by greater intervals either. For nothing is added to
it and nothing is lost from it. So the motion the bodies of the atoms
are in now is the motion they were in in ages gone by, and after this
they will always be carried along in the same way; and the things that
have been used to being born will be born on the same terms, and will
exist and grow and be strong in their force, as much as has been
granted to each by the laws of nature. And no force can change the sum
of things. For there is nothing outside the whole for any kind of
matter to escape into out of it, and nowhere outside from which a new
force could rise up and burst into the whole and change the whole
nature of things and turn their motions about.

There is nothing to wonder at in this either: that while all the atoms
of things are in motion, the sum total still seems to stand at
absolute rest, except where something makes motions with a body of its
own. For the whole nature of the atoms lies far below the reach of our
senses; and so, since you cannot make out the atoms themselves, their
motions must be stolen away from you as well. Especially since things
we can make out often hide their motions all the same, when they are
separated from us by a stretch of ground.

Often on a hillside woolly sheep crop the glad pasture and creep about
wherever the grass, jewelled with fresh dew, is calling each of them
and inviting her, and the well-fed lambs play and butt gently at each
other; and all of it seems blurred together to us at that distance,
and looks like a patch of white standing still on a green hill.
Besides that, when great legions fill the ground of the plains at a
run, stirring up the images of war, when the flash of them rises to
the sky and the whole earth all around shines back with bronze, and
beneath them a noise is struck out by the strength of the men's feet,
and the mountains, hit by the shouting, throw the voices back to the
stars of the world, and the cavalry fly around them and suddenly ride
across the middle of the plain, shaking it with the force of their
charge — even then there is a certain place up in the high mountains
from which they seem to be standing still, and the flash of them to be
sitting motionless on the plains.

Now then, next take in what the atoms of all things are like, and how
far apart they are in their forms, and how varied they are with shapes
of many kinds. Not that only a few of them are furnished with a like
form, but that they are not generally all the same as one another. And
that is no wonder: since there is such a supply of them that, as I
taught you, they have no end and no total, they must certainly not all
be spun of the same thread and marked with the same shape.

Besides, take the human race, and the mute swimming flocks of the
scaly kind, and the glad herds, and the wild beasts, and the different
birds — the ones that crowd the glad places of the water around the
banks and the springs and the lakes, and the ones that flit about and
fill the trackless woods. Go and take any one of them you like, kind
by kind, and you will still find that they differ from each other in
shape. There is no other way the young could recognise their mother,
or the mother her young — which we see they can do, and they are known
to each other no less than human beings are.

For often, in front of the beautiful shrines of the gods, a calf falls
slaughtered beside the incense-burning altars, breathing out a hot
river of blood from its breast. But the mother, robbed of it, ranges
over the green woodland and knows the footprints pressed into the
ground by cloven hoofs. She goes over every place with her eyes to see
if she can catch sight anywhere of the young one she has lost, and she
stands still and fills the leafy wood with her complaints, and comes
back again and again to the stall, pierced through with longing for
her calf. Tender willows, and grasses quickened with dew, and rivers
gliding along at the top of their banks cannot give her mind any
delight or turn that sudden pain away, and the other shapes of calves
about the glad pastures cannot draw her mind off it or lighten her
care. That is how much she is looking for something of her own that
she knows.

Besides, tender kids with their trembling voices know their horned
mothers, and butting lambs know the flocks of bleating sheep; and so,
as nature demands, each of them runs to its own udder of milk. Lastly,
take any grain you like: you will still see that not every kernel in
its own kind is like the others, and that some difference of shape
runs through them. In the same way we see the kinds of shells painting
the lap of the earth where the sea with its soft waves feeds the
thirsty sand of a curving shore. So again and again, by the same
reasoning, since the atoms of things exist by nature and were not made
by hand to one fixed pattern, some of them must fly about with shapes
unlike each other.

It is very easy for us to work out by the reasoning of the mind why
the fire of lightning is far more penetrating than our fire, which
rises from pine torches on earth. You can say that the heavenly fire
of the lightning is finer and is made of smaller shapes, and so passes
through holes that this fire of ours, born from wood and made out of
pine, cannot get through. Besides, light passes through horn, but rain
is thrown back. Why, unless the bodies of light are smaller than the
ones the kindly liquid of water is made of? And we see wine flow
through a strainer as quickly as you like, while sluggish olive oil
hangs back — no doubt because it is made either of larger elements, or
of ones more hooked and folded together with each other, and so it
comes about that its atoms cannot be pulled apart from one another
suddenly enough to seep, one by one, through the holes of a thing, one
by one.

Add to this that the liquids of honey and milk are handled with a
pleasant feeling by the tongue in the mouth, while the foul nature of
wormwood and of savage centaury twists the mouth up with its
disgusting taste. So you can easily recognise that the things able to
touch the senses pleasantly are made of smooth round atoms, while
whatever seems bitter and rough is held tied together by more hooked
ones, and so is in the habit of tearing open a way into our senses and
breaking through the body as it comes in.

In short, everything good to the senses and everything bad to the
touch are at war with each other, having been made of unlike shapes.
Do not imagine, for instance, that the harsh horror of a screeching
saw is made of elements as smooth as the tunes of the Muses which the
players shape on the strings, woken by their moving fingers. And do
not think that atoms of a like form work their way into people's
nostrils when foul corpses are being burned and when the stage is
freshly drenched with Cilician saffron and the altar beside it is
breathing out the perfumes of Panchaia. And do not set it down that
the good colours of things, the ones that can feed the eyes, are made
of a like seed as the ones that stab at the eyesight and force it to
water, or that look foul and ugly with a foul appearance. For
everything that soothes the senses has not been created without some
smoothness in its atoms; while whatever is troublesome and rough is
not found without some roughness of matter.

There are some, too, which are rightly thought to be neither smooth
nor altogether hooked with bent points, but rather to have little
angles sticking out a little way, so that they can tickle the senses
rather than hurt them. The lees of wine are of that kind by now, and
the flavours of elecampane.

Finally, that hot fires and icy frost bite at the senses of the body
with differently toothed atoms is shown to us by the touch of each of
them. For touch — by the holy powers of the gods! — touch is the sense
of the body: whether it is something from outside working its way in,
or something born in the body that hurts, or that delights as it goes
out through the begetting works of Venus, or whether the seeds are
disturbed by a collision inside the body itself and, stirred up,
confuse the sense with one another. You can find that out if you
happen to strike any part of your own body with your hand and try it.
So the shapes of the atoms must differ widely, since they can produce
such different sensations.

Finally, whatever seems to us hardened and thick must be held together
by atoms more hooked into each other and compacted deep down by ones
that are, so to speak, branched. In this class, in the front rank,
stand diamond stones, used to despising blows, and strong flints, and
the toughness of hard iron, and the bronze bars that scream as they
resist the bolts. But the things that are liquid and made of a flowing
body must be made rather of smooth round atoms, because the little
globes of them are not held together with each other; a drink of poppy
seeds goes down as easily as a drink of water, and once it is knocked
it likewise rolls away down a slope.

Finally, whatever you see scattering in a moment of time, like smoke
and cloud and flame, must, if it is not made altogether of smooth
round atoms, at any rate not be tangled up with interlocking ones —
since they can prick the body and get through rocks, and yet do not
stick to each other. Whatever you see is toothed to the senses you can
easily recognise as made not of interlocking atoms but of sharp ones.

But when you see that some of the same things which are fluid are
bitter as well, like the sweat of the sea, that ought not to surprise
you in the least. Because it is fluid, it is made of smooth round
atoms, but there are painful hooked bodies mixed in among the smooth
ones. And these do not have to be hooked to be held: clearly, though
they are rough, they are still round enough to roll along and hurt the
senses at the same time. And so that you may believe all the more that
rough atoms are mixed with smooth ones, which is where the harsh body
of Neptune comes from, there is a way of separating them and seeing
them apart. Sweet water, when the same water is filtered over and over
again through the earth, runs into a hollow and grows mild: it leaves
the atoms of the foul brine behind it up above, since the rough ones
can more easily stick fast in the earth.

Now that I have taught you this, I will go on to link on to it
something that follows from it and draws its credit from it: the atoms
of things vary in a limited number of shapes. If that were not so,
some seeds would have to be of infinite bodily size. For within one
and the same small size of any body, the shapes cannot vary much from
each other. Suppose the first bodies consist of three least parts, or
add a few more. When you have put all the parts of one body at the top
and at the bottom, swapped the right ones and the left, and tried out
in every possible way what appearance of form each arrangement gives
to the whole of that body, then, for what is left, if you happen to
want to vary the shapes further, you will have to add other parts.
After that it follows, by the same reasoning, that the arrangement
demands still other parts, if you happen to want to vary the shapes
further again. So an increase in the body follows on every novelty of
form. Therefore you cannot believe that the seeds differ in an
infinite number of forms, unless you want to force some of them to be
of a monstrous hugeness, which I taught above cannot be proved.

By now barbarian robes, and the gleaming Meliboean purple touched with
the Thessalian colour of the shellfish, and the golden generations of
peacocks steeped in their laughing charm, would all be lying beaten by
some new colour of things; the smell of myrrh and the flavours of
honey would be despised; and the swan's tune and the artful songs of
Apollo on the strings would be crushed in the same way and go silent.
For something better than the rest would always be coming up. And in
the same way everything could go backward into worse parts, just as we
said it could go forward into better ones; for something more
disgusting than the rest would also be coming up the other way, for
the nostrils, the ears, the eyes and the taste of the mouth. Since
that is not how things are, and since fixed limits have been assigned
to things which hold the sum in on either side, you have to admit that
matter differs in a limited number of shapes too.

Finally, from fire all the way to the icy frosts of winter is a
limited distance, and it is measured back again the same way. For all
the heat and cold and the middling warmths lie between the two,
filling out the total in their order. So created things differ within
a limited range, since they are marked off at each end by a
double-headed point, plagued by flames on one side and by stiff frost
on the other.

Now that I have taught you this, I will go on to link on to it
something that follows from it and draws its credit from it: the atoms
of things which are made of a shape like one another are infinite in
number. Since the variety of forms is limited, the ones that are alike
must be infinite, or else the total of matter would be finite — and I
have proved that it is not. Let me show you in these verses that the
little bodies of matter hold up the sum of things out of infinity,
with an unbroken team of blows pulling from every side.

For although you see that some animals are rarer than others, and can
make out that nature is less fruitful in them, still in another region
and place, in far-off lands, there may be many of that kind and the
number may be filled up. Just as among four-footed animals we see the
snake-handed elephants first of all: India is walled about with many
thousands of them, an ivory rampart, so that it cannot be penetrated
deep inside — such is the force of those beasts, of which we see very
few examples.

But even so, let me grant this as well: let there be, if you like,
some one unique thing born with a body of its own, with nothing like
it in the whole round of the lands. Still, unless there is an infinite
force of matter for it to be conceived and produced from, it will not
be able to be created, or, what is more, to grow and be fed. Suppose
in fact that a limited number of the begetting bodies of one thing
were tossed about through the whole: where and when and by what force
and in what way would they meet and come together in such a great sea
of matter and in a crowd of strangers? They have no way, I think, of
coming together at all.

But just as, when great and many shipwrecks have come about, the great
sea usually scatters the rowing benches, the hulls, the yardarms, the
prow, the masts and the swimming oars, so that along all the shores of
the lands the broken stern-fittings are seen floating and give a sign
to mortals, so that they may be willing to avoid the traps and the
force and the trickery of the treacherous sea, and never at any time
trust it when the sly seduction of the calm water is smiling: in just
the same way, if you once lay it down that certain atoms are limited
in number, then the different tides of matter must scatter them and
fling them apart through the whole of time, so that they can never be
driven together into a union, or stay in a union once they are in it,

obviously happen: that things are begotten, and that once begotten
they can grow. So it is plain that in every kind whatever the atoms of
things are infinite, and that everything is supplied from them.

And so the motions that bring destruction cannot win forever and bury
life for eternity; and, on the other hand, the motions that beget
things and make them grow cannot keep what has been created forever
either. So the war of the atoms, drawn out from infinite time, is
fought on equal terms. Now here, now there, the life forces of things
win, and are beaten in their turn. Mixed in with the funeral is the
wailing that children raise as they come to see the shores of light.
No night has ever followed a day, and no dawn a night, that has not
heard, mixed in with those sickly cries, the sound of mourning, the
companion of death and of a black funeral.
