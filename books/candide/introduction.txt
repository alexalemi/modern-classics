On the morning of 1 November 1755, a holiday, with the churches full, an earthquake destroyed Lisbon. Fire and a wave finished what the shock started, and tens of thousands of people died in a few hours. Voltaire, who was living near Geneva at the time, wrote a poem about it almost at once, arguing that no account of a good and orderly universe survives a day like that. Three years later he wrote Candide.

The position he is attacking is a real one and was held by serious people. Leibniz had argued that God, choosing among all the worlds he might have made, necessarily chose the best; every particular evil is a thread in a larger good we are too small to see. Alexander Pope put the same thing into English verse. Pangloss, the tutor who teaches Candide that all is for the best in this best of all possible worlds and goes on teaching it while he is hanged, dissected and sent to the galleys, is a caricature of that argument and not an invention. Readers in 1759 recognised him.

The book is short. Thirty small chapters, readable in an evening, moving at a pace that leaves a reader slightly winded. It is also extremely violent, and the violence is the argument: armies burning villages by the rules of international law, the Inquisition burning people to prevent further earthquakes, a slave on a Surinam sugar plantation missing a hand and a leg, who explains the arithmetic of how he came to be missing them. Each of these is narrated in the same brisk, polite tone as a dinner party, and that flat tone is the joke and the accusation at once. Voltaire never pauses to be indignant. He does not have to.

It was published in 1759 without his name on it, in several cities at once, and was banned within weeks in Paris and in Geneva. He denied having written it for years. It sold anyway, in numbers nothing else of his ever matched.

The ending is not a happy one and not exactly a sad one. Candide ends on one short sentence that most French schoolchildren can still finish, and there is no point in printing it here.

One note on the source. Every other from-the-original volume in this collection works from the author's own language; this one does not. What is modernised here is an English translation of Voltaire's French, and it is worth saying so rather than implying otherwise.
