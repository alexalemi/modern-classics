Simon Newcomb was the most celebrated American astronomer of his day. He was born in Nova Scotia in 1835, taught himself most of the mathematics he knew, and at twenty-two found work computing tables for the American Nautical Almanac. From 1877 to 1897 he ran the Nautical Almanac Office, and he spent those twenty years recalculating the motions of the sun, the moon and the planets. The tables he and his staff produced were adopted by observatories across the world and were still in use half a century after his death in 1909.

This book is the other side of that career. It grew, he says in the preface, out of articles for McClure's Magazine, and it is meant for a reader who has never opened an astronomy book. He takes that reader from the turning sky overhead to the telescope and the spectroscope, then to the sun, the earth and the moon, the planets one by one, comets and meteors, and finally the fixed stars and how to find the constellations on a clear night. He explains the seasons by asking the reader to imagine a round level platform with the sun at its centre, and standard time by the travellers who missed their trains because every railway kept its own clock. He is careful to say when something is measured and when it is only believed.

It is astronomy as it stood in 1902, and some of it has changed. The sun's heat is supplied, he writes, by the sun slowly shrinking; nobody yet knew about nuclear energy. Light is a wave in an ether that fills all space. The channels on Mars are drawn as the Lowell Observatory drew them, and he reports the disagreement over what they are. He expects Halley's comet back in eight or ten years, and it came in 1910. These are his statements, as he made them.

This is a restored edition, and the words are Newcomb's. The text is Wikisource's proofread transcription of the first edition, checked against two scans of the 1902 printing. A few slips of the transcription are corrected against the scans, as is one misprint of the printer's, and in two tables the ditto marks are spelled out, because a ditto mark cannot sit under the word it repeats once the text reflows. The one reference to a printed page now points to the plate it meant. All sixty-four figures are here, on sixty-two plates including the frontispiece photograph of the eclipse of 1900. Each has its printed caption and a new description for readers who cannot see it.
