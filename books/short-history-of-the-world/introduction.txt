H. G. Wells (1866–1946) was famous first as the author of The Time Machine and The War of the Worlds. After the First World War he turned to history, and The Outline of History, issued in parts from 1919 and as a book in 1920, sold in enormous numbers on both sides of the Atlantic. A Short History of the World followed in 1922. His preface is careful to say it is not a condensation of the Outline but a new book, "planned and written afresh", meant to be read straight through almost as a novel is read.

It runs from the nebulae to the treaties after the war in sixty-seven short chapters, and it ends in the year it was written. The shape is Wells's argument: that the history worth knowing is the history of the whole human species, not of one nation, and that the Great War had shown what nations left to themselves would do. The last chapters call President Wilson's League of Nations "a sketchy, inadequate and dangerous scheme", and warn that the war "will certainly return upon a still more disastrous scale in twenty or thirty years' time" if no political unification of the world prevents it.

Two things are worth knowing before starting. The first is that it is a book of 1922, and its science and anthropology are those of 1922. It describes the Piltdown remains from Sussex as a genuine early human, which is what most authorities then believed; they were exposed as a forgery in 1953. Its talk of races, and a diagram of their relationships, belongs to the same date. It has been left as Wells wrote it. The second is that the plates are part of the book: two hundred and eight photographs, museum objects, drawings and maps, set in the text, many with a printed note beneath that adds something the text does not say.

This is a restored edition: Wells's words are unchanged. The Project Gutenberg transcription it is built from was compared word by word with two copies of the 1922 Macmillan printing, and where both copies agree against it this edition follows them. That corrected about twenty words the transcription had misread ("the fad that" for "the fact that", "or" for "of"), a sentence with words doubled, three stray dates, and a dozen captions, all checked on the page images. It also restores Wells's preface, which the transcription leaves out. Captions that referred to other plates by page number now name the chapter instead. The index is omitted. Every plate has a new description as well as its printed caption, and this edition adds modern typesetting, a proper ebook, and this introduction.
