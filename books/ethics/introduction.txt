Aristotle was born in 384 BC in Stagira, in the north of Greece, came to Athens at seventeen to study with Plato, and stayed at the Academy for twenty years. He left after Plato died, spent some time tutoring the young Alexander of Macedon, and came back to Athens to start a school of his own, the Lyceum. He wrote on almost everything: logic, rhetoric, physics, weather, the parts of animals, the constitutions of Greek cities, poetry, dreams. He died in 322 BC, a year after leaving Athens for the last time.

This book reads like lecture notes because it probably is. It starts abruptly, doubles back, repeats itself, refers to arguments it has not made yet, and occasionally stops mid-thought. The books Aristotle polished for readers are all lost; what survived is the working material of the school. Knowing that in advance is worth a good deal, because the texture is not the reader's fault and it is not the translation's either. The title comes from the name Nicomachus, which belonged to both his father and his son.

Three of its key words are false friends in English. Happiness, in Book One, is not a mood and not a feeling you could have on a Tuesday afternoon; it is a whole life going well, an activity rather than a state, and Aristotle says flatly that one swallow does not make a spring. Virtue is not primness. The word means something closer to being good at the job of being a person, and his list includes courage, generosity, proper anger and being good company, with a long stretch on the man who is witty rather than either a buffoon or a bore. The mean is not moderation for its own sake and it is not a midpoint you calculate. Courage sits between cowardice and recklessness, and where it sits depends on who you are and what is in front of you. His own version: anyone can get angry, that is easy, but to be angry with the right person, to the right degree, at the right time, for the right reason and in the right way is not easy.

The other thing usually left out of accounts of this book is how much of it is about friendship. Books Eight and Nine, about a fifth of the whole, are on what friends are for, the three reasons people have them, why the best kind is rare and slow, whether a happy man needs any, and what to do when a friend changes and you no longer like him.

This edition is the complete ten books, modernized from F. H. Peters' 1893 English translation rather than from the Greek. Aristotle's teacher and his successor are both in this collection: Plato's Dialogues, and the Characters of Theophrastus, who took over the Lyceum when he left it.
