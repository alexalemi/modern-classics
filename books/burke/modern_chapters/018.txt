Section Eleven: The New Constitution, and How It Was Built

(Part 4 of 4)

"What incomprehensible spirit of delirium and delusion has led them astray all at once? While you are working tirelessly to establish uniformity in the empire and to mould the whole into one coherent and consistent body; while the French are being taught by you at once the respect the laws owe to the rights of man and the respect the citizens owe to the laws — the administration of the army presents nothing but disturbance and confusion. In more than one corps I see the bonds of discipline slackened or broken; the most unheard-of pretensions avowed directly and without any disguise; the ordinances without force; the commanders without authority; the military chest and the colours carried off; the authority of the king himself [_risum teneatis_ — try not to laugh] proudly defied; the officers despised, degraded, threatened, driven away, and some of them prisoners in the middle of their own corps, dragging out a precarious life in an atmosphere of disgust and humiliation. To fill up the measure of all these horrors, the commandants of garrisons have had their throats cut before the eyes and almost in the arms of their own soldiers.

"These evils are great, but they are not the worst consequences that such military insurrections may produce. Sooner or later they may threaten the nation itself. _The nature of things requires_ that the army should never act except as _an instrument_. The moment it sets itself up as a deliberative body and acts on its own resolutions, _the government, be it what it may, will immediately degenerate into a military democracy_ — a species of political monster that has always ended by devouring those who produced it.

"After all this, who can fail to be alarmed at the irregular consultations and turbulent committees formed in some regiments by the common soldiers and non-commissioned officers, without the knowledge of their superiors or even in contempt of their authority? — although the presence and concurrence of those superiors could give no authority to such monstrous democratic assemblies [_comices_]."

There is no need to add much to this finished picture — finished so far as its canvas allows, but not, I think, taking in the whole nature and complexity of the disorders of this military democracy, which, as the war minister truly and wisely observes, must be the real constitution of the state wherever it exists, by whatever formal name it goes. For although he informs the Assembly that the greater part of the army has not thrown off its obedience but is still attached to its duty, travellers who have seen the corps whose conduct is best observe in them the absence of mutiny rather than the existence of discipline.

I cannot help pausing here a moment to reflect on the expressions of surprise this minister lets fall about the excesses he relates. To him the departure of the troops from their ancient principles of loyalty and honour seems quite inconceivable. Surely the men he is addressing know the causes of it only too well. They know the doctrines they have preached, the decrees they have passed, the practices they have countenanced. The soldiers remember the sixth of October. They remember the French guards. They have not forgotten the taking of the king's castles in Paris and at Marseilles. That the governors of both places were murdered with impunity is a fact that has not slipped out of their minds. They do not abandon the principles of the equality of men, laid down so ostentatiously and so laboriously. They cannot shut their eyes to the degradation of the whole nobility of France and the suppression of the very idea of a gentleman. The total abolition of titles and distinctions is not lost on them. Yet M. du Pin is astonished at their disloyalty, when the doctors of the Assembly have taught them at the same time the respect due to the laws. It is easy to judge which of the two sorts of lesson men with arms in their hands are likely to learn. As for the authority of the king, we may gather from the minister himself, if any argument on that head were not quite superfluous, that it counts for no more with these troops than it does with everybody else. "The king," he says, "has repeated his orders over and over again to put a stop to these excesses; but in so terrible a crisis _your_" — the Assembly's — "concurrence has become indispensably necessary to prevent the evils that threaten the state. _You_ unite to the force of the legislative power _that of opinion_, which is still more important." To be sure, the army can have no opinion of the power or authority of the king. Perhaps by this time the soldier has learned that the Assembly itself does not enjoy a much greater degree of liberty than that royal figure.

We are now to see what has been proposed in this emergency, one of the greatest that can happen in a state. The minister asks the Assembly to array itself in all its terrors and to call forth all its majesty. He wishes the grave and severe principles they have announced to give vigour to the king's proclamation. After this we should have looked for courts civil and martial, the breaking of some regiments, the decimating of others, and all the terrible means that necessity has employed in such cases to arrest the progress of the most terrible of all evils. In particular one might expect a serious inquiry into the murder of commandants before their soldiers' eyes. Not one word of all this, or of anything like it. After being told that the soldiery trampled on the decrees of the Assembly promulgated by the king, the Assembly passes new decrees and authorises the king to make new proclamations. After the secretary at war had stated that the regiments had paid no regard to oaths _prêtés avec la plus imposante solennité_, sworn with the most imposing solemnity, they propose — what? More oaths. They renew decrees and proclamations as fast as they find them insufficient, and they multiply oaths in proportion as they weaken the sanctions of religion in men's minds. I hope that handy abridgements of the excellent sermons of Voltaire, D'Alembert, Diderot and Helvétius on the immortality of the soul, on a particular superintending providence, and on a future state of rewards and punishments are being sent down to the soldiers along with their civic oaths. Of this I have no doubt, since I understand that a certain kind of reading makes no small part of their military exercises, and that they are quite as well supplied with the ammunition of pamphlets as with cartridges.

To prevent the mischiefs arising from conspiracies, irregular consultations, seditious committees and monstrous democratic assemblies [_comitia, comices_] among the soldiers, and all the disorders arising from idleness, luxury, dissipation and insubordination, I believe the most astonishing means have been used that ever occurred to men, even among all the inventions of this prolific age. It is nothing less than this: the king has promulgated in circular letters to all the regiments his direct authority and encouragement for the several corps to join the clubs and confederations in the several municipalities, and to mix with them in their feasts and civic entertainments. This jolly discipline, it seems, is to soften the ferocity of their minds, to reconcile them to their bottle companions of other descriptions, and to merge particular conspiracies in more general associations. That this remedy would be pleasing to the soldiers, as M. de La Tour du Pin describes them, I can readily believe — and that, mutinous as they may be in other respects, they will dutifully submit themselves to _these_ royal proclamations. But I should question whether all this civic swearing and clubbing and feasting would dispose them any more than they are disposed at present to obey their officers, or teach them to submit better to the austere rules of military discipline. It will make them admirable citizens in the French mode, but not quite such good soldiers in any mode. A doubt might well arise whether the conversation at these good tables would fit them a great deal better for the character of _mere instruments_, which this veteran officer and statesman justly observes that the nature of things always requires an army to be.

Footnote: "Comme sa Majesté a reconnu, non un système d'associations particulières, mais une réunion de volontés de tous les François pour la liberté et la prospérité communes, ainsi que pour le maintien de l'ordre publique, il a pensé qu'il convenoit que chaque régiment prît part à ces fêtes civiques pour multiplier les rapports, et resserrer les liens d'union entre les citoyens et les troupes." In English: as His Majesty has recognised in this not a system of particular associations but a union of the wills of all Frenchmen for the common liberty and prosperity, and likewise for the maintenance of public order, he has thought it fitting that each regiment should take part in these civic festivals, so as to multiply the contacts and tighten the bonds of union between the citizens and the troops. I insert the words authorising the troops to feast with the popular confederacies, in case I should not be believed.

As to how likely this improvement of discipline is, by the free conversation of the soldiers with the municipal festive societies, thus officially encouraged by royal authority and sanction, we may judge from the state of the municipalities themselves as the war minister gives it to us in this very speech. He has good hopes of the success of his efforts to restore order _for the present_, from the good disposition of certain regiments; but he finds something cloudy about the future. As to preventing the return of confusion, "for that," he says, "the administration cannot answer to you, as long as they see the municipalities claiming an authority over the troops which your own institutions have reserved wholly to the monarch. You have fixed the limits of the military authority and of the municipal authority. You have confined the action you have allowed the second over the first to the right of requisition; but never did the letter or the spirit of your decrees authorise the commons in these municipalities to break the officers, to try them, to give orders to the soldiers, to drive them from the posts committed to their guard, to stop them on marches ordered by the king, or, in a word, to enslave the troops to the caprice of every city or even market town they have to pass through."

Such is the character and disposition of the municipal society that is to reclaim the soldiery, bring them back to the true principles of military subordination, and render them machines in the hands of the supreme power of the country. Such are the disorders of the French troops. Such is their cure. As the army is, so is the navy. The municipalities override the orders of the Assembly, and the seamen in their turn override the orders of the municipalities. From my heart I pity the condition of a respectable servant of the public like this war minister, obliged in his old age to pledge the Assembly in their civic cups and to enter with a grey head into all the fantastic vagaries of these juvenile politicians. Such schemes are not like propositions coming from a man of fifty years' wear and tear among mankind. They seem rather what is to be expected from those grand compounders in politics who take the short road to their degrees in the state and have a certain inward fanatical assurance and illumination on all subjects — on the credit of which one of their doctors has thought fit, with great applause and greater success, to caution the Assembly not to attend to old men, or to anyone who values himself on his experience. I suppose all the ministers of state must qualify and take this test, wholly abjuring the errors and heresies of experience and observation. Every man has his own taste; but I think that if I could not attain to the wisdom of age, I would at least preserve something of its stiff and peremptory dignity. These gentlemen deal in regeneration; but at no price would I yield my rigid fibres to be regenerated by them, or begin, in my grand climacteric, to squall in their new accents, or to stammer in my second cradle the elementary sounds of their barbarous metaphysics. _Si isti mihi largiantur ut repuerascam, et in eorum cunis vagiam, valde recusem!_ — if they were to grant me the favour of becoming a child again, and squalling in their cradle, I should firmly refuse.

The feebleness of any part of the puerile and pedantic system they call a constitution cannot be laid open without revealing the utter insufficiency and mischief of every other part it comes into contact with, or that bears the remotest relation to it. You cannot propose a remedy for the incompetence of the crown without displaying the debility of the Assembly. You cannot deliberate on the confusion of the army of the state without disclosing the worse disorders of the armed municipalities. The military lays open the civil, and the civil betrays the military anarchy. I wish everybody would read M. de La Tour du Pin's eloquent speech — for such it is — carefully. He attributes the salvation of the municipalities to the good behaviour of some of the troops. These troops are to preserve the well-disposed part of the municipalities, which is admitted to be the weakest, from the pillage of the worst-disposed part, which is the strongest. But the municipalities claim a sovereignty and will command the troops that are necessary for their protection. Indeed they must either command them or court them. The municipalities, by the necessity of their situation and by the republican powers they have obtained, must be, in relation to the military, either masters or servants or confederates, or each of these in turn, or they must make a jumble of all of them together according to circumstances. What government is there to coerce the army but the municipality, or the municipality but the army? To preserve concord where authority is extinguished, at the hazard of all consequences, the Assembly tries to cure the diseases by the diseases themselves, and hopes to preserve itself from a purely military democracy by giving that democracy a debauched interest in the municipal one.

If the soldiers once come to mix for any length of time in the municipal clubs and cabals and confederacies, an elective affinity will draw them to the lowest and most desperate part of them. Their habits and affections and sympathies will go with them. The military conspiracies that are to be remedied by civic confederacies; the rebellious municipalities that are to be made obedient by furnishing them with the means of seducing the very armies of the state that are meant to keep them in order — all these chimeras of a monstrous and portentous policy must aggravate the confusion they arose from. There must be blood. The want of common judgment shown in the construction of all their descriptions of forces, and in all their kinds of civil and judicial authority, will make it flow. Disorders may be quieted at one time and in one part. They will break out in others, because the evil is radical and intrinsic. All these schemes of mixing mutinous soldiers with seditious citizens must weaken the military connection between soldiers and their officers more and more, as well as adding a military and mutinous audacity to turbulent artisans and peasants. To secure a real army, the officer must be first and last in the eye of the soldier — first and last in his attention, his observance and his esteem. There are to be officers, it seems, whose chief qualification must be temper and patience. They are to manage their troops by electioneering arts. They must carry themselves as candidates and not as commanders. But since power may occasionally come into their hands by such means, the authority by which they are nominated becomes of high importance.

What you may do in the end does not appear, nor does it much matter, while the strange and contradictory relation between your army and all the parts of your republic, and the puzzled relation of those parts to each other and to the whole, remain as they are. You seem to have given the provisional nomination of the officers in the first instance to the king, with a reserved approval by the National Assembly. Men who have an interest to pursue are extremely sagacious in discovering the true seat of power. They must soon perceive that those who can veto indefinitely are the ones who really appoint. The officers must therefore look to their intrigues in the Assembly as the only certain road to promotion. Yet by your new constitution they must still begin their solicitations at court. This double negotiation for military rank seems to me a contrivance as well adapted as if it had been studied for the purpose to promote faction in the Assembly itself over this vast military patronage — and then to poison the corps of officers with factions of a kind still more dangerous to the safety of government, on whatever footing it may be placed, and destructive in the end to the efficiency of the army itself. Those officers who lose the promotions the crown intended for them must become part of a faction opposed to the Assembly that rejected their claims, and must nourish discontent against the ruling powers in the heart of the army. Those officers, on the other hand, who carry their point through an interest in the Assembly, and feel themselves at best only second in the goodwill of the crown though first in that of the Assembly, must slight an authority that could neither advance nor retard their promotion. If, to avoid these evils, you will have no rule for command or promotion but seniority, you will have an army of formality; and at the same time it will become more independent and more of a military republic. It is not they but the king who is the machine. A king is not to be deposed by halves. If he is not everything in the command of an army, he is nothing. What is the effect of a power placed nominally at the head of an army that is to that army no object of gratitude and no object of fear? Such a cipher is not fit to administer the most delicate of all objects, the supreme command of military men. They must be constrained — and their inclinations lead them to what their necessities require — by a real, vigorous, effective, decided, personal authority. The authority of the Assembly itself suffers by passing through such a debilitating channel as they have chosen. The army will not long look up to an Assembly acting through the organ of false show and palpable imposition. They will not seriously yield obedience to a prisoner. They will either despise a pageant or pity a captive king. This relation of your army to the crown will, unless I am greatly mistaken, become a serious dilemma in your politics.

It is also to be considered whether an Assembly like yours, even supposing it possessed a different sort of organ for its orders to pass through, is fit to promote the obedience and discipline of an army. It is known that armies have hitherto yielded a very precarious and uncertain obedience to any senate or popular authority, and they will yield it least of all to an Assembly that is to last only two years. The officers must lose the characteristic disposition of military men entirely if they can see the dominion of pleaders with perfect submission and due admiration — especially when they find they have a new court to pay to an endless succession of those pleaders, whose military policy and genius for command, if they have any, must be as uncertain as their tenure is brief. In the weakness of one kind of authority and the fluctuation of all, the officers of an army will remain mutinous and full of faction for some time, until some popular general who understands the art of conciliating the soldiery and possesses the true spirit of command draws the eyes of all men on himself. Armies will obey him on his own personal account. There is no other way of securing military obedience in this state of things. But the moment that happens, the man who really commands the army is your master — the master of your king, which is a small thing, the master of your Assembly, the master of your whole republic.

How did the Assembly come by its present power over the army? Chiefly, to be sure, by debauching the soldiers away from their officers. They have begun with a most terrible operation. They have touched the central point about which the particles that compose armies are at rest. They have destroyed the principle of obedience in the great, essential, critical link between the officer and the soldier, just where the chain of military subordination begins and on which the whole of that system depends. The soldier is told that he is a citizen and has the rights of man and citizen. The right of a man, he is told, is to be his own governor and to be ruled only by those to whom he delegates that self-government. It is very natural that he should think he ought most of all to have his choice where he is to yield the greatest degree of obedience. In all probability, therefore, he will do systematically what he does at present occasionally: that is, he will exercise at least a veto in the choice of his officers. At present the officers are known at best to be only permitted, and to serve on good behaviour. In fact there have been many instances of their being cashiered by their own corps. Here is a second veto on the king's choice, a veto at least as effective as the Assembly's. The soldiers know already that it has been a question not ill received in the National Assembly whether they ought not to have the direct choice of their officers, or of some proportion of them. When such matters are under deliberation, it is no extravagant supposition that they will incline to the opinion most favourable to their own claims. They will not bear to be reckoned the army of an imprisoned king while another army in the same country, which they are also to feast and confederate with, is to be considered the free army of a free constitution. They will cast their eyes on the other and more permanent army: I mean the municipal one. That corps, they know very well, does actually elect its own officers. They may not be able to see the grounds of the distinction on which they are not to elect a Marquis de La Fayette — or whatever his new name is — of their own. If this election of a commander-in-chief is part of the rights of men, why not part of theirs? They see elective justices of the peace, elective judges, elective parish priests, elective bishops, elective municipalities, and an elective commander of the Parisian army. Why should they alone be excluded? Are the brave troops of France the only men in that nation who are not fit judges of military merit and of the qualifications a commander-in-chief needs? Are they paid by the state, and do they therefore lose the rights of men? They are part of that nation themselves and contribute to that pay. And is not the king paid, and the National Assembly, and all who elect the National Assembly? Instead of seeing all these forfeit their rights by receiving a salary, they see that in every one of these cases a salary is given for exercising those rights. All your resolutions, all your proceedings, all your debates, all the works of your doctors in religion and politics have been industriously put into their hands; and you expect them to apply to their own case just as much of your doctrines and examples as suits your pleasure.

Everything depends on the army in a government like yours, for you have industriously destroyed all the opinions and prejudices, and so far as in you lay all the instincts, that support government. Therefore the moment any difference arises between your National Assembly and any part of the nation, you must have recourse to force. Nothing else is left to you — or rather, you have left nothing else to yourselves. You see from the report of your war minister that the distribution of the army is made largely with a view to internal coercion. You must rule by an army; and you have infused into that army by which you rule, as well as into the whole body of the nation, principles which after a time must disable you from making the use of it that you intend. The king is to call out troops to act against his people, when the world has been told, and the assertion is still ringing in our ears, that troops ought not to fire on citizens. The colonies claim an independent constitution and a free trade for themselves. They must be constrained by troops. In what chapter of your code of the rights of men can they read that it is part of the rights of men to have their commerce monopolised and restrained for the benefit of others? As the colonists rise against you, the people they hold enslaved rise against them. Troops again — massacre, torture, hanging! These are your rights of men! These are the fruits of metaphysical declarations wantonly made and shamefully retracted! It was only the other day that the farmers of land in one of your provinces refused to pay certain sorts of rent to the lord of the soil. In consequence you decree that the country people shall pay all rents and dues except those you have abolished as grievances; and if they refuse, then you order the king to march troops against them. You lay down metaphysical propositions that imply universal consequences, and then you try to limit logic by despotism. The leaders of the present system tell the people of their rights as men to take fortresses, to murder guards, to seize on kings without the least appearance of authority even from the Assembly, while that Assembly was sitting as the sovereign legislative body in the name of the nation; and yet these leaders presume to order out the very troops that took part in those disorders, to coerce men who judge on the principles and follow the examples their own approval has guaranteed.

The leaders teach the people to abhor and reject all feudalism as the barbarism of tyranny, and then tell them afterwards how much of that barbarous tyranny they are to bear with patience. As prodigal as they are of light about grievances, the people find them extremely sparing about redress. They know that certain quit-rents and personal duties, which you have allowed them to buy out but for which you have provided no money, are as nothing beside the burdens you have made no provision for at all. They know that almost the whole system of landed property is feudal in its origin — that it is the distribution of the possessions of the original proprietors, made by a barbarous conqueror to his barbarous instruments — and that the most grievous effects of the conquest are the land-rents of every kind, as beyond question they are.

The peasants are in all probability the descendants of those ancient proprietors, Romans or Gauls. But if their titles fail in any degree on the principles of antiquaries and lawyers, they retreat into the citadel of the rights of men. There they find that men are equal, and that the earth, the kind and equal mother of all, ought not to be monopolised to feed the pride and luxury of men who by nature are no better than themselves, and who, if they do not labour for their bread, are worse. They find that by the laws of nature the occupier and cultivator of the soil is the true proprietor; that there is no prescription against nature; that the agreements made with the landlords during the time of slavery, where there are any, are only the effect of duress and force; and that when the people re-entered into the rights of men, those agreements were made as void as everything else that had been settled under the prevalence of the old feudal and aristocratic tyranny. They will tell you they see no difference between an idler in a hat with a national cockade and an idler in a cowl or a rochet. If you ground the title to rents on succession and prescription, they tell you, out of M. Camus's speech published by the National Assembly for their information, that things ill begun cannot avail themselves of prescription; that the title of those lords was vicious in its origin; and that force is at least as bad as fraud. As for the title by succession, they will tell you that the succession of those who have cultivated the soil is the true pedigree of property, and not rotten parchments and silly entails; that the lords have enjoyed their usurpation too long; and that if they allow these lay monks any charitable pension, the monks ought to be thankful for the bounty of the true proprietor, who is so generous towards a false claimant to his goods.

When the peasants give you back that coin of sophistic reason on which you have stamped your own image and superscription, you cry it down as base money and tell them you will pay in future with French guards and dragoons and hussars. To chastise them you hold up the second-hand authority of a king who is only the instrument of destroying, with no power of protecting either the people or his own person. Through him, it seems, you will make yourselves obeyed. They answer: "You have taught us that there are no gentlemen; and which of your principles teaches us to bow to kings we have not elected? We know without your teaching that lands were given for the support of feudal dignities, feudal titles and feudal offices. When you took down the cause as a grievance, why should the more grievous effect remain? Since there are now no hereditary honours and no distinguished families, why are we taxed to maintain what you tell us ought not to exist? You have sent our old aristocratic landlords down to us in no other character and with no other title than that of collectors under your authority. Have you tried to make these rent-gatherers of yours respectable to us? No. You have sent them to us with their arms reversed, their shields broken, their devices defaced — so displumed, degraded and metamorphosed, such unfeathered two-legged things, that we no longer know them. They are strangers to us. They do not even go by the names of our ancient lords. Physically they may be the same men, though we are not quite sure even of that on your new philosophical doctrines of personal identity. In every other respect they are totally changed. We do not see why we have not as good a right to refuse them their rents as you have to abolish all their honours and titles and distinctions. We never commissioned you to do that, and it is one instance among many of your assuming an undelegated power. We see the burghers of Paris, through their clubs and mobs and national guards, directing you at their pleasure, and laying down as law to you what is then handed down as law to us under your authority. Through you these burghers dispose of the lives and fortunes of us all. Why should you not attend as much to the wishes of the labouring husbandman about our rent, which affects us in the most serious manner, as you do to the demands of these insolent burghers about distinctions and titles of honour, which affect neither them nor us at all? But we find you pay more regard to their fancies than to our necessities. Is it among the rights of man to pay tribute to his equals? Before this measure of yours we might have thought we were not perfectly equal; we might have kept up some old, habitual, unmeaning prepossession in favour of those landlords. But we cannot conceive that you could have made the law that degrades them with any other view than of destroying all respect for them. You have forbidden us to treat them with any of the old formalities of respect; and now you send troops to sabre and bayonet us into a submission to fear and force which you would not let us yield to the mild authority of opinion."

The ground of some of these arguments is horrid and ridiculous to all rational ears; but to the politicians of metaphysics, who have opened schools for sophistry and made establishments for anarchy, it is solid and conclusive. It is obvious that, on a mere consideration of the right, the leaders in the Assembly would not have scrupled in the least to abolish the rents along with the titles and family arms. It would only be following up the principle of their reasonings and completing the analogy of their conduct. But they had newly possessed themselves of a great body of landed property by confiscation. They had this commodity on the market, and the market would have been wholly destroyed if they had allowed the husbandmen to riot in the speculations they had so freely intoxicated themselves with. The only security any one description of property enjoys comes from the interest their rapacity has in some other kind. They have left nothing but their own arbitrary pleasure to determine what property is to be protected and what subverted.

Neither have they left any principle by which any of their municipalities can be bound to obedience, or even conscientiously obliged not to separate from the whole, to become independent, or to attach itself to some other state. The people of Lyons, it seems, have lately refused to pay taxes. Why should they not? What lawful authority is left to exact them? The king imposed some of them. The old States, organised by orders, settled the older ones. They may say to the Assembly: "Who are you, who are not our kings, nor the States we elected, nor sitting on the principles we elected you on? And who are we, that when we see the _gabelles_ you ordered to be paid shaken off altogether, and see the act of disobedience afterwards ratified by yourselves — who are we, that we are not to judge what taxes we ought or ought not to pay, and are not to make use of the same powers whose validity you have approved in others?" To this the answer is, "We will send troops." The last argument of kings is always the first with your Assembly. This military aid may serve for a time, while the impression of the increase of pay lasts and the vanity of being umpires in every dispute is flattered. But this weapon will snap short and prove unfaithful to the hand that uses it. The Assembly keeps a school where, systematically and with unremitting perseverance, they teach principles and make regulations destructive of all spirit of subordination, civil and military — and then they expect to hold an anarchic people in obedience by means of an anarchic army.

The municipal army, which by their new policy is to balance this national army, considered in itself alone, is of a much simpler constitution and in every respect less objectionable. It is a mere democratic body, unconnected with the crown or the kingdom, armed and trained and officered at the pleasure of the districts the several corps belong to; and the personal service of the individuals who compose it, or the fine paid instead of personal service, is directed by the same authority. Nothing could be more uniform. If, however, it is considered in any relation to the crown, to the National Assembly, to the public tribunals or to the other army, or with a view to any coherence or connection between its parts, it seems a monster, and can hardly fail to end its perplexed movements in some great national calamity. It is a worse preservative of a general constitution than the systasis of Crete, or the confederation of Poland, or any other ill-devised corrective yet imagined among the necessities produced by an ill-constructed system of government.
