Section Eleven: The New Constitution, and How It Was Built

(Part 3 of 4)

Though human eyes cannot trace them, one would be tempted to think that some great offences in France must cry to heaven, which has thought fit to punish her with subjection to a vile and inglorious domination, in which no comfort or compensation is to be found even in the false splendours that play about other tyrannies and keep mankind from feeling themselves dishonoured while they are oppressed. I confess I am touched with a sorrow mixed with some indignation at the conduct of a few men, once of great rank and still of great character, who, deluded by specious names, have engaged in a business too deep for the line of their understanding to fathom — who have lent their fair reputation and the authority of their high-sounding names to the designs of men they could not possibly have known, and have thereby made their very virtues work towards the ruin of their country.

So much for the first cementing principle.

The second material of cement for their new republic is the superiority of the city of Paris, and this, I admit, is strongly connected with the other cementing principle of paper circulation and confiscation. It is in this part of the project that we must look for the cause of the destruction of all the old boundaries of provinces and jurisdictions, ecclesiastical and secular, and the dissolution of all ancient combinations of things, as well as the formation of so many small unconnected republics. The power of the city of Paris is plainly one great spring of all their politics. It is through the power of Paris, now become the centre and focus of jobbing, that the leaders of this faction direct, or rather command, the whole legislative and the whole executive government. Everything must therefore be done that can confirm the authority of that city over the other republics. Paris is compact; she has an enormous strength, wholly out of proportion to the force of any of the square republics, and this strength is collected and condensed within a narrow compass. Paris has a natural and easy connection between its parts, which no scheme of a geometrical constitution will affect; nor does it much matter whether its proportion of representation is greater or smaller, since it has the whole draught of fishes in its net. The other divisions of the kingdom, being hacked and torn to pieces and separated from all their habitual means and even principles of union, cannot for some time at least confederate against her. Nothing was to be left in all the subordinate members but weakness, disconnection and confusion. To confirm this part of the plan, the Assembly has lately resolved that no two of their republics shall have the same commander-in-chief.

To anyone who takes a view of the whole, the strength of Paris, formed in this way, will appear a system of general weakness. It is boasted that the geometrical policy has been adopted so that all local ideas should be sunk, and that the people should no longer be Gascons, Picards, Bretons, Normans, but Frenchmen, with one country, one heart and one Assembly. But instead of being all Frenchmen, the greater likelihood is that the inhabitants of that region will shortly have no country at all. No man was ever attached by a sense of pride, partiality or real affection to a description of square measurement. He will never glory in belonging to square number 71, or to any other badge ticket. We begin our public affections in our families. No cold relation is a zealous citizen. We pass on to our neighbourhoods and our habitual provincial connections. These are inns and resting places. Such divisions of our country as have been formed by habit, and not by a sudden jerk of authority, were so many little images of the great country, in which the heart found something it could fill. Love of the whole is not extinguished by this subordinate partiality. Perhaps it is a sort of elementary training for those higher and larger regards by which alone men come to be affected, as with a concern of their own, in the prosperity of a kingdom as extensive as France. In that general territory itself, as in the old name of provinces, the citizens are interested by old prejudices and unreasoned habits, and not on account of the geometrical properties of its figure. The power and pre-eminence of Paris does certainly press down and hold these republics together as long as it lasts; but for the reasons I have already given you, I do not think it can last very long.

Passing from the civil creating and civil cementing principles of this constitution to the National Assembly, which is to appear and act as sovereign, we see a body constituted with every possible power and no possible external control. We see a body without fundamental laws, without established maxims, without respected rules of proceeding, which nothing can hold firm to any system whatever. Their idea of their own powers is always taken at the utmost stretch of legislative competence, and their precedents for common cases are taken from the exceptions of the most urgent necessity. The future Assembly is to be in most respects like the present one; but by the manner of the new elections and the tendency of the new circulations it will be purged of the small degree of internal control that exists in a minority originally chosen from various interests and preserving something of their spirit. If it is possible, the next Assembly must be worse than the present. The present one, by destroying and altering everything, will leave its successors apparently nothing popular to do. They will be roused by rivalry and example to the boldest and most absurd enterprises. To suppose such an Assembly sitting in perfect quiet is ridiculous.

Your all-sufficient legislators, in their hurry to do everything at once, have forgotten one thing that seems essential, and which I believe no projector of a republic has ever omitted before, in theory or in practice. They have forgotten to constitute a _senate_, or something of that nature and character. Never before this time was a body politic heard of that consisted of one legislative and active assembly and its executive officers, without such a council — without something for foreign states to connect themselves with, something the people could look up to in the ordinary detail of government, something to give a bias and a steadiness and to preserve something like consistency in the proceedings of state. Kings generally have such a body as a council. A monarchy may exist without one, but it seems to be of the very essence of a republican government. It holds a sort of middle place between the supreme power exercised by the people or immediately delegated from them, and the mere executive. There are no traces of this in your constitution; and in providing nothing of the kind your Solons and Numas have shown a sovereign incapacity, as much as in anything else.

Let us now turn our eyes to what they have done towards forming an executive power. For this they have chosen a degraded king. This first executive officer of theirs is to be a machine, without any sort of deliberative discretion in any single act of his function. At best he is only a channel for conveying to the National Assembly such matter as that body may need to know. If he had been made the exclusive channel, the power would not have been without its importance, though infinitely perilous to anyone who chose to exercise it. But public intelligence and statements of fact may reach the Assembly with equal authenticity by any other conveyance. As a means of giving direction to measures by the statement of an authorised reporter, therefore, this office of intelligence is nothing at all.

Consider the French scheme of an executive officer in its two natural divisions, civil and political. In the first it must be observed that under the new constitution the higher parts of judicature, in either of its lines, are not in the king. The king of France is not the fountain of justice. Neither the original nor the appellate judges are of his nomination. He neither proposes the candidates nor has a veto on the choice. He is not even the public prosecutor. He serves only as a notary, to authenticate the choice of judges made in the several districts. Through his officers he is to carry out their sentence. When we look into the true nature of his authority, he appears to be nothing more than a chief of bailiffs, sergeants-at-mace, catchpolls, jailers and hangmen. It is impossible to place anything called royalty in a more degrading light. It would have been a thousand times better for the dignity of this unhappy prince if he had had nothing at all to do with the administration of justice, deprived as he is of everything venerable and everything consoling in that function, with no power of originating any process and no power of suspension, mitigation or pardon. Everything in justice that is vile and odious is thrown on him. It was not for nothing that the Assembly took such pains to remove the stigma from certain offices, when they were resolved to place the man who had lately been their king in a situation only one degree above the executioner's, and in an office of nearly the same quality. It is not in nature that the king of the French, situated as he now is, can respect himself or be respected by others.

View this new executive officer on the side of his political capacity, as he acts under the orders of the National Assembly. To execute laws is a royal office; to execute orders is not to be a king. However, a political executive magistracy, even a merely political one, is a great trust. It is a trust that has much depending on its faithful and diligent performance, both in the person presiding over it and in all his subordinates. The means of performing this duty ought to be given by regulation, and the dispositions towards it ought to be infused by the circumstances attending the trust. It ought to be surrounded with dignity, authority and consideration, and it ought to lead to glory. The office of execution is an office of exertion. It is not from impotence that we are to expect the tasks of power. What sort of person is a king to command executive service who has no means whatever of rewarding it — not with a permanent office, not with a grant of land, no, not with a pension of fifty pounds a year, not with the vainest and most trivial title? In France the king is no more the fountain of honour than he is the fountain of justice. All rewards, all distinctions, are in other hands. Those who serve the king can be moved by no natural motive but fear — fear of everything except their master. His functions of internal coercion are as odious as those he exercises in the department of justice. If relief is to be given to any municipality, the Assembly gives it. If troops are to be sent to reduce a municipality to obedience to the Assembly, the king is to execute the order; and on every occasion he is to be spattered over with the blood of his people. He has no veto, yet his name and authority are used to enforce every harsh decree. Indeed, he must join in the butchery of anyone who attempts to free him from his imprisonment, or who shows the slightest attachment to his person or to his ancient authority.

Executive magistracy ought to be constituted in such a way that those who compose it are disposed to love and venerate the people they are bound to obey. A deliberate neglect, or, what is worse, a literal but perverse and malignant obedience, must be the ruin of the wisest counsels. The law will attempt in vain to anticipate or to follow such studied neglects and fraudulent attentions. To make men act zealously is not within the competence of law. Kings, even such as are truly kings, may and ought to bear with the freedom of subjects who are obnoxious to them. They may also, without derogating from themselves, bear even the authority of such persons if it promotes their service. Louis the Thirteenth mortally hated Cardinal Richelieu, but his support of that minister against his rivals was the source of all the glory of his reign and the solid foundation of his throne itself. Louis the Fourteenth, when he came to the throne, did not love Cardinal Mazarin, but he kept him in power for his own interests. When he was old he detested Louvois, but for years, while Louvois faithfully served his greatness, he endured the man. When George the Second took Mr Pitt, who certainly was not agreeable to him, into his councils, he did nothing that could humble a wise sovereign. But these ministers, who were chosen by affairs and not by affections, acted in the name of kings and in trust for them, not as their avowed constitutional and ostensible masters. I think it impossible that any king, once he has recovered from his first terrors, can cordially infuse vivacity and vigour into measures that he knows to be dictated by men whom he must be persuaded are in the highest degree ill disposed towards his person. Will any ministers who serve such a king — or whatever he is to be called — with even a decent appearance of respect cordially obey the orders of men whom only the other day they had committed to the Bastille in his name? Will they obey the orders of men whom they believed they were treating with leniency while they were exercising despotic justice on them, and for whom they thought they had provided an asylum in a prison? If you expect such obedience, then among your other innovations and regenerations you ought to make a revolution in nature and provide a new constitution for the human mind; otherwise your supreme government cannot harmonise with its executive system. There are cases in which we cannot make do with names and abstractions. You may call half a dozen leading individuals whom we have reason to fear and hate the nation. It makes no difference except to make us fear and hate them the more. If it had been thought justifiable and expedient to make such a revolution by such means and through such persons as you have made yours, it would have been wiser to have completed the business of the fifth and sixth of October. The new executive officer would then owe his position to men who were his creators as well as his masters; and he might be bound, by interest, by the fellowship of crime, and, if crimes could have virtues, by gratitude, to serve those who had promoted him to a place of great profit and great sensual indulgence — and of something more besides, for more he must have received from men who certainly would not have limited a creature they had aggrandised as they have limited a submitting antagonist.

A king in the present one's circumstances, if he is so totally stupefied by his misfortunes as to think it not the necessity but the privilege and prize of life to eat and sleep with no regard to glory, can never be fit for the office. If he feels as men commonly feel, he must be aware that an office so circumstanced is one in which he can win no fame and no reputation. He has no generous interest to rouse him to action. At best his conduct will be passive and defensive. To inferior people such an office might be a matter of honour. But to be raised to it and to descend to it are different things, and suggest different sentiments. Does he _really_ name the ministers? Then they will have a sympathy with him. Are they forced on him? Then the whole business between them and the nominal king will be mutual counteraction. In every other country the office of minister of state is of the highest dignity. In France it is full of peril and incapable of glory. Rivals they will still have in their nothingness, as long as shallow ambition exists in the world or the desire of a miserable salary is an incentive to short-sighted avarice. Your constitution enables those competitors of the ministers to attack them in their vital parts, while the ministers have no means of repelling the charges except in the degrading character of culprits. The ministers of state in France are the only persons in that country who are incapable of a share in the national councils. What ministers! What councils! What a nation! But they are responsible. Responsibility is a poor sort of service to get. The elevation of mind derived from fear will never make a nation glorious. Responsibility prevents crimes. It makes all attempts against the laws dangerous. But as a principle of active and zealous service, none but idiots could think of it. Is the conduct of a war to be trusted to a man who may abhor its very principle — who with every step he takes to make it succeed confirms the power of those by whom he is oppressed? Will foreign states seriously treat with a man who has no prerogative of peace or war, not so much as a single vote, by himself or his ministers or anyone he can possibly influence? A state of contempt is no state for a prince: better be rid of him at once.

I know it will be said that these ill humours in the court and the executive government will last only through this generation, and that the king has been brought to declare that the dauphin shall be educated in conformity with his situation. If he is made to conform to his situation, he will have no education at all. His training must be worse even than that of an arbitrary monarch. If he reads — and whether he reads or not — some good or evil genius will tell him that his ancestors were kings. From that moment his object must be to assert himself and to avenge his parents. This, you will say, is not his duty. That may be; but it is nature; and while you pit nature against you, you are unwise to trust to duty. In this futile scheme of politics, the state nurses in its bosom for the present a source of weakness, perplexity, counteraction, inefficiency and decay, and it prepares the means of its own final ruin. In short, I see nothing in the executive force — I cannot call it authority — that has even an appearance of vigour, or the smallest degree of just correspondence or symmetry or friendly relation with the supreme power, either as it now exists or as it is planned for the future government.

You have settled, by an economy as perverted as the policy, two establishments of government, one real and one fictitious, both maintained at a vast expense — but the fictitious one at the greater, I think. Such a machine as the second is not worth the grease of its wheels. The expense is exorbitant, and neither the show nor the use deserves a tenth part of the charge. Oh, but I am not doing justice to the talents of the legislators; I am not making the allowance I ought for necessity. Their scheme of executive force was not their own choice. This pageant must be kept up. The people would not consent to part with it. Right; I understand you. In spite of your grand theories, to which you would have heaven and earth bend, you do know how to conform yourselves to the nature and circumstances of things. But when you were obliged to conform this far to circumstances, you ought to have carried your submission further and made what you were obliged to take a proper instrument, useful to its end. That was in your power. For instance, among many others, it was in your power to leave your king the right of peace and war. What! Leave the executive magistrate the most dangerous of all prerogatives? I know none more dangerous, nor any that more needs to be trusted to him. I do not say that this prerogative ought to be trusted to your king unless he enjoyed other auxiliary trusts along with it, which he does not now hold. But if he did possess them, hazardous as they undoubtedly are, advantages would arise from such a constitution more than compensating the risk. There is no other way of keeping the several potentates of Europe from intriguing separately and personally with the members of your Assembly, from meddling in all your concerns, and from fomenting in the heart of your country the most pernicious of all factions — factions in the interest and under the direction of foreign powers. From that worst of evils, thank God, we are still free. Your skill, if you had any, would be well employed in finding indirect correctives and controls on this perilous trust. If you did not like the ones we have chosen in England, your leaders might have exerted their abilities in contriving better. If it were necessary to illustrate the consequences of such an executive government as yours in the management of great affairs, I should refer you to M. de Montmorin's recent reports to the National Assembly and to all the other proceedings relating to the differences between Great Britain and Spain. It would be treating your understanding with disrespect to point them out to you.

I hear that the persons who are called ministers have signified an intention of resigning their places. I am rather astonished that they did not resign long ago. Not for the universe would I have stood in the position they have occupied for the last twelvemonth. They wished well to the Revolution, I take it for granted. However that may be, placed as they were on an eminence — though an eminence of humiliation — they could not help being the first to see collectively, and to feel each in his own department, the evils that Revolution has produced. In every step they took, or refrained from taking, they must have felt the degraded situation of their country and their own utter incapacity to serve it. They are in a species of subordinate servitude in which no men before them were ever seen. Having the confidence neither of their sovereign, on whom they were forced, nor of the Assembly, which forced them on him, all the noble functions of their office are performed by committees of the Assembly, without any regard whatever to their personal or their official authority. They are to execute, without power; to be responsible, without discretion; to deliberate, without choice. In their bewildered situation under two sovereigns, over neither of whom they have any influence, they must act in such a way as, whatever they may intend, to betray sometimes the one and sometimes the other, and always to betray themselves. Such has been their situation, and such must be the situation of those who succeed them. I have much respect and many good wishes for M. Necker. I am obliged to him for attentions. When his enemies had driven him from Versailles, I thought his exile a subject for the most serious congratulation. _Sed multæ urbes et publica vota vicerunt_ — but many cities and the public prayers prevailed. He is now sitting on the ruins of the finances and of the monarchy of France.

A great deal more might be observed on the strange constitution of the executive part of the new government; but fatigue must set bounds to the discussion of subjects which in themselves have hardly any limits.

I am able to perceive as little genius and talent in the plan of judicature formed by the National Assembly. Following their invariable course, the framers of your constitution have begun by utterly abolishing the parlements. These venerable bodies, like the rest of the old government, needed reform, even if no change had been made in the monarchy. They needed several more alterations to adapt them to the system of a free constitution. But they had particular features in their constitution, and not a few of them, that deserved the approval of wise men. They possessed one fundamental excellence: they were independent. The most doubtful circumstance attending their office, that it was purchasable, actually contributed to this independence of character. They held for life. Indeed they may be said to have held by inheritance. Appointed by the monarch, they were regarded as nearly out of his power. The most determined exertions of that authority against them only showed their radical independence. They formed permanent bodies politic, constituted to resist arbitrary innovation; and from that corporate constitution, and from most of their forms, they were well calculated to give both certainty and stability to the laws. They had been a safe asylum securing those laws through all the revolutions of humour and opinion. They had saved that sacred deposit of the country through the reigns of arbitrary princes and the struggles of arbitrary factions. They kept alive the memory and the record of the constitution. They were the great security for private property, which might be said, at a time when personal liberty had no existence, to be in fact as well guarded in France as in any other country. Whatever is supreme in a state ought as far as possible to have its judicial authority so constituted as not only not to depend on it but in some sort to balance it. It ought to give a security to its justice against its power. It ought to make its judicature, as it were, something exterior to the state.

Those parlements had furnished, not the best certainly, but some considerable corrective to the excesses and vices of the monarchy. Such an independent judicature was ten times more necessary when a democracy became the absolute power of the country. In that constitution, elective, temporary, local judges such as you have contrived, exercising their dependent functions in a narrow society, must be the worst of all tribunals. It will be vain to look in them for any appearance of justice towards strangers, towards the obnoxious rich, towards the minority of defeated parties, towards everyone who supported unsuccessful candidates at the election. It will be impossible to keep the new tribunals clear of the worst spirit of faction. We know by experience that all contrivances by ballot are vain and childish as a way of preventing inclinations from being discovered. Where they best answer the purposes of concealment, they answer by producing suspicion, and that is a still more mischievous cause of partiality.

If the parlements had been preserved instead of being dissolved at such ruinous cost to the nation, they might have served in this new commonwealth — not precisely the same purposes, I do not mean an exact parallel, but near the same purposes as the court and senate of the Areopagus served in Athens: that is, as one of the balances and correctives to the evils of a light and unjust democracy. Everyone knows that this tribunal was the great stay of that state; everyone knows with what care it was upheld and with what religious awe it was consecrated. The parlements were not wholly free from faction, I admit; but that evil was external and accidental, and not so much a vice of their constitution itself as it must be in your new contrivance of six-yearly elective judicatures. Several English people commend the abolition of the old tribunals, supposing that they determined everything by bribery and corruption. But they have stood the test of both monarchic and republican scrutiny. The court was well disposed to prove corruption on those bodies when they were dissolved in 1771; the men who have dissolved them again would have done the same if they could; but since both inquisitions failed, I conclude that gross pecuniary corruption must have been rather rare among them.

It would have been prudent, along with the parlements, to preserve their ancient power of registering all the decrees of the National Assembly, and at least of remonstrating against them, as they did with the decrees passed in the time of the monarchy. It would be a means of squaring the occasional decrees of a democracy to some principles of general jurisprudence. The vice of the ancient democracies, and one cause of their ruin, was that they ruled, as you do, by occasional decrees, _psephismata_. This practice soon broke in on the tenor and consistency of the laws; it lowered the people's respect for them, and in the end destroyed them altogether.

Your vesting the power of remonstrance, which in the time of the monarchy belonged to the Parlement of Paris, in your principal executive officer — whom, in spite of common sense, you persist in calling king — is the height of absurdity. You ought never to allow remonstrance from the man who is to execute. This is to understand neither counsel nor execution, neither authority nor obedience. The person you call king ought not to have this power, or else he ought to have more.

Your present arrangement is strictly judicial. Instead of imitating your monarchy and seating your judges on a bench of independence, your object is to reduce them to the blindest obedience. Having changed all things, you have invented new principles of order. You first appoint judges, who I suppose are to decide according to law, and then you let them know that at some time or other you intend to give them some law to decide by. Whatever studies they have made, if they have made any, are to be useless to them. But to make up for those studies, they are to be sworn to obey all the rules and orders and instructions they receive from time to time from the National Assembly. If they submit to these, they leave the subject no ground of law at all. They become complete and most dangerous instruments in the hands of the governing power, which, in the middle of a case or in prospect of one, may wholly change the rule of decision. If these orders of the National Assembly come to be contrary to the will of the people who locally choose those judges, such confusion must follow as is terrible to think of. For the judges owe their place to the local authority, and the commands they are sworn to obey come from men who have no share in appointing them. Meanwhile they have the example of the court of the _Châtelet_ to encourage and guide them in the exercise of their functions. That court is to try criminals sent to it by the National Assembly, or brought before it by other means of accusation. They sit under a guard to save their own lives. They do not know by what law they judge, nor under what authority they act, nor by what tenure they hold. It is thought that they are sometimes obliged to condemn at the peril of their lives. This is perhaps not certain, and cannot be ascertained; but when they acquit, we know that they have seen the men they discharge hanged at the door of their court, with perfect impunity to those who did it.

The Assembly does indeed promise that they will form a body of law that shall be short, simple, clear and so forth. That is, by their short laws they will leave much to the discretion of the judge, while they have exploded the authority of all the learning that could make judicial discretion — a perilous thing at best — deserve the name of a _sound_ discretion.

It is curious to observe that the administrative bodies are carefully exempted from the jurisdiction of these new tribunals. That is, the very persons who ought to be most entirely subject to the laws are exempted from their power. Those who execute public financial trusts ought of all men to be held most strictly to their duty. One would have thought that it must have been among your earliest cares, unless you meant those administrative bodies to be real, sovereign, independent states, to form an awe-inspiring tribunal, like your late parlements or like our King's Bench, where all corporate officers might obtain protection in the legal exercise of their functions and would meet with coercion if they trespassed against their legal duty. But the reason for the exemption is plain. These administrative bodies are the great instruments of the present leaders in their progress through democracy to oligarchy. They must therefore be put above the law. It will be said that the legal tribunals you have made are unfit to coerce them. Undoubtedly they are. They are unfit for any rational purpose. It will be said too that the administrative bodies will be accountable to the general Assembly. This, I fear, is talking without much consideration of the nature of that Assembly or of these corporations. However, to be subject to the pleasure of that Assembly is not to be subject to law, either for protection or for constraint.

This establishment of judges still wants something to complete it. It is to be crowned by a new tribunal. This is to be a grand state judicature, and it is to judge crimes committed against the nation — that is, against the power of the Assembly. It seems as if they had something in view of the nature of the high court of justice erected in England during the great usurpation. As they have not yet finished this part of the scheme, it is impossible to form a direct judgment on it. However, unless great care is taken to form it in a spirit very different from the one that has guided them in their proceedings on state offences, this tribunal, subservient to their inquisition _the Committee of Research_, will extinguish the last sparks of liberty in France and settle the most dreadful and arbitrary tyranny ever known in any nation. If they wish to give this tribunal any appearance of liberty and justice, they must not remove cases relating to their own members from it, or send such cases to it, at their pleasure. They must also move the seat of that tribunal out of the republic of Paris.

Has more wisdom been displayed in the constitution of your army than can be discovered in your plan of judicature? Arranging this part ably is the more difficult, and requires the greater skill and attention, not only as a great concern in itself but because it is the third cementing principle in the new body of republics that you call the French nation. Truly it is not easy to guess what that army may become in the end. You have voted a very large one, and on good pay, at least fully equal to your apparent means of paying it. But what is the principle of its discipline? Or whom is it to obey? You have got the wolf by the ears, and I wish you joy of the happy position you have chosen to place yourselves in, and in which you are so well circumstanced for a free deliberation about that army, or about anything else.

The minister and secretary of state for the War Department is M. de La Tour du Pin. This gentleman, like his colleagues in the administration, is a most zealous supporter of the Revolution and a sanguine admirer of the new constitution that originated in it. His statement of the facts about the military of France is important, not only from his official and personal authority but because it displays very clearly the actual condition of the army in France, and because it throws light on the principles the Assembly proceeds by in administering this critical object. It may enable us to judge how far it would be expedient in this country to imitate the martial policy of France.

On the fourth of last June M. de La Tour du Pin comes to give an account of the state of his department as it exists under the auspices of the National Assembly. No man knows it so well; no man can express it better. Addressing the National Assembly, he says:

"His Majesty has sent me _this day_ to inform you of the multiplied disorders of which _every day_ he receives the most distressing news. The army [_le corps militaire_] threatens to fall into the most turbulent anarchy. Entire regiments have dared to violate at once the respect due to the laws, to the king, to the order established by your decrees, and to the oaths they have taken with the most awful solemnity. Compelled by my duty to inform you of these excesses, my heart bleeds when I consider who it is that has committed them. Those against whom I have no power to withhold the most grievous complaints are part of that very soldiery which until this day has been so full of honour and loyalty, and among whom I have lived for fifty years as comrade and friend.
