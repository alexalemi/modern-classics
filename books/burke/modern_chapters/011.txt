Section Ten: The Church Establishment, and the Seizure of Its Property

(Part 2 of 5)

But this act of seizing property, it seems, is a judgment in law and not a confiscation. They have discovered, it seems, in the academies of the Palais Royal and the Jacobins, that certain men had no right to the possessions they held under law, usage, the decisions of courts and the accumulated prescription of a thousand years. They say that clergymen are fictitious persons, creatures of the state, whom the state may destroy at pleasure and of course limit and modify in every particular; that the goods they possess are not properly theirs but belong to the state that created the fiction; and that we need not therefore trouble ourselves about what they may suffer in their natural feelings and their natural persons on account of what is done to them in this constructed character of theirs. What does it matter under what names you injure men and deprive them of the just earnings of a profession that the state not only permitted but encouraged them to enter, and on the supposed certainty of those earnings had made the plan of their lives, contracted debts, and led multitudes into entire dependence on them?

You do not imagine, Sir, that I am going to compliment this miserable distinction of persons with any long discussion. The arguments of tyranny are as contemptible as its force is dreadful. Had your confiscators not obtained, by their early crimes, a power that secures them indemnity for every crime they have since been guilty of or may yet commit, it is not the logician's syllogism but the executioner's lash that would have refuted a sophistry which makes itself an accomplice of theft and murder. The sophistical tyrants of Paris are loud in their declamations against the dead royal tyrants who vexed the world in former ages. They are bold in that because they are safe from the dungeons and iron cages of their old masters. Shall we be more tender towards the tyrants of our own time, when we see them acting worse tragedies before our eyes? Shall we not take the same liberty they take, when we can take it with the same safety, and when speaking honest truth requires nothing but a contempt for the opinions of men whose actions we abhor?

This outrage on all the rights of property was at first covered by what, on their system of conduct, was the most astonishing of all pretexts — a regard for the national honour. The enemies of property at first pretended a most tender, delicate and scrupulous anxiety about keeping the king's engagements with the public creditor. These professors of the rights of men are so busy teaching others that they have no leisure to learn anything themselves; otherwise they would have known that the first and original faith of civil society is pledged to the property of the citizen, not to the demands of the creditor of the state. The citizen's claim is prior in time, paramount in title, superior in equity. The fortunes of individuals, whether held by purchase or by descent or by a share in the goods of some community, were no part of the creditor's security, expressed or implied. They never so much as entered his head when he made his bargain. He knew perfectly well that the public, whether represented by a monarch or by a senate, can pledge nothing but the public estate, and that it can have no public estate except what it derives from a just and proportioned levy on the citizens at large. That was pledged to the public creditor, and nothing else could be. No man can mortgage his injustice as a pledge for his good faith.

It is impossible to avoid remarking on the contradictions produced by the extreme rigour and the extreme laxity of this new public faith, which operated in this transaction not according to the nature of the obligation but according to the description of the persons it was owed to. No acts of the old government of the kings of France are held valid in the National Assembly except its money engagements — of all its acts the most doubtful in legality. The rest of that royal government's acts are regarded in so odious a light that to have a claim under its authority is looked on as a sort of crime. A pension given as a reward for service to the state is surely as good a ground of property as any security for money advanced to the state. It is better, for money is paid, and well paid, to obtain that service. Yet we have seen multitudes of people of that description in France, who had never been deprived of their allowances by the most arbitrary ministers in the most arbitrary times, robbed without mercy by this assembly of the rights of men. They were told, in answer to their claim to the bread earned with their blood, that their services had not been rendered to the country that now exists.

This laxity of public faith is not confined to those unfortunate people. The Assembly, with perfect consistency it must be owned, is engaged in a respectable deliberation as to how far it is bound by the treaties made with other nations under the former government; and its committee is to report which of them they ought to ratify and which not. By this means they have put the external good faith of this virgin state on a level with its internal.

It is not easy to conceive on what rational principle the royal government should not, of the two, rather have possessed the power of rewarding service and making treaties by virtue of its prerogative than the power of pledging the revenue of the state, actual and possible, to creditors. Of all things, the treasure of the nation has been the least allowed to the prerogative of the king of France, or to the prerogative of any king in Europe. To mortgage the public revenue implies sovereign dominion over the public purse in the fullest sense. It goes far beyond the trust even of a temporary and occasional taxation. Yet the acts of that dangerous power — the distinctive mark of a boundless despotism — are the only ones held sacred. How did a democratic assembly come to give this preference to a body of property that derives its title from the most critical and objectionable of all the exertions of monarchical authority? Reason can furnish nothing to reconcile an inconsistency, nor can partial favour be accounted for on equitable principles. But a contradiction and a partiality that admit no justification are not for that reason without an adequate cause, and I do not think that cause is difficult to discover.

Through the vast debt of France a great moneyed interest has imperceptibly grown up, and with it a great power. Under the ancient usages that prevailed in that kingdom, the general circulation of property, and in particular the mutual convertibility of land into money and money into land, had always been difficult. Family settlements, rather more general and more strict than they are in England; the _jus retractûs_; the great mass of landed property held by the crown and, by a maxim of French law, held inalienably; the vast estates of the ecclesiastical corporations — all these had kept the landed and the moneyed interests more separate in France, less mixable, and had left the owners of the two distinct kinds of property less well disposed towards one another than they are in this country.

Moneyed property was long looked on with rather an evil eye by the people. They saw it connected with their distresses and aggravating them. It was no less envied by the old landed interests — partly for the same reasons that made it obnoxious to the people, but much more because it eclipsed, by the splendour of an ostentatious luxury, the unendowed pedigrees and bare titles of several of the nobility. Even when the nobility, who represented the more permanent landed interest, allied themselves by marriage with the other sort, as sometimes happened, the wealth that saved the family from ruin was supposed to contaminate and degrade it. So the enmities and heartburnings of these parties were increased even by the usual means through which discord is made to cease and quarrels are turned into friendships. Meanwhile the pride of wealthy men who were not noble, or newly noble, grew along with its cause. They resented an inferiority whose grounds they did not acknowledge. There was no measure they were not willing to lend themselves to in order to be revenged for the outrages of this rival pride and to raise their wealth to what they considered its natural rank and estimation. They struck at the nobility through the crown and the church. They attacked them particularly on the side where they thought them most vulnerable — that is, the possessions of the church, which through the patronage of the crown generally fell to the nobility. The bishoprics and the great commendatory abbeys were held, with few exceptions, by that order.

In this state of real though not always perceived warfare between the old noble landed interest and the new moneyed interest, the greatest strength — because the most usable — was in the hands of the latter. The moneyed interest is by its nature readier for any adventure, and its possessors more disposed to new enterprises of every kind. Being recently acquired, it falls in more naturally with novelties. It is therefore the kind of wealth that everyone who wants change will turn to.

Along with the moneyed interest, a new description of men had grown up with whom that interest soon formed a close and marked union: I mean the political men of letters. Men of letters, being fond of distinguishing themselves, are rarely averse to innovation. Since the decline of the life and greatness of Louis the Fourteenth they had not been so much cultivated by him, or by the Regent, or by his successors on the throne; nor were they attached to the court by favours and salaries as systematically as they had been during the splendid period of that ostentatious and not impolitic reign. What they lost in the old court protection they tried to make up by joining in a sort of incorporation of their own, to which the two academies of France, and afterwards the vast undertaking of the Encyclopédie, carried on by a society of these gentlemen, contributed not a little.

The literary cabal had some years ago formed something like a regular plan for the destruction of the Christian religion. They pursued this object with a zeal that had until then been found only in the propagators of some system of piety. They were possessed by a spirit of proselytism to the most fanatical degree — and from that, by an easy progress, by the spirit of persecution so far as their means allowed. What could not be done towards their great end by any direct and immediate act might be worked out by a longer process through the medium of opinion. To command opinion, the first step is to establish a dominion over those who direct it. They contrived to possess themselves, with great method and perseverance, of all the avenues to literary fame. Many of them did indeed stand high in the ranks of literature and science. The world had done them justice, and in favour of their general talents forgave the evil tendency of their peculiar principles. That was true liberality — which they repaid by trying to confine the reputation for sense, learning and taste to themselves and their followers. I will venture to say that this narrow, exclusive spirit has been no less damaging to literature and taste than to morals and true philosophy. These atheistical fathers have a bigotry of their own, and they have learned to talk against monks with the spirit of a monk. But in some things they are men of the world. The resources of intrigue are called in to supply the defects of argument and wit. To this system of literary monopoly was joined an unremitting industry to blacken and discredit, in every way and by every means, everyone who did not belong to their faction. To anyone who has observed the spirit of their conduct it has long been clear that nothing was wanting but the power to carry the intolerance of the tongue and the pen into a persecution that would strike at property, liberty and life.

The desultory and feeble persecution carried on against them, which came more from a compliance with form and decency than from any serious resentment, neither weakened their strength nor slackened their efforts. The upshot of the whole was that, what with opposition and what with success, a violent and malignant zeal of a kind till then unknown in the world had taken entire possession of their minds and made their whole conversation, which would otherwise have been pleasing and instructive, perfectly disgusting. A spirit of cabal, intrigue and proselytism pervaded all their thoughts, words and actions. And since controversial zeal soon turns its thoughts to force, they began to insinuate themselves into a correspondence with foreign princes, in hopes that through their authority, which at first they flattered, they might bring about the changes they had in view. It was all one to them whether those changes were to be accomplished by the thunderbolt of despotism or by the earthquake of popular commotion. The correspondence between this cabal and the late king of Prussia will throw no small light on the spirit of all their proceedings. For the same purpose for which they intrigued with princes, they cultivated the moneyed interest of France in a marked manner; and partly through the means furnished by men whose particular offices gave them the most extensive and certain channels of communication, they carefully occupied all the avenues to opinion.

Writers, especially when they act as a body and in one direction, have great influence on the public mind; the alliance of these writers with the moneyed interest therefore had no small effect in removing the popular odium and envy that attended that species of wealth. These writers, like the propagators of all novelties, pretended to a great zeal for the poor and the lower orders, while in their satires they made the faults of courts, of the nobility and of the priesthood hateful by every kind of exaggeration. They became a sort of demagogues. They served as a link uniting obnoxious wealth with restless and desperate poverty in favour of one object.

Footnote: Their connection with Turgot and almost all the people of the finance.

As these two sorts of men appear as the principal leaders in all the recent transactions, their junction and their politics will account — not on any principles of law or of policy, but as a _cause_ — for the general fury with which all the landed property of ecclesiastical corporations has been attacked, and for the great care that has been taken, contrary to their professed principles, of a moneyed interest originating in the authority of the crown. All the envy against wealth and power was artificially directed against other kinds of riches. On what other principle than the one I have stated can we account for anything so extraordinary and unnatural as the ecclesiastical possessions, which had stood through so many successions of ages and shocks of civil violence, and were guarded at once by justice and by prejudice, being applied to the payment of debts that are comparatively recent, invidious, and contracted by a decried and overthrown government?

Was the public estate a sufficient stake for the public debts? Suppose it was not, and that a loss _must_ be incurred somewhere. When the only estate lawfully possessed, and the only one the contracting parties had in view at the time the bargain was made, happens to fail, who according to the principles of natural and legal equity ought to be the sufferer? Certainly it ought to be either the party who trusted, or the party who persuaded him to trust, or both — and not third parties who had nothing to do with the transaction. In any insolvency those ought to suffer who were weak enough to lend on bad security, or those who fraudulently held out a security that was not valid. The law knows no other rules of decision. But by the new institute of the rights of men, the only persons who in equity ought to suffer are the only persons who are to be held harmless: those are to answer the debt who were neither lenders nor borrowers, neither mortgagors nor mortgagees.

What had the clergy to do with these transactions? What had they to do with any public engagement beyond the extent of their own debt? To that, to be sure, their estates were bound to the last acre. Nothing leads more directly to the true spirit of the Assembly, which sits for public confiscation with its new equity and its new morality, than attending to their proceedings with regard to this debt of the clergy. The body of confiscators, true to that moneyed interest for which they were false to every other, have found the clergy competent to incur a legal debt. Of course by doing so they declared them legally entitled to the property which their power of incurring the debt and mortgaging the estate implied — recognising the rights of those persecuted citizens in the very act by which they so grossly violated them.

If, as I said, anyone besides the public at large is to make good the deficiencies to the public creditor, it must be those who managed the agreement. Why, then, are the estates of all the comptrollers-general not confiscated? Why not those of the long succession of ministers, financiers and bankers who have been enriched while the nation was impoverished by their dealings and their counsels? Why is the estate of M. Laborde not declared forfeit, rather than that of the Archbishop of Paris, who has had nothing to do with the creation or the jobbing of the public funds? Or if you must confiscate old landed estates in favour of the money-jobbers, why is the penalty confined to one description of men? I do not know whether the Duke de Choiseul's expenses have left anything of the infinite sums he drew from his master's bounty during the transactions of a reign that contributed largely, by every species of prodigality in war and peace, to the present debt of France. If any of it remains, why is that not confiscated? I remember being in Paris during the time of the old government. I was there just after the Duke d'Aiguillon had been snatched, as it was generally thought, from the block by the hand of a protecting despotism. He was a minister, and had some part in the affairs of that prodigal period. Why do I not see his estate handed over to the municipalities it lies in? The noble family of Noailles have long been servants — meritorious servants, I admit — to the crown of France, and have of course had some share in its bounties. Why do I hear nothing of applying their estates to the public debt? Why is the estate of the Duke de la Rochefoucauld more sacred than that of the Cardinal de la Rochefoucauld? The former is, I do not doubt, a worthy person; and, if it were not a sort of profanity to talk of the use of property as affecting the title to it, he makes a good use of his revenues. But it is no disrespect to him to say what authentic information well warrants me in saying: that the use his brother, the Cardinal Archbishop of Rouen, made of a property equally valid was far more laudable and far more public-spirited. Can anyone hear of the proscription of such persons and the confiscation of their effects without indignation and horror? A man who does not feel such emotions on such occasions is not a man. He does not deserve the name of a free man who will not express them.

Few barbarous conquerors have ever made so terrible a revolution in property. None of the heads of the Roman factions, when they set up _crudelem illam hastam_ — that cruel spear which marked a public auction of plunder — in all their auctions of rapine, ever put up for sale the goods of the conquered citizen to such an enormous amount. It must be allowed in favour of those tyrants of antiquity that what they did could hardly be said to be done in cold blood. Their passions were inflamed, their tempers soured, their understandings confused with the spirit of revenge, with innumerable recent and reciprocal inflictions and retaliations of blood and rapine. They were driven beyond all bounds of moderation by the fear that power would return along with property to the families they had injured beyond all hope of forgiveness.

These Roman confiscators, who were still only in the elements of tyranny and had not been instructed in the rights of men so as to practise every sort of cruelty on one another without provocation, thought it necessary to spread a sort of colour over their injustice. They considered the vanquished party as made up of traitors who had borne arms or otherwise acted with hostility against the commonwealth. They regarded them as persons who had forfeited their property by their crimes. With you, in your improved state of the human mind, there was no such formality. You seized five million pounds sterling of annual rent and turned forty or fifty thousand human creatures out of their houses, because "such was your pleasure". The tyrant Henry the Eighth of England, not being better enlightened than the Roman Mariuses and Sullas, and not having studied in your new schools, did not know what an effective instrument of despotism was to be found in that grand arsenal of offensive weapons, the rights of men. When he resolved to rob the abbeys, as the club of the Jacobins has robbed all the clergy, he began by setting up a commission to examine the crimes and abuses prevailing in those communities. As might be expected, his commission reported truths, exaggerations and falsehoods. But truly or falsely, it reported abuses and offences. However, since abuses might be corrected, since a crime committed by persons does not always mean a forfeiture for the community, and since in that dark age property had not yet been discovered to be a creature of prejudice, all those abuses — and there were plenty of them — were hardly thought sufficient ground for the sort of confiscation it suited his purposes to make. He therefore procured a formal surrender of these estates. All these laborious proceedings were adopted by one of the most decided tyrants in the rolls of history as necessary preliminaries, before he could venture — by bribing the members of his two servile Houses with a share of the spoil, and holding out to them an eternal immunity from taxation — to demand a confirmation of his iniquitous proceedings by an act of Parliament. Had fate reserved him for our times, four technical terms would have done his business and saved him all this trouble; he would have needed nothing more than one short form of incantation: "_Philosophy, Light, Liberality, the Rights of Men_."

I can say nothing in praise of those acts of tyranny, which no voice has ever yet commended under any of their false colours; and yet in those false colours despotism paid a homage to justice. The power that was above all fear and all remorse was not set above all shame. While shame keeps watch, virtue is not wholly extinguished in the heart, nor will moderation be utterly banished from the minds of tyrants.

I believe every honest man shares the reflections of our political poet on that occasion, and will pray to avert the omen whenever these acts of rapacious despotism present themselves to his view or his imagination:

This same wealth, which is at all times treason and _lèze-nation_ to a needy and rapacious despotism under every form of government, was your temptation to violate property, law and religion, all united in one object. But was the state of France so wretched and undone that no resource but plunder remained to preserve its existence? On this point I should like some information. When the States met, was the condition of the finances of France such that, after economising on principles of justice and mercy through every department, no fair distribution of the burdens over all the orders could possibly have restored them? If such an equal levy would have been sufficient, you know very well that it might easily have been made. M. Necker, in the budget he laid before the orders assembled at Versailles, gave a detailed account of the state of the French nation.

If we credit him, there was no need to resort to any new impositions at all to bring the receipts of France into balance with its expenses. He put the permanent charges of every description, including the interest on a new loan of four hundred millions, at 531,444,000 livres, and the fixed revenue at 475,294,000, making the deficiency 56,150,000, or a little under £2,200,000 sterling. But to balance it he brought forward savings and improvements of revenue, considered entirely certain, to rather more than the amount of that deficiency; and he concludes with these emphatic words, on page 39: "Quel pays, Messieurs, que celui, où, _sans impôts_ et avec de simples objets _inaperçus_, on peut faire disparoître un déficit qui a fait tant de bruit en Europe!" — what a country, gentlemen, where without new taxes, and by simple items nobody had noticed, a deficit that has made so much noise in Europe can be made to disappear! As to the repayment, the sinking of the debt and the other great objects of public credit and political arrangement indicated in M. Necker's speech, there could be no doubt that a very moderate and proportioned assessment on the citizens without distinction would have provided for all of them to the fullest extent of their demand.

If this representation of M. Necker's was false, then the Assembly is culpable in the highest degree for having forced the king to accept him as his minister, and, since the king's deposition, for having employed as _their_ minister a man capable of so notoriously abusing his master's confidence and their own — and in a matter of the highest moment, belonging directly to his particular office. But if the representation was exact, as, having always shared your high respect for M. Necker, I have no doubt it was, then what can be said for men who, instead of a moderate, reasonable and general contribution, have in cold blood and driven by no necessity resorted to a partial and cruel confiscation?

Was that contribution refused on a pretext of privilege, either by the clergy or by the nobility? Certainly not. As for the clergy, they even ran ahead of the wishes of the third order. Before the States met, they had in all their instructions expressly directed their deputies to renounce every immunity that put them on a footing distinct from their fellow subjects. The clergy were even more explicit in this renunciation than the nobility.

But let us suppose the deficiency had remained at the fifty-six millions, or £2,200,000 sterling, as first stated by M. Necker. Let us grant that all the resources he set against that deficiency were impudent and groundless fictions, and that the Assembly, or its lords of articles at the Jacobins, were thereby justified in laying the whole burden of that deficiency on the clergy. Even granting all that, a necessity of £2,200,000 sterling will not support a confiscation to the amount of five millions. Imposing £2,200,000 on the clergy would, as a partial measure, have been oppressive and unjust, but it would not have been altogether ruinous to those it was imposed on; and therefore it would not have answered the managers' real purpose.

Perhaps people unacquainted with the state of France, on hearing that the clergy and the nobility were privileged in the matter of taxation, may imagine that before the Revolution these bodies contributed nothing to the state. That is a great mistake. They certainly did not contribute equally with each other, nor either of them equally with the commons. Both, however, contributed largely. Neither nobility nor clergy enjoyed any exemption from the excise on consumable goods, from customs duties, or from any of the other numerous _indirect_ taxes which in France, as here, make up so very large a proportion of all payments to the public. The nobility paid the poll tax. They also paid a land tax, called the twentieth penny, sometimes as high as three and sometimes four shillings in the pound — both of them _direct_ taxes, of no light kind and no trivial yield. The clergy of the provinces annexed to France by conquest, which make about an eighth part of the whole in extent but a much larger proportion in wealth, likewise paid the poll tax and the twentieth penny at the rate the nobility paid. The clergy in the old provinces did not pay the poll tax, but they had bought themselves out of it at a cost of about twenty-four millions, a little more than a million sterling. They were exempt from the twentieths; but then they made free gifts, they contracted debts for the state, and they were subject to some other charges, the whole reckoned at about a thirteenth part of their clear income. They ought to have paid about forty thousand pounds a year more to put them on a par with the contribution of the nobility.

When the terrors of this tremendous proscription hung over the clergy, they made an offer of a contribution, through the Archbishop of Aix, which for its extravagance ought not to have been accepted. But it was evidently and obviously more advantageous to the public creditor than anything the confiscation could rationally promise. Why was it not accepted? The reason is plain. There was no desire that the church should be brought to serve the state. The service of the state was made a pretext for destroying the church. On their way to destroying the church they would not scruple to destroy their country — and they have destroyed it. One great end of the project would have been defeated if a plan of extortion had been adopted instead of the scheme of confiscation. The new landed interest connected with the new republic, and connected with it for its very existence, could not have been created. This was among the reasons why that extravagant ransom was not accepted.
