Section Four: The Right to Frame a Government for Ourselves

The third head of right asserted from the pulpit of the Old Jewry — the "right to form a government for ourselves" — has at least as little support from anything done at the Revolution, in precedent or in principle, as the first two of their claims. The Revolution was made to preserve our _ancient_ indisputable laws and liberties, and that _ancient_ constitution of government which is our only security for law and liberty. If you want to know the spirit of our constitution, and the policy that prevailed in that great period which has secured it to this hour, look for both in our histories, in our records, in our acts of Parliament and journals of Parliament, and not in the sermons of the Old Jewry and the after-dinner toasts of the Revolution Society. In the first you will find other ideas and another language. Such a claim is as ill suited to our temper and our wishes as it is unsupported by any appearance of authority. The very idea of manufacturing a new government is enough to fill us with disgust and horror. At the time of the Revolution we wished, and we still wish, to derive everything we possess as _an inheritance from our forefathers_. On that body and stock of inheritance we have taken care not to graft any shoot alien to the nature of the original plant. All the reforms we have made so far have proceeded on the principle of referring back to antiquity, and I hope — indeed I am persuaded — that any that may be made hereafter will be carefully framed on precedent, authority and example by analogy.

Our oldest reformation is Magna Carta. You will find that Sir Edward Coke, that great oracle of our law, and indeed all the great men who follow him down to Blackstone, are industrious in proving the pedigree of our liberties. They set out to prove that the ancient charter, the Magna Carta of King John, was connected with another positive charter from Henry the First, and that both were nothing more than a re-affirmation of the still more ancient standing law of the kingdom. As a matter of fact these authors appear to be right for the most part, though perhaps not always. But if the lawyers are mistaken in some particulars, that proves my point all the more strongly, because it shows the powerful predisposition towards antiquity with which the minds of all our lawyers and legislators, and of all the people they wish to influence, have always been filled, and the settled policy of this kingdom in regarding its most sacred rights and franchises as an _inheritance_.

Footnote: See Blackstone's Magna Carta, printed at Oxford, 1759.

In the famous law of the 3rd of Charles the First, called the _Petition of Right_, Parliament says to the king, "Your subjects have _inherited_ this freedom" — claiming their franchises not on abstract principles, "as the rights of men", but as the rights of Englishmen, and as a patrimony handed down from their forefathers. Selden and the other profoundly learned men who drew up this Petition of Right were at least as well acquainted with all the general theories about the "rights of men" as anyone who discourses in our pulpits or on your tribune — quite as well as Dr Price, or as the Abbé Sieyès. But for reasons worthy of the practical wisdom that displaced their theoretical science, they preferred this positive, recorded, _hereditary_ title to everything that can be dear to a man and a citizen, over that vague speculative right which would have exposed their secure inheritance to be scrambled for and torn to pieces by every wild and litigious spirit.

The same policy runs through all the laws that have been made since for the preservation of our liberties. In the 1st of William and Mary, in the famous statute called the Declaration of Right, the two Houses do not utter a syllable about "a right to frame a government for themselves". You will see that their whole care was to secure the religion, laws and liberties that had long been possessed and had lately been endangered. "Taking into their most serious consideration the _best_ means for making such an establishment that their religion, laws, and liberties might not be in danger of being again subverted," they open all their proceedings by naming among those _best_ means, "in the _first place_", to do "as their _ancestors in like cases have usually_ done for vindicating their _ancient_ rights and liberties, to _declare_"; and then they pray the king and queen "that it may be _declared_ and enacted that _all and singular_ the rights and liberties _asserted and declared_ are the true _ancient_ and indubitable rights and liberties of the people of this kingdom."

Footnote: 1 William and Mary.

You will notice that from Magna Carta to the Declaration of Right it has been the uniform policy of our constitution to claim and assert our liberties as an _entailed inheritance_, handed down to us from our forefathers and to be handed on to our posterity — as an estate belonging specifically to the people of this kingdom, without any reference whatever to any more general or prior right. By this means our constitution preserves a unity in so great a diversity of parts. We have an inheritable crown, an inheritable peerage, and a House of Commons and a people inheriting privileges, franchises and liberties from a long line of ancestors.

This policy seems to me the result of profound reflection — or rather the happy effect of following nature, which is wisdom without reflection, and above it. A spirit of innovation is generally the product of a selfish temper and narrow views. People who never look back to their ancestors will not look forward to their posterity either. Besides, the people of England know very well that the idea of inheritance supplies a sure principle of conservation and a sure principle of transmission, without in the least excluding a principle of improvement. It leaves acquisition free, but it secures what it acquires. Whatever advantages a state gains by proceeding on these maxims are locked up fast, as in a sort of family settlement, held as in a kind of perpetual trust. By a constitutional policy that works on the pattern of nature, we receive, we hold and we transmit our government and our privileges in the same way that we enjoy and transmit our property and our lives. The institutions of statecraft, the goods of fortune, the gifts of Providence come down to us, and pass on from us, in the same course and order. Our political system is placed in a just correspondence and symmetry with the order of the world, and with the mode of existence decreed to a permanent body made up of transitory parts — in which, by the disposition of a stupendous wisdom that moulds together the great mysterious incorporation of the human race, the whole at any one moment is never old, or middle-aged, or young, but, in a condition of unchangeable constancy, moves on through the varied course of perpetual decay, fall, renewal and progress. So, by keeping to the method of nature in the conduct of the state, in what we improve we are never wholly new, and in what we retain we are never wholly obsolete. By sticking to our forefathers in this way and on these principles, we are guided not by the superstition of antiquarians but by the spirit of philosophic analogy. In this choice of inheritance we have given our frame of government the image of a relation in blood: binding up the constitution of our country with our dearest domestic ties, adopting our fundamental laws into the bosom of our family affections, and keeping inseparable, and cherishing with the warmth of all their combined and mutually reflected affections, our state, our hearths, our graves and our altars.

By this same plan of conforming to nature in our artificial institutions, and by calling in the aid of her unerring and powerful instincts to fortify the fallible and feeble contrivances of our reason, we have derived several other benefits, and no small ones, from regarding our liberties as an inheritance. Always acting as though in the presence of canonised forefathers, the spirit of freedom, which left to itself leads to misrule and excess, is tempered with an awful gravity. This idea of a liberal descent inspires us with a sense of habitual inborn dignity, which prevents that upstart insolence almost inevitably clinging to, and disgracing, those who are the first acquirers of any distinction. By this means our liberty becomes a noble freedom. It has an imposing and majestic aspect. It has a pedigree and illustrious ancestors. It has its bearings and its heraldic arms. It has its gallery of portraits, its monumental inscriptions, its records, its evidences and its titles. We win reverence for our civil institutions on the same principle by which nature teaches us to revere individual men: on account of their age, and on account of those they are descended from. All your sophists cannot produce anything better adapted to preserving a rational and manly freedom than the course we have followed, who have chosen our nature rather than our theories, our hearts rather than our inventions, as the great storehouses and magazines of our rights and privileges.
