This is a letter. A young Frenchman had written to Edmund Burke asking what he thought of events in Paris, and the reply ran to a book, published in London in November 1790. Burke was Irish-born and had spent thirty years in the House of Commons attacking the abuse of power. He had defended the American colonists, pressed the case for Ireland, and was in the middle of a seven-year impeachment of Warren Hastings over what the East India Company had done in Bengal. Everyone expected him to congratulate the French. The immediate provocation was a sermon preached in London to the Revolution Society by Richard Price, welcoming the Revolution, and the first stretch of the book is about that sermon and the clubs that applauded it.

When he wrote, the Revolution was a year old. Louis XVI was still king, there was no Terror, no war, no guillotine, and most educated English opinion thought the French were doing what England had done in 1688. Burke predicted that the paper money would collapse, that the confiscations would go further, that the Assembly could not control its own army, and that the thing would end when some popular general who understood the art of conciliating the soldiery drew the eyes of all men on himself and became master of the whole republic. Napoleon was twenty and took power in 1799, two years after Burke died. At the time the book was treated as the raving of a man who had lost his judgment. It cost him most of his political friendships, and it was answered immediately, by Thomas Paine in Rights of Man and by Mary Wollstonecraft, whose Vindication of the Rights of Woman followed two years later and is in this collection.

One word will mislead a reader who does not know what it meant. Burke's defence of prejudice is not a defence of bigotry. Prejudice for him is inherited judgment, the stock of habits and attachments a people takes for granted and cannot justify on demand, and his claim is that it holds more accumulated reason than any individual can generate in an argument. From that follows the rest: that a society is not a machine that can be taken apart and rebuilt to a drawing, and that rage and frenzy will pull down more in half an hour than prudence and foresight can build up in a hundred years.

He is also, often, indefensible, and none of it has been softened here. The passage on Marie Antoinette and the ten thousand swords embarrassed his own allies. The phrase about the swinish multitude was thrown back at him for a hundred years. The jeering page about Lord George Gordon's conversion to Judaism is the ugliest thing in the book. The text is complete, with his footnotes kept in place and his long French and Latin quotations given in English beside the originals. Hobbes and Rousseau are in this collection too, and Burke is arguing with Rousseau by name.
