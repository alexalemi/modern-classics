REFLECTIONS ON THE REVOLUTION IN FRANCE (1790)
==============================================
TRANSLATION STRATEGY

WHAT THIS BOOK IS
-----------------
Not a treatise. A letter, to a young Frenchman who had written asking
what Burke thought of events in Paris, published in November 1790 when
the Revolution was fourteen months old, the King still on his throne,
and educated London still cheering. Burke was a Whig who had spent his
career defending the American colonists, prosecuting Warren Hastings
for what the East India Company did in Bengal, and arguing for
conciliation with Ireland. His own party thought he had lost his mind.
Then the Terror came, and the book turned out to have predicted the
shape of it — the confiscations, the paper money, the collapse into
military dictatorship — from arguments made before any of it happened.
It is the founding text of modern conservatism, and it was written by
a man in a rage.

THE JUSTIFICATION
-----------------
Screening (ROADMAP.md): calque 39.7, archaism 0.25, mean sentence 33.9
words with 34% over 35. That is the hume/mill class 3 signature — near
zero archaism, very high abstraction — and it is the highest remaining
calque score on the list. But Burke's real difficulty is a fourth thing
that the screening measures only partly:

  THE SENTENCES ARE ENORMOUS AND THEY ARE BUILT LIKE ORATORY.

He was a working parliamentary speaker, and the prose is speech: 150
and 200-word periods that pile subordinate clause on subordinate clause
and then land, hard, on a short one. Unstacking them is nearly the
whole job. But the RHYTHM of the landing is the point, and a
translation that levels every sentence to 20 words destroys the book
while making it technically clearer. Break the long ones where they
have joints. KEEP THE SHORT ONES SHORT.

AND THE MEASURE OVERSTATES IT, exactly as with hume and mill. Burke's
famous sentences are already perfect modern English:
    "But the age of chivalry is gone."
    "A state without the means of some change is without the means of
     its conservation."
    "...a partnership in all science, a partnership in all art..."
Every must_contain pin is pinned UNCHANGED. IF A SENTENCE IS ALREADY
CLEAR, LEAVE IT ALONE.

WHAT ACTUALLY BLOCKS A MODERN READER
------------------------------------
1. TOPICAL DENSITY. Burke assumes you read the newspapers of 1790. Dr
   Richard Price's sermon of 4 November 1789, the Revolution Society,
   the Society for Constitutional Information, the events of 5-6
   October, the assignats, the Caisse d'Église, M. Necker, the Bishop
   of Autun, Bailly, the National Assembly's decrees by date. NAME THEM
   PLAINLY AND GLOSS ON THE SPOT, in a clause, never in a bracket and
   never in a footnote of ours: "Dr Price, the Dissenting minister
   whose sermon began all this".
2. THE VOCABULARY OF EIGHTEENTH-CENTURY POLITICS, used as terms of art:
      prescription    = a right established by long undisputed use
      establishment   = the Church as established by law
      the moneyed
        interest      = holders of government debt, against the landed
      corporation     = a chartered body, not a company
      policy          = statecraft; a settled course of governing
      speculation     = theorising (NOT financial), and always a sneer
      manners         = the whole texture of how people behave
      opinion         = settled public sentiment
      interest        = a stake, a standing claim
      economist       = a political economist; in Burke's mouth an insult
      the people      = the politically constituted nation, NOT everyone
3. FALSE FRIENDS (the bunyan class): suffer (allow), want (lack),
   prevent (go before), sensible (aware), condescend (stoop
   graciously), improve (make use of), pretend/pretension (claim),
   discover (reveal), address (a formal petition), commit (entrust).
4. LATIN AND FRENCH, quoted untranslated because his readers had both.
   Keep the tag, gloss it inline once, and never drop it. The long
   French quotations near the end are set as indented blocks and stay
   that way.

EMPHASIS IS LOAD-BEARING — 442 SPANS
------------------------------------
Gutenberg sets Burke's italics as _x_, which is already what
assemble.EMPH renders. He italicises the word he is about to turn
against his opponent, and the sarcasm lives in the italic. Parity is
checked per file and every marker is checked to RENDER. Do not invent
spans and do not drop them.

SENSITIVE CONTENT — THE VERNE RULE, WITH NO NOTE ANYWHERE
----------------------------------------------------------
This is a book of sustained political anger and the anger is the
argument. Kept entire and unhedged:
  - "a swinish multitude". It is the single most notorious phrase Burke
    ever wrote, it was thrown back at him for a century, radicals
    printed journals called Pig's Meat in reply, and he never took it
    back. Softening it would delete the reception history of the book.
  - The whole treatment of the National Assembly as country attorneys
    and obscure practitioners raised above their station — contempt of
    a specifically social kind.
  - The Marie Antoinette passage entire, including the ten thousand
    swords, which even sympathetic contemporaries thought absurd, and
    which is the emotional centre of the book.
  - His account of the 5-6 October march on Versailles, which is
    partisan and in places factually wrong, as HIS account.
  - The defence of an established church, of hereditary rank, of
    prescription, and of prejudice — that last argued as a positive
    good, which is the passage modern readers find hardest.
  - His contempt for Dissenters, for "the political men of letters",
    and for Jewish financiers (the "Jew brokers" of the assignat
    passages), which is real and is not softened. It is the ugliest
    thing in the book and it is his.
NO EDITOR'S NOTES ANYWHERE IN THE VOLUME. The book is an argument, and
a note saying that some of it has aged badly would be the wink the
Verne rule forbids. A reader in 2026 does not need help noticing.

REGISTER
--------
Furious, grieving, sarcastic and — this is what surprises people — very
funny. He is not calm. He is a man watching something he loves being
dismantled by people he considers fools, writing at speed, and the
prose swings between forensic constitutional argument and open
lamentation, sometimes inside one sentence. Keep the swings. Keep the
sarcasm sharp; Burke's irony is dry and it dies if it is explained.
Keep the direct address to "Sir" and "you" throughout: this is a letter
to one young man, and it never stops being one.
