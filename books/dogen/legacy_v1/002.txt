Tenzo Kyokun: Instructions for the Cook (1237)

From of old the house of the buddhas has had six officers. They are all
children of the Buddha, and together they do the Buddha's work. Among
them the cook is the one whose office is to look after the food for the
assembly.

The Rules of the Zen Monastery say: he exists in order to make offering
to the assembly. From ancient times the post has been filled by
teachers who had the mind of the way and by men of high resolve who had
raised the aspiration. It is one whole piece of practising the way. If
a man has no mind for the way, he will labour and suffer over it and in
the end get nothing from it.

The Rules say: put the mind of the way to work; change what you do as
the occasion changes; and let the assembly be at ease and get the good
of it. In the past Guishan and Dongshan held this office, and so did
many other great ancestors. It is not the same thing as being a cook or
a steward in the world.

While I was in China, I used my free days to ask former officers and
old hands about it, and they told me a little of what they had seen and
heard. What they said is the very marrow left to us by the buddhas and
ancestors of old who had the way.

You should read the Rules of the Zen Monastery through until you know
them, and then listen to the old hands on the details.

Here is a day and a night of the work.

After the midday meal, go to the prior and the steward and draw the
materials for tomorrow's rice and gruel — the rice, the vegetables and
the rest. Once you have them, guard them as you would your own eyes.
Chan master Yong of Baoning said: guard the permanent property of the
monastery as your own eyes. Handle them with the same respect you would
show food set before the emperor. Raw or cooked, keep that attitude
towards all of it.

Then the officers meet in the office and settle what tomorrow will be:
what flavours, what vegetables, what kind of gruel. The Rules say that
the materials drawn and the number of dishes for the rice and the gruel
are to be settled beforehand with the officers of the storehouse — the
officers being the prior, the steward, the assistant, the rector, the
cook and the labour steward. When the dishes are settled, write them
out and post them at the abbot's quarters and outside the monks' hall.

Then set about preparing the next morning's gruel. Wash the rice and
prepare the vegetables with your own hands and your own eyes on them,
working with diligence and a sincere mind. Do not for one instant be
slack or careless. Do not attend to one thing and let another go. Do
not let a single drop out of the ocean of merit; do not fail to add a
single grain of dust to the mountain of good.

The Rules say: if the six tastes are not right and the three virtues
are not there, the cook is not serving the assembly properly.

Watch the rice, and watch for the grit in it. Look carefully, again and
again, and do not let your mind wander from it. Then the three virtues
will be complete of themselves and the six tastes will all be there.

When Xuefeng was cook under Dongshan, he was washing rice one day.
Dongshan asked him: do you strain out the sand and keep the rice, or
strain out the rice and keep the sand?

Xuefeng said: I throw out sand and rice together.

Dongshan said: then what will the assembly eat?

Xuefeng turned the basin over.

Dongshan said: some day you will meet another man, and he will settle
it for you.

That is how the men of high resolve of the old days who had the way did
this work — with their own hands, and with exactness. How can those of
us who come later be casual about it? The old ones used to say: for the
cook, the mind of the way turns on rolling up his sleeves.

If any grit gets thrown out with the rice, pick it out yourself. The
Rules say: when you are preparing food, look at it yourself, and it
will be clean of itself.

Do not throw away the white water the rice was washed in. In the old
days they used a cloth bag to strain it. Put the water for the gruel
into the pot, and then look after it: do not let mice get at it or let
idle passers-by handle it.

While the gruel is cooking, see to the vegetables, and then to the
other dishes. Take the bowls and buckets and the utensils that will be
used at the midday meal today, wash them with real care until they are
clean, and put them all where they belong. What belongs high, put high;
what belongs low, put low. High things settle at the high level, low
things at the low. Tongs and ladles, everything of that kind — bring
them all together, handle them with a true mind, and put them down
gently.

Then attend to the materials for tomorrow's midday meal. Pick out any
weevils, green beans, chaff, dust, grit or stones in the rice. While
you are picking over the rice and the vegetables, the attendants chant
a sutra and dedicate it to the god of the stove.

Then see to the vegetables and the rest of the material, according to
what the storehouse has issued. Never mind whether it is much or
little; never mind whether it is coarse or fine. Only prepare it with
sincerity. Above all do not let your face show anything or your mouth
say anything about how much or how little of it there is.

Through the whole day and the whole night, let things come to your mind
and let your mind go back to them. Be one with the thing, and put the
same effort into the way. Before midnight, look after what is wanted at
dawn; after midnight, get on with making the gruel.

When the gruel is finished for the day, wash the pot, steam the rice
and prepare the soup. While the rice for the midday meal is soaking,
the cook is not to leave the sink. Watch it with a clear eye and do not
waste a single grain. Wash it properly, put it in the pot, light the
fire and steam it.

There is an old saying: treat the pot as your own head, and treat the
water as your own life.

When the rice is steamed, put it in the basket and then into the tub
and set it on the stand, and see to the vegetables and the soup. The
cook is to be there in person while the rice is steaming and while the
soup is being prepared. He may set the attendants, or the servants, or
the fire-tender to handle the utensils. In the big monasteries lately
there are rice-officers and soup-officers, but they work under the
cook. In the old days there were no such officers; the cook managed the
whole of it.

In handling any material at all, do not look at it with ordinary eyes
and do not think about it with ordinary feelings. Take up a single
blade of grass and build a sanctuary for the treasure-king; enter a
single mote of dust and turn the great wheel of the dharma.

So when you make a soup out of the coarsest greens, do not let a mind
of dislike or contempt or carelessness come up. When you make a soup
rich with cream, do not let a mind of delight and pleasure come up. If
you have no attachment, how can you have any aversion? So although you
are handling something coarse, be in no way slack about it; although
you come across something fine, be all the more diligent with it.

Above all, do not change your mind because the thing has changed, and
do not change your words because of who is in front of you. That is not
a man of the way.

Rouse your resolve and give it your whole mind, hoping to be cleaner
than the men of old, more exact than those who went before. The way to
do this is to make the working of the mind into the substance of the
thing: they of old did it with three coins, and today we do the same
with three coins and make a soup rich with cream.

That is hard to do. Why? Because now and then are utterly different,
and heaven and earth lie between; how could we stand shoulder to
shoulder with them? And yet, when you go about it closely and exactly,
there is a way of seeing that looks down on the very principle of the
ancients. That this is so is not yet clear to you only because your
thoughts fly about like a wild horse and your feelings gallop off like
a monkey in the trees. Let the monkey and the horse once take a step
back and turn their light inward, and it all becomes a single piece.
This is the means by which you, who are turned about by things, can
turn things about instead.

Handle the food, then, and make it clean, and do not lose the one eye.
Take up a single stalk of greens and make it into the sixteen-foot
golden body; take the sixteen-foot golden body and make it into a
single stalk of greens. This is the working of miracles and the work of
transformation; it is the Buddha's work and it benefits living beings.

When the food is ready, close your eyes and count carefully. How many
are on the platforms in the hall; how many are former officers and old
hands; how many are living apart; how many are pensioners and old men
retired; how many are on leave; how many wandering monks are passing
through; how many leather bags there are inside. Go over it and go over
it again. If there is the smallest doubt in it, ask the hall office, or
the head monks, or the officers, and clear the doubt away.

Eat one grain of rice and add one grain of rice. Divide one grain and
you get two half grains, three parts, four parts, one half, two halves.
Add those two half grains together and they make one grain again. Add
nine parts, and how many parts are over? Take in nine parts now, and
see how many he has. Eat one grain of Luling rice and you see Guishan;
add one grain of Luling rice and you see the water buffalo. The water
buffalo eats Guishan; Guishan pastures the water buffalo.

Have I got the measure of it? Have you got the reckoning of it? Not
until you have gone over it and checked it and it is perfectly clear.
Then speak as the moment asks, and say it straight to the man in front
of you.

Do this work as though the second and third day were the first, and do
not forget it for a moment.

When a donor comes to the monastery and gives money for a feast, the
officers are to consult together over that too, which is the old custom
of the monasteries. Share out what has been given by common agreement.
Do not encroach on another's authority or confuse the offices.

When the meal has been properly prepared and set out on the table, the
cook puts on his robe, spreads his sitting-cloth, faces the monks' hall,
offers incense and makes nine full bows. Only after the bows is the
food sent in.

A whole day and night spent preparing the rice and the gruel, with no
time idled away and everything really made ready — every movement of it
becomes of itself the work of nurturing the sacred embryo. To step back
and turn yourself around is exactly the way by which the whole assembly
comes to be at ease.

It is a long time now since the name of the buddha-dharma was first
heard in our country of Japan. But that the food of the monks should be
made according to the dharma is something nobody before us wrote down;
as for the nine bows before the monks' food, it has not been seen even
in a dream. People in this country talk about the food of monks as
though it were the way animals feed. It is genuinely pitiable. How has
it come to this?

When I was at Tiantong, a monk of that district held the office of
cook. One day after the midday meal I was going along the east corridor
towards my quarters, and the cook was in front of the buddha hall
drying things in the sun. He had a bamboo staff in his hand and no hat
on his head. The sun was blazing and the paving was scorching, and the
sweat ran off him as he went up and down working at it. He was clearly
having a hard time of it. His backbone was bent like a bow and his
eyebrows were as white as a crane's.

I went up to him and asked how old he was. He said: sixty-eight.

I said: why do you not get the attendants or a workman to do it?

He said: other people are not me.

I said: you are quite right, sir. But the sun is so hot — why do it
now?

He said: when, if not now?

I said no more. As I walked on along the corridor I began to feel, deep
down, how essential this office is.

Again, in the fifth month of the sixteenth year of Jiading, the year of
the sheep, I was at Qingyuan, and I was talking with the ship's master
aboard the vessel when an old monk came up. He was about sixty-one. He
came straight aboard and asked the master to sell him some Japanese
mushrooms.

I invited him to take tea and asked him where he was from. He said: I
am a man of western Shu. I left my home country forty years ago, and I
am sixty-one this year. I have been round a fair number of monasteries.
Some years ago I was living at Guyun, and then I got a place at
Ayuwang, and I have muddled along there. Last year, when the summer
retreat ended, I was made cook of that monastery. Tomorrow is the fifth
day, and I have nothing good at all to offer. I wanted to make a noodle
soup, and I had no mushrooms, so I came specially to buy some to offer
to the monks who have come from every quarter.

I asked him when he had left the monastery. He said: after the midday
meal.

I said: how far is Ayuwang from here?

He said: thirty-four or thirty-five miles.

I said: and when will you go back?

He said: as soon as I have bought the mushrooms.

I said: we have met today without expecting it, and we are talking here
on the ship. Is that not a good connection? Let me make an offering of
a meal to you, reverend cook.

He said: I cannot. If I am not there to manage tomorrow's offering it
will not be done properly.

I said: surely there is somebody else in the monastery who understands
the rice and the gruel? What is missing if the cook alone is not there?

He said: I have held this office in my old age. It is the practice of
the way of an old man. How could I hand it over to somebody else?
Besides, when I came out I did not get leave to stay a night.

I asked him again: reverend sir, at your age, why do you not sit and
practise the way, or study the sayings of the old masters, instead of
troubling yourself with the office of cook and just doing chores? What
is the good of it?

He laughed out loud and said: my good foreigner, you have not yet
understood what practising the way is, and you do not yet know what
words are.

When I heard him say that I was suddenly ashamed and startled. I asked
him: what are words? What is practising the way?

He said: if you do not stumble over what you are asking, you will be
the man for it.

I did not understand him then. He said: if you have not got it yet,
come to Ayuwang some day and we will talk over the principle of words.

With that he got up and said: it is getting late and I must hurry. And
he went off home.

In the seventh month of the same year, when I was staying at Tiantong,
that cook came to see me. He said: when the retreat ended I gave up
being cook and I am going home. I happened to hear the brothers say
that you were here. How could I not come and see you?

I was delighted and moved, and while we were talking I brought up what
he had said that day on the ship about words and practising the way.

He said: a man who studies words does it in order to know what words
are; a man who works at practising the way does it in order to affirm
what practice is.

I asked him: what are words?

He said: one, two, three, four, five.

I asked him: what is practising the way?

He said: nothing in the whole world is hidden.

We talked of a good many other things, which I will not set down here.
That I know a little of what words are and what practice is, I owe to
that old man.

I told my late master, Myozen, about all this, and he was extremely
pleased.

Later I came across a verse Xuedou wrote for a monk:

	One word, seven words, three words, five words —
	you may search the ten thousand things to the bottom
	and none of them is the ground of it.
	At midnight, the moon white, down over the blue sea:
	hunting for the dragon's pearl, and how many there turn out to be.

What that cook said the year before about words and what Xuedou shows
here agree exactly, and I know all the better that he was a true man of
the way. So the words I had been looking at until then were one, two,
three, four, five; and the words I look at today are six, seven, eight,
nine, ten.

Brothers who come after, look at this side from that side and at that
side from this side, and go about it in that way, and you will get the
one flavour of Zen in words. If you do not, you will be poisoned by the
five flavours of Zen that are current everywhere, and you will not be
able to prepare the food of the monks with a good hand.

Whoever holds this office should hold to three minds: the joyful mind,
the parental mind, and the magnanimous mind.

The joyful mind is the mind that is glad. Think: if I had been born in
the heavens I would be stuck in pleasure without a break, I would not
raise the aspiration, and practice would not be convenient — much less
would I be making food as an offering to the three treasures. Of all
things in the world the three treasures are the most honourable and the
most excellent. The lord of the gods is no comparison, and a
world-turning king is nothing beside them. The Rules say: honoured in
the world, at leisure beyond things, pure and unconditioned — of all
these the assembly of monks is first.

Now I have been born a human being, luckily, and I am making the food
that the three treasures will use. Is that not a great connection? It
is a thing to be very glad about.

Think again: if I had been born in hell, or as a hungry ghost, or as an
animal, or as a fighting demon, or in any of the eight difficult
conditions, then although I might have wanted the shelter of the
monastic community, I could not with these hands have made pure food as
an offering to the three treasures; my body and mind would be bound and
suffering in that vessel of suffering. In this life I can already do
it. This is a birth to be glad of; this is a body to be glad of. It is
a good connection made across a vast stretch of ages; it is merit that
will not rot. Take ten thousand lives and a thousand lives and gather
them into this one moment, and do the work in it. That is how to bind a
good connection for bodies through ten thousand lives.

A mind that sees this clearly is the joyful mind. Truly, even if you
were born the body of a world-turning sage king, if you do not make food
as an offering to the three treasures it comes to nothing in the end;
it is only froth on water, a bubble, a flame.

The parental mind is the mind of a mother and father. It is to hold the
three treasures in mind as parents hold an only child in mind. However
poor and hard-pressed they are, they raise that one child with all
their strength. What that feeling is like, no outsider knows; only a
man who has become a father, or a woman who has become a mother, knows
it. They do not care whether they themselves are rich or poor; they
think only of the child growing up. They do not mind their own cold, and
they cover the child. There is nothing in it but the closest and most
urgent care.

Only a man who has raised such a mind knows it; only a man who is used
to such a mind understands it. So look after the water, look after the
grain, with all the tenderness of raising a child.

Our great teacher Shakyamuni gave up twenty years of the life due to
him so as to shelter us in the last age. What did he mean by it?
Nothing but the parental mind. He was not looking for any result from
it, and he was not looking for wealth.

The magnanimous mind is a mind that is like a great mountain and like
the great sea: it takes no side and it belongs to no party. It picks up
an ounce and does not call it light; it lifts a pound and does not call
it heavy. It does not go chasing after spring because of the sound of
spring, and although it sees the colours of autumn it has no autumn
mind about them. It puts the four seasons into one scene and looks at
light and heavy with the same eye.

At this point you should write the character for "great." You should
know the character for "great." You should study the character for
"great." If the cook of Jiashan had not studied the character for
"great," he would not have laughed the laugh that saved a man. If Chan
master Guishan had not written the character for "great," he could not
have blown three times on the dead ashes. If Dongshan had not known the
character for "great," he could not have wrapped up the whole of it in
three pounds of hemp for a monk who asked.

You should know that all the great teachers who came before us learned
the character for "great" on the tips of a hundred blades of grass, and
now, freely, they make the great sound and speak the great meaning and
take up the great matter and bring great people to completion. All of
it is that one great matter and its causes.

Abbots, officers, head monks, and monks of the assembly — who among you
would ever forget these three minds?

Recorded in the spring of the third year of Katei, the year of the
rooster, and shown to gentlemen who study the way hereafter.

Recorded by the sramana Dogen, abbot and transmitter of the dharma of
Kannon-dori Kosho Horin Zen monastery.
