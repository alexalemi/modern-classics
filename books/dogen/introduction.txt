Dōgen was born in Kyoto in 1200, lost both his parents as a small child, and entered a monastery at thirteen. The question that eventually drove him out of Japan was one his teachers could not answer for him: if every person already has the buddha-nature, as the scriptures he was given kept insisting, then why does anyone need to practise at all? In 1223 he sailed to China to ask it there. He spent four years in Chinese monasteries, found a teacher he trusted in an old abbot named Rujing, and came home in 1227 with no relics and no scriptures.

The two short works in this book are what he wrote about it. The Fukanzazengi, which he composed in the year he got back, is a manual for sitting still: where to put the cushion, what to do with your legs, your hands, your eyes, your teeth and your tongue, and how to let the breath settle. The Gakudō Yōjinshū followed in 1234 — ten sections of advice to students, a good deal of it about how to tell a real teacher from a fraud.

Two facts about the setting change how it reads. The first is that Dōgen wrote in classical Chinese rather than Japanese. That was the learned language of Buddhism in Japan, roughly as Latin was in Europe, which is why a Japanese author is being translated here from the Chinese. The second is that he was writing against the current of his own century. It was widely held that the age had grown too degenerate for anyone to wake up by their own effort, and that the only hope left was rescue by a buddha somewhere else. Dōgen's answer is the flat opposite. Sit down, now, and the sitting is not a technique for arriving somewhere later. It is already the thing.

He is not vague and he is not soothing, which tends to surprise readers who have met Zen in its English incarnation. He is a young man recently back from abroad, certain that he is right, and openly contemptuous of teachers who, as he puts it, count another man's treasure without owning half a coin themselves. Section five of the Gakudō Yōjinshū states that Japan has never produced a true teacher and that a serious student should go to China instead; it is printed here as he wrote it. The most quoted sentence in Zen is also in these pages, sitting in the middle of a posture instruction, between what to do with your tongue and what to do with your breath.
