Bertrand Russell (1872–1970) wrote The Problems of Philosophy in 1911, in the middle of the ten years he spent with Alfred North Whitehead on Principia Mathematica, the attempt to derive mathematics from logic. It was published in 1912 as a small volume in the Home University Library of Modern Knowledge, a series of cheap introductions written by leading authorities for readers with no special training. It has been used as a first book in philosophy ever since.

The book starts from a table. Its colour changes with the light and with where you stand, its shape changes as you walk round it, and what your hand feels depends on how hard you press. Russell asks what, if anything, we know about the table itself, and follows the question outward: to whether matter exists at all, to the difference between knowing a thing directly and knowing it only by description, to induction, to a priori knowledge and the universals it seems to require, and to what truth is. He tries throughout to separate what philosophy can establish from what it can only make less certain, and the last chapter argues that the uncertainty is itself the value.

Two conventions are worth knowing. "Sense-data" (singular "sense-datum") is Russell's term for what is immediately known in sensation, the particular colour, shape or hardness, as distinct from the physical object that is supposed to cause it. And "knowledge by acquaintance" and "knowledge by description" are his, introduced in Chapter V and used through the rest of the book. The Preface thanks G. E. Moore and J. M. Keynes, whose unpublished work he drew on for sense-data and for probability.

This is a restored edition: Russell's words are unchanged. The Project Gutenberg transcription it is built from turned out to follow a later state of the text, in Oxford house spelling and with a few sentences reworded. It was compared word by word with three printings of 1912, two of the British edition and one of the American, and where all three agree this edition follows them. They differ with each other in one place: the British edition says that Russell had not the honour to be acquainted with the Emperor of Russia, where the American printing says the Emperor of China. This edition follows the British first edition. The index, whose page numbers mean nothing in a reflowable book, is omitted. The edition also adds modern typesetting, a proper ebook, and this introduction.
