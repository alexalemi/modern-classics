Michael Faraday started the Royal Institution's Christmas lectures for young people in 1825, and they have run ever since. He gave nineteen of the courses himself. This is the last of them, delivered over Christmas 1860 and the new year of 1861, when he was sixty-nine and near the end of his working life, and it is the most famous course of popular science lectures ever given. Several other volumes from the same series are in this collection, including his own course of the previous winter, On the Various Forces of Nature, which came first.

The device is one candle, and he never drops it. Every question in chemistry is reached by asking something about the flame burning on the table in front of him. How does the wax get up the wick? Why is the flame that shape? Why is it bright? What does it take out of the air, and what does it put back? By the fourth lecture he is making water out of hydrogen and oxygen and taking it apart again; by the sixth he has got from the candle to coal gas, to the air itself, and finally to the audience, because breathing turns out to be the same chemistry as burning and the analogy between a life and a taper is not a figure of speech. The separate Lecture on Platinum, given in February 1861 and appended by the first edition, is here too.

Everything happens in front of the audience and in the present tense. Faraday is doing it while he says it, and the stage directions the shorthand writer took down are kept in square brackets where they fall. He is warm in a way that surprises people — my boys and girls, apologies for his own enthusiasm, constant interruptions to point out that something is beautiful. He was a bookbinder's apprentice who taught himself chemistry out of the books he was sewing, and he never once talks down to the children in the room.

His chemical names are the old ones, and they are kept. He reasons with carbonic acid and carbonic oxide, so those stay his words, each glossed once with the modern name and then left alone. A reader who finishes the book can go and read other Victorian chemistry.

The lectures were taken down and edited by William Crookes, a chemist who was about to make his own name. His explanatory notes sat at the back of the 1861 book behind page numbers; here each one is printed beside the passage it explains, marked as an editor's note, and written in his dry third-person register rather than Faraday's. All thirty-eight of the original woodcuts are restored, with descriptive captions.

The last page of the sixth lecture is the part people quote. It is a wish addressed to the children, and it is kept close to word for word.
