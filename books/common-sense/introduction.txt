Thomas Paine had been in America a little over a year when he wrote this. He was born at Thetford in Norfolk in 1737, made corsets for a living, worked twice as a tax collector and was dismissed both times, and landed at Philadelphia at the end of 1774 with a letter of introduction from Benjamin Franklin and not much else. Common Sense was published anonymously in January 1776, credited on the title page to an Englishman. The text here is the enlarged edition of 14 February, which adds the appendix.

The argument it makes was a minority one at the time. Fighting had been going on since Lexington and Concord in April 1775, but most colonists still understood the quarrel as being with Parliament and the ministry, and still hoped for terms. Loyalty to the King was the thing not in question. Paine attacks the King, and behind the King the whole idea of kings, and then says plainly that the colonies should separate now and set up a government of their own. Nobody had put it in print like that before. The pamphlet sold something like a hundred thousand copies in three months, in a country of roughly two and a half million people, and it was reprinted, pirated and read aloud everywhere. Paine took no profit from it.

The other thing he did deliberately was write it in the plainest English he could manage, for readers who were not gentlemen. There are no Latin tags and no display of classical learning. The arguments are made out of things a farmer or a shipwright already knew: a continent is bigger than an island, a child outgrows milk, nature does not make the satellite larger than the planet. That decision is the reason the pamphlet worked, and it is the ancestor of what this whole collection is trying to do.

The case runs in four sections. Government is a necessary evil and not the same thing as society. Monarchy has no warrant in nature and, Paine argues at length out of the book of Samuel, none in scripture either; hereditary succession is worse, and William the Conqueror was a French bastard who landed with an armed gang. Reconciliation is finished. America has the timber, the ports and the men to manage alone, and no national debt. The appendix answers the King's speech to Parliament and then turns on the Quakers, who had declared against the war.

Paine is funny, rude and in a hurry, and the pamphlet ends on the sentence about having it in our power to begin the world over again. Locke's Two Treatises of Government, which supplies much of the underlying theory, and the Federalist Papers, which argue about what to build eleven years later, are both in this collection.
