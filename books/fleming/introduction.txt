J. A. Fleming gave this course of six lectures at the Royal Institution over Christmas 1901, and published it the next year. The timing is the point. In December of that year Marconi had just pushed a signal across the Atlantic from Cornwall to Newfoundland, Fleming was consulting for him at the time, and the last lecture is a report from the middle of that work. He was professor of electrical engineering at University College London, and he would shortly invent the vacuum tube. The Christmas courses at the Royal Institution are addressed to a juvenile audience, which Fleming says in his preface he takes to include anyone young in spirit; he never talks down, and he assumes a room that can follow a number if it is explained.

The whole course is built on one claim: that a ripple crossing a pond, the bow wave of a liner, a note sung into a tube, the colour of a geranium petal and Marconi's signal are the same thing happening in different media. So he starts by dropping stones into a tank of water, and ends at the aerial on the Cornish coast. In between: why a ship's wake makes the angle it does and what that costs the designers of America's Cup yachts, a row of golf balls on strings standing in for the air, a prism cut from paraffin wax that bends a beam nobody can see, a flame that ducks and roars when two coins are clinked across the room because it can hear a note no human ear can, and a soap film that sings.

Fleming is also a good reporter of the science of his own moment. The air wave from Krakatoa went round the earth seven times. The guns at Queen Victoria's funeral were heard 139 miles away and not at all near the Solent. The firefly's cold light is something we still cannot manufacture.

Underneath all of it is the aether: a real elastic medium filling all space, in which light and radio waves are strains and vibrations. It is the book's organising idea, a third of the text argues for it in detail, and it is wrong — Michelson and Morley had already failed to find it, and Einstein was three years off. It is left here exactly as Fleming has it, asserted and unhedged, with no correction and no note. The same goes for the rest of what has dated. Part of the interest of the book is a first-rate mind being entirely confident about something that was about to evaporate.
