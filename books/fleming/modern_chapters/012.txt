Chapter Three: Waves and Ripples in the Air

(Part 2 of 5)

Let us next consider how a very simple sound, such as an explosion, is produced. Suppose a small quantity of guncotton is detonated. It makes a sound, and therefore an air wave; and the wave is made as follows.

The explosion of the guncotton suddenly creates a large quantity of gas, which gives the air a very violent outward push, a blow. Because of the inertia of the air, it cannot respond to that force everywhere at once; so a certain spherical layer of air is compressed into a smaller volume. That layer almost immediately expands again, and in expanding compresses the next layer outside it and rarefies itself. Then the second layer, expanding in turn, compresses a third, and so on.

So a state of compression is handed on from layer to layer, each compression followed by a rarefaction. The individual air particles are made to move to and fro along the radii of the sphere whose centre is the source of the explosion. We have here what is called a spherical longitudinal wave.

Each air particle swings backwards and forwards along the line in which the wave travels. The actual motion of each particle is exceedingly small.

The speed at which this zone of compression travels outwards is called the velocity of the sound wave, and the distance each air particle moves backwards and forwards is called the amplitude of the wave.

Suppose next that instead of a merely momentary sound like an explosion we have a continuous musical sound. What sort of air movement is carried out then? The experiments already shown will have convinced you that with a musical sound each air particle must repeat the same kind of motion over and over again.

The precise nature of the displacement is best illustrated by two models. In front of you is a frame with a series of golf balls hung from it by threads (see Figure 4 in Chapter One), with a spiral brass spring between each pair of balls which elastically resists both compression and stretching. You will see that the row of balls and springs therefore has properties like those of air. By virtue of the springs it resists compression and expansion; and by virtue of the mass, the inertia, of the balls, any ball which is displaced and then allowed to move back overshoots its position of equilibrium, because it persists in motion.

If we now give the first ball a little pat, you will see a wave-motion run along the line of balls. Each ball in turn moves a little way to and fro, and hands its movement on to its neighbours. Here is an example of a longitudinal wave-motion resembling that of air when a sound wave passes through it.

Another and more elaborate model shows us the sort of motion made in a tube when a sound wave from a continuous musical note passes along it. It consists of a glass disc, blackened, with the paint removed along certain eccentric circular lines. The disc is made to revolve in front of a wide slit in a piece of metal. With an optical lantern we throw an image of the slit onto the screen, and you see it crossed by bright bars of light, crowded together in some places and further apart in others. When the disc revolves, each of those bars moves to and fro in turn, and the result is that the crowded place travels along, is displaced.

A wave of compression is propagated along the slit, and the places where the bars of light are crowded or spread out continually change position. If we imagine the air in a tube divided into slices, represented by these bars of light, the motion of the model exactly represents the motion of the air in the tube when a series of sound waves passes through it.

The distance from one place of greatest compression to the next is called the wave-length of the sound wave. So although a sound such as an explosion may consist in the travel of a single layer of compression, producing a continuous musical note involves the passing on of a whole series of equally spaced zones of compression — of waves.

These models will, I hope, have helped you to form a clear idea of the nature of a sound wave in air. It is a very different thing from a wave on the surface of water, but it has the same general characteristics of wave-motion. It is a state of longitudinal periodic motion in a row of particles, handed on from one to the next. Each particle of air oscillates along the line in which the wave travels, moving a little way backwards and forwards on either side of its undisturbed position.

So a solitary sound wave is a state of air-compression travelling along through otherwise stationary air. The air is squeezed more tightly together in a certain region, and successive layers take up that condition in turn. With water-surface waves, the wave is a region of elevation where the water stands above the general level, and that elevated region is transferred from place to place over otherwise stationary water. With a train of air waves we have similar regions of compression following one another at distances which may be a fraction of an inch or several feet.

In ordinary speech or song the waves are from 2 to 8 feet long — that is, from one compressed region to the next. In a whistle the wave-length may be an inch or two, while the deepest note of an organ produces a sound whose wave-length is about 32 feet.

As in every other case of wave-motion, air waves may differ from one another in three respects: in wave-length, in amplitude, and in wave-form. The first settles what we call the tone, that is, whether the sound is high or low, treble or bass. The second settles the intensity, whether it is faint or loud. The third settles the quality — or, as the Germans expressively call it, the sound-colour, Klangfarbe.

We recognise at once a difference between the sound of a vowel, say "ah," sung by different people to the same note of the piano and equally loudly. There is a personal element, an individuality, about voices which catches our attention at once, quite apart from tone or loudness. This quality of sound is settled by the form of the wave-motion — that is, by the nature of the movement of the air particle during the little excursion to and fro by which it takes its part in making a zone of compression or rarefaction in the air, and so forms a sound wave.

Next we must discuss the speed at which this air compression travels. Everyone knows it is not instantaneous. We see the flash of a gun in the distance and a second or so later we hear the bang. We notice that thunder is often heard long after the lightning is seen.

It would take too long to describe the experiments made to determine the speed of sound waves precisely. It is enough to say that all the best experiments show the velocity of a sound wave in air, at the temperature of melting ice — 0° C., or 32° Fahrenheit — to be very nearly 1,087 feet per second, or 33,136 centimetres per second. That is equivalent to 741 miles an hour, more than ten times the speed of an express train. At that rate a sound wave would take 4 hours to cross the Atlantic, 16 hours to travel half round the world to the antipodes, and about 2 minutes to cross from Dover to Calais.

An opportunity of observing this speed on a gigantic scale came about 20 years ago, at the time of a great volcanic eruption near Java. If you open a map of Asia and look for Java and Sumatra in the Asiatic Archipelago, you will easily find the Sunda Strait; and on a good map you will see a small island marked Krakatoa. This island has — or rather had — a volcano which until 1883 had never been known to erupt. In that year, however, it burst into activity again; and after preliminary warnings a final stupendous outburst came on 27 August 1883.

The roar of that volcanic explosion was probably the loudest noise ever heard on this earth. The pent-up volcanic gases and vapours burst out of some underground prison with such appalling power that they created an air wave which not only circled the earth but travelled to and fro seven times before it finally faded away.

The zone of compressed air forming this mighty wave caused, as it passed from point to point over the earth's surface, a rise of atmospheric pressure which left its record on all the self-registering barometers, and so allowed its progress to be traced. A diligent examination of those records, collected in the celebrated Report of the Royal Society on the Eruption of Krakatoa, showed exactly how the great air wave expanded.

Starting from Krakatoa at 10 a.m. on 27 August 1883, the air wave sped outwards in a circle of ever-increasing diameter until, by 7 p.m. the same day — 9 hours later — it formed a girdle embracing the whole world. This stupendous circular wave, 24,000 miles round, then contracted again, and in another 9 hours had gathered itself to a point in the northern part of South America, which is the antipodes of Krakatoa. There it rebounded and, expanding once more — exactly like a water wave reflected from the side of a circular trough — returned along its own path, so that 36 hours after it set out it had come back to its starting place. Again and again it made the same double journey, each time weaker than before, until after seven times the echoes of this mighty air wave had completely died away.

This is no fancy picture but a sober record of fact, taken from the infallible records of self-registering instruments for measuring air pressure. And we have evidence that the actual sound of the explosion was heard by human ears four hours after it happened, on the other side of the Indian Ocean — which gives us the velocity of sound measured on the largest scale it has ever been measured on.

There are many curious and interesting facts about the transmission of a sound wave through air which affect the distance at which sounds can be heard. The speed of sound in air is much influenced by the temperature of the air and by wind.

The speed of sound increases with temperature. For every degree Fahrenheit above the melting point of ice (32° F.) the speed increases by one foot per second. A more accurate rule is this: take the temperature of the air in degrees Centigrade and add 273 to it — that is, find the value of 273 + t, where t is the temperature. There is one point about the velocity of a sound wave which should not be left unexplained. It has been said that the velocity of a wave in any medium is got by dividing the square root of the elasticity of the medium by the square root of its density. The number representing the elasticity of a gas is the same as the one representing its absolute pressure per square unit of surface; so the volume elasticity of air may be measured by the absolute pressure it exerts on a unit of area, such as a square foot. At the earth's surface the pressure of the air at 0° C. is about 2,116.4 lb. per square foot.

The absolute unit of force in mechanics is the force which gives a velocity of 1 foot per second to a mass of 1 lb. after acting on it for 1 second. If we let a mass of 1 lb. fall from rest under gravity at the earth's surface, after one second it has a velocity of 32.2 feet per second. So the force usually called "a pressure of 1 lb." equals 32.2 absolute units of force. The atmospheric pressure at the earth's surface is therefore 2,116.4 × 32.2 = 68,148 absolute units of force, in the system where the foot, the pound and the second are the fundamental units.

The absolute density of air is the mass of 1 cubic foot. 13 cubic feet of air at freezing point, with the barometer at 30 inches, weigh nearly 1 lb.; more exactly, 1 cubic foot under those conditions weighs 0.080728 lb. avoirdupois. If we divide the number representing the absolute pressure of the air by the number representing its absolute density, we get 844,168; and the square root of that is 912.6.

This calculation was first made by Newton, and he was unable to explain why the velocity of the air wave worked out this way from the general formula for wave speed — 912.6 — should be so much less than the observed velocity of sound, which is 1,090 feet per second at 0° C.

The true explanation of the difference was first given by the celebrated French mathematician Laplace. He pointed out that in air, as in all other gases, the elasticity when it is compressed slowly is less than when it is compressed quickly. A gas is heated when it is compressed; and if we give that heat time to escape, the gas resists the compression less than if the heat stays in it. So air is a little more resilient to a very sudden compression than to a slow one.

Laplace showed that the ratio of the elasticity under sudden compression to that under slow compression is the same as the ratio of the quantities of heat needed to raise a unit mass of air by 1° C. at constant pressure and at constant volume. That ratio is called the ratio of the two specific heats, and is a number close to 1.41. So the velocity calculated above must be corrected by multiplying 844,168 by 1.41 and then taking the square root of the product. When that is done we get 1,091 — which is exactly the observed velocity of sound in feet per second at 0° C. and atmospheric pressure.

The velocity of sound is much affected by wind, by any movement of the air. Sound travels faster with the wind than against it, so the presence of wind distorts the shape of the sound wave by making parts of it travel faster or slower than the rest.

These two facts explain how it comes about that loud sounds are sometimes heard at great distances from their source and yet not heard at places close by.
