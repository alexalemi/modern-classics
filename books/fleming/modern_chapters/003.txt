It can be explained further as follows. Consider a wave-motion model like the one shown in Figure 7, in which a number of hanging heavy balls are connected to one another by elastic threads. Draw one ball in the centre aside and then release it. It will swing to and fro and will start a wave outwards in both directions.

If the row of balls is long enough, it will be seen that the ball which started the wave soon comes to rest, and that the wave-motion is confined to a certain group of balls on either side. As time goes on, the wave-motion in each group dies away on the side nearest the origin and extends on the side further away. So the particular group of balls which is the seat of the visible wave-motion is continually being shifted along.

The rate at which the centre of this active group of vibrating balls moves may be called the velocity of the wave-train. The velocity of the wave itself is something greater, since the waves are all the while travelling through the group. That wave-velocity is worked out numerically by taking the product of the wave-length and the frequency of the motion.

At this point I must explain that waves are not merely a mode of motion; they are a means of conveying energy. It is difficult to give any simple compact definition of what modern scientific writing means by the word energy.

Briefly, we may say that there are two fundamental agencies, or things, in nature with which we are in contact, showing themselves in many different forms but of which the total quantity cannot be changed by anything we do.

One of them is called matter. The term is the collective name for all the substance, all the stuff, we can see or touch, which can be weighed or has weight. All known solids, liquids and gases — ice, water, steam, iron, oil, air — are called material substances, and they have in common the two qualities of taking up room and of having weight.

Experiment has shown that there are some eighty different kinds of simple matter which cannot be turned into one another, and these are called the elements. Every other material substance is made up of mixtures or combinations of them. The elements are therefore like the letters of the alphabet, which taken in groups make up words — the words corresponding to compound chemical bodies.

Exact research has shown that no chemical change taking place in a closed space can alter the total weight, the total amount of gravitating matter, inside it. If a chemist and a quantity of chemicals were shut up in a great glass ball, and the ball were balanced on a gigantic but very sensitive pair of scales, nothing the chemist could do inside his crystal laboratory would alter the total weight of it all by the ten-thousandth part of a grain. He might analyse his chemicals or combine them, burn them or mix them as he pleased; so long as nothing entered or escaped from the ball, the total gravitating mass would stay precisely the same.

This great fact is called the law of the conservation of matter, and it teaches us that although a scuttle of coal may seem to disappear when it is burnt, yet the weight of the ashes and of all the gaseous products of the burning together equals the weight of the original coal plus the air needed to burn it.

Besides the various material substances, we find we have to recognise different forms of something called energy, associated with matter. An iron ball may be more or less hot, more or less electrified or magnetised, or moving with more or less speed. Producing these states of heat, electrification, magnetisation or movement involves transferring energy to the iron, and they are themselves forms of energy.

Energy in all its various forms can be evaluated, measured, in terms of the energy of movement. The energy needed to heat a ball of iron weighing one imperial pound from the temperature of melting ice to that of boiling water is nearly equal to the energy needed to give it a speed of 1,000 feet a second.

In the same way, every definite state of electrification or magnetisation can be expressed in what is called its mechanical equivalent. Moreover, it is found that we can never create any quantity of heat, or mechanical motion, or any other form of energy, without putting out of existence an equivalent amount of energy in some other form.

We are therefore obliged to consider that energy stands on the same footing as matter as regards our inability to create or destroy it; and its constancy in total amount, so far as we can tell, gives it the same character of permanence. The difference is that we cannot, so to speak, earmark a given quantity of energy and follow it through all its transformations in the way we can mark and identify a certain portion of matter.

The moment we go beyond these merely quantitative ideas, however, and start asking further questions about the nature of energy and matter, we find ourselves in the presence of inscrutable mysteries. We are not yet able to analyse into anything simpler this "something" we call energy, which presents itself in the guise of heat or light, electricity or magnetism, movement or chemical action. It is protean in form, intangible, yet measurable in magnitude, and all its changes are by definite equivalent amount and value.

There is a most rigid system of book-keeping in the transactions of the physical universe. You may have anything you like in the way of energy served out to you, but the amount is debited to your account immediately, and the bill has to be settled by paying an equivalent in some other form of energy before you can take the goods away from the counter.

Matter in its various forms serves as the vehicle of energy. We have no experience of energy apart from matter of some kind, nor of matter altogether devoid of energy. We do not even know whether the two things can exist separately, and we can give no definition of one which does not somehow presuppose the existence of the other.

Coming back, then, to waves: we may say that a true wave can only exist where energy can be associated with a medium in two forms, and that the wave is a means by which that energy is carried from place to place.

It has already been explained that a true wave can only be created in a medium which elastically resists some kind of deformation and which persists in motion by virtue of inertia. When a material has such a resistance to some kind of strain or deformation, of such a character that the deformation disappears when the force creating it is withdrawn, it is called an elastic material.

This elasticity may arise from various causes. Air resists being compressed, and if the compressing force is removed the air expands again: it has what is called elasticity of bulk. In the case of water with a free surface there is, as we have seen, a resistance to any change of level at the surface; this might be called an elasticity of surface form.

Whenever an elastic material is strained or deformed, energy has to be spent on it to create the deformation. Winding up a watch spring, stretching a piece of rubber, compressing some air, or bending a bow all require an expenditure of energy.

As long as the material is held strained, it is said to have potential energy associated with it. That term is not a very expressive one, and it would be better to call it energy of strain, or of deformation. If we relax the bent bow, however, or release the compressed air, the energy of strain disappears and we find it replaced by energy of motion. The arrow that flies from a bow carries away with it, as energy of motion, part of the energy of strain that was in the bent bow.

A little examination of wave-motion shows us, then, that at any instant we always have both energy of strain and energy of motion associated with the material the wave is travelling in. It can be shown that in a true wave of permanent type, the whole energy at any one moment is half energy of strain and half energy of motion — or, as it is called, half potential and half kinetic.

So if we consider a wave travelling along a line of elastically connected balls, at any one moment some of the balls are moving at their greatest speed and some are at the extremity of their swing. The first have energy of motion; the second have energy of strain.

Or look at a train of sea waves. Some parts of the water are at any moment lifted high above the average level of the sea, or lie far below it, but are otherwise nearly at rest. Those portions have what is called potential energy, or energy of position. Other parts of the water are at the average level of the sea but are moving with considerable speed, and those portions have energy of motion. Every other part of the wave has both energy of motion and energy of position to some degree; and it can be shown that the energy of the whole wave is half of the one kind and half of the other.

As a wave travels over the surface, wave-energy is continually being given to portions of the water in front of it and taken away from others behind. In the very act of setting a fresh particle of water oscillating, the portions already vibrating must diminish their own motion. They may hand on the whole of their energy to their neighbours, or only part of it. This distinction is a very important one, and it decides whether a single act of disturbance will create a solitary wave in a medium or a wave-train.

[Figure 12: A row of glass or steel balls hung by threads so that they just touch. Lift the first and let it fall against the second, and the last ball of the row flies off — the whole of the energy has been handed along from ball to ball.]

The difference may be illustrated as follows. Consider a row of glass or steel balls hung by threads so that they are quite close to each other (see Figure 12). Draw the first ball back and let it fall against the second. The result is that the last ball of the row flies off with a jerk. Here the whole energy given to the first ball is transmitted along the row.

The first ball, falling against the second, exerts a pressure on it which slightly squeezes both out of shape; and that pressure is just enough to bring the first ball to rest. The second ball in turn expands after the blow and squeezes the third, and so on. So, by Newton's third law of motion — that action and reaction are equal and opposite — the pressure produced by the blow of the first ball is handed on from ball to ball, and finally makes the last ball fly off.

In this case, because of the rigid connection between the elastic balls, each hands on to its neighbour the whole of the energy it receives. But suppose we separate the balls slightly and give the first ball a transverse, side-to-side swing. Then, since there is no connection between the balls, the energy given to the first would not be handed on at all, and no wave would travel.

Between these two extremes — all the energy transferred and a solitary wave produced, and no energy transferred and no wave produced — we have a condition in which an initial disturbance of one ball gives rise to a wave-train, and part of the energy is transferred.

For if we connect the balls to one another by loose elastic threads and then give the first ball a sideways impulse as before, it will pull the second one and set it swinging, but it will itself be pulled back and to some extent robbed of its motion. The same sharing or division of energy will take place between the second and third balls, and the third and fourth, and so on.

So the initial solitary vibration of the first ball is drawn out into a wave-train, and the energy originally given is spread out over a number of balls instead of being concentrated in one. As time goes on, therefore, the wave-train is always growing longer and the oscillation of each ball is dying away; the original energy gets spread over a wider and wider area, over more and more balls — but it travels more slowly than the wave-velocity for that medium.

There need be no difficulty in distinguishing between the idea of a wave-velocity and that of a wave-train velocity, if we remember that a wave travels a distance equal to one wave-length in the time taken by one oscillation. So the wave-velocity is measured by dividing the wave-length by the time of one complete vibration.

If, for example, the wave-length of a water wave is 4 inches, and we observe twelve waves passing a given point in 3 seconds, we can infer at once that the wave-velocity is 16 inches per second. The transfer of energy, however, may take place in such a way that the whole group of waves moves forward much more slowly. The group moves forward because waves are dying out at its rear and being created at its front; and in the case of deep-water waves the rate at which the group moves is half the velocity of a single wave.

A very rough illustration of this difference between a group velocity and an individual velocity may be given by supposing a barge being slowly towed along a river. Let a group of boys run along the barge, dive over the bows, come up at the stern and climb in again. Then the velocity of the group of boys on the barge is the same as the speed of the barge; but the speed of each individual boy through space is the speed of the barge plus the speed of the boy relative to the barge. If the barge is towed at 3 miles an hour and the boys run along the boat at 3 miles an hour too, then the velocity of the group of boys is only half that of an individual boy — the one being 3 miles an hour and the other 6.
