Note: The wave-velocity for waves on deep water varies as the square root of gλ/2π, where λ is the wave-length. The rule in the text is deduced from this formula.

The rule above for the speed of deep-sea waves — wave-velocity equals the square root of 2¼ times the wave-length — combined with the general rule that wave-velocity equals wave-length multiplied by frequency, gives us a useful practical way of finding the speed of deep-sea waves passing any fixed point.

Suppose that well out at sea there is a fixed buoy or rock, and we notice waves racing past it and want to know their speed. We may do it as follows. Count the number of waves that pass the fixed point in a minute, and divide that number into 198; the answer is the speed of the waves in miles per hour. So if ten waves a minute race past a fixed buoy, their velocity is very nearly 20 miles an hour.

Note: If V is the velocity of the wave in feet per minute and V′ the velocity in miles per hour, then (V′ × 5280)/60 = V. But V′ = √(2¼λ), and V = nλ, where λ is the wave-length in feet and n the frequency per minute; from which we get V′ = 198/n, which is the rule given in the text.

Waves have been observed by the Challenger 420 to 480 feet long with a period of 9 seconds. These waves were 18 to 22 feet high, so their speed was 50 feet per second, or nearly 30 knots. Atlantic storm waves are very often 500 to 600 feet long with a period of 10 to 11 seconds. Waves half a mile in length, with a period of 23 seconds, have been observed by officers of the French navy.

It has already been explained that in deep-sea waves the individual particles of water move in circular paths. It can be shown that the diameter of those circular paths decreases very rapidly with the depth of the particle below the surface, so that at a depth equal to only one wave-length, the circle described by each water particle has a diameter only 1/535 of the one at the surface. So storm waves at sea are a purely surface effect. A few hundred feet down — a small distance compared with the depth of the ocean — the water is quite still even when the surface is being tossed by fearful storms, except for whatever steady movement may be due to ocean currents.

Note: The amplitude of disturbance of a particle of water at a depth equal to one wave-length is 1/e^2π of its amplitude at the surface. (See Lamb's Hydrodynamics, p. 189.)

By a more elaborate examination of how wave-motion travels on a fluid, Sir George Stokes showed many years ago that besides the circular motion of the water particles making up the wave, there is also a transfer of water in the direction the wave is moving; the speed of that transfer depends on the depth and falls off rapidly as the depth increases.

This effect, known to sailors as the "heave of the sea," can be clearly seen by watching waves on water that is not very deep. The crest of the wave will be seen to advance faster than the hollow until the wave falls over and breaks; then a fresh wave forms behind it and the process is repeated. So waves break if the depth of water beneath them decreases, and we know from the presence of breakers at any place that there is a shoal or sandbank there.

Next I must point out the difference between a mere wave-motion and a true wave. It has been explained that in a wave-motion each of a series of neighbouring objects carries out the same movement in turn. We have all seen the wind blowing across a cornfield on a breezy day, producing a sort of dark shadow which sweeps along the field. That is plainly caused by the wind bending down each row of cornstalks in turn; and as row after row bows and springs up again, we get the appearance of a wave-motion in the form of a rift rushing across the field.

[Figure 6: The painter's comb and the glass slip. The slip is painted with black enamel and a wavy line scratched through it; moved to and fro in front of the comb's teeth, it shows a row of points of light rising and falling in a travelling wave.]

[Figure 7: Heavy balls hung like pendulums and tied to one another by elastic threads. Because the threads resist stretching and the balls have inertia, drawing the end ball aside and letting it go sends a true wave along the row.]

A very similar effect can be produced, and another illustration of a wave-motion given, in this way. Coil a piece of brass wire into an open spiral like a corkscrew and fix a small fragment of sealing-wax to it (see Figure 5). Hold it in the sun and let its shadow fall on paper. Then turn it round like a screw. We shall see that the shadow of the spiral is a wavy line, and that as it turns, the humps appear to travel along just as the crests of sea waves do — but the shadow of the little bit of sealing-wax simply moves up and down.

Another wave-motion model can be made as follows. Get a painter's comb — a thin steel plate cut into long narrow teeth. Get also a slip of glass about 3 inches wide and 12 inches long. Paint one side of the glass with black enamel varnish, and when it is quite dry scratch a wavy line on it (see Figure 6). Place the glass slip close in front of the comb, in front of the light, and, holding the comb still, move the glass slip to and fro lengthways. The observer will see a row of dots of light lying along a wavy line, and as the glass moves these will rise and fall. If the movement is quick enough, a wave will appear to travel along.

In all these displays of wave-motion the movement of the particles is due to a common cause, but the moving particles do not control each other's motion. There is no connection or tie between them.

Suppose, however, that we hang up a series of heavy balls like pendulums and connect them to one another with elastic threads (see Figure 7). Then we have an arrangement along which we can propagate a true wave. Draw the end ball to one side and notice what happens when it is released. The first ball, being displaced, pulls the second one through a smaller distance, and that one the third, and the third the fourth, and so on. This happens because the balls are tied together by elastic threads which resist stretching.

When the first ball is released it is pulled back by the tension of the thread joining it to its neighbours, and it starts to return to its old place. But the ball has a quality called inertia, so that once set moving, its motion persists until some opposing force brings it to rest. So the returning ball overshoots the mark and passes to the other side of its original resting place. That displacement stretches the elastic threads joining it to its fellows, and so creates a restraining or retarding force which brings it to rest and drives it back again the way it came.

We see, then, that each ball must oscillate, must swing to and fro, and that its movement is gradually passed on to its neighbours. A wave-motion is thus started, and a true wave travels along the line of balls, in consequence of the presence of elasticity and inertia.

The necessary conditions for producing a true wave in a medium of any kind are therefore: first, that the medium must elastically resist some sort of deformation; and second, that when it has been deformed at any place and returns to its original state, it must overshoot the mark, must persist in movement, because of inertia or something equivalent to it.

Note: This can easily be shown to an audience by projecting the apparatus on a screen with an optical lantern.

Briefly, any material or medium in which, or on which, a true self-propagating wave-motion can be made must resist and must persist. It must have an elastic resistance to some change or deformation, and it must have an inertia which makes it persist in movement once it is set moving. These two qualities, or others equivalent to them, must always be present if we are to get a true wave in a medium.

These things are best understood by considering, for example, how surface waves are produced on water. Let us ask ourselves first what alteration, what change, a water surface resists. The answer is that, for one thing, it resists being made unlevel. A still water surface is everywhere level. If we try to make it unlevel by pouring water onto it at one point, or by heaping it up, the surface resists. We can dig a hole in sand, or heap sand up into a hillock, but we know perfectly well we cannot do the same with water. If, for instance, some water is put into a glass tube shaped like the letter U, it stands at the same level in both limbs.

Again, water being a heavy substance, once it is set moving it cannot be brought to rest instantly. Like every other body, it has inertia. So if we do manage by any means to make a depression in a water surface for an instant, the water immediately presses in to fill the hole — and more than that: it overshoots the mark, and because of its inertia it creates a momentary hump, an elevation, in the very place where an instant before there was a depression.

That elevation subsides into a hollow again, and the process goes on until the motion of the water is brought to rest by friction, or by the gradual dispersal of the original energy.

The way a wave is started on the surface of water by these two qualities — resistance to being made unlevel, and persistence in motion — is beautifully shown by studying the waves made when stones are thrown into a pond. The events that give rise to the expanding wave are over so quickly, however, that they can only be studied with the help of instantaneous photography.

The most interesting work on this subject is Professor A. M. Worthington's, who has photographed, by the exceedingly brief light of an electric spark, the various stages of what happens when a drop of water or a stone falls into water. These photographs show us everything that happens as the falling object touches the water, and the way it gives rise to the wave or ripple that results. That hole, at a certain stage, begins to fill up. The water rushes in from all sides, and the impetus carries the inrushing water up so far that it builds a tall pillar of water in the very place where an instant before there was a hole (see Figure 10). Nobody could have anticipated so extraordinary an effect; but the instantaneous photographs which reveal it, taken by the light of an electric spark, cannot but be truthful.

The next stage is that this pillar of water breaks up and falls back onto the surface. So the water where the drop plunges in is subjected to two violent impulses — a downward one followed by an uplifting one. The effect of that is exactly like giving a blow to the connected string of balls in the model shown in Figure 7: it sends off a wave. So much for the events revealed by the flash of an electric spark. But after them comes a long train of interesting wave-making performance which can be watched with the eye, or photographed stage by stage with a hand camera. This wave production is best seen when a large stone is thrown into the calm water of a lake or pond.

There is a story told of the great artist Turner, that he once spent a morning throwing stones into a pond. A friend reproved him for his idleness. "No," said the painter, "I have not been idle; I have learnt how to paint a ripple." If an artist's eye has to be so carefully trained to notice all there is to see when a stone is thrown into a pond, it is not strange that a careless observer cannot take in at once what really happens to the water in this everyday event.

[Figure 11: A photograph of the expanding wave-train made by a stone thrown into still water: a circular band of ripples, growing outwards and multiplying as it goes, with ripples being born at its inner edge and dying at its outer one.]

The photograph in Figure 11 will show one stage of the event. As soon as the first wave-crest is formed — and we have already explained how it arises — it begins to move outwards in a circle; and as it moves it gives rise to a wave-train, that is, it multiplies itself into a series of concentric ripples, or waves, which move outwards, growing in number but getting smaller as they go.

So if a large stone is thrown far out into a deep, still lake, then after the first splash we shall see a circular wave spreading out from the place where the disturbance was made. As we watch this wave we shall see it growing in size and multiplying itself. At first there is only a single wave; then two, four, seven, ten or more concentric ripples appear, each circular wave expanding and getting fainter but seeming to give birth to others as it moves.

What is more, a very careful examination will show us that the whole group of waves — the wave-train — moves outwards more slowly than any individual wave in it. This observation will introduce the ideas of a wave-train and of a wave-group velocity.

At first it is hard to understand that a group of waves may move more slowly than the individual waves composing it. But if we throw a stone into a pond and look very carefully at what happens, we shall see that the circular expanding band of ripples has an ill-defined but visible inner edge and outer edge, and that the wavelets or ripples composing it are continually being brought into existence at the inner edge and dying away at the outer one. Waves, so to speak, pass through the band of ripples faster than the band as a whole moves forward.

This rather difficult but important idea — the distinction between the velocity of a group of waves and that of an individual wave — was first suggested by Sir George Stokes, who set a question on the subject in a Cambridge examination in 1876; it was afterwards worked out by Professor Osborne Reynolds and Lord Rayleigh.
