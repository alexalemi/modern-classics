Chapter Two: Waves and Ripples Made by Ships

(Part 2 of 4)

We can now gather up the various facts about fluid flow that have been explained, and apply them to the problems raised by the passage of a ship, or a fish, through water.

Consider first a body wholly submerged — a fish, a torpedo, a submarine boat — and ask why there is any resistance at all when we try to drag or push such a body through water. The old-fashioned notion was that the water has to be pushed out of the way to make room for the fish to go forward, and then sucked in again to fill the cavity left behind. Most people who have not been taught the subject probably still have the idea that this so-called "head resistance" is the chief cause of the resistance we meet when we move a body of any shape through water. It is also commonly assumed that the point of making a ship's bows sharp is that they may cut into the water like a wedge and push it aside more easily.

Scientific investigation has shown, however, that both these notions are wrong. The resistance we feel in pulling or pushing a boat through water is not due to any resistance the water offers by virtue of its inertia. No part of the resistance comes from the effort of displacing the water, of pushing it out of the way.

The Schoolmen of the Middle Ages used to debate how a fish could move through water at all. They said the fish could not move until the water got out of the way, and the water could not get out of the way until the fish moved. This and similar perplexities were not cleared up until the true theory of the motion of a solid through a liquid had been worked out.

Briefly, there are three causes, and only three, for the resistance we feel and have to overcome when we try to drag a boat or a ship through water. First, skin friction, from the rubbing of the ship's surface against the water. Second, eddy resistance, from the energy taken up in making eddies in the water. Third, wave resistance, from the energy taken up in making waves on the surface. The skin friction and the eddy resistance both arise from the fact that water is not a perfect fluid. The wave resistance arises, as I shall show, from the unavoidable making of waves by the boat's motion through the water.

For a wholly submerged body such as a fish, only the first two causes apply. The fish, moving along entirely below the surface, makes no waves; but the water clings to its skin, and there is friction between them as it goes. It also creates eddies in the water, which require energy to produce; and wherever mechanical work has to be done, energy being drawn off from a moving body, that implies a resistance to its motion which must be overcome.

So nature, always economical with energy, has fashioned the fish to reduce as far as possible the power it must spend in moving through water. The fish has a smooth, slippery skin — we say "as slippery as an eel." It is covered neither with fur nor with feathers but with shiny scales, so as to keep skin friction to a minimum. It is also regular and smooth in outline: no long ears, no square shoulders, no projecting limbs or organs to give it an irregular outline and set up eddies in the water as it moves.

So when we want to design a body to move quickly under water, we must copy the fish in these respects. That is why a Whitehead torpedo, that deadly instrument of naval warfare, is made smooth and fish-shaped, and why a submarine boat is made cigar-shaped and as smooth as possible.

If the floating object is partly above the surface, there is still skin friction and eddy resistance so far as the submerged part is concerned. So in building a racing yacht the greatest care is taken to make the surface below water of polished metal, or varnished wood, or some other very smooth material, to reduce the skin friction as far as possible.

With bodies as regular in outline as a ship or a fish, the proportion of the driving power taken up in making eddies is not large, and we may say without appreciable error that in their case the whole resistance falls under the two heads of skin friction and wave-making resistance. Which of these two predominates depends on the nature of the surface of the body moving through the water, and on its shape and speed.

Here we may pause to notice that if we could obtain a perfect fluid in practice, we should find that an object of any shape whatever, wholly submerged in it, could be moved about any way we liked without meeting the least resistance. This theoretical conclusion is at first sight so contrary to ordinary preconceived notions that it deserves some attention. As I have said, it is difficult for most people who have not studied the subject carefully to rid their minds of the idea that there is a resistance to the motion of a solid through a liquid arising from the effort of pushing the liquid aside. But that notion, as I have explained, is entirely wrong.

In the light of the stream-line theory of liquid motion, however, the truth of the statement is easy to prove.

Let us begin by supposing that a solid body of regular, symmetrical shape — an oval, say (see Figure 30) — is moved through a fluid with no stickiness or viscosity at all, which therefore does not cling to the solid. Then, if the solid is wholly submerged, the mutual action of liquid and solid will be the same whether we suppose the liquid at rest and the solid moving through it, or the solid at rest and the liquid flowing past it.

[Figure 30: A regular oval solid wholly immersed in a flowing liquid, with the stream-lines dividing the space round it into tubes of flow.]

[Figure 31: Two neighbouring stream-lines defining a single tube of flow, shaded. The tube is narrower opposite the middle of the obstacle than at its ends, so the liquid runs faster and presses less there — and the pressures at the two ends exactly balance.]

If, then, we suppose the perfect fluid to flow round the obstacle, it will distribute itself in a certain way, and its motion can be mapped by stream-lines. There will be no eddies, no rotations, because the liquid is perfect by assumption. Now consider any two neighbouring stream-lines (see Figure 31). They define a tube of flow — the shaded portion — which is narrower in the middle than at the ends. So the liquid, which we shall also suppose incompressible, must flow faster past the middle of the obstacle, where the stream-tubes are narrow, than at the ends, where they are wider.

By the principle already explained, it is clear that the pressure of the fluid will therefore be less in the narrow part of the stream-tube; and from the perfect symmetry of the stream-lines it is plain that there will be greater and equal pressures at the two ends of the immersed solid. The flow of liquid past the solid in fact subjects it to a number of equal and balanced pressures at the two ends, which exactly cancel each other.

It is not quite so easy to see at once that the same is true if the solid is not symmetrical in shape, but it can be established by strict reasoning. The result is to show that when a solid of any shape is immersed in a perfect liquid, it cannot be moved by the liquid flowing past it — and, correspondingly, no force would be needed to move it against and through the liquid. In short, there is no resistance whatever to the motion of a solid of any shape drawn through a perfect, frictionless liquid. With real liquids, which are not entirely free of viscosity, such resistance as does exist is due, as I have said, to skin friction and eddy formation.

Leaving now the movement of wholly submerged bodies through liquids, perfect or imperfect, let us take up the important question of the resistance water offers to the motion of a floating object such as a ship or a swan. Here we have to take the wave-making properties of the floating solid into account.

We have already seen that making a wave on water requires energy to be spent, mechanical work to be done. If a wave is made and travels away over the water, it carries energy with it, and so it can only be created if we have a store of energy to draw on.

Suppose skin friction were absent altogether and the ship floated on a perfect fluid: it would still be true that if the moving object creates waves, it thereby reduces its own motion and needs force applied to it to keep it going. We may say, then, that if any floating object makes waves on the liquid it moves over, those waves rob it of some of its energy of motion. Making the waves will bring it to rest in time unless it is continually urged forward by some outside impressed force; and wave-making is the reason for at least part of the resistance we meet when we try to push it along.

One element of the problem of designing a ship, therefore, is to find a form which will make as little wave-disturbance as possible in moving over the water. It is comparatively easy to find a shape for a floating solid which makes a considerable disturbance when it is dragged along; it is not so easy to design one that makes no waves, or only very small ones.

If we look carefully at a yacht gliding along before a fresh breeze on a sea or lake surface not much ruffled by other waves, we can make out that a ship going through the water creates four distinct systems of waves. Two of them are very easy to see and two are harder to identify. These systems are called the oblique bow and stern waves, and the transverse and rear waves. We shall take each in turn.

The most important and the most easily seen of the four is the oblique bow wave. It is easiest to observe when a boy's boat skims across a pond, and it is readily seen whenever we watch a duck paddling along. Look at a duck swimming on a pond and you will see two trains of little waves or ripples set at an angle to the line of the duck's motion. Both trains are made up of a number of short waves, each of which extends beyond, or overlaps, its neighbour (see Figure 32).

[Figure 32: The two trains of little oblique waves left by a duck swimming on a pond. Each short wave overlaps its neighbour, giving the stepped or echelon arrangement.]

For this reason, from a common French word, these are called echelon waves, and I shall call them so here.

Note: The French word échelon means a step-ladder-like arrangement, but it is usually applied to rows of objects set so that each row extends a little beyond its neighbour. Soldiers are said to march in echelon when the ranks are ordered in that way.

[Figure 33: The same echelon wave system alongside a real yacht under way on smooth water, where the two trains of oblique waves can be identified quite easily.]

Look at a boy's model yacht moving on the water and the same system of waves will be seen; and in any real yacht or steamer moving on smooth water they are quite easy to identify (see Figure 33).

The full explanation of how these bow, or echelon, waves are formed is hard to follow, but in a general way it can be put like this. Suppose we have a flat piece of wood held upright in water, and we give it a sudden push. We notice that, because of the inertia of the liquid, it starts a wave which travels away over the surface at a certain speed. The sudden movement of the wood raises the water just in front of it, and that displacement forms the crest of a wave which is then handed on, propagated, over the surrounding surface.

If two pieces of wood are fastened together at an angle, as in Figure 34, and held partly submerged in water, we find that when this wedge is suddenly thrust forward it starts two oblique waves which move off parallel to the sloping wooden sides. The bows of a ship, roughly speaking, form such a wedge.

[Figure 34: Two pieces of wood fastened together to make a wedge, held upright in the water. Thrust suddenly forward, it starts two oblique waves which travel away parallel to its sloping sides — the bows of a ship in miniature.]

So if we take this wedge, or a ship's bows, to be standing in still water and then pushed suddenly forward, they will start two inclined waves which travel off parallel to themselves.

If we then imagine the wedge leaping forward and repeating the process, two more inclined waves are formed in front of the first pair; and imagining it repeated again, a third pair is formed. The different positions of the ship's bows are shown at 1, 2 and 3 in Figure 35, and c, e and f are the three corresponding sets of echeloned waves. For simplicity the waves are drawn on one side only.

If we now imagine the ship moving forward steadily, her bows are always producing fresh inclined waves that move with her, and she is always, so to speak, leaving the old ones behind. All these echelon waves made by the bow lie within two sloping lines, each of which makes an angle of 19° 28′ with the line of the ship's motion.

That angle can be laid off as follows. Draw a circle (see Figure 36) and produce the diameter BC of the circle by a further length CA equal to its own. From the end A of the produced diameter draw a pair of lines AD, AD′ — tangents — to touch the circle. Each of those lines makes an angle of 19° 28′ with the diameter. If we suppose a ship to be at the point marked A in the diagram, all the echelon waves she makes will lie within the lines AD and AD′.

Note: See Lord Kelvin on "Ship Waves," Popular Lectures, vol. iii, p. 482.

[Figure 35: The bows of a ship shown at three successive positions, 1, 2 and 3, with the corresponding sets of echeloned waves c, e and f left behind. For clearness the waves are drawn on one side only.]

Moreover, the angle of those lines does not alter whether the ship goes fast or slow. This is easily seen with a duck swimming on a lake. Throw bits of bread to a duck so as to make it swim faster or slower, and watch the system of oblique, echelon ripples its body makes. You will see that the angle at which the two lines enclosing both trains of ripples meet does not change as the duck changes speed.

[Figure 36: The construction for the echelon angle. The diameter BC of a circle is produced by its own length to A, and the two tangents AD, AD′ are drawn; each makes an angle of 19° 28′ with the diameter, and all a ship's bow waves lie within them.]

This echelon system of oblique waves is really only part of a system which is completed by a transverse group behind the vessel. Lord Kelvin gave a drawing of the complete system in his lecture on "Ship Waves," part of which is shown by the firm lines in Figure 37. The complete system is hard to see with a real ship under way. The inclined rear system can sometimes be seen well from the deck of a lake steamer, such as those on the great Swiss or Italian lakes, and can sometimes be caught in a snapshot of a boy's yacht skimming across a pond.

[Figure 37: Lord Kelvin's drawing of the complete ship-wave system — the oblique echelon waves and the transverse group astern together — of which the firm lines show a part.]

Besides the oblique bow waves there is a similar system produced by the stern of a vessel, though it is much harder to detect. The other two systems a ship makes are generally called the transverse waves. There is a system whose crest-lines lie at right angles to the ship, and these can be seen in profile against the side of any ship or yacht as she moves along. These transverse waves are really due to the unequal pressures that result from the way the stream-lines are distributed as the water moves past the ship.
