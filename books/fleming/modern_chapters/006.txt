If we make two sets of ripples from origins P and Q (see Figure 18) at different distances from a flat reflecting boundary, it is not hard to trace out that each set is reflected independently, and by the rule just given. Here we catch a glimpse of a principle which will come before us again when we speak of aether waves, and which explains the familiar optical fact that when we look at our own reflection in a mirror, the image appears to be as far behind the glass as we are in front of it.

[Figure 17: A circular ripple meeting a straight barrier. The reflected ripple, shown by the dotted curves, is also circular, and travels as though it came from a point Q as far behind the barrier as the true origin P lies in front of it.]

[Figure 18: Two ripple origins, P and Q, at different distances from the same flat boundary. Each set is reflected independently and by the same rule, each producing its own image behind the barrier.]

A very pretty experiment can be shown by fitting into the trough an oval band of metal bent into the form of an ellipse. If two pins are stuck into a sheet of card, a loop of thread laid loosely round them, and a pencil used to trace a curve by holding the loop taut and carrying it round, we get a closed curve called an ellipse (see Figure 19). The positions of the two pins, A and B, are called the foci.

It is a property of the ellipse that the two lines AP and BP, called radii vectores, drawn from the foci to any point P on the curve, make equal angles with a line TT′ drawn to touch the ellipse at that point, called a tangent. If we draw the tangent TT′ at P, then only a little geometry is needed to see that the line PB lies in the same position and direction as if it had been drawn through P from a false focus A′, as far behind the tangent as the real focus A lies in front of it.

It follows that circular ripples diverging from one focus A of an ellipse must, after reflection at the elliptical boundary, be brought together at the other focus B. This can be shown very prettily with the apparatus I have described.

[Figure 19: The ellipse traced with two pins and a loop of thread. A and B are the foci; the tangent TT′ touches the curve at P, and A′ is the false focus, as far behind the tangent as A is in front of it.]

A strip of thin metal is bent into an elliptical band and set in the lantern trough. The band is wide enough for the water in the trough to come about halfway up it. At a point corresponding to one focus of the ellipse, drops of water are let fall on the surface to start a series of diverging ripples. When the stroboscopic disc is set turning and its speed properly adjusted, we see that the diverging ripples setting out from one focus are all brought together, concentrated, at the other. Indeed the ripples seem to set out from one focus and to be swallowed up at the other.

When, in a later chapter, we come to discuss how sound waves are produced and reflected in air, you will be able to call this to mind; and it will be clear to you that if, instead of waves on water, we made waves in the air inside a room of the same elliptical shape, then waves started at one focus would all be gathered at the other, and the tick of a watch or a whisper would be heard at the point corresponding to that other focus, though it might not be heard anywhere else in the room.

With the appliances described here, many beautiful effects can be shown illustrating the independence of different wave-trains and their interference. If we throw two stones into a lake a little way apart, and so create two sets of circular ripples (see Figure 20), we notice that the two ripple-trains pass freely through one another, each behaving as though the other did not exist. A careful examination will show, however, that in some places the water surface is not raised or disturbed at all, and in others the disturbance is increased.

[Figure 20: Two sets of circular ripples, from two stones thrown in a little way apart, passing freely through one another — with places where the disturbance is doubled and places where it disappears altogether.]

If two sets of waves set out from different origins and arrive at the same spot together, then clearly, if the crests of both, or the hollows of both, reach that point at the same instant, the agitation of the water will be increased. But if the crest of a wave from one source reaches it at the same moment as the hollow of an equal wave from the other, it is not hard to see that the two waves will wipe each other out.

This mutual destruction of wave by wave is called interference, and it is a very important fact about wave-motion. It is not too much to say that whenever we can prove interference exists, that alone is almost decisive proof that we are dealing with a wave-motion.

The conditions under which interference can happen must be looked at a little more closely. Suppose two wave-trains of equal velocity, equal wave-length and equal amplitude, or wave-height, set out from two points A and B (see Figure 21). Consider any point P. What is the condition for the waves from the two sources to destroy each other there?

Plainly it is that the difference between the distances AP and BP shall be an odd number of half wave-lengths. For if in the length AP there are 100 waves, and in the distance BP there are 100½, or 101½, or 103½ waves, then the crest of a wave from A will reach P at the same instant as the hollow of a wave from B, and there will be no wave at all at P. This holds for every position of P at which the difference of its distances from A and B is that same amount.

[Figure 21: Two sources of equal waves, A and B. At the point P the difference of the distances is an odd number of half wave-lengths and the waves cancel; at Q it is an even number and they reinforce, doubling the wave-height.]

But again, we may choose a point Q such that the difference of its distances from A and B is an even number of half wave-lengths, so that while there are, say, 100 waves in the length AQ, there are 101, or 102, or 103 in BQ. When that is so, the two wave effects conspire, help each other, at Q, and the wave-height is doubled.

So if we have any two points A and B which are sources of equal waves, we can mark out curved lines along which the difference of the distances from the two origins is constant. These curves are called hyperbolas (see Figure 22).

[Figure 22: The system of hyperbolas drawn between two wave sources. Along each curve the difference of the distances from the two origins is constant, so the combined disturbance is either doubled or destroyed all along it.]

[Figure 23: The shadow pattern of two interfering sets of ripples, with the white lines along which the waves are annulled tracing out hyperbolic curves.]

All along each hyperbola the disturbance produced by the combined effect of the waves is either doubled or wiped out, compared with what each wave-train would give by itself. With the apparatus described, we can arrange to create and adjust two similar sets of water ripples from origins not far apart; and looking at the complicated shadow pattern their interference makes, we can trace out certain white lines along which the waves are annulled, and those lines are hyperbolic curves (see Figure 23).

With the same appliances another characteristic of wave-motion, equally important, can be shown well.

[Figure 24: The circular tank with a thick semicircular plate of glass making one half of it shallower. Ripples starting in the deep half are bent where they cross the boundary, and run shorter and closer together in the shallow half.]

We make one half of the circular tank in which the ripples are generated much shallower than the other, by laying a thick semicircular plate of glass in it. It has already been explained that the speed at which long waves travel in a canal increases with the depth of the water. The same is true, with certain restrictions, of ripples made in a confined space or tank one part of which is much shallower than the rest. If waves are made by dropping water on the surface in the deeper part of the tank, they will travel faster there than in the shallower part.

We can then set the water-dropping jet so that it creates circular ripples which start in deep water but at certain places cross a boundary into shallower water (see Figure 24). The left-hand side of the circular tank shown in the diagram is shallower than the right.

When this is done we notice two interesting facts: that the wave-lines are bent, or refracted, where they cross the boundary; and that the waves are shorter, closer together, in the shallower region. This bending, or refraction, of a wave-front as it crosses the boundary between two districts in which the wave has different velocities is an exceedingly important characteristic of wave-motion, and we shall meet the analogous facts again when we speak of waves in air and waves in aether.

[Figure 25: A row of soldiers, ab, marching from smooth grass onto a rough field across the oblique boundary SS. The man on the left crosses first and is slowed while his comrades on the right march on freely, so the whole line is swung round — which is refraction.]

I must explain in a little more detail how it comes about that the wave-line is bent. Imagine a row of soldiers, ab, marching over smooth grass but heading towards a very rough field, the line of separation SS between the smooth ground and the rough being oblique to the line of soldiers (see Figure 25). Suppose further that the soldiers can march 4 miles an hour over the smooth grass but only 3 over the rough field.

Let the man on the extreme left of the line be the first to step across the boundary. He at once passes into a region where his marching speed is reduced, while his comrade at the extreme right is still going easily over the smooth grass. It is plain, then, that the direction of the line of soldiers must be swung round; for while the man on the extreme left marches, say, 300 feet, the one on the extreme right will have gone 400 feet forward. So by the time all of them have stepped across the boundary, the row of soldiers is no longer going in the same direction as before: it has been bent, refracted.

The same thing happens with waves. If a wave meets obliquely a boundary between two regions, in one of which it travels more slowly than in the other, then for the same reason that the row of soldiers was bent — each man in turn being slowed as he steps across the dividing line — the wave-line, the wave-front, is bent in passing from a place where it moves fast to a place where it moves more slowly. The ratio of the wave's speeds in the two regions is called the index of refraction.

By arranging suitably curved reflecting surfaces, or properly shaped shallow places in a tank of water, we can illustrate all the facts about the changes that reflection and refraction make in wave-fronts.

We can generate circular waves or ripples diverging from a point and turn them, by reflection from a parabolic reflector, into plane waves; and then, by refraction at a curved or lens-shaped shallow, bring those waves to a focus.

Interesting experiments of this kind have been made with capillary ripples on a mercury surface by Mr. J. H. Vincent, who has photographed the ripples so formed and given examples of their reflection and refraction which are well worth study.

Note: "On the Photography of Ripples," by J. H. Vincent, Philosophical Magazine, vol. 43, 1897, p. 411, and also vol. 48, 1899. These photographs of ripples have been reproduced as lantern slides by Messrs. Newton and Co., of Fleet Street, London.

We do not need elaborate apparatus, however, to see these effects, once we know what to look for.

A stone thrown into a lake creates a ripple or wave-train which moves outwards at a few feet a second. If the pond or lake happens to have a submerged wall as part of its boundary, that may make an effective reflecting surface, and as each circular wave meets the wall it will be turned back on itself as a reflected wave.

At the edge of an absolutely calm sea at low tide I once watched little parallel plane waves coming in obliquely to the coast. The edge of the water happened to lie against a rather steep ledge of hard sand, and each wavelet, as it met this reflecting surface, was turned back and reflected at an angle equal to the angle at which it arrived.

It is worth noticing that a plane wave — one whose wave-front is a straight line — may be thought of as made up of a great number of circular waves diverging from points set closely together along a straight line. So if we suppose that a, b, c, d and so on (see Figure 26) are source-points, origins of independent sets of circular waves shown by the firm semicircular lines, and if they send out simultaneous waves equal in all directions, the effect will be nearly the same as a plane wave, shown by the straight thick black line — provided the source-points are very numerous and close together.

Suppose, then, that this plane wave strikes a boundary obliquely. It will be reflected, and its subsequent course will be exactly as if it had come from a series of closely spaced source-points a′, b′, c′, d′ and so on lying behind the boundary, each being the image of the corresponding real source-point and lying as far behind the boundary as the real point lies in front.

[Figure 26: A plane wave built up out of many circular waves from source-points a, b, c, d set close together along a line, and its reflection, which behaves as though it came from the corresponding image points a′, b′, c′, d′ behind the boundary.]

An immediate consequence is that the reflected plane wave-front makes the same angle with the plane reflecting surface as the arriving wave does; and so we establish the law, so familiar in optics, that the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection when a plane wave meets a plane reflecting surface.

At the seaside, when the tide is low and the sea calm, or ruffled only by wavelets raised by a slight wind, one may often notice trains of small waves being reflected at sharp edges of sand, or refracted as they pass into sudden shallows, or interfering after passing round the two sides of a rock. A careful observer can teach himself all the laws of wave-motion in this school of nature, and gather a fund of knowledge on the subject in an hour's dalliance at low tide on some sandy coast, or in the quiet study of seaside pools whose surfaces are corrugated by trains of ripples raised by the breeze.
