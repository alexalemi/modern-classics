Chapter 23: How Kriemhild Planned to Avenge Her Wrongs

They lived together in great honor, it is the plain truth,
until the seventh year. In that time the queen gave birth to a son,
and nothing could have made King Etzel happier. She would not
rest until she had brought it about that Etzel's child was baptized
according to Christian rite. He was named Ortlieb, and there was great
rejoicing over all of Etzel's lands.

Whatever good qualities Lady Helche had possessed, Lady
Kriemhild now worked hard for many a day to match them. Herrat, the
exiled maiden, taught her the customs of the court; in secret, Herrat
still grieved deeply for Helche. Strangers and household alike
knew Kriemhild well. They said that no lady had ever ruled a king's
land better or more generously, and they held that to be the truth.
She carried that praise among the Huns until the thirteenth year.

By now she had learned that no one ever crossed her, as a
king's warriors still do for a prince's wife, and that at every hour
she saw twelve kings standing before her. And she thought of the many
wrongs that had been done her at home. She thought too of the
many honors in the Nibelung land that had once been hers to command,
and that Hagen's hand had stripped from her entirely with Siegfried's
death, and whether that could ever still be made to cost him sorrow at
her hands. "It would, if I could only bring him into this
land." She dreamed that her brother Giselher walked often hand in
hand with her, and that they kissed each other again and again in
gentle sleep. Later they both came to know hardship.

I believe it was the evil devil himself who counseled Kriemhild
to break the friendship she had made with Giselher, the brother she
had kissed in Burgundy as a sign of peace. Then her dress began to be
stained once more with hot tears. It lay on her heart early and
late: how, through no fault of her own, she had been brought to the
point of having to love a pagan man. Hagen and Gunther had forced that
misery on her.

She rarely let that wish go from her heart. She thought: "I am
so powerful, and I have such great wealth, that I can still do my
enemies a wrong. For Hagen of Tronje I would be ready and willing.
My heart aches so often for my loyal kin. If I could be near
the ones who did me wrong, then my husband's death would be well
avenged. I can hardly wait for it," said Etzel's wife.

All the king's men, Kriemhild's warriors, held her dear, and
that was only right. Eckewart had charge of her treasury, and it won
him friends. No one could stand in the way of what Kriemhild wanted.
All the time she was thinking: "I will ask the king." She would
ask him, in the kindest way, to let her kinsmen be brought into the
land of the Huns. No one detected the evil purpose in the queen.

One night, as she lay beside the king and he held her in his
arms, as he always did when he caressed the noble lady — she was as
dear to him as his own life — that glorious woman thought of her
enemies. She said to the king: "My dearest lord, I would like
to ask you something, if it may be done with your good will: that you
let me see whether I have earned it, and whether you are truly fond
of my kinsmen."

The mighty king answered, and his heart was loyal: "I will
make that plain to you. Whatever good fortune comes to those
warriors, in their lives or their goods, I must take joy in it, for a
woman's love never brought me better kin."

The queen said: "You have been told, I am sure, that I have
very high-born kinsmen. That is why it grieves me so that they so
seldom care to see me here. I hear the people call me nothing but an
exile."

King Etzel said: "My dearest lady, if it did not seem too far
to them, I would invite across the Rhine whoever you would like to see
here in my lands." The lady rejoiced when she learned his will.

She said: "If you will keep faith with me, my lord, then send
messengers to Worms across the Rhine. I will let my kinsmen know what
I have in mind, and then many a good and noble knight will come here
to our land."

He said: "Whenever you command it, let it be done. You could
not be gladder to see your kinsmen than I would be to see noble Ute's
children. It troubles me deeply that they have been strangers to us
so long. If it pleases you, my dearest lady, I will gladly send
my fiddlers to Burgundy for your kinsmen." He had the good fiddlers
sent for at once.

They hurried to where the king sat beside the queen. He told
the two of them that they were to be his messengers to Burgundy, and
he had splendid clothes made ready for them. Clothing was
prepared for twenty-four warriors, and the king gave them their
message too: how they were to invite Gunther and his men. Lady
Kriemhild would speak with them separately.

The mighty king said: "I will tell you what to do. I send my
kinsmen my love and every good wish, and ask that they be so kind as
to ride here into my land. I have known very few guests I would be so
glad of. And if Kriemhild's kinsmen wish to do anything at all
for my sake, let them not fail to come to my festival this summer, for
so much of my joy depends on my wife's kinsmen."

Then Swemmel, the proud fiddler, asked: "When is your festival
to be held in these lands, so that we can tell your kinsmen over
there?"

King Etzel said: "At next midsummer."

"We will do whatever you command," said Werbel. The queen asked
that the messengers be brought to her chamber in secret, and there she
spoke with them. Many a warrior later saw little joy from it.

She said to the two messengers: "Now earn yourselves great
wealth, by doing what I want faithfully and well, and by saying what I
send home to our land. I will make you rich, and I will give you
splendid clothes. And whichever of my kinsmen you may see at
Worms on the Rhine, you must never tell them that you ever saw me
troubled in spirit. And carry my service to those bold and good
heroes.

"Ask them to do what Rüdiger asked of them, and so set me free
from all my distress. The Huns think I have no kinsmen at all. If I
were a knight, I would go to them myself now and then. And tell
Gernot too, my noble brother, that no one in the world could be fonder
of him than I am. Ask him to bring our best kinsmen with him into this
land, so that it brings us honor. And tell Giselher to remember
well that I never suffered any wrong through any fault of his. My eyes
would be so glad to see him here. I would love to have him here, for
his great loyalty.

"Tell my mother too of the honor I enjoy. And if Hagen of
Tronje means to stay behind over there, who is to guide them through
the lands? He has known the roads here to the Huns since he was a
child."

The messengers had no idea why they were being told not to let
Hagen of Tronje stay behind on the Rhine. Later it brought them
sorrow. Along with him, many a warrior was marked for a grim death.
Letters and messages were now given to them. They set off rich
in goods, and they could live in style. Etzel gave them leave to go,
and so did his beautiful wife, and they were handsomely dressed in
fine clothes.
