Chapter 25: How the Lords All Rode to the Huns

Now let us leave the story of how they lived at Etzel's court.
Warriors in such high spirits never rode so splendidly into any
king's land: they had everything they wanted, both weapons and
clothes. The lord of the Rhine fitted out his men — a thousand
and sixty knights, as I have heard, and nine thousand squires — for
the festival. Those they left at home would weep for it later.

The gear was being carried across the courtyard at Worms when
an old bishop from Speyer said to beautiful Ute: "Our friends mean to
ride to the festival. May God keep their honor safe there."

Then noble Ute said to her sons: "You should stay here, good
heroes. Last night I dreamed of a terrible danger: every bird in this
land lay dead."

"Whoever goes by dreams," said Hagen, "does not know what the
right thing to say is when his honor is fully at stake. I want my lord
to go to court and take his leave. We should be glad to ride to
Etzel's land. A good hero's hand can serve kings well there, where we
shall see Kriemhild's festival." So Hagen advised the journey; but
later he regretted it.

He would have advised against it, if Gernot had not thrown such
rough words at him. Gernot reminded him of Siegfried, Lady Kriemhild's
husband, and said: "That is why Hagen wants to stay out of the great
journey to court."

Hagen of Tronje answered: "I do nothing out of fear. Whenever
you give the order, heroes, set to work. Truly, I will gladly ride
with you to Etzel's land." Later many a helmet and shield-rim was hewn
apart by his hand.

The boats were ready and many men were there. Whatever clothes
they had were carried on board. They were busy until evening, and then
they left home in the best of spirits. Tents and pavilions were
pitched on the grass on the far side of the Rhine. When that was done,
the king asked his beautiful wife to stay with him a little longer,
and that night she still lay in the arms of her handsome husband.
Trumpets and flutes sounded early in the morning, the signal to
ride, and they got themselves ready. Whoever held a lover in his arms
held her close. Later King Etzel's wife parted many of them in sorrow.

Beautiful Ute's sons had a vassal who was bold and loyal. When
they were about to leave, he told the king privately what was on his
mind. "I have to grieve," he said, "that you are making this journey
to court." His name was Rumold, and he was a hero with his
hands. He said: "Who will you leave your people and your lands to?
What a shame no one can turn you warriors from your purpose! That
news from Kriemhild never seemed good to me."

"The land is in your care, and my little child. Serve the
ladies well; that is my wish. If you see anyone weeping, comfort them.
Truly, King Etzel's wife will never do us any harm."

The horses stood ready for the kings and their men. Many a man
who lived there in high spirits parted with loving kisses. Later many
a lovely woman would have to weep for it.

When the swift
warriors were seen going to their horses, many ladies could be seen
standing there sorrowfully. Their hearts told them that this long
parting meant great harm to come; that never sits easily on anyone's
heart.

The swift Burgundians set out. There was a great stir in the
land; on both sides of the mountains, women and men wept. But whatever
their people did behind them, they rode away cheerfully. The
Nibelung warriors rode with them, a thousand coats of mail strong;
they had left many beautiful women at home whom they never saw again.
Siegfried's wounds still pained Kriemhild.

Gunther's men set their course toward the Main, up through
eastern Franconia. Hagen led them that way, for he knew the road well.
Their marshal was Dankwart, the hero from the land of Burgundy.
As they rode from eastern Franconia toward Schwanfeld, you could
tell the princes and their kinsmen, those praiseworthy heroes, by
their lordly bearing. On the twelfth morning the king came to the
Danube.

Hagen of Tronje rode in front of everyone; he was a steadying
comfort to the Burgundians — the Nibelungs, as they were called now.
The bold warrior got down on the sandy bank and quickly tied his horse
to a tree. The river was in flood, the boats hidden away. It
was a great worry to the Nibelungs how they would get across; the
current was far too wide. Many a fine knight dismounted.

"Harm may well come to you here, lord of the Rhine," said
Hagen. "Now you can see for yourself: the river is in flood and its
current is very strong. Truly, I think we are going to lose many a
good hero here today."

"Why blame me, Hagen?" said the noble king. "For your own
honor's sake, discourage us no more. Find us the ford across to the
far bank, so that we can get both horses and gear away from here."

"I am not so tired of my life," said Hagen, "that I want to
drown in these wide waters. Before that, many a man in Etzel's lands
shall die by my hands; I have every intention of it. Stay by
the water, you proud good knights. I will look for the ferrymen myself
along the river, the ones who will bring us over into Gelfrat's land."
Then strong Hagen took up his good shield.

He was very well armed. He carried the shield off with him, his
helmet laced on, bright enough; and over his coat of mail he wore a
sword so broad that it cut terribly on both edges. He searched
up and down for the ferrymen. Then he heard water splashing and began
to listen: in a beautiful spring, wise women were cooling themselves
and bathing. Hagen noticed them and crept up quietly. When they
realized it, they fled in a hurry, and they were very proud of having
got away from him. The hero took their clothes, and did them no other
harm.

Then one of the mermaids — her name was Hadeburg — said: "Noble
knight Hagen, we will tell you here, if you give us our clothes back,
bold warrior, how this journey to the Huns' court will go for you."

They floated in front of him on the water like birds, and so
their wits seemed to him strong and good. Whatever they wanted to tell
him, he believed it all the more. And what he asked them, they told
him very clearly. She said: "You may ride to Etzel's land in
safety. I pledge my loyalty on it, here and now, that heroes never
rode into any kingdom to greater honor than you will. Believe that,
truly."

Hagen's heart was glad at those words. He gave them their
clothes and waited no longer. But when they had put on their strange
garments, they told him the truth about the journey to Etzel's land.
The other mermaid — her name was Sieglinde — said: "I want to
warn you, Hagen, son of Aldrian. For the sake of the clothes, my aunt
has lied to you. If you go to the Huns, you are badly deceived.
You should turn back; there is still time for that. For you
bold heroes have been invited so that you may die in Etzel's land.
Whoever rides there has taken death by the hand."

Hagen said: "You are lying for no reason. How could it come
about that we should all stay there dead, because of anyone's hatred?"
They began to tell him the story more plainly.

One of them went on: "It must be so: not one of you can
survive there, except the king's chaplain. We know this for certain.
He will come home safe and sound to Gunther's land."

Bold Hagen said in a grim mood: "That would be a hard thing to
tell my lords, that we should all lose our lives among the Huns. Now
show us the way over the water, you wisest of all women."

She said: "Since you will not give up the journey: up there by
the water stands an inn, and inside is a ferryman, and nowhere
else." He asked no more about what he wanted to know.

But one of them called after the troubled warrior: "Wait, Lord
Hagen, you are in far too much of a hurry. Hear the rest, how you can
get across to the far bank. The lord of this border country is called
Else. His brother is the warrior Gelfrat, a lord in Bavaria.
It will go hard with you if you mean to pass through his territory;
guard yourselves well, and deal very carefully with the ferryman.
He is so fierce that he will not let you live unless you keep
your wits about you with the hero. If you want him to ferry you over,
pay him his fee. He guards this land and is loyal to Gelfrat.
And if he does not come in good time, shout across the water
and say your name is Amelrich. He was a good hero who left this land
because of a feud. The ferryman will come to you when he hears that
name."

Arrogant Hagen bowed to the women. He said nothing more, only
kept his silence. Then he went along the water, upstream over the
sand, until he found an inn on the far bank. He began to
shout loudly across the river: "Now come and fetch me, ferryman," said
the good warrior, "and I will give you an arm-ring of red gold for
your trouble. I truly need this crossing, you should know."

The ferryman was so rich that it did not suit him to serve
anyone, and so he very seldom took pay from any man; and his servants
were very proud too. Hagen stood there all the while alone on his side
of the river. Then he shouted with such force that the whole
river rang with it, for the hero's strength was huge: "Now fetch me:
I am Amelrich, Else's man, who fled this land because of a bitter
feud."

High up on his sword he held out an arm-ring to him, bright and
beautiful, of red gold, to be ferried over into Gelfrat's land. The
arrogant ferryman took the oar in his own hand. This same
ferryman had married only recently. Greed for great wealth comes to a
very bad end: he wanted to earn Hagen's red gold, and so he died a
grim death by the sword at the warrior's hands.

The ferryman rowed hard across to the bank. When he did not
find the man he had heard calling, he grew angry in earnest, and when
he saw Hagen he spoke to the warrior in a fury. "You may well
be called Amelrich, but you are nothing like the man I expected to
find here. He was my brother by father and mother. Now that you have
tricked me, you can stay on this side."

"No, by almighty God," said Hagen. "I am a warrior from far
away, with warriors in my care. Take my fee today as a friend and
ferry me over. I am truly well disposed toward you."

The ferryman answered: "That cannot be. My dear lords have
enemies, and that is why I ferry no stranger into this land. If you
value your life, step out onto the sand this minute."

"Do not refuse me," said Hagen, "for my heart is heavy. Take
this good gold from me as a token of friendship and ferry us over: a
thousand horses, and as many men." The grim ferryman said: "That will
never be done."

He raised a strong oar, big and broad, and struck Hagen —
Hagen was not pleased by that — so that he stumbled to his knees in
the boat. So fierce a ferryman had never come the way of the man of
Tronje. Then he wanted to anger the arrogant stranger even
more. He struck him with a pole so that it broke clean apart over
Hagen's head — Hagen was a strong man — and from that Else's ferryman
came to great harm. In a grim mood Hagen reached at once for
his scabbard, where he found a sword. He struck off the ferryman's
head and flung it into the river. The news soon reached the proud
Burgundians.

At the very moment he killed the boatman, the boat drifted
downstream, which troubled him enough. He began to tire before he
could right it again; King Gunther's man pulled with all his strength.
With very swift strokes the stranger turned it, until the
strong oar snapped in his hand. He wanted to get to the warriors out
on a stretch of bank, and there was no other oar. Hey, how quickly he
bound it up with a shield-strap, a narrow band! He steered
downstream toward a wood, and there he found his lord standing on the
bank. Many a handsome man came down to meet him.

The swift good knights received him with a warm greeting. Then
they saw blood steaming in the boat from the deep wound he had dealt
the ferryman, and the warriors asked Hagen plenty of questions.
When King Gunther saw the hot blood sloshing in the boat, how
quickly he spoke: "Why do you not tell me, Hagen, where the ferryman
has gone? I suspect your great strength has cost him his life."

Hagen answered with a lie: "I found the boat by a wild willow
and loosed it with my own hand. I have seen no ferryman here today,
and no one has come to any harm here through my fault."

Lord Gernot of Burgundy said: "Today I must fear for the lives
of dear kinsmen. Since we have no boatmen ready, how are we to get
across? I stand here grieving over that."

Hagen called out loudly: "Lay the gear down on the grass,
squires. I remember that I was once the very best ferryman to be found
on the Rhine. I am confident I can bring you across into Gelfrat's
land."

So that they would cross the river sooner, they drove the
horses in, and the swimming went well, for the strong current took
not one of them; some drifted a long way downstream as they tired.
Then they carried their gold and their clothes to the boat,
since there was no way around the crossing. Hagen was the master
there, and he ferried many a mighty warrior over onto the sand of the
unknown land. First he brought across a full thousand splendid
knights, then his own warriors; and there were still more. Nine
thousand squires he ferried over to that shore. The hand of the man of
Tronje was anything but idle that day.

When he had brought them all safely over the river, the swift
good warrior remembered the strange news the wild mermaids had told
him before. Because of it the king's chaplain nearly lost his life.

He found the priest beside the chapel baggage, leaning with his
hand on the relics. That did him no good at all once Hagen saw him;
God's poor priest had to suffer for it. He flung him out of the
boat, in a great hurry about it. Plenty of them cried: "Grab him, sir,
grab him!" Young Giselher grew angry, but Hagen would not let any of
them stop him.

Lord Gernot of Burgundy said: "What good does the chaplain's
death do you, Hagen? If anyone else did this, you would take it badly.
What has the priest done to you that you have turned on him?"

The priest swam hard; he wanted to survive, if only someone
would help him. But that could not be, for strong Hagen was in a rage
and pushed him under, which no one thought well of. When the
poor priest saw no help coming, he turned back the other way; he had
a hard time of it. He could not swim, but God's hand helped him, so
that he came safe and sound back onto the bank he had left.
There the poor priest stood and shook out his robe. By that,
Hagen saw plainly there was no way around what the wild mermaids had
told him. He thought: these warriors must lose their lives.

When they had unloaded the boat and carried everything off it
that the three kings' men had brought aboard, Hagen smashed it to
pieces and threw it into the river. The bold good warriors wondered
greatly at that.

"Why are you doing that, brother?" said Dankwart. "How are we
going to get across when we ride back from the Huns, home to the
Rhine?" Later Hagen told him that could never be. The hero of
Tronje said: "I do it on this chance: if we have any coward on this
journey who wants to run away from us out of cowardly fear, he will
still have to die a shameful death in this water."

They had with them one man from the land of Burgundy, a hero
with his hands, whose name was Volker. He spoke his whole mind with
great wit. Whatever Hagen did, it seemed good to the fiddler.
Their horses were ready, the pack-horses well loaded. On the
journey so far they had taken no harm that troubled them — except the
king's chaplain, who had to walk back to the Rhine on his own feet.
