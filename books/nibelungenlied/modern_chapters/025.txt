Chapter 26: How They Fought Else and Gelfrat

When they had all come up onto the sand, the king began to ask:
"Who will show us the right roads through this land, so that we do not
ride astray?" Strong Volker said: "I will see to that alone."

"Now hold still, all of you," said Hagen, "knights and squires.
One should listen to one's friends; truly, that seems right to me. I
have grim news to tell you: we shall never come back to the land of
Burgundy. Two mermaids told me so early this morning, that we
shall not come home again. Now I will advise you what to do: arm
yourselves, heroes, and guard yourselves well. We have strong enemies
here, so let us ride ready for war. I thought I would catch
those wild mermaids in a lie. They swore that not one of us would get
home to our land alive, except the chaplain. That is why I wanted so
badly to drown him today."

The news flew from company to company, and swift heroes went
pale with grief as they began to fear a hard death on this journey to
court. Truly, they had good reason. They had crossed at
Möringen, where Else's ferryman had lost his life, and now Hagen spoke
again: "Since I have earned myself enemies on the road, we shall
certainly be attacked. I killed that ferryman early this
morning, and they know the story well by now. So set to work, heroes:
if Gelfrat and Else fall on our company here today, let it end badly
for them. I know them for such bold men that they will not
leave it undone. Let the horses go at an easier pace, so that no one
thinks we are running away along the road."

"I will follow that advice," said the warrior Giselher. "But who
is to guide the company across the land?" They answered: "Let Volker
do it. The bold minstrel knows every path and road here." Before the
wish was fully spoken, you could see the swift fiddler standing there
armed. He laced on his helmet; his battle gear was of a
splendid color, and to his spear-shaft he tied a pennon, and it was
red. Later he came with the kings into terrible peril.

By now sure word of the ferryman's death had reached Gelfrat,
and the very strong Else had heard it too. Both of them grieved for
it. They sent for their heroes, who were quickly ready. In a
very short time — I will let you hear it — men were seen riding toward
them who had done great damage in hard wars, a huge force: seven
hundred or more came to Gelfrat. As they set off after their
fierce enemies, they waited for their lords, who were somewhat too
eager; they wanted to take out their anger on the bold strangers.
Because of that, more of the lords' kinsmen were lost later.

Hagen of Tronje had arranged things well — how could any hero
have guarded his kinsmen better? He took the rear guard with his own
men and his brother Dankwart. That was very wisely done. The
daylight had run out on them; they had none left. He feared harm and
sorrow for his friends. They rode under their shields through the
Bavarian land, and a short while later the heroes were attacked.

On both sides of the road, and hard behind them, they heard
hooves clattering; the men coming were in too much of a hurry. Bold
Dankwart said: "They mean to attack us here. Now lace on your helmets;
I think that would be wise." They halted on their way, as they
had to. In the dark they saw the gleam of bright shields. Hagen would
keep silent no longer: "Who is hunting us on the road?" And Gelfrat
had to answer him.

The margrave of Bavaria said: "We are looking for our enemies,
and we have ridden hard after them. I do not know who killed my
ferryman today. He was a hero with his hands, and I grieve for him
badly."

Hagen of Tronje said: "So the ferryman was yours? He would not
ferry us over. The fault is mine: I killed the warrior, and truly, I
had no choice. I very nearly got my death from his hands. I
offered him gold and clothing as his fee, hero, to carry us over into
your land. That made him so angry that he struck me with a heavy pole,
and I was grim enough about it. Then I got to my sword and
fought off his anger with a deep wound. That is how the hero died. I
will make amends for it to you, however seems good to you." But it
came to a fight; they were hard-set men.

"I knew very well," said Gelfrat, "when Gunther rode through
here with his company, that Hagen of Tronje would do us harm. Now he
shall not live. The hero must be the pledge here for the ferryman's
death."

Gelfrat and Hagen leaned their spears over their shields for
the thrust; each of them wanted at the other. Else and Dankwart rode
splendidly; they tested who the other was, and there was fierce
fighting. How could heroes ever have tested each other better?
A mighty joust from Gelfrat's hand sent bold Hagen over the back of
his horse. His breast-strap broke, and he learned what fighting was.
The crash of shafts rang out from their companies. Then Hagen,
who had gone down from the thrust onto the grass, got up again. I
think he was in no gentle mood toward Gelfrat.

Who held their horses for them I do not know. Hagen and Gelfrat
had both come down onto the sand, and they ran at each other; their
companions helped them, and fighting broke out all around. But
however fiercely Hagen sprang at Gelfrat, the noble margrave struck
such a great piece off his shield that fire flew from it. Gunther's
bold vassal very nearly died of it. He began to call to
Dankwart: "Help me, dear brother! A hero with his hands has set on me,
and he will not let me live." Bold Dankwart said: "I will be the one
to settle this."

The hero sprang closer and struck Gelfrat a blow with a sharp
weapon, and from it he fell dead. Else wanted badly to avenge the man
then, but he and his company left the field with heavy losses.
His brother had been killed, and he himself was wounded; a good
eighty of his warriors stayed there on the spot in grim death. The
lord had to turn and flee from Gunther's men.

As the men of Bavaria gave way and fled, you could hear the
terrible blows ringing out behind them. The men of Tronje chased their
enemies; those who had never thought they would pay for anything were
all in a great hurry now. Still in the pursuit, the warrior
Dankwart said: "We should turn back on this road at once and let them
ride; they are wet with blood. Let us hurry back to our friends. I
advise that in earnest."

When they came back to where the damage had been done, Hagen of
Tronje said: "Heroes, you must see what we are missing here, and whom
we have lost in this fight through Gelfrat's anger." They had
lost four men, and they had to mourn them. But those four were well
paid for: against them a hundred of the Bavarians or more lay dead.
The shields of the men of Tronje were dull with it and wet with blood.

The bright moon broke partly through the clouds. Hagen spoke
again: "No one is to tell my dear lords what we have done here. Let
them stay free of worry until morning."

When the men who had been fighting back there caught up with
the rest, the whole company was suffering badly from weariness. "How
much longer must we ride?" many a man asked. Bold Dankwart said: "We
can have no lodging tonight. You must all ride on until it is
day." Swift Volker, who had charge of the company, had the marshal
asked: "Where are we to spend the night, so that our horses can rest,
and my dear lords too?"

Bold Dankwart said: "I cannot tell you that. We cannot rest
until it begins to get light. Wherever we find a place then, we will
lie down on the grass." When they heard that news, some of them took
it hard.

No one gave them away, red with hot blood as they were, until
the sun sent its bright shining out over the hills to the morning and
the king saw that they had been fighting. The hero said angrily:
"What is this, friend Hagen? I think you did not care to have
me with you when your mail-rings got wet with blood like this. Who
did it?"

He said: "Else did it. He attacked us in the night; we were set
upon because of his ferryman. Then my brother Dankwart's hand struck
Gelfrat down. Else got away from us after that, and he had good reason
to. A hundred of theirs and four of ours were left dead in the fight."

I cannot tell you where they lay down to rest. Afterward all
the people of the countryside learned that noble Ute's sons were
riding to court. Later they were well received at Passau. The
noble kings' uncle, Bishop Pilgrim, was very glad when his nephews
came into his land with so many warriors. That he wished them well
they learned very soon. Friends welcomed them along the roads,
but at Passau there was no way to house them all. They had to cross
the river to where they found open ground, and there huts and tents
were pitched.

They had to stay there a whole day, and the full night as well,
and how well they were looked after! After that they had to ride into
Rüdiger's land, and the news of it reached him very soon.

When the road-weary men had taken their rest and were drawing
nearer to that land, they found a man asleep on the border, and Hagen
of Tronje took a strong weapon from him. The good strong
knight's name was Eckewart. He was heavy-hearted about it, that he had
lost his weapon through the heroes' coming. They had found Rüdiger's
border badly guarded.

"Oh, the shame of this," said Eckewart. "Truly, I grieve
bitterly for this journey of the Burgundians. Since I lost Siegfried,
my joy has been gone. Oh, Lord Rüdiger, how badly I have served you."

Hagen heard the noble warrior's distress plainly. He gave him
back his weapon, and six red-gold arm-rings besides. "Take these,
hero, as a token that you are my friend. You are a bold warrior,
however alone you lie out here on the border."

"God reward you for your rings," said Eckewart. "But still I
grieve bitterly for your journey to the Huns. You killed Siegfried,
and you are hated here. Guard yourselves well; I advise you that in
all loyalty."

"Now may God protect us," said Hagen. "These warriors, the
kings and their men too, have no other worry now than lodging: where
in this land we are to find a night's shelter tonight. Our
horses are worn out on the hard roads," said the warrior Hagen, "and
our food has run out. We find nothing for sale anywhere. We need a
host who, out of his own goodness, would give us his bread tonight."

Eckewart answered: "I will show you a host so good that you
have seldom been lodged as well in any land as you may be here, if
you swift warriors are willing to go and see Rüdiger. He lives
right by the road, and he is the best host who ever kept a house. His
heart brings forth good things the way sweet May brings the grass
its flowers. Whenever he can serve heroes, he is glad at heart."

King Gunther said: "Will you be my messenger, and ask whether
my dear friend Rüdiger will take us in for my sake, my kinsmen and our
men? I will repay that with my service forever, as well as I ever can."

"I will gladly be your messenger," said Eckewart. With a very
good will he set out on the road and told Rüdiger what he had heard.
In many a year no news had come to him that pleased him so much.

At Pöchlarn a warrior was seen riding hard. Rüdiger himself
recognized him and said: "There comes Eckewart hurrying along the
road, one of Kriemhild's men." He thought enemies had done him some
harm. He went out in front of the gate, where he found the
messenger. Eckewart unbuckled his sword and laid it from his hand. The
news he brought was not kept back; it was quickly told to the host and
his kinsmen.

He said to the margrave: "Gunther, lord of the land of
Burgundy, has sent me to you, and his brother Giselher, and Gernot
too. Each of those warriors sends you his service here. Hagen
and Volker send the same, loyally and eagerly. And I will tell you
more: the king's marshal sends you word by me that the good squires
are badly in need of your lodging."

Rüdiger answered with a laugh: "What good news this is for me,
that the noble kings are willing to accept my service! It will not be
refused them. If they come to my house, I am glad and happy."

"Dankwart the marshal asked me to let you know whom you would
have to house along with them: sixty swift warriors, a thousand good
knights, and nine thousand squires." At that Rüdiger was glad at
heart.

"How lucky I am in these guests," said Rüdiger, "that these
warriors are coming to my house, when I have had so little chance to
serve them. Now ride out to meet them, kinsmen and vassals alike."

Knights and squires hurried to their horses; whatever their
lord commanded seemed right to all of them, and they set about his
service all the faster. And still Lady Gotelind knew nothing of it,
sitting in her chamber.
