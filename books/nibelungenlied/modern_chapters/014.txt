Chapter 15: How Siegfried Was Betrayed

On the fourth morning thirty-two men were seen riding to court,
and mighty Gunther was told that war had been declared on him. Out of
that lie grew the greatest sorrow Lady Kriemhild ever knew. The
men were given leave to come before the king, and they claimed to be
Liudeger's men, the king whom Siegfried's hand had beaten before and
brought as a hostage into Gunther's land. The king greeted the
messengers and told them to sit down. One of them spoke up: "My lord,
let us stand until we have delivered the message we were sent to bring
you. Believe me, you have many a mother's son for an enemy.
Liudegast and Liudeger, whom you once hurt so badly, declare war on
you. They mean to ride into this land with an army." The king began to
rage when he heard the news.

The false messengers were sent off to their lodgings. How could
Lord Siegfried have guarded himself, he or anyone else, against what
they were plotting? In the end that plot brought great sorrow on the
plotters themselves. The king went about whispering with his
kinsmen, and Hagen of Tronje never let him rest. Plenty of the king's
men would still have put a stop to it, but Hagen would not be turned
from his plan for a single day.

One day Siegfried found them whispering, and the hero from the
Netherlands began to ask: "Why are the king and his men going about so
sadly? If anyone has done him any wrong, I will always help him avenge
it."

Lord Gunther said: "I have good reason to be sad. Liudegast and
Liudeger have declared war on me. They mean to ride openly into my
land."

"Then Siegfried's hand will stop that," said the bold warrior,
"eagerly, and in a way that does full justice to your honor. I will
deal with those warriors as I dealt with them before: I will lay their
castles and their lands in ruins before I turn back, and my head is
your pledge of it. You and your warriors are to stay at home, and
let me ride against them with the men I have. I will let you see how
gladly I serve you. Believe me, your enemies are going to suffer at my
hands."

"How glad I am to hear it," said the king, as if he were
honestly delighted at the help; and the treacherous man bowed low to
him, with falseness in his heart. Lord Siegfried said: "You need not
worry at all."

So they prepared the campaign with the squires, all of it staged
for Siegfried and his men to see. He had his men from the Netherlands
get ready too, and Siegfried's warriors looked out their fighting
gear. Then strong Siegfried said: "My father Siegmund, you are to
stay here. If God gives us luck, we will be back on the Rhine in a
short while. You are to stay here with the king and enjoy yourself."

They fastened on their banners as if they meant to march, and
plenty of Gunther's men there had no idea what it was really about.
A great company could be seen gathered around Siegfried. They
tied their helmets and their coats of mail onto the horses, and many a
strong knight made ready to leave the land. Then Hagen of Tronje went
to find Kriemhild and asked her leave to go, since they were about to
leave the country.

"How glad I am," said Kriemhild, "that I ever won a husband who
dares to stand up so well for my dear kinsmen, as my lord Siegfried is
doing for my family. That puts me in high spirits," said the queen.
"Dear friend Hagen, remember this: that I serve you gladly and
never once bore you any hatred. Let my dear husband have the benefit
of that. He must not pay for it if I did anything to Brunhild. I
have been sorry for it since," said the noble lady. "And he has beaten
me black and blue for it, because I ever said the things that troubled
her heart. That bold and good hero has avenged it thoroughly."

He said: "You and she will be reconciled soon enough, in a few
days' time. Kriemhild, my dear lady, tell me how I can serve you in the
matter of Siegfried, your husband. I will gladly do it, my lady, for
there is no one I would rather do it for."

"I would be free of all worry," said the noble lady, "that anyone
might take his life in battle, if only he would not follow his own
arrogance. Then that bold and good warrior would be safe."

"My lady," Hagen said, "if you have any fear that he can be
wounded, you must let me know what kind of cunning I need to prevent
it. I will always ride and walk at his side to guard him."

She said: "You are my kinsman, and I am yours. I entrust my dear
love to your loyalty, so that you will guard my beloved husband well."
And she told him things that had better been left unsaid.

She said: "My husband is bold, and strong enough too. When he
killed the dragon at the mountain, the gallant warrior bathed in its
blood, and since then no weapon has ever cut him in battle. And
yet I am afraid whenever he stands in the fighting and so many spears
fly from heroes' hands, afraid that I will lose my dear husband there.
Oh, the worry I so often suffer over Siegfried! I am telling you
this, dearest friend, trusting in your good faith, so that you will
keep your loyalty to me: I am going to let you hear where my dear
husband can be cut down. I do it in trust. When the hot blood
flowed from the dragon's wounds and the bold good knight bathed in it,
a broad linden leaf fell between his shoulder blades. There he can be
wounded, and that is why I worry so much."

Hagen of Tronje said: "Sew a small mark on his clothing, so I
know by it where I must guard him when we stand in battle." She
thought she was saving the hero's life; it was done to bring about his
death. She said: "With fine silk I will sew a secret cross on his
clothing. There, hero, your hand must guard my husband when it comes
to the thick of it, when he stands in the fighting before his enemies."

"I will do that, my dear lady," said Hagen. And the lady thought
it would be to his good, but with that Kriemhild's husband was
betrayed. Hagen took his leave, and went away cheerfully.

The king's whole household was in good spirits. I do not believe
any warrior will ever again commit such a monstrous betrayal as Hagen
did then, when Queen Kriemhild put her trust in his loyalty.

The next morning Lord Siegfried rode out cheerfully with a
thousand of his men. He thought he was going to avenge the wrong done
to his kinsmen. Hagen rode so close beside him that he could study his
clothes. As soon as he had seen the mark, he secretly sent off
two of his men, who brought back a different message: that Gunther's
land was to remain at peace, and that Liudeger had sent them to the
king. How unwillingly Siegfried turned back now, before he had
avenged any of his kinsmen's wrong! Gunther's men could hardly turn
him round. Then he rode to the king, and his host began to thank him.

"God reward you for your good will, friend Siegfried, for doing
so willingly what I ask of you. I will always serve you for it, as I
rightly should. I trust you more than any of my kinsmen. Now
that we are rid of this campaign, I mean to ride out hunting bear and
boar in the Vosges forest, as I have done so often." That was Hagen's
counsel, that treacherous man. "Tell all my guests that we ride
out early tomorrow. Those who want to hunt with me should get ready;
and those who would rather stay here and pass the time with the ladies
will please me just as well."

Lord Siegfried said, in his lordly way: "Whenever you ride out
hunting, I will gladly come along. You must lend me a tracker and a
few hounds, and I will ride into the forest."

"Will you take only one?" the king answered at once. "If you
like, I will lend you four who know the woods well, and the trails the
game follows, so that they will not let you lose your way back to
camp."

Then the gallant warrior rode off to his wife, and in no time
Hagen had told the king: "We shall have that excellent warrior yet."
No man should ever practice treachery like that.
