Chapter 21: How Kriemhild Left Worms for the Land of the Huns

Let the messengers ride. Now we will tell you how the queen
traveled through the lands, and where Giselher and Gernot parted from
her. They had served her as their loyalty required. They rode
with her as far as Pföring on the Danube, and there they began to ask
the queen's leave, for they meant to ride back to the Rhine. Good
kinsmen could not part like that without tears.

Swift Giselher said to his sister: "Whenever you have need of
me, my lady, if anything troubles you, send me word, and I will ride
into Etzel's land to serve you."

She kissed those who were her kinsmen on the mouth. Then you
could see the margrave's men, Rüdiger's men, taking a loving leave of
them. The queen had many lovely girls with her, a hundred and
four of them, wearing costly dresses of rich patterned silk, and many
broad shields were carried close beside the ladies on the road. Then
many a splendid warrior turned back from her and rode home.

They moved on quickly, down through Bavaria. Then the news was
told that a great many unknown strangers had hurried on ahead, to the
place where a monastery still stands today, where the Inn flows into
the Danube. In the city of Passau there lived a bishop. The
lodgings emptied, and the prince's court as well, as everyone hurried
up into Bavaria to meet them, to the place where Bishop Pilgrim found
beautiful Kriemhild. The warriors of that country were not at
all sorry to see so many lovely girls riding in her train; their eyes
courted the daughters of the noble knights. Afterwards good lodgings
were given to the guests.

The bishop rode to Passau with his niece. When the townspeople
were told that Kriemhild was coming, their prince's sister's child,
the merchants gave her a fine welcome. The bishop had hoped
they would stay, but Lord Eckewart said: "That cannot be. We must
travel on down into Rüdiger's land. Many warriors are waiting for us
there, for they all know very well that we are coming."

Beautiful Gotelind knew the news by now. She made herself ready
with great care, and her noble daughter too. Rüdiger had sent her word
that he thought it right for her to comfort the queen's spirits
by riding out to meet her with his men, up to the Enns. When
that word was given, the roads were busy on every side: people set out
to meet the guests on horseback and on foot. By now the queen
had come to Eferding. There were plenty of men in Bavaria who, as was
their habit, might have robbed travelers on the road, and they could
easily have done the guests harm. The noble margrave had seen
to it that this did not happen: he led a thousand knights and more.
Gotelind, Rüdiger's wife, had come as well, and with her came many a
noble warrior in splendid style.

When they had crossed the Traun, onto the field by the Enns,
they saw huts and tents set up, where the guests were to spend the
night. Rüdiger paid for all of it. Beautiful Gotelind left her
lodgings behind her and went along the roads, and many a fine palfrey
went with her, its bridle jingling. The welcome was a fine one, and it
pleased Rüdiger greatly. The warriors who came along the roads
from both sides rode so that you had to praise them, and there were
many of them. They practiced their knightly games, which many a girl
watched, and the knights' service did not displease the queen either.
When Rüdiger's men reached the guests, you could see splinters
of lances flying up into the air from the warriors' hands, as knights
do, and they rode before the ladies as if a prize hung on it.

Then they let that be, and many men greeted one another very
warmly. Then they escorted beautiful Gotelind to where she could see
Kriemhild. The men who knew how to serve ladies had little rest.
The lord of Pöchlarn rode to his wife. The noble margravine
was not sorry that he had come home from the Rhine safe and sound; a
good part of her worry gave way to great joy. When she had
welcomed him, he told her to dismount on the grass with all the ladies
who were with her. Many a noble man was kept busy then, serving the
ladies with the greatest care.

Then Lady Kriemhild saw the margravine standing there with her
household. She would let her horse go no nearer, but pulled the
palfrey up by the bridle and asked to be lifted down from the saddle
at once. You could see the bishop leading his sister's child,
he and Eckewart together, to Gotelind. Everyone drew back at once.
Then the exiled queen kissed Gotelind on the mouth. Rüdiger's
wife spoke with great tenderness: "How happy I am, dear lady, that I
have seen your lovely self in these lands with my own eyes. Nothing
dearer could have happened to me in all this time."

"God reward you, noble Gotelind," said Kriemhild. "If I keep my
health, and Botelung's son keeps his, it may yet bring you joy that you
have seen me." Neither of them knew what was bound to happen later.

Many girls went up to one another with perfect manners, and the
warriors were eager to serve them. After the greetings they sat down
on the clover, and they made many acquaintances that had been strangers
to them before. The ladies were served wine; it was already
midday. The noble company did not linger there any longer but rode on
to where they found the many broad tents, and there plenty of service
waited for the noble guests.

That night they rested until early morning, and the people of
Pöchlarn made ready to lodge so many foreign guests. Rüdiger had
arranged it so well that they lacked for very little. The
windows in the walls stood open; the castle at Pöchlarn was thrown
wide. In rode the guests, a welcome sight, and the noble host had good
comfort prepared for them. Rüdiger's daughter went with her
household to greet the queen with great affection, and her mother the
margrave's wife was there too. Many a young lady was greeted with love.
They took one another by the hand and went into a wide hall,
very finely built, with the Danube flowing beneath it. They sat facing
the breeze and passed the time very happily.

What more they did there I cannot tell you. But Kriemhild's
warriors were heard complaining that the journey was going so slowly,
for it grieved them. And what good warriors rode with her from
Pöchlarn! Rüdiger offered them loving service, and the queen
gave Gotelind's daughter twelve red arm-rings, and clothes as good as
any she had brought into this land. The Nibelung gold had been
taken from her, but even with the little she still had, she won the
heart of everyone who saw her. The host's household received great
gifts. In return Lady Gotelind showed the guests from the Rhine
such honor, and so kindly, that very few of the strangers could be
found who were not wearing her jewels or her splendid clothes.

When they had eaten and were ready to leave, the lady of the
house offered Etzel's wife her loyal service, and the young lady, her
daughter, was hugged and kissed a great deal. The girl said to
the queen: "Whenever you think it right, I know my dear father will
gladly send me to you in the land of the Huns." Kriemhild saw very
well how loyal the girl was to her.

The horses were saddled and brought before Pöchlarn, and the
noble queen took her leave of Rüdiger's wife and his daughter. Many a
lovely girl parted from them with a greeting too. They very
seldom saw one another after those days. From Melk people came out
carrying in their hands many rich golden cups filled with wine, to
welcome the guests on the road. A lord lived there named
Astolt, and he showed them the road into Austria, down the Danube
toward Mautern. There the queen was served very well later on.

The bishop parted lovingly from his niece. How earnestly he
urged her to conduct herself well, and to win honor for herself as
Helche had done! And what great honor she won later among the Huns!
They brought the guests on to the Traisen, and Rüdiger's men
cared for them attentively until the Huns came riding across the
country. Then great honor was shown to the queen. Near the
Traisen the king of the Huns had a mighty castle, famous far and wide,
called Traismauer. Lady Helche had lived there before, and had shown
such great goodness that it will surely never happen again,
unless it is through Kriemhild, who also knew how to give. After
all
her sorrow she could well enjoy the joy of it: that Etzel's men honored
her too, and in time she won that honor in full measure among the
heroes.

Etzel's power was so widely known that at all times you could
find at his court the boldest warriors ever heard of, among Christians
and among pagans alike, and they had all come there with him.
At all times there were both at his court, which will surely
never be seen again: Christian ways and also the pagan faith. Whatever
kind of life each man led, the king's generosity saw to it that they
were all given enough.
