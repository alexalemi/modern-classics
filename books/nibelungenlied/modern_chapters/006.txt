Chapter 7: How Gunther Won Brunhild

By now their ship had come so close to the castle that the king
could see many beautiful girls standing high up at the windows. It
annoyed Gunther that he did not know who any of them were. He
asked his companion Siegfried: "Do you know anything about these girls
looking down at us on the water? Whoever their lord may be, they carry
themselves very proudly."

Lord Siegfried said: "Now look quietly over the young ladies and
tell me which one you would take, if you had the choosing of it."

"I will," said Gunther, a bold and daring knight. "I see one of
them standing in that window over there, in a dress white as snow. She
is so beautiful that my eyes would choose her for her loveliness alone.
If I had the power, she would have to be my wife."

"Your eyes have chosen well for you. That is noble Brunhild, the
beautiful girl your heart has been straining after, and your mind and
your spirit with it." Everything about her looked good to Gunther.

Then the queen ordered her fine ladies away from the windows.
They were not to stand there for strangers to stare at, and they
obeyed. What the women did next has since been told to us as well.
They dressed up for the strangers, as pretty women have always
done, and then came back to the narrow windows where they could see
the heroes. It was done for the sake of looking.

There were only four of them who had come to the land. Through
the windows the fine ladies saw bold Siegfried lead a horse out onto
the sand, and King Gunther felt himself honored by it. Siegfried
held that handsome horse by the bridle, a good and beautiful animal,
very big and very strong, until King Gunther was sitting in the
saddle. That is how Siegfried served him, and later Gunther forgot it
completely.

Then Siegfried led his own horse off the ship as well. He had
very seldom done such a service before, standing at any hero's
stirrup; and the fine, noble ladies saw it all through the windows.
The two handsome heroes were a perfect match: their horses and
their clothes were snow-white and exactly alike, and their fine shields
shone in the splendid men's hands. Their saddles were set with
jewels and their breast-straps were narrow. They rode in lordly style
up to Brunhild's hall, with bells of bright red gold hanging from the
straps. So they came into the land, as their courage demanded,
with newly sharpened spears and well-made swords that hung down
to these fine men's spurs; the bold warriors' swords were sharp and
broad. And Brunhild, that splendid girl, saw all of it.

Dankwart and Hagen came with them. We hear it told how those
warriors wore costly clothes of raven black, and their shields were
handsome, big, good and broad. The jewels they wore were seen to
be from the land of India, and glittered gloriously on their clothes.
They left their little ship by the water with no one to guard it, and
so the bold, good heroes rode up to the castle. Inside it they
saw eighty-six towers, three wide palaces, and one well-built hall of
fine marble, green as grass; and there Brunhild herself was, with her
household.

The castle was unlocked and the gates stood wide open. Brunhild's
men ran out to meet them and welcomed the guests into their lady's
land, and gave orders for their horses to be stabled and their shields
taken from their hands. Then a chamberlain said: "You must give
us your swords, and your bright coats of mail as well."

"That you will not get," said Hagen of Tronje. "We mean to carry them
ourselves."

Then Siegfried began to tell him how things really stood. "The
custom in this castle, I will tell you, is that no guest may carry
weapons here. Let them take them away; that is the right thing to do."
Hagen, Gunther's man, went along with it very unwillingly. The
guests were given wine to drink and made comfortable, and many quick
warriors in princely clothes were seen walking about the court on
every side. And the bold strangers were stared at, a great deal.

Now Lady Brunhild was told the news that unknown warriors had
come sailing in on the water, in magnificent clothes, and the good and
beautiful girl began to ask about them. "Let me hear," said the
queen, "who these unknown warriors standing so grandly in my castle
may be, and for whose love these heroes have traveled here."

One of her household said: "My lady, I can say that I have never
seen any one of them before, but one standing among them looks like
Siegfried. You should welcome him well; that is my loyal advice.
The second of the companions is so impressive that he would
easily be a mighty king over wide princely lands, if he had the power
and could hold them. He stands among the others in a truly lordly way.
The third of the companions is grim, and yet handsome too, great
queen. From the fierce looks he keeps giving, I think his mind runs to
anger. The youngest of them is so fine; I see that rich warrior
standing there with a maiden's modesty, so well-mannered and so
winning. And yet we would all have cause to fear if anyone here did
him any harm. However gently he keeps his manners, and however
handsome he is, he could make many a fine woman weep if he once got
angry. He is built for it: in every quality he is a bold and daring
warrior."

Then the queen said: "Now bring me my clothes. And if strong
Siegfried has come into this land for the sake of my love, it will
cost him his life. I am not so afraid of him that I would become his
wife."

Beautiful Brunhild was soon well dressed. A great many lovely
girls went out with her, a good hundred or more, all finely adorned;
the fine women wanted to see the guests. And with them went
warriors from Isenland, Brunhild's men, carrying swords in their
hands, five hundred or more; the guests did not like that at all. The
bold and gallant heroes rose from their seats.

When the queen saw Siegfried, now you may gladly hear what the
girl said. "Welcome, Siegfried, here in this land. What does your
journey mean? I would like to know."

"You do me very great grace, my Lady Brunhild, generous prince's
daughter, in greeting me first, ahead of this noble warrior standing
here in front of me. He is my lord. That honor I would rather do
without. He was born on the Rhine; what more need I tell you? It
is for your love that we have traveled here. He means to woo you,
whatever comes of it for him. Think it over now, while there is time:
my lord will not let you off. His name is Gunther and he is a
great king. If he won your love, he would ask for nothing more. Yes,
it was that handsome warrior who ordered me to come here. If I could
have refused him, I would gladly have stayed away."

She said: "If he is your lord and you are his vassal, then let
him dare to try the games I set him. If he comes out the master, I
will be his wife; and if I win, it will cost every one of you his
life."

Then Hagen of Tronje said: "My lady, let us see these mighty
games of yours. Before Gunther, my lord, had to yield to you, it would
have to go very hard indeed. He is quite confident he can win a girl
as beautiful as you."

"He must throw the stone and jump after it, and throw the javelin
against me. Do not be in too much of a hurry. You may well lose your
honor here, and your lives as well. Think it over very carefully,"
said the lovely woman.

Bold Siegfried went over to the king and told him to say
everything that was on his mind to the queen; he need have no fear of
her. "I will protect you from her with my arts."

Then King Gunther said: "Great queen, set whatever tasks you
command, and even if there were more of them, I would face them all
for the sake of your beauty. I will lose my head unless you become my
wife."

When the queen heard that, she asked for the games to be hurried
on, as was only proper. She had good fighting gear brought for
herself: a coat of mail of red gold and a good shield. The girl
put on a silk surcoat that no weapon had ever cut through in any
fight, of fine silk from Libya, beautifully made, with bright
embroidered borders shining on it.

All this while the warriors were being jeered at and threatened.
Dankwart and Hagen were in no cheerful mood; they were worried about
how the king would come out of it. They thought: "This trip is not
going to do us warriors any good."

Meanwhile Siegfried, that fine man, had gone back to the ship
before anyone noticed, where he found the cloak of invisibility lying
where he had hidden it. He slipped into it quickly, and then no one
could see him. He hurried back and found a great crowd of
warriors where the queen was setting out her lofty games. He went in
among them unseen, by the cloak's arts, so that not one of all those
present saw him.

The ring had been marked out where the games were to take place,
in front of many bold warriors who were to watch them. More than seven
hundred of them were seen carrying weapons, and whoever won the game,
they were to say so.

Brunhild had come. They found her armed as if she meant to fight
for a whole kingdom. Over the silk she wore many bars of gold, and her
lovely coloring shone out gloriously beneath. Then her household
came, carrying a shield of pure red gold with bands of hard steel, very
big and broad, which the lovely girl meant to fight behind. The
lady's shield-strap was a noble band with stones on it green as grass,
of many kinds, that flashed against the gold. The man that lady gave
her heart to would have to be very bold. The shield the girl was
to carry was a good three spans thick under the boss, so we are told,
and rich enough in steel and gold that her chamberlain and three
others could hardly carry it.

When strong Hagen saw that shield brought in, the hero of Tronje
spoke in a grim temper: "What now, King Gunther? How we are going to
lose our lives! The one you want to love is the devil's wife."

Hear more about her clothes, for she had plenty of them. She
wore a surcoat of silk from Azagouc, noble and costly, and against its
color many a splendid stone shone on the queen. Then they brought
the lady a javelin, very heavy and big, sharp, strong and monstrous,
huge and broad, the one she always threw, and its edges cut most
horribly. Hear wonders told of that javelin's weight: a good
three and a half measures of iron had gone into the forging of it, and
three of Brunhild's men could barely carry it. Noble Gunther began to
be badly afraid.

He thought to himself: "What is this? How could the devil out of
hell survive her? If I were back in Burgundy with my life, she could
stay free of my love for a long, long time."

Then Hagen's brother, bold Dankwart, said: "I am sorry from the
bottom of my heart that we ever came to this court. We have always
been called warriors; how we are going to lose our lives, if women are
to get the better of us in this land! It galls me badly that I
ever came into this country. If my brother Hagen had his weapon in his
hand, and I had mine, then all Brunhild's men might walk a little more
softly with their arrogance. You can be sure of that; they would
take good care. And even if I had sworn a thousand oaths to keep the
peace, before I watched my dear lord die, that beautiful girl would
have to lose her life."

"We could leave this land uncaptured," said his brother Hagen,
"if we had the armor we need in this extremity, and our good swords
too. Then the strong lady's arrogance would be nicely tamed."

The noble girl heard very well what the warrior said. With a
smile on her lips she looked back over her shoulder. "Since he thinks
himself so bold, bring them their armor and put their sharp weapons
into the warriors' hands."

When they had their swords again, as the girl ordered, bold
Dankwart went red with joy. "Now let them play whatever they like,"
said the quick man. "Gunther cannot be beaten, now that we have our
weapons."

Brunhild's strength was shown on a grand scale. A heavy stone was
carried to her in the ring, huge and monstrous, very big and round;
twelve bold, quick heroes could barely carry it. She always threw
that after she had thrown the javelin. The Burgundians' fears grew very
great. "God help us," said Hagen, "what has the king got himself for a
sweetheart? She ought to be the foul devil's bride in hell!"

She rolled the sleeves back on her white arms. She took up the
shield on her arm and raised the javelin high, and so the fight began.
Gunther and Siegfried feared Brunhild's hatred, and if Siegfried
had not come to help him, she would have had the king's life. He went
up to him unseen and touched his hand, and Gunther, very alarmed, felt
his arts at work. "What touched me?" thought the bold man. He
looked all round and found no one standing there. Then Siegfried said:
"It is I, Siegfried, your dear friend. You need have no fear at all of
the queen. Give me the shield from your hand and let me carry it,
and mark carefully what you hear me say. You make the motions; I will
do the work." When Gunther knew who it was, it was a joy to him.
"Now keep my arts hidden; you must never tell anyone. That way
the queen will win very little glory from you, whatever she has in
mind. Look how carelessly the lady stands there in front of you."

Then the splendid girl threw with all her strength, at a shield
new, big and broad, that Sieglinde's son carried on his arm. The fire
sprang from the steel as if the wind were blowing it. The edge of
that mighty javelin broke clean through the shield, so that you could
see flame leap out of the rings of the mail. The two strong men both
staggered under the throw, and but for the cloak of invisibility they
would have lain there dead. Blood burst from bold Siegfried's
mouth. But he sprang back fast, and the good hero took hold of the
javelin she had driven through his shield, and strong Siegfried's hand
sent it back at her. He thought: "I will not pierce that
beautiful girl." So he turned the javelin's edge behind his back and
threw it butt-end first against her armor, so hard from his powerful
hand that it rang out loud. The fire flew from her mail-rings as
if the wind were driving it. Siegmund's son had thrown with real
force: with all her strength she could not keep her feet against that
throw. Truly, King Gunther could never have done it.

Beautiful Brunhild was on her feet again fast. "Gunther, noble
knight, my thanks for that throw!" She thought he had done it with his
own strength. A far stronger man had crept up on her. Then she
went quickly, in a furious temper, and the noble, good girl lifted the
stone high, swung it with great force far from her hand, and sprang
after her throw, and all her armor rang. The stone had landed a
full twelve fathoms away, and with her leap the beautiful girl
outdid the throw. Then Lord Siegfried went to where the stone lay.
Gunther heaved at it, and it was the hero who threw it. Siegfried
was bold, very strong and very tall. He threw the stone further and
jumped further too, and by his fine arts he had strength enough to
carry King Gunther with him in the jump.

The jump was over, the stone was lying there, and nobody could be
seen but Gunther the warrior. Beautiful Brunhild went red with anger.
Siegfried had put King Gunther's death a long way off. When she
saw him standing safe and sound at the edge of the ring, she spoke to
her household, rather too loudly: "Come here, quickly, all my kinsmen
and my vassals. You are all to be King Gunther's subjects now."

Then the bold men laid their weapons down and bowed at the feet
of mighty Gunther of Burgundy, many a bold man of them. They thought
he had won the games with his own strength. He greeted them
lovingly; truly, he was rich in every grace. Then the admirable girl
took him by the hand and granted him power over everything there, and
Hagen, that bold and daring warrior, rejoiced at it.

She asked the noble knight to go with her into the wide great
hall, and so it was done. Then the warriors were served better than
before. Dankwart and Hagen had to let that be, with no ill will.
Quick Siegfried was wise enough. He went and put his cloak of
invisibility away, and came back to where many ladies were sitting,
and said to the king, very cleverly: "What are you waiting for,
my lord? Why do you not begin the games the queen is setting you so
many of? Let us see at once what they are like." The cunning man acted
as if he knew nothing about it.

Then the queen said: "How does this come about, Lord Siegfried,
that you have not seen the games that Gunther's hand has won here?"
Hagen of Burgundy answered her. He said: "You had put us in low
spirits, my lady. Siegfried, that good hero, was down by the ship when
the lord of the Rhine won the games from you. That is why he knows
nothing about it," said Gunther's man.

"Good news for me," said Siegfried the warrior, "that your pride
has been laid so low here, and that a man lives who can be your master.
Now, noble maiden, you must follow us home to the Rhine."

The beautiful woman said: "That cannot be. My kinsmen and my
vassals must hear of it first. I cannot leave my land so lightly as
that; my best friends must be sent for first."

Then she had messengers ride out in all directions. She summoned
her friends, her kinsmen and her vassals, and asked them to come to
Isenstein without delay, and ordered that all of them be given rich and
splendid clothes. Daily, late and early, they came riding in
troops to Brunhild's castle. "Oh, no," said Hagen. "What have we done?
We have waited here for beautiful Brunhild's men to our own great
harm. If they come into this land now in strength, we do not know
what the queen intends. What if she is so angry that we are lost? Then
that noble girl was born to be the ruin of us."

Strong Siegfried said: "I will take care of that. What you are
worried about, I will not let happen. I will bring you help here into
this land, chosen warriors you have never yet known. Do not ask
after me; I am going away. Meanwhile may God keep your honor safe. I
will be back soon, and I will bring a thousand men, the very best
warriors I have ever known."

"Only do not be too long," said the king. "We have good reason
to be glad of your help."

"I will be back in a very few days," he said. "Tell Brunhild that you
sent me."
