Chapter 6: How Gunther Sailed to Isenland for Brunhild

Fresh news came up the Rhine. People said that over there lived
many a beautiful girl, and good King Gunther thought he would win one of
them for himself, and the idea sent the warrior's spirits soaring.

There was a queen who lived across the sea, and no one knew of
her equal anywhere. She was beautiful beyond measure and her strength
was enormous. For her love she threw the javelin against swift
warriors; she hurled the stone a great distance and then leaped
far after it. Any man who wanted her love had to win three games
against that highborn lady, every one of them without fail, and if he
lost at even one, his head was forfeit. The young lady had done
this many, many times. A handsome knight by the Rhine heard of it, and
he set his mind on that beautiful woman, and because of that, heroes
would later lose their lives.

The lord of the Rhine said: "I will go down to the sea, to
Brunhild, whatever comes of it for me. For her love I will stake my
life, and I will lose it unless she becomes my wife."

"I advise against it," said Siegfried. "The queen keeps such
terrible customs that any man who courts her love pays a high price for
it. You would truly do well to give up the journey."

"Then here is my advice," said Hagen. "Ask Siegfried to carry the
heavy load with you. That is my counsel now, since he knows so well how
things stand with Brunhild."

The king said: "Will you help me, noble Siegfried, to win that
lovely woman? Do what I ask of you, and if she becomes my beloved wife,
I will stake my honor and my life for your sake."

Siegfried, Siegmund's son, answered: "Give me your sister,
beautiful Kriemhild, that noble princess, and I will do it. I want no
other reward for my pains."

"I pledge it, Siegfried, in your hand," said Gunther. "If
beautiful Brunhild comes here into this land, I will give you my sister
for your wife, and you can live happily with that beauty for the rest
of your days."

So the noble warriors swore oaths on it. And from that their
hardships grew far greater before they brought the lady to the Rhine;
because of it, those bold men later found themselves in deep trouble.

Siegfried had to take the cloak away with him, the one the bold
hero had won, at great risk, from a dwarf named Alberich. The bold and
mighty warriors made ready for the journey. Whenever strong
Siegfried wore that cloak of invisibility he had strength enough: the
strength of a full twelve men on top of his own. With great cunning he
won that splendid woman. The magic cloak was made in such a way
that anyone inside it could do whatever he liked, and still no one
could see him. That is how he won Brunhild, and sorrow came to him for
it.

"Now tell me, Siegfried, before my journey begins: if we are to
arrive at the sea with honor, should we take warriors into Brunhild's
land? Thirty thousand fighting men could be summoned quickly enough."

"However many men we took," Siegfried answered, "the queen keeps
such terrible customs that every one of them would die of her
arrogance. I will show you a better way, bold and worthy warrior.
We will go down the Rhine like knights traveling on their own,
and I will name you the men it must be: just four warriors, we will go
to the sea, and that is how we will win the lady, whatever happens to
us afterward. I am one of the four; you must be the second; let
Hagen be the third, and we may well come through alive; and the fourth
Dankwart, that very bold man. A thousand others would never dare to
stand against us in a fight."

"I would like to know one thing," the king said, "before we leave
here, and I would be very glad of it: what clothes should we wear in
front of Brunhild, what would suit us there? You must tell Gunther
that."

"The very best clothing anyone ever found is worn at all times in
Brunhild's land. So we must wear rich clothes before the lady, or we
will be shamed when people hear the story told."

The good warrior said: "Then I will go myself to my dear mother
and see if I can persuade her to have her beautiful maids help make us
clothes we can wear with honor before that glorious girl."

Hagen of Tronje said, in his lordly way: "Why would you ask your
mother for a service like that? Let your sister hear what you have in
mind. Her help will serve you well on this journey to a foreign court."

So he sent word to his sister that he wished to see her, and the
warrior Siegfried too. Before they came, the beauty had dressed herself
to perfection. The coming of those bold men gave her no grief at all.
Her household too was dressed as befitted it. The two princes
came, and when she heard it she rose from her seat and went with
perfect courtesy to receive the noble guest and her brother.

"My brother is welcome, and his companion with him. I would like
to know," the girl said, "what you lords want, since you come to court.
Let me hear how things stand with you noble warriors."

King Gunther said: "My lady, I will tell you. Along with our high
spirits we have great cares to carry. We mean to ride courting, far
into foreign lands, and for the journey we ought to have fine clothes."

"Now sit down, dear brother," said the king's daughter, "and let
me hear exactly who these ladies are that you want to woo in other
kings' lands." The lady took both those chosen men by the hand
and went with them to where she had been sitting before, on rich
cushions, I know it well, worked with fine figures raised in gold.
There the men could pass the time pleasantly among the ladies.
Friendly glances and kindly looks: a great many of those passed
between the two of them. He carried her in his heart; she was as dear
to him as his own life. Later beautiful Kriemhild became strong
Siegfried's wife.

The mighty king said: "My dearest sister, without your help it
cannot be done. We want to go adventuring in Brunhild's land, and there
we will need splendid clothes to wear in front of the ladies."

The young lady said: "My dear brother, whatever help of mine can
be of any use, I will show you that I am ready to give it. If anyone
else refused you, that would grieve Kriemhild. Noble knight, you
should not ask me anxiously; you should command me, like a lord.
Whatever you want of me, I am ready, and I will do it gladly," said the
lovely girl.

"We want to wear good clothes, dear sister, and your noble hand
must help to make them. Let your maidens see it through so that it fits
us properly, for nothing will talk us out of the journey."

The young lady said: "Now listen to what I say. I have silk of my
own; now arrange to have gems brought to us on shields, and we will
work the clothes." Gunther and Siegfried were ready to do that.

"Who are the companions," the princess said, "who are to be
dressed to go to that court with you?" He said: "I make one of four. Two
of my men, Dankwart and Hagen, go to court with me. And mark
carefully what I tell you, my lady: each of us four is to wear, over
four days, three changes of clothes apiece, and clothes so good that we
leave Brunhild's land without shame."

The lords took proper leave and departed. Then Kriemhild the
princess called thirty of her young ladies out of her chambers, maidens
with great skill at that kind of work. Arabian silk as white as
snow, and fine silk from Zazamanc as green as clover: into these they
set the gems, and that made good clothes. Kriemhild, that glorious
girl, cut them out herself. Well-made linings of the skins of
strange fish, a rare sight for strangers' eyes, as many as could be got
hold of, they covered over with silk, as the clothes were to be worn.
Now hear great wonders told of that shining clothing. From the
land of Morocco, and from Libya too, the very best silks that any
king's house ever owned: they had plenty of those, and Kriemhild made
it plain how fond she was of the two men. Since the heroes had
set their hearts on so grand a journey, ermine furs seemed nothing
special to them; over the fur lay silk as black as coal, the kind that
would still look fine on swift heroes at a feast today. Out of
Arabian gold shone many a gem. The ladies' labor was no small thing:
within seven weeks they had the clothes ready. And by then the good
warriors' weapons were ready too.

When they were all prepared, a strong little ship had been
carefully built for them on the Rhine, to carry them all the way down
to the sea. The noble young ladies ached from their work. The
warriors were told that the fine clothes they were to take with them
were now ready; just as they had wished, it was done. And now they would
not stay by the Rhine a moment longer.

A messenger was sent to the companions, asking if they would
like to look over their new clothes, in case anything was too short or
too long for the heroes. Everything fit exactly, and they thanked the
ladies for it. Everyone who came to look had to admit they had
never seen anything better in all the world. So the men could wear
them proudly at that court; no one could tell of finer clothes on a
warrior. Great thanks were given, and not sparingly. Then the
gallant heroes asked leave to go, and the lords did it with knightly
courtesy. And bright eyes grew dim and wet with weeping.

She said: "Dear brother, you could still stay here and court
other ladies, where your life would not hang on the outcome; I would
call that well done. You could find a woman just as highborn closer to
home."

I think their hearts told them what would come of it. They all
wept alike, no matter what anyone said. The gold on their breasts grew
dull with the tears that poured so thickly down from their eyes.

She said: "Lord Siegfried, let my dear brother be entrusted to
you, on your loyalty and your good faith, so that nothing harms him in
Brunhild's land." The bold man promised it into Lady Kriemhild's hand.

The mighty warrior said: "As long as my life lasts, my lady, you
may be free of every worry. I will bring him safely back to you here on
the Rhine; know that for certain." The beautiful girl bowed to him.

Their gold-colored shields were carried down to the sand for
them, and all their gear was brought to them. Their horses were led
up; they were ready to ride away. And the beautiful women wept and
wept. The lovely girls stood at the windows. A strong wind
stirred the ship and its sail. The proud companions took their places
on the Rhine, and King Gunther said: "Now who is to be master of the
ship?"

"I will," said Siegfried. "I can steer you well on the water
from here, good heroes, believe me; the right waterways are well known
to me." So they set out in high spirits from the Burgundians' land.

Siegfried quickly took up a pole, and the powerful man pushed
them off from the bank. Bold Gunther took an oar himself, and so the
swift, praiseworthy knights put out from the shore. They carried
rich food with them and very good wine, the best to be found anywhere
along the Rhine. Their horses stood comfortably; they were well looked
after. The ship ran very smoothly; very little went wrong for them.
Their strong sail-ropes were drawn taut, and with a good wind
they sailed twenty of the old long miles before night fell, down
toward the sea. Their hard labor would later bring those high-spirited
men pain.

On the twelfth morning, as we hear tell, the winds had carried
them far away, to Isenstein in Brunhild's land. Not one of them knew
it except Siegfried. When King Gunther saw so many castles and
the wide marches too, how quickly he spoke up: "Tell me, friend
Siegfried, do you know whose castles these are, and whose this splendid
land is?"

Siegfried answered: "I know it well. It is Brunhild's land and
Brunhild's people, and the fortress of Isenstein, as you heard me say.
There you may still see many a beautiful woman today. And I will
give you heroes some advice: be of one mind and tell the same story; I
think that is best. For when we go before Brunhild today, we will have
to stand before the queen with great care. When we see that
lovely woman among her household, you famous heroes must tell one tale
and one only: that Gunther is my lord and I am his vassal. Then
everything he hopes for will come to pass."

They were ready to promise whatever he asked of them; not one of
them let arrogance stop him. They agreed to everything he wanted, and
it served them well when King Gunther saw beautiful Brunhild.

"And I am not doing this so much out of love for you as for your
sister, that beautiful girl. She is as dear to me as my soul and my
own life. I will gladly earn it, that she becomes my wife."
