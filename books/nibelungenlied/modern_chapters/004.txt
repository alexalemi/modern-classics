Chapter 5: How Siegfried First Saw Kriemhild

Every day now you could see them riding to the Rhine, the men who
wanted to be at the festival. Many of those who came into the land for
the king's sake were given horses and splendid clothes. Seats
were made ready for them all, for the highest and the best, as we are
told: thirty-two princes at that festival. And the beautiful women
dressed for it as if in a contest with one another.

Young Giselher was kept very busy. He and Gernot, and both their
companies of men, received the guests, strangers and old acquaintances
alike, with great kindness, and truly they greeted the warriors as
honor required. The visitors brought a great many gold-red
saddles into the land, and fine shields and splendid clothes, to the
festival on the Rhine. Many a wounded man was seen in good spirits
afterwards. Even the men lying in their beds in the pain of their
wounds had to forget how hard death was. People stopped mourning for
the sick and injured and rejoiced instead at the news of the festival
days coming, and at how well they would live at the feast. All
the people who could be found there had joy beyond measure and more
delight than they could hold, and great rejoicing rose over the whole
of Gunther's land.

On a Whitsun morning, a great many bold men were seen going out
in glorious clothes, five thousand of them or more, to the festival,
and on every side the entertainments began, each outdoing the next.
The host had his wits about him. He knew very well how deeply the
hero from the Netherlands loved his sister, the girl everyone called
more beautiful than any maiden alive, though Siegfried had never seen
her.

Then the warrior Ortwin said to the king: "If you want to be at
this festival with full honor, then you should let people see the
lovely girls who live here in Burgundy in such great honor. What
is a man's delight, what gladdens his heart, if not beautiful girls and
splendid women? Let your sister go out before your guests." That
advice was given to the joy of many a hero.

"I will gladly do that," said the king, and everyone who learned
of it was very glad. He sent word to Lady Ute and her lovely daughter
that they should come to court with their maidens. Then good
clothes were fetched out of the chests, all the noble garments they
could find folded away there, and bracelets and braided trimmings,
of which they had plenty, and many a lovely girl dressed herself with
care. Many a young warrior that day set his heart on being a fine
sight for the ladies, and would not have traded the chance for a
mighty king's land. They were eager to look at the women they had never
seen before.

Then the mighty king ordered a good hundred of his men, his
kinsmen and hers, to go with his sister and attend her; they carried
swords in their hands. That was the court household of Burgundy.
Ute, that great lady, was seen coming with her. She had chosen
beautiful women for her company, a hundred or more, in rich clothes,
and behind her daughter came many a lovely girl. They were seen
coming all together out of the women's chambers, and then there was a
great crush of heroes shoving forward, every one of them hoping, if it
could possibly be done, to catch a happy sight of the noble girl.

Now the lovely one came out, like the red of dawn breaking from
dark clouds. And the man who had carried her in his heart for so long
was free of many a sorrow in that moment: he saw the lovely girl
standing there before him in all her glory. Precious stones
shone all over her dress, and her rose-red color glowed. Whatever
anyone might have wished for, no one could say he had ever seen
anything more beautiful in this world. As the bright moon stands
before the stars, when its light falls so clear from the clouds, so she
stood now before many a fine lady, and the spirits of the handsome
heroes rose high at the sight.

The richly dressed chamberlains walked ahead of her, but the
high-spirited warriors would not stop pressing forward to wherever
they could see the lovely girl. And Lord Siegfried felt both joy and
sorrow. He thought to himself: "How could it ever happen that I
should love you? That is a fool's dream. But if I must stay a stranger
to you, I would sooner be dead." Those thoughts turned him pale and red
by turns. Siegmund's son stood there so graceful, it was as if a
master artist had drawn him on parchment; people said that no hero so
handsome had ever been seen. The ladies' escort ordered everyone
to make way on all sides, and many a warrior did so. Those proud hearts
gladdened many a man, and many a splendid woman was seen there in
perfect courtesy.

Then Lord Gernot of Burgundy spoke: "Gunther, dear brother, the
man who offered you his service so kindly — you should do the same for
him, here before all these warriors. I will never be ashamed of that
advice. Have Siegfried come to my sister, so that the girl may
greet him. We will always profit by it. She who has never greeted a
warrior shall greet this one, and that way we win that fine warrior for
ourselves."

So the host's kinsmen went to where the hero was. They said to
the warrior from the Netherlands: "The king has given you leave to go
to court. His sister is to greet you. This is done to honor you."
The lord was glad of it in his heart, and he carried joy there
with no sorrow in it, that he was going to see fair Ute's child. With
lovely grace she greeted Siegfried then.

When she saw that high-spirited man standing in front of her, his
color flared. The beautiful girl said: "Welcome, Lord Siegfried, noble
and good knight." And at her greeting his spirits rose high. He
bowed to her with care, and she took him by the hand. How lovingly he
walked at the lady's side! They looked at each other with tender
glances, the lord and the lady, very secretly. Whether a white
hand was pressed there in affection, out of heartfelt love, I cannot
tell you. But I cannot believe it was left undone. She had quickly let
him know that she wished him well. In the summertime, and in the
days of May, he would never again carry in his heart so much high joy
as he won there, when the woman he wanted for his love walked at his
hand.

Many a warrior thought: "If only that had happened to me, to walk
beside her the way I see him do, or to lie beside her — I would not
object to that!" No warrior ever earned a princess so well. The
guests, whatever kings' lands they had come from, had eyes for only
those two. She was permitted to kiss the handsome man, and nothing so
sweet had ever happened to him in all the world.

The king of Denmark said at once: "Because of this high greeting,
many a man lies wounded by Siegfried's hand, and I feel that keenly
myself. God never let him come into my kingdom again."

Then on every side they called for people to make way for fair
Kriemhild, and many a bold warrior was seen walking with her in
courtly order to church. There the handsome man was parted from her.
She went into the cathedral with many women following her. The
princess was so beautifully adorned that many a high hope was lost
there; she had been made to be a feast for many a warrior's eyes.
Siegfried could hardly wait for the singing of the Mass to end.
He could thank his good fortune forever that the girl he carried in his
heart was so well disposed toward him. And he loved the beautiful girl,
as he had every reason to.

When she came out of the cathedral, the bold warrior was asked to
go to her again, as he had done before. Now for the first time the
lovely girl began to thank him for having fought so gloriously before
so many heroes. "God reward you, Lord Siegfried," said the
beautiful girl, "for earning the warriors' devotion with such true
loyalty, as I hear them all say." Then he began to look lovingly at
Lady Kriemhild.

"I will serve them always," said the warrior, "and I will never
lay my head to rest until I have done their will, as long as I live.
And all of it, my lady Kriemhild, is done to win your favor."

For twelve days, every one of them, the praiseworthy girl was seen
at the warrior's side whenever she had to go to court before her
kinsmen. That service was done for the warrior out of great affection.
Every day there was joy and delight and a great noise in front of
Gunther's hall, outside it and inside too, from many a bold man. Ortwin
and Hagen performed great wonders. Whatever anyone wanted to
play, those gallant heroes were ready for it, in full measure, and so
the guests came to know the warriors well, and the whole of Gunther's
land was adorned by it. Even the men who had lain wounded were
seen coming out; they wanted their share of the sport with the
household, fencing with shields and throwing javelin after javelin.
Plenty of men helped them; there were great numbers of them.

For the whole festival the host had them cared for with the best
of food. He had made up his mind to avoid every kind of disgrace that
ever befell a king, and he was seen going among his guests in a
friendly way. He said: "Good warriors, before you ride away, take
my gifts. That is my intention; I will always be grateful if you do.
Do not scorn what I have. I want to share it with you, and I do so
gladly."

The men of Denmark answered at once: "Before we ride home to our
own land, we want a lasting peace. We warriors need it. We have many a
dear kinsman lying dead at your warriors' hands." Liudegast was
healed of his wounds, and the lord of the Saxons had recovered after
the battle, though they were leaving some of their dead in the land.
Then King Gunther went to find Siegfried.

He said to the warrior: "Advise me what to do. Our enemies want
to ride out early tomorrow, and they are asking me and my men for a
lasting peace. Now advise me, warrior Siegfried, what you think is the
right thing. I will tell you what the lords are offering: as much
gold as five hundred horses could carry, and they would give it gladly
if I let them go free."

Strong Siegfried said: "That would be badly done. You should let
them ride away free, on the condition that those noble warriors never
again come riding into your land as enemies. Have both lords give you
their hands on it, here, as surety."

"I will follow that advice." With that they parted. His enemies
were told that no one wanted the gold they had offered. At home their
dear kinsmen were longing for the battle-weary men. Many shields
heaped with treasure were carried in, and Gunther shared it out
unweighed among his friends, five hundred marks apiece and for some a
good deal more. Bold Gernot had advised him to do that. Then they
all took their leave, since they wanted to go. The guests were seen
going before Kriemhild and before Lady Ute, the queen, where she sat.
Never were warriors sent on their way more handsomely.

The lodgings stood empty when they had ridden off. But the king
stayed on at home in lordly style, with his kinsmen and many a noble
man, and every day they were seen going to Lady Kriemhild. Now
Siegfried, that good hero, wanted to take his leave too; he had no
hope of winning what his heart was set on. The king heard that he
meant to go, and young Giselher talked him out of the journey
completely.

"Where would you ride to now, noble Siegfried? Stay with the
warriors, do as I ask you, stay with King Gunther and his men. There
are many beautiful women here, and we will gladly let you see them."

Strong Siegfried said: "Leave the horses standing. I meant to ride
away, but I give it up. Take the shields back too. Truly, I wanted to
go home to my own land, but Lord Giselher has turned me from it with
great loyalty."

So the bold man stayed, for the love of his friends, and there was
nowhere in all the lands he could have been so content; and the reason
was that now he saw fair Kriemhild every day. For her boundless
beauty the lord stayed on. They passed the time with many pastimes,
except that her love tormented him and often gave him great distress.
And for that love, later, the bold man lay pitifully dead.
