Chapter 17: How Kriemhild Mourned Her Husband, and How He Was Buried

They waited for nightfall and then crossed the Rhine. No heroes
ever hunted worse. The one beast they killed was wept for by noble
girls, and truly, many good fighting men would pay for it later.
Now you can hear about great arrogance and terrible revenge.
Hagen had Siegfried of the Nibelung land carried, dead as he was, to
the door of the chamber where Kriemhild lodged. He had him laid
there secretly, against the door, so that she would find him when she
went out to matins before daybreak—the early service that Lady
Kriemhild hardly ever slept through.

The bells rang from the cathedral as they always did. Lady
Kriemhild the beautiful woke her many maids and asked for a light to be
brought, and her clothes. Then a chamberlain came along and found
Siegfried. He saw him red with blood, his clothes soaked
through. That this was his lord he had no idea. He carried the light
on into the chamber, and that was how Lady Kriemhild learned the
terrible news. As she was about to set out with her ladies for
the cathedral, the chamberlain said: "Stop where you are. A knight is
lying outside this room, struck dead." At that Kriemhild began to wail
beyond all measure.

Before she had even made sure that it was her husband, she began
to think of Hagen's question—how he could protect him. Then her sorrow
truly began. With his death she gave up every joy she had. She
sank to the floor without a word, and there the beautiful woman lay,
all her happiness gone. Kriemhild's anguish grew past measuring. When
she came out of her faint, she screamed until the whole chamber rang
with it.

Her household said: "What if it is some stranger?" The blood
burst from her mouth, from the pain in her heart, and she said: "It is
Siegfried, my own dear husband. Brunhild planned this, and Hagen did
it."

The lady had herself led to where the hero lay. She lifted his
handsome head with her white hand, and red with blood as it was, she
knew him at once. There lay the hero of the Nibelung land, a piteous
sight. Then the gracious lady cried out in her grief: "Oh, my
sorrow! Your shield is not hacked by any sword. You lie here murdered.
If I knew who did this, I would plot his death for the rest of my
life."

All her household wailed and cried out with their dear lady, for
they were sick at heart for the noble lord they had lost. Hagen had
avenged Brunhild's anger, and avenged it hard.

Then the grieving woman said: "Chamberlain, go now, and quickly
wake Siegfried's men. And tell Siegmund of my anguish, and ask whether
he will help me mourn bold Siegfried."

A messenger ran fast to where Siegfried's heroes from the
Nibelung land lay sleeping, and with his terrible news he took their
joy away. They would not believe it until they heard the weeping.
The messenger soon came to where the king lay too. Lord Siegmund
was not asleep; I think his heart had told him what had happened to
him. He would never see his dear son alive again.

"Wake up, Lord Siegmund. My lady Kriemhild sent me for you. A
wrong has been done to her that cuts her to the heart more than any
sorrow before, and you must help her mourn it, for it touches you
deeply too."

Siegmund sat up. He said: "What is this sorrow of beautiful
Kriemhild's that you are telling me about?"

The messenger said, weeping: "I cannot keep it from you. Bold Siegfried
of the Netherlands has been killed."

Lord Siegmund said: "Stop this joking, for my sake, and stop
telling anyone such evil news, that he has been killed. I could never
finish mourning him as long as I lived."

"If you will not believe what you hear me say, then you can go
and hear for yourself how Kriemhild and all her household are mourning
Siegfried's death." Then Siegmund was badly frightened, and truly he
had reason.

He and a hundred of his men sprang from their beds and snatched
up their long sharp weapons, and they ran toward the wailing, sick with
grief. Then came a thousand warriors, bold Siegfried's men. When
they heard the women lamenting so pitifully, some of them remembered
they ought to be wearing clothes. Truly, their grief had robbed them of
their wits. A great heaviness had buried itself in their hearts.

Then bold Siegmund came to where Kriemhild was. He said: "Alas
for the journey to this land! Who has robbed me of my child, and you of
your husband, so murderously, here among such good friends?"

"Oh, if I knew him," said the noble woman, "neither my heart nor
my body would ever wish him well again. I would plan such harm for him
that his kinsmen would be weeping because of me."

Lord Siegmund wrapped his arms around the prince. Then the
anguish of his kinsmen grew so great that the palace and the hall, and
the whole town of Worms, rang with their crying and weeping.
No one could comfort Siegfried's wife. They stripped the clothes
from his handsome body, washed his wounds, and laid him on the bier.
His people ached with the greatness of their grief.

Then his warriors from the Nibelung land said: "Our hands will
avenge him, and gladly. The man who did this is here in this castle."
And all Siegfried's men rushed for their weapons. The chosen
fighters came with their shields, eleven hundred warriors; Lord
Siegmund had that many in his company. He wanted to avenge his son's
death, and truly he had reason. They did not know whom they
should fight, unless it was Gunther and his men, with whom Lord
Siegfried had ridden to the hunt.

Kriemhild saw them armed, and it grieved her terribly. However
great her anguish and however deep her distress, she was so afraid the
Nibelungs would die at the hands of her brothers' men that she stopped
it. She warned them kindly, as loving kinsmen warn their kin.
The grief-stricken woman said: "My lord Siegmund, what are you
starting? You do not understand how things stand here. Truly, King
Gunther has so many bold men that you will all be lost if you take
those warriors on."

With their shields raised they were eager to fight. The noble
queen begged and commanded the brave warriors to leave it alone, and
when they would not give it up, truly it grieved her. She said:
"Lord Siegmund, you must let it rest until a better time comes. Then I
will avenge my husband with you, always. Once I have proof of who took
him from me, I will make him pay. There are too many arrogant
men here on the Rhine, so I will not advise you to fight. They have a
good thirty men for every one of yours. Now may God give them what they
have earned from us. Stay here and bear my sorrow with me, and
when day begins to break, you brave heroes, help me lay my dear husband
in his coffin." The fighters said: "That shall be done."

No one could tell you the whole wonder of it, how knights and
ladies were heard lamenting, until the wailing was noticed in the town
itself. The noble townspeople came hurrying. They mourned with
the guests, for it grieved them deeply that no one had told them of any
guilt of Siegfried's, any reason why the noble warrior should have lost
his life. The wives of the good townsmen wept with the ladies of the
court. Smiths were ordered to hurry and make a coffin of silver
and gold, big and strong, and to band it tight with good steel. Every
heart was heavy.

The night was gone; they said day was dawning. Then the noble
lady had Lord Siegfried, her dear husband, carried to the cathedral.
Whatever friends he had there were seen weeping as they went.
When they brought him into the cathedral, many bells rang, and
on every side you could hear the chanting of many priests. Then King
Gunther came with his men, and grim Hagen too, toward the sound of
wailing.

He said: "Dearest sister, alas for your sorrow, and that we
cannot be spared this great loss. We will mourn Siegfried's death
forever."

"You do that without cause," said the grieving woman. "If it
grieved you, it would never have happened. You forgot me, I can say
that now, when I was parted from my dear husband. Would to God," said
Kriemhild, "that it had been done to me instead."

They denied it firmly. Then Kriemhild said: "Whoever claims he is
innocent, let him prove it. He must walk up to the bier in front of
everyone. That way the truth will be known quickly enough."

It is a great wonder, and it still happens often today: whenever
a murderer, stained with the killing, is seen beside the dead man, the
wounds begin to bleed. And that is what happened here, and so the guilt
was seen to be Hagen's. The wounds flowed freely, as they had
before, and those who had been weeping already now wept far more.

Then King Gunther said: "I want you to know that robbers killed him.
Hagen did not do it."

"I know those robbers well," she said. "Now may God let his
kinsmen's hands avenge it. Gunther and Hagen, you did this." Siegfried's
fighters were ready to go to war. Then Kriemhild said once more:
"Bear this distress with me."

Now these two came to where they found him dead: Gernot, her brother,
and young Giselher. They mourned him loyally, along with the others,
and wept from their hearts for Kriemhild's husband. Mass was to
be sung, and so from every side men, women, and children went to the
cathedral. Even those who could easily have spared him wept for
Siegfried then.

Gernot and Giselher said: "Sister, comfort yourself after this
death, since it must be so. As long as we live we will try to make it up
to you." But no one in the world could give her any comfort.

His coffin was ready around midday. They lifted him from the bier
where he lay, but still the lady would not let him be buried, and that
cost everyone a great deal of trouble. They wrapped the dead man
in rich silk, and I think there was no one there who did not weep. Ute,
that noble woman, and all her household mourned his handsome body with
all their hearts. When word went around that there was singing in
the cathedral and that he had been laid in his coffin, a great crowd
gathered. What offerings they brought for his soul! He had good friends
enough, even among his enemies.

Poor Kriemhild said to the chamberlains: "Those of you who wished
him well and are loyal to me must take some trouble for my sake. For
Siegfried's soul, share out his gold." No child was so small, if
it had any sense at all, that it did not go up to make an offering
before he was buried. More than a hundred masses were sung that day, and
there was a great crush of Siegfried's friends.

When the singing was over, the people went away. Then Lady
Kriemhild said: "Do not let me keep watch over this chosen warrior alone
tonight. All my joy lies in his body. I will let him lie three
days and three nights, until I have had my fill of looking at my dear
husband. And what if God wills that death should take me too? Then poor
Kriemhild's misery would be well ended."

The people of the town went back to their lodgings. She asked the
priests and the monks to stay, and all his household who had served the
hero. They had hard nights and weary days. Many a man went
without food or drink, and those who wanted some were told that they
would be given plenty; Lord Siegmund saw to that. Those were hard days
for the Nibelungs.

For those three days, as we have heard, the men who could sing
had to bear a great deal of labor. And what offerings people brought
them! Those who had been very poor became rich enough. Whatever
poor people were found who had nothing to give were sent to the offering
with gold from Siegfried's own treasury. Since he could not live, many
thousand marks were given for his soul. They gave away lands
with their rents across the whole country, wherever cloisters and good
people were found, and gave the poor silver and clothing in plenty. By
what she did she showed plainly how much she loved him.

On the third morning, at the hour of Mass, the wide churchyard
beside the cathedral was full of country people weeping. They served him
after his death as one should serve beloved kin. In those four
days, so it has been said, thirty thousand marks or even more were given
to the poor for his soul. His great beauty and his life lay low.

When God had been served and the singing was finished, a great
many people struggled with overwhelming grief. He was carried out of the
cathedral to the grave, and those who could hardly bear to lose him were
seen weeping and lamenting. The people went with him, crying out
loud; no one was glad there, neither woman nor man. Before they buried
him they sang and read the service. Oh, what good priests were at his
burial!

Before Siegfried's wife had even reached the grave, her loyal
heart wrestled so hard with its anguish that again and again they had
to douse her with water from the well. Her misery was beyond all
measure. It was a great wonder that she survived it at all. Many
a lady helped her lament. Then the queen said: "You men of Siegfried,
by your loyalty, show me this one mercy. After all my sorrow let
me have this small joy, that I may see his handsome head once more." She
begged so long, with the strength of her anguish, that they had to break
open the splendid coffin.

They brought the lady to where he lay. She lifted his handsome
head with her white hand and kissed the good and noble knight, dead as
he was. Her bright eyes wept blood for grief. It was a parting of
terrible anguish. They carried her away from there, for she could not
walk, and then they found the glorious woman lying senseless. Her lovely
body might have died of grief.

When the noble lord had been buried at last, all those who had
come with him from the Nibelung land were seen to suffer grief beyond
measure, and Siegmund was very seldom seen glad. There were some
among them who for three days neither ate nor drank for the greatness of
their sorrow. But they could not let their bodies waste away entirely.
They recovered from their grief, as plenty of people still do.
