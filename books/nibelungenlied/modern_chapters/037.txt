Chapter 38: How All of Lord Dietrich's Warriors Were Killed

On every side you could hear grief so great that the hall and
the towers rang with the wailing. One of Dietrich's men from Bern
heard it too, and how fast he hurried off with this heavy news!
He said to his prince: "Listen, my lord Dietrich. In all my
life until now I have never heard a lament so impossible to bear as
the one I have just heard. I think King Etzel himself has come to
harm. How else could they all be in such distress? One of the
two, the king or Kriemhild, lies dead, struck down by the bold
strangers out of hatred. Many a fine warrior is weeping beyond all
measure."

The hero of Bern said: "My dear men, do not be in too great a
hurry. Whatever the warriors far from home have done here, they are in
great danger now; let them have the benefit of the peace I offered
them."

Bold Wolfhart said: "I will go over there and ask what they
have done, and then I will tell you, my dear lord, just as I find it
out, what this lament may be about."

Lord Dietrich said: "Where men expect anger, and rude questions
are put to them on top of it, that can easily spoil good warriors'
high spirits. No, Wolfhart, I do not want you to be the one who
questions them."

He asked Helfrich to go over quickly and told him to find out,
from Etzel's men or from the guests themselves, what had happened
there; for no one had ever seen such grief among people. The
messenger asked: "What has been done here?" One of them answered:
"Whatever joy we had in the land of the Huns is utterly gone. Here
lies Rüdiger, killed by Burgundian hands, and of those who
came in with him not one has survived."

Helfrich could never have been sorrier. Never had he carried news so
unwillingly; the messenger went back to Dietrich weeping bitterly.
"What have you found out for us?" Dietrich said. "Why are you
weeping so hard, warrior Helfrich?" The noble warrior said: "I have
good reason to grieve. The Burgundians have killed good Rüdiger."

The hero of Bern said: "God forbid! That would be a terrible
revenge, and the devil's own mockery. What has Rüdiger ever done to
deserve that from them? I know very well that he is a friend to the
strangers."

Wolfhart answered: "If they have done it, it must cost them all
their lives. If we let it pass, we would be disgraced; good Rüdiger's
hand has served us many a time."

The lord of the Amelungs ordered the matter looked into more
carefully. In bitter grief he sat down in a window, and he asked
Hildebrand to go to the guests and find out from them what had been
done there. Master Hildebrand, that warrior bold in any storm
of battle, carried neither shield nor weapon in his hand: he meant to
go to the guests in the courteous way. For that his sister's son
rebuked him. Grim Wolfhart said: "If you go over there
unarmed, you will never get away without abuse; you will have to come
back in disgrace. But if you go armed, every one of them will think
twice about that."

So the wise man armed himself on the young man's advice. Before
he knew it, all of Dietrich's warriors had put on their armor and
stood with swords in their hands. The hero was sorry to see it; he
would gladly have turned them back. He asked them where they
meant to go. "We are coming with you. Then perhaps Hagen of Tronje
will think twice before speaking to you with scorn, which he knows so
well how to do." When he heard that, the warrior let them come.

Bold Volker saw the warriors of Bern, Dietrich's men, coming
well armed, swords belted on and shields in their hands. He told his
lords from the land of Burgundy. The fiddler said: "I see
Dietrich's men coming over there in a very hostile way, armed and
helmeted. They mean to attack us. I think it is going to go badly for
us strangers."

At the same moment Hildebrand arrived. He set the rim of his
shield down in front of his feet and asked Gunther's men: "Alas, you
good heroes, what had Rüdiger done to you? My lord Dietrich
has sent me here to you: if the hand of any of you has killed the
noble margrave, as we have been told, we could never get over so great
a sorrow."

Hagen of Tronje said: "The news is no lie. I would gladly wish
for your sake that the messenger had deceived you, for love of
Rüdiger, and that he were still alive, a man whom men and women alike
may weep for forever."

When they heard for certain that he was dead, the warriors
mourned him, as their loyalty required of them. You could see the
tears running down over the beards and chins of Dietrich's warriors.
Great sorrow had been done to them.

Sigestab, the duke of Bern, said: "Now there is an end to the
comfort Rüdiger gave us after our days of sorrow. The joy of a whole
people far from home lies killed in Rüdiger."

Wolfwin, the warrior of the Amelungs, said: "If I saw my own
father dead today, I could not be sorrier than I am for his life.
Alas, who will comfort the noble margrave's wife now?"

The warrior Wolfhart said in an angry mood: "Who will lead the
warriors now on so many a campaign, as the margrave so often did?
Alas, noble Rüdiger, that we have lost you like this!"

Wolfbrand and Helfrich and Helmnot too, with all their kinsmen,
mourned his death. Hildebrand could ask no more for sighing. He said:
"Now, warriors, do what my lord has sent us here for. Give us
Rüdiger's dead body out of the hall, the man in whom all our joy lies
fallen in grief, and let us repay him for the great loyalty he always
showed to us and to many another man. We are exiles too, as
the warrior Rüdiger was. Why do you keep us waiting? Let us carry him
away, so that we can still repay him after his death. We ought by
rights to have done it while he was alive."

King Gunther said: "No service was ever as good as the one a
friend does for a friend after his death. I call that steadfast
loyalty, in whoever can do it. You are right to repay him; he has
shown you much love."

"How long are we to go on begging?" said the warrior Wolfhart.
"Since our best comfort lies dead by your hands, and we sadly can
never have him again, let us carry him out of here so that we can
bury the warrior."

Volker answered: "No one is going to give him to you. Take him
from the house where the warrior lies fallen in the blood with his
deep wounds. Then it will be a full service that you do for Rüdiger."

Bold Wolfhart said: "God knows, sir minstrel, you had better
not provoke us; you have done us wrong enough. If I dared, in front of
my lord, you would be in trouble. We have to let it be, because he has
forbidden us to fight here."

The fiddler said: "A man has far too much fear if he gives up
everything he is forbidden. I cannot call that a true hero's spirit."
Hagen thought his comrade's words were well said.

"Do not let that trouble you," said Wolfhart. "I will put your
strings so badly out of tune that you will have something to tell
about it when you ride home to the Rhine. I cannot put up with your
arrogance and keep my honor."

The fiddler said: "If you throw my strings off their good tune,
the shine of your helmet will have to go very dull at my hand,
however I ride home to the land of Burgundy."

Then he wanted to spring at him, but his uncle Hildebrand would
not let him; he gripped him hard. "I think you would go mad with your
foolish temper. You would have lost my lord's favor forever."

"Let the lion go, master; he is in such a fierce mood," said
Volker the good warrior. "If he comes within reach of my hands, even
if he had killed the whole world with his own hand, I will strike him
so that he never tells the story afterward."

At that the men of Bern grew very angry. Wolfhart, a swift good
warrior, snatched up his shield and ran at him in front of the others
like a wild lion, and his friends came hard after him. But
however great the strides he took toward the wall of the hall, old
Hildebrand caught him before the steps; he did not want him to get
into the fight ahead of himself. They found what they were looking
for among the strangers soon enough.

Master Hildebrand sprang at Hagen. You could hear the swords
ring in both their hands. They were in a fury; that was plain to see
afterward, for a fire-red wind came off the two men's swords.
They were parted in the press of the battle by the men of Bern,
as their strength demanded. At once Hildebrand turned away from Hagen,
and strong Wolfhart ran at bold Volker. He struck the fiddler
on his good helmet so that the sword's edge bit through to the clasps.
The bold minstrel repaid that with courage: he struck Wolfhart so that
the sparks flew off him. They hewed plenty of fire from each
other's mail, for each of them hated the other. Then Wolfwin, the
warrior of Bern, parted them — if he were not a hero, he could never
have done it.

Gunther the warrior received the famous heroes from the land of
the Amelungs with a very willing hand. Lord Giselher made many a
shining helmet red and wet with blood. Dankwart, Hagen's
brother, was a grim man. Whatever he had done before to Etzel's
warriors in battle was nothing but wind: now bold Aldrian's son fought
in a rage. Ritschart and Gerbart, Helfrich and Wichart had very
seldom spared themselves in many a battle, and they made Gunther's
men well aware of it. Wolfbrand could be seen going splendidly through
the fight. Old Hildebrand fought as if he were mad. Many good
warriors had to fall dead into the blood at Wolfhart's hand, cut down
by swords. So the bold good warriors avenged Rüdiger.

Lord Sigestab fought as his courage told him to. Hey, what good
helmets of his enemies Dietrich's sister's son split in that fight! He
could never have done better in battle. When strong Volker saw
bold Sigestab hewing a stream of blood from the hard mail-rings, the
hero grew angry. He sprang to meet him, and there Sigestab very
quickly lost his life at the fiddler's hands: Volker gave him such a
share of his art that he had to lie dead by his sword. Old Hildebrand
avenged that, as his courage demanded.

"Alas for my dear lord," said Master Hildebrand, "who lies dead
here at Volker's hand. Now the fiddler shall live no longer." How
could bold Hildebrand ever have been fiercer? He struck Volker
so that the helmet-clasps flew on every side to the walls of the hall,
off the bold minstrel's helmet and shield. That was how strong Volker
met his end.

Then Dietrich's men pressed into the fight. They struck so that
mail-rings flew far away and you could see sword-points fly high
through the air, and they drew a hot stream of blood out of the
helmets. Hagen of Tronje saw Volker dead. That was the greatest
sorrow he had suffered at this whole festival, in kinsman or in
vassal. Alas, how fiercely Hagen began to avenge the hero! "Old
Hildebrand shall not profit by this. My help lies killed by the hero's
hand, the best comrade I ever won." He raised his shield higher and
went off hewing.

Bold Helfrich killed Dankwart. Gunther and Giselher were sorry
enough when they saw him fall in that great extremity; but he had well
avenged his own death with his hands. Meanwhile Wolfhart went
back and forth, hewing all the while at Gunther's men. He had come
through the hall for the third time, and many a warrior fell at his
hands.

Lord Giselher called out to Wolfhart: "Alas, that I ever won so
grim an enemy! Noble bold knight, now turn and face me. I will help
to end this; it cannot go on any longer."

Wolfhart turned to Giselher in the fight, and each struck the
other many a wide wound. He drove at the king with such force that the
blood under his feet sprayed up over his head. With swift grim
blows beautiful Ute's son received bold Wolfhart. However strong the
warrior was, he could not survive: no king so young could ever have
been bolder. He struck Wolfhart through his good coat of mail
so that the blood ran down from the wound; he wounded Dietrich's man
to the death. Truly, no one but a hero could have done it.

When bold Wolfhart felt the wound, he let his shield fall and
lifted a strong weapon higher in his hand — it was strong enough —
and the hero struck Giselher through helmet and mail-rings.
They had each dealt the other a grim death. Now not one of
Dietrich's men was left alive. Old Hildebrand saw Wolfhart fall; I
think nothing so painful had ever happened to him in his life.

Now Gunther's men were all dead, and Dietrich's too. Hildebrand
had gone to where Wolfhart had fallen down into the blood, and he took
the bold good warrior in his arms. He wanted to carry him out
of the house, but he was a little too heavy, and he had to let him
lie. Then the stricken man looked up out of the blood. He saw very
well that his uncle would gladly have helped him away.

The mortally wounded man said: "My dear uncle, you cannot be of
any use to me now. Guard yourself against Hagen; that seems good
advice to me. He carries a grim spirit in his heart. And if my
kinsmen want to mourn me after my death, tell the nearest and the best
of them from me not to weep for me; there is no need for that. I lie
here gloriously dead at a king's hands. And I have paid for my
life in here so dearly that the wives of good knights may well weep
for it. If anyone asks you about it, you can say straight out: a good
hundred lie killed by my hand alone."

Then Hagen too remembered the minstrel, whose life bold
Hildebrand had taken. He said to the warrior: "You will pay for my
sorrows. You have robbed us here of many a fine warrior." He
struck at Hildebrand so that you could plainly hear Balmung ring — the
sword bold Hagen had taken from Siegfried when he killed the hero.
The old man defended himself; truly, he was bold enough.
Dietrich's warrior swung a broad weapon, one that cut terribly,
at the hero of Tronje, but he could not wound Gunther's man. Then
Hagen struck him through his well-made coat of mail. When old
Hildebrand felt the wound, he feared more harm from Hagen's hand.
Dietrich's man threw his shield over his back, and with his deep wound
the hero escaped from Hagen.

Now none of all those warriors was alive except the two alone,
Gunther and Hagen. Old Hildebrand went streaming with blood to where
he found Dietrich, and brought him the painful news. He saw the
man sitting there in sorrow; and now the prince gained far more sorrow
still. He saw Hildebrand in his red coat of mail, and he asked for the
news, as his fear drove him to.

"Now tell me, Master Hildebrand, how do you come to be so wet
with lifeblood? Who did that to you? I think you have been fighting
with the guests in the hall. I forbade you so strictly; you should
rightly have kept away from it."

He told his lord: "Hagen did it. He gave me this wound in the
hall as I was trying to turn away from the warrior. I barely escaped
from the devil with my life."

The lord of Bern said: "It serves you right. You heard me
promise those warriors friendship, and you broke the peace I had given
them. If it were not an everlasting shame to me, you would lose your
life for it."

"Do not be so angry, my lord Dietrich. The harm to me and my
kinsmen is far too great already. We wanted to carry Rüdiger away, and
King Gunther's men would not allow it."

"Oh, what grief is mine! Is Rüdiger really dead? That is a
sorrow to me beyond all my other distress. Noble Gotelind is my aunt's
child. Alas for the poor orphans there at Pöchlarn!" His death
reminded him of loyalty and sorrow, and he began to weep hard; the
hero had cause to. "Alas for the loyal hero I have lost! Truly I shall
never get over the loss of King Etzel's vassal. Can you tell me
the truth, Master Hildebrand: who was the warrior who killed him?" He
said: "Strong Gernot did it, with all his might. And the hero fell dead
at Rüdiger's hands too."

He said to Hildebrand: "Now tell my men to arm themselves
quickly, because I mean to go over there, and have my bright battle
gear brought to me. I will question the heroes from the land of
Burgundy myself."

Master Hildebrand said: "Who is going to go with you? All the
living men you have, you see standing here beside you. That is I,
alone. The others are dead." Dietrich was shaken by the news, and well
he might be, for he had never known such sorrow in all the
world. He said: "If all my men are dead, then God has forgotten me,
poor Dietrich. I was a mighty king, powerful and rich.

"How could it come about," Lord Dietrich said, "that they are
all dead, those praiseworthy heroes, at the hands of men worn out by
battle, who were themselves in such peril? If it were not for my ill
luck, death would still be a stranger to them. Since my
misfortune would not let it be otherwise, tell me: is any of the
guests still alive?" Master Hildebrand said: "God knows, no one but
Hagen alone, and Gunther the noble king."

"Alas, dear Wolfhart, if I have lost you, I may well be sorry I
was ever born. Sigestab and Wolfwin and Wolfbrand too! Who will help
me now to the land of the Amelungs? Helfrich the bold — is he
killed too, and Gerbart and Wichart? How could I ever mourn them
enough? This is the last day of my joys. Alas, that no one can die of
grief!"
