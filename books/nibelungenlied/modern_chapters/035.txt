Chapter 36: How the Queen Had the Hall Set on Fire

"Now unlace your helmets," said Hagen the warrior. "My comrade
and I will keep watch over you, and if Etzel's men want to try us
again, I will warn my lords the moment I can."

Then many a good knight bared his head. They sat down on the
wounded, who had fallen into the blood in front of them, killed by
their own hands. The noble guests were watched with evil
eyes. Before evening came, the king and the queen too saw to it
that the Hunnish warriors made a better try of it. A good twenty
thousand of them could be seen standing before the guests; they had to
go into the fight. At once a hard storm broke against the
guests. Dankwart, Hagen's brother, that very swift man, sprang from his
lords' side to meet the enemy outside the door. Everyone thought he was
dead, but he came back through unharmed. The hard fight lasted
until night put an end to it. The guests defended themselves against
Etzel's men the whole summer-long day, as good heroes should. Oh, how
many bold warriors lay dead before them! It was at midsummer
that this great murder happened, when Lady Kriemhild avenged her
heart's grief on her closest kinsmen and on many another man; and
King Etzel never knew joy again because of it.

The day had run out on them, and they were in fear and peril.
They thought a quick death would be better for them than to be
tormented a long time with monstrous suffering. So those proud, fine
knights asked for a truce. They asked that the king be brought
to them. Out of the hall stepped the heroes, the color of blood and the
color of their armor, the three noble kings. They did not know who to
complain to about their very great hurt. Etzel and Kriemhild
both came there. The land was theirs, so their numbers kept growing.
Etzel said to the guests: "Now tell me, what do you want of me? You
think you can win a truce here; that would be hard to do, after
harm as great as you have done me. You shall get nothing by it, as long
as I have my life. My child, whom you killed, and so many of my
kinsmen — for that, truce and reconciliation shall be flatly denied
you."

Gunther answered: "Great need forced us to it. All my household
lay dead in the lodgings before your heroes. How had I deserved that? I
came to you in good faith; I thought you were my friend."

Then young Giselher of Burgundy said: "You heroes of Etzel's who
are still alive here — what do you blame me for, warriors? What have I
done to you? I rode into this land as a friend."

They said: "Your friendliness has filled this whole castle with
misery, and the land with it. Truly, we would have been glad if you
had never come from Worms across the Rhine. You and your brothers have
left this land full of orphans."

Then Gunther the warrior said angrily: "If you would set this
bitter hatred aside and make peace with us warriors who are so far from
home, that would be good for both sides. We have done nothing to
deserve what Etzel is doing to us."

Then the host said to his guests: "My grief and yours are not
alike at all. The great hardship, the loss and the shame on top of it,
that I have taken here — for that, not one of you shall ever leave here
alive."

Then strong Gernot said to the king: "Then may God command you
to act with honor. Kill us, who are far from home; but let us first
come down to you into the open. That would do you honor.
Whatever is going to happen to us, let it happen quickly. You have so
many men still unhurt, who dare to take us on; they will not let us
battle-weary men live. How long must we warriors go on in this
hardship?"

Etzel's warriors had almost agreed to it; they were going to let
them come out of the hall. Kriemhild heard that, and it was deeply
hateful to her. So the men far from home were refused their truce too
soon.

"No, no, you Hunnish warriors! What you have in mind — I advise
you in true loyalty, do not do it. Do not let those murderers out of
the hall, or your kinsmen will suffer a deadly fall. If none of
them were left alive but Ute's sons, my noble brothers, and they got
out into the breeze and cooled their mail rings, you would all be lost.
Bolder warriors were never born into the world."

Then young Giselher said: "My beautiful sister, my trust was badly
repaid when you invited me from across the Rhine here to this land,
into this great peril. How have I earned death here from the Huns? I
was always loyal to you; I never did you any wrong. I rode here to this
land on one hope: that you still loved me, my dear sister. Think of us
with mercy; there is nothing else to be done now."

"I cannot show you mercy. I have none. Hagen of Tronje has done
me such great wrong that it can never be made right as long as I live.
You must all pay for it," said King Etzel's wife. "If you will
give me Hagen alone as a hostage, I will not refuse to let you live,
for you are my brothers and children of one mother. Then I will speak
of peace with these heroes who are here."

"God in heaven forbid," said Gernot. "If there were a thousand
of us, we would all lie dead, we of your own blood, before we gave you
a single man as hostage. It will never be done."

"We have to die in any case," said Giselher. "No one is going to
part us from a knight's defense. Whoever wants to fight us — well, we
are still here. For I never failed a friend of mine in loyalty."

Then bold Dankwart spoke; it would not have suited him to keep
quiet. "Truly, my brother Hagen does not stand alone. Those who refuse
us the truce here may yet be sorry for it. I will make you well aware
of that, I tell you truly."

Then the queen said: "You fine heroes, now go closer to the
stairs and avenge my wrongs. I will always repay you for it, as by
right I should. I will pay Hagen back for his arrogance. Do not
let a single one of them out of the house." She ordered the hall to be
set on fire at all four ends. "Then all my wrongs will be well
avenged." Etzel's warriors were quickly ready.

Those of the guests who still stood close by outside were driven
into the hall with blows and missiles; the noise of it was very great.
But the princes and their men would not be parted; for their loyalty
they could not leave one another. Then King Etzel's wife had the
hall set alight, and the warriors' bodies were tortured there with
fire. A wind caught the house and very soon the whole of it was
burning. I do not think any people ever knew greater fear.

Many inside cried out: "Oh, this misery! We would far rather
have died in the fighting. God could have pity on it, how we are all
lost! Now the queen is wreaking her anger on us without measure."

One of them inside said: "We are going to lie dead here. What
good to us now is the greeting the king sent us? The thirst from this
terrible heat hurts me so badly that I think my life will soon end in
this misery."

Then Hagen of Tronje said: "You noble good knights, whoever is
tormented by thirst, let him drink the blood here. In heat like this it
is better than wine. There is nothing better to be had just now."

Then one of the warriors went to where he found a dead man. He
knelt down by the wound, unlaced his helmet, and began to drink the
flowing blood. Unused to it as he was, it seemed very good to him.

"God reward you, Sir Hagen," said the weary man, "that I have
drunk so well at your advice. I have seldom been served better wine. If
I live any while longer, I will always be your friend."

When the others heard that it had seemed good to him, there were
many more of them who drank the blood too. Some of them got great
strength from it. Later many a lovely woman paid for that in dear
kinsmen.

The fire kept falling on them in the hall, and they turned it
aside with their shields and down to the floor. The smoke and the heat
both hurt them badly. I do not think such misery will ever come to
heroes again.

Then Hagen of Tronje said: "Stand close to the walls of the hall.
Do not let the burning brands fall on your helmet straps; tread them
down into the blood with your feet. It is an evil festival the queen is
giving us."

In such suffering the night at last wore away. Still the bold
fiddler stood outside the house, and Hagen his comrade with him,
leaning on the rims of their shields. They expected more harm from the
men of Etzel's land.

Then the fiddler said: "Now let us go into the hall. Then the
Huns will think we are all dead of the torment that has been done to
us. They will yet see a good few of us warriors standing against them
in the fight."

Then young Giselher of Burgundy said: "I think it is going to be
day; a cooler wind is rising. Now may God in heaven let us live to see
a happier time. My sister Kriemhild has given us a wretched festival."

Then another said: "I can make out the day. Since nothing can be
better for us now, arm yourselves, heroes, and think of your lives.
Truly, King Etzel's wife will be at us again soon."

The host fully expected that the guests were dead from their
hardship and the terror of the fire. But six hundred bold men were
still alive in there, and no king ever had better warriors.
Those set to guard the men far from home had seen clearly that the
guests still lived, however much harm and grief had been done to the
lords and their men. They could be seen standing in the hall, still
sound and well. Kriemhild was told that a great many of them had
survived. The queen said: "That can never be, that any of them should
have lived through the terror of that fire. I would sooner believe they
all lie dead."

The princes and their men would still gladly have lived, if
anyone had been willing to show them mercy. But they could find none
among the men of the Huns' land. So they avenged their own dying with
very ready hands.

That day, toward morning, they were offered a greeting of hard
battle, and it brought heroes into peril. Many a hard spear was flung
at them, and the bold, proud warriors defended themselves like knights.
Etzel's household were in high spirits over it: they meant to
earn Kriemhild's gold, and to carry out, besides, what the king
commanded. Many of them would soon have to look death in the face for
it. Of the promises and the gifts there are wonders to tell. She
had the red gold carried in on shields, and she gave it to anyone who
cared for it and would take it. Truly, greater pay was never offered
against enemies. A great force of warriors went armed toward
the hall.

Then bold Volker said: "Well, we are still here. I never saw heroes
come more gladly to a fight than these who have taken the king's gold
to do us harm."

Then plenty of them called out: "Closer, heroes, closer! Let us
earn what we are to earn, and do it in good time. No one stays here
except the man who has to die anyway." Soon their shields could be seen
stuck full of thrown spears.

What more can I say? A good twelve hundred men tried them hard,
surging back and forth. The guests cooled their fury well with wounds.
No one could part the fight, and the blood could be seen flowing.
Many a man there was struck with a deadly deep wound. Each man
could be heard grieving bitterly for his friends. All the great king's
brave warriors died, and their loving kinsmen had terrible sorrow for
them.
