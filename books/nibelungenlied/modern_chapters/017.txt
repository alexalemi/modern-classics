Chapter 18: How Kriemhild Stayed Behind and Siegmund Rode Home

Kriemhild's father-in-law went to find her. He said to the
queen: "We must go home to our own land. I think we are unwelcome
guests here on the Rhine. Kriemhild, dear lady, come with me now to my
lands. Treachery has robbed us of your noble husband here in
this country, but you shall not pay for that. For my son's sake I will
stand by you; have no doubt of it. You shall have, my lady, all
the power that bold Siegfried gave you before. The land and the crown
too shall be yours to command, and all Siegfried's men will serve you
gladly."

Then the squires were told that they were to ride, and there was
a great rush for the horses; they hated being among their strong
enemies. The ladies and the girls were told to pack their clothes.

When King Siegmund was ready to ride, Kriemhild's kinsmen began
begging her to stay there with her mother. The lady said: "My lord,
that could never be. How could I ever look at the man who has
brought such grief on me, poor woman that I am?"

Then young Giselher said: "My dear sister, for your loyalty's sake you
should stay here with your mother. The people who have hurt you
and darkened your spirit, you need nothing from them. Live on my goods
alone."

She said to the warrior: "No, it cannot be. I would die of grief every
time I had to look at Hagen."

"I will see to that, my dear sister. You shall live with your
brother Giselher, and truly I will make up to you for your husband's
death." Then the grief-stricken woman said: "Kriemhild has need of
that."

Since young Giselher offered it so kindly, Ute and Gernot and her
loyal kinsmen began to plead with her too. They begged her to stay, for
she had few relatives among Siegfried's men. "They are all
strangers to you," said Gernot. "No man alive is so strong that he will
not lie dead one day. Think of that, dear sister, and comfort your
heart. Stay with your family; truly, it will be good for you."

She promised Giselher that she would stay. The horses had been
led out for Siegmund's men, since they meant to ride to the Nibelung
land, and all the warriors' gear was loaded on the pack-horses.
Then Lord Siegmund went and stood beside Kriemhild. He said to
the lady: "Siegfried's men are waiting by the horses. Now we must ride,
for I have no wish to stay among the Burgundians."

Lady Kriemhild said: "My kinsmen advise me, all the loyal ones I
have, that I should stay here with them. I have no family in the
Nibelung land." It grieved Siegmund deeply when he found out what
Kriemhild had decided.

King Siegmund said: "Let no one tell you that. Before all my
kinsmen you shall wear the crown with all the power you had before. You
shall not suffer for it that we have lost the hero. And come
back with us for your little child's sake; my lady, you must not leave
him an orphan. When your son is grown, he will comfort your heart.
Until then many a bold and good hero shall serve you."

She said: "Lord Siegmund, no, I cannot ride. Whatever becomes of
me, I must stay here with my kinsmen, who help me mourn." That answer
did not please the good warriors at all. They all said the same
thing: "Then we may well say that only now has real sorrow come to us.
If you stay here among our enemies, no heroes ever rode home from a
court more sorrowfully."

Kriemhild answered: "You shall ride without fear, in God's
keeping. You will be given a good escort; I will have you guarded well
all the way to Siegmund's land. And my dear little child I commend to
your care, you warriors."

When they understood that she would not come, all Siegmund's men
wept together. How bitterly Siegmund parted from Lady Kriemhild! Now
he knew what misery was. "Curse this festival!" said the noble
king. "Never again will a king and his kinsmen suffer, for the sake of
a pastime, what has happened to us here. We shall never be seen among
the Burgundians again."

Then Siegmund's men said openly: "There may yet be a journey
back to this land, once we find out for certain who killed our lord.
They have enemies enough among his kinsmen, and strong ones."

He kissed Kriemhild. How sorrowfully he spoke, when he saw for
certain that she would stay: "Now let us ride home to our land, with
no joy in us. Only now do I know all my sorrows."

They rode from Worms down to the Rhine without an escort. They
could be very sure that if anyone attacked them with hostile intent,
the bold Nibelungs would know how to defend themselves. They
asked no one's leave to go. But then Gernot and Giselher were seen
coming to Siegmund in friendship; his loss grieved them, and the bold,
spirited heroes made sure he knew it. Prince Gernot said
courteously: "God in heaven knows well that I bear no guilt for
Siegfried's death, and I never heard it said who his enemy was here. I
have good reason to mourn him."

Then young Giselher gave them a good escort. He brought the king
and his warriors, full of sorrow, out of the land and home to the
Netherlands. How few of their kinsmen were found in good spirits there!

How they fared after that, I cannot tell you. At Worms,
Kriemhild could be heard lamenting at every hour, and no one comforted
her heart or her spirit except Giselher; he was loyal and good.
Beautiful Brunhild sat in her arrogance. However much Kriemhild
wept, it meant nothing to her, and she was never again ready to show
her any good faith. Later Lady Kriemhild caused her the bitterest
sorrow of heart in return.
