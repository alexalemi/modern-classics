Chapter 22: How Kriemhild and Etzel Were Married in Vienna

She stayed at Traismauer until the fourth day. All that while the
dust on the road never settled; it rose on every side as if the land
were burning, as King Etzel's men came riding through Austria. By
now the king too had been told exactly how splendidly Kriemhild was
coming through the lands, and at the thought of it his sorrows melted
out of his mind. The king hurried to where he would find the beautiful
woman.

On the roads ahead of Etzel rode many bold warriors of many
tongues, Christians and pagans, troop after wide troop, and when they
found the lady they came up to her in splendor. Many a man rode
there from Russia and from Greece. Poles and Wallachians were seen going
at speed on their fine horses, riding hard, and whatever the customs of
their lands, they left very little of them out. From the land of
Kiev rode many a warrior, and the wild Pechenegs, who were busy shooting
with their bows at the birds that flew overhead; they drew their arrows
back hard, right to the bow's edge.

By the Danube, in the Austrian land, lies a town called Tulln.
There she came to know many a foreign custom she had never seen before.
Many who received her there would later come to grief through her.

In front of King Etzel rode a retinue, glad and very rich,
courtly and merry: a good twenty-four princes, noble and lordly. They
wanted nothing more than to see their lady. Duke Ramung from
Wallachia came galloping up before her with seven hundred men; they were
seen moving like birds in flight. Then came Prince Gibeche with splendid
troops. Hornboge the swift, with a full thousand men, turned away
from the king and toward his lady. There was loud shouting, after the
custom of that land, and the kinsmen of the Huns rode wildly there too.

Then came bold Hawart of Denmark and swift Iring, a man with no
treachery in him, and Irnfried of Thuringia, a handsome man. They
received Kriemhild in a way that did her honor, with twelve
hundred men they led in their troop. Then came Lord Blödel, Etzel's
brother from the land of the Huns, with three thousand; in great
splendor he came to where he found the queen. Then came King
Etzel, and Lord Dietrich too with all his companions. There was many a
noble knight there, worthy and good, and all of it lifted Lady
Kriemhild's spirits high.

Then Lord Rüdiger said to the queen: "My lady, here I will
receive the noble king. Whomever I tell you to kiss, you must kiss.
Truly, you cannot greet all of Etzel's men alike."

Then they lifted the noble queen down from her palfrey. Mighty
Etzel waited no longer; he got down from his horse with many a bold man,
and he was seen going joyfully toward Kriemhild. Two mighty
princes, as we are told, walked beside the lady and carried her train as
King Etzel came up to meet her, and she received the noble prince
graciously with a kiss. She pushed back her headdress, and her
lovely color shone out of the gold. Many a man there said that Lady
Helche could not have been more beautiful. Close by stood the king's
brother Blödel. Rüdiger, the mighty margrave, told her to kiss
him, and King Gibeche too; Dietrich stood there as well. Etzel's wife
kissed twelve of the warriors, and many another knight she received with
a greeting.

All the while Etzel stood beside Kriemhild, the young men did
what people still do today: many a splendid charge with lances was
ridden there, by Christian heroes and by pagans too, each after their
own custom. How truly knightly it was, the way Dietrich's men
sent their lance-shafts flying in splinters high over the shields from
good knights' hands! The German guests pierced many a shield's rim.
A great din of breaking shafts was heard. By now all the warriors
of the land had come, and the king's guests too, many a noble man. Then
the mighty king went off with Lady Kriemhild.

They saw a magnificent tent standing near them, and all around it
the field was filled with pavilions where they were to rest after their
hardships. Many a beautiful girl was led by the heroes to her place
under it, where they sat with the queen on richly cushioned
seats. The margrave had arranged it so well that everyone found it very
fine, and Kriemhild's seat gladdened Etzel's heart. What Etzel
said then, I cannot tell you. Her white hand lay in his right hand, and
they sat together lovingly, for Rüdiger the warrior would not let the
king be alone with Kriemhild in private.

Then the buhurt was called off everywhere, and the great noise
ended with honor. Etzel's men went to the pavilions, and lodgings were
given them far and wide on every side. The day was now at its
end. They took their ease until the bright morning was seen shining
again, and then many a man had come to the horses. Oh, what
entertainments were begun in the king's honor! The king asked the
Huns to arrange everything with due honor. Then they rode from Tulln to
the city of Vienna, where they found many a lady adorned, and they
received King Etzel's wife with every honor. Everything they
needed was prepared for them in great abundance. Many a glad hero
rejoiced at the celebration; they began to find lodgings, and the king's
wedding feast began joyfully.

They could not all be lodged in the city, so Rüdiger asked those
who were not guests to take lodgings out in the country. I think that at
every hour you could find at Kriemhild's side Lord Dietrich and
many another warrior. They had given up rest and taken on labor to keep
the guests in good spirits, and Lord Rüdiger and his friends had fine
entertainment. The wedding fell on a Whitsunday, when King Etzel
lay beside Kriemhild in the city of Vienna. I think she had never, with
her first husband, gained so many men for her service. With gifts
she made herself known to men who had never seen her before, and many
among them said to the guests: "We thought Lady Kriemhild had no wealth
at all; now she has done wonders here with her gifts."

The wedding feast lasted seventeen days. I think no one tells of
any king whose wedding was greater; if there was one, it has been kept
from us. Everyone who was there wore brand-new clothes. In the
Netherlands, I think, she had never before sat among so many warriors;
and I believe this too: however rich in wealth Siegfried was, he never
gained so many noble warriors as she saw standing before Etzel. Nor
did anyone ever give at his own wedding so many rich mantles, long
and wide, or such good clothes, of which there was plenty to be had, as
they all gave now for Kriemhild's sake. Her kinsmen and the
guests too were of one mind: they would spare no kind of goods. Whatever
anyone asked of them they gave at once, so that many warriors stood
there stripped of their clothes out of sheer generosity.

She thought of how she had sat by the Rhine beside her noble
husband, and her eyes grew wet; she hid it hard, so that no one could
see. After so much sorrow, so much honor had come to her.

Whatever anyone else did in generosity was nothing but wind
beside Dietrich: whatever Etzel, Botelung's son, had given him was now
spent away entirely. The hand of generous Rüdiger worked great wonders
there too. Prince Blödel from Hungary had many traveling chests
emptied of their silver and gold, and all of it was given away. The
king's heroes were seen living in high delight. Werbel and
Swemmel, the king's minstrels, each of them won at the wedding, I think,
a good thousand marks or better still, where beautiful Kriemhild sat
crowned beside Etzel.

On the eighteenth morning they rode out of Vienna. Many shields
were cut to pieces in knightly sport by the spears the warriors carried
in their hands. So King Etzel came all the way into the land of the
Huns. At old Hainburg they stayed the night. No one could tell
the number of the people, or with what strength they rode across the
land. Oh, what beautiful women they found in his homeland! At
mighty Wieselburg they went aboard ships. The water was covered with
horses and men as if it were solid ground, wherever you saw it flowing.
The travel-weary ladies had rest and comfort. Many a good ship
was lashed together, so that neither the waves nor the current could
harm them, and over them many a good tent was spread, as if they still
had both land and field beneath them.

Then the news came from there to Etzel's castle, and inside it
women and men rejoiced. Etzel's household, which the lady Helche had
looked after before, lived many a happy day afterward with Kriemhild.
Many a noble girl stood waiting there who had suffered great
grief from Helche's death. Kriemhild still found seven kings' daughters
there, and all Etzel's land was adorned by them. The young lady
Herrat still looked after the household — Helche's sister's daughter,
full of virtues, Dietrich's betrothed, the child of a noble king,
Nentwin's daughter; much honor came to her later. Her heart
rejoiced at the coming of the guests, and great wealth had been made
ready for it too. Who could tell you how the king held court afterward?
Never did people live better among the Huns with any queen.

When the king rode up from the shore with his wife, each lady was
named to noble Kriemhild, and she greeted them all the better for it.
Oh, how mightily she sat later in Helche's place! Much loyal
service was shown to her. Then the queen dealt out gold and clothing,
silver and jewels; whatever she had brought with her over the Rhine to
the Huns had to be given away entirely. All the king's kinsmen
and all his vassals later became subject to her service, so that Lady
Helche had never held such power as the woman they now served — and
would serve until Kriemhild's death.

The court and the land stood in such honor that at all times one
found there whatever amusement each heart desired, through the king's
love and the goodness of the queen.
