Chapter 1: The Nibelungs

{1} The old stories tell us many wonders: of heroes worthy of praise, of
great hardship, of joy and feasting, of weeping and lament; and now you
can hear wonders told of bold warriors' battles.

{2} In Burgundy there grew up a girl of such noble birth that in all the
lands there was none more beautiful. Her name was Kriemhild, and she
grew into a lovely woman, and for her sake many fighting men would lose
their lives.

{3} She was made to be loved: bold warriors longed for her, and no one
ever wished her ill. Her beauty was beyond all measure, and the young
lady's graces would have been an ornament to any woman.

{4} Three kings had her in their keeping, noble and powerful: Gunther
and Gernot, two warriors of great renown, and young Giselher, a chosen
fighter. The lady was their sister, and the princes had her in their
care. {5} These lords were open-handed, high-born, and bold beyond all
measure in their strength, the pick of all warriors. Their land was
called Burgundy. Later they did great wonders in Etzel's land.

{6} They lived in their power at Worms on the Rhine, and the proud
knights of their lands served them in honor until their final day. In
the end they died wretchedly, through the hatred of two noble women.

{7} Their mother was a mighty queen, the lady Ute. Their father was
Dankrat, who left them his lands when his life was over, a man of great
courage, who had won high honor in his own youth too. {8} These three
kings, as I have said, were of great courage, and the best warriors
anyone has ever heard of were their vassals: strong, very bold, and
fearless in the hardest fighting. {9} There was Hagen of Tronje, and
his brother Dankwart the swift; Ortwin of Metz; the two margraves Gere
and Eckewart; and Volker of Alzey, a man of courage through and
through. {10} There was Rumold, master of the kitchen, a fine warrior;
and Sindold and Hunold, who with him had charge of the court and its
honors, the three kings' men. And they had many another warrior whose
name I cannot give you. {11} Dankwart was marshal; his nephew Ortwin of
Metz was the king's steward; Sindold, a chosen warrior, was cupbearer,
and Hunold was chamberlain. They knew how to keep up the honor of a
great house. {12} As for the strength of that court, the reach of its
power, its high dignity and the knightly life these lords led with joy
all their days: truly, no one could ever tell you the end of it.

{13} In the midst of all this splendor Kriemhild had a dream. She
dreamed she was raising a falcon, strong and beautiful and wild, and
that two eagles tore it to pieces before her eyes. Nothing in this
world could have grieved her more. {14} She told the dream to her
mother Ute, and Ute could explain it to the good girl no better than
this: "The falcon you are raising is a noble man. Unless God protects
him, you will lose him soon."

{15} "Why are you talking to me about a man, dearest mother? I mean to
live without any warrior's love. I want to stay as beautiful as I am
until the day I die, and never have a man's love bring me sorrow."

{16} "Don't swear it off so hard," her mother answered. "If you are
ever to be happy with your whole heart in this world, it will be
through a man's love. You will be a beautiful woman, if God sends you a
truly good knight."

{17} "Let it be, my lady," she said. "It has been proved in so many
women how love is repaid with sorrow in the end. I will keep clear of
them both, and then nothing can ever go wrong for me."

{18} So Kriemhild shut her heart entirely against love. The good girl
lived many a happy day after that without knowing anyone she wanted to
love. Later, in all honor, she became the wife of a very bold warrior.
{19} He was the very falcon she had seen in her dream and her mother
had explained to her. How terribly she would avenge him on her own
nearest kin, who killed him in the end! For the death of that one man,
many a mother's son would die.
