Chapter 39: How Lord Dietrich Overcame Gunther and Hagen

{2324} Then Lord Dietrich fetched his own armor, and Master Hildebrand
helped him put it on. The powerful man lamented so loudly that the whole
house rang with the force of his voice. {2325} Then he recovered a true
hero's spirit. The good hero was armed now, in grim earnest; he took a
strong shield in his hand, and he and Master Hildebrand went quickly
from there.

{2326} Hagen of Tronje said: "I see Lord Dietrich coming this way. He
means to fight us, after the great sorrow that has befallen him here.
Today we shall find out who deserves to be called the best. {2327} However
strong of body Lord Dietrich of Bern thinks himself, and however
terrible, if he means to take revenge on us for what has been done to
him" — so Hagen said — "I am quite ready to stand against him."

{2328} Dietrich and Hildebrand heard this. He came to where he found the
two warriors standing outside the house, leaning against the hall, and
Dietrich set his good shield down on the ground. {2329} In grief and
sorrow he said: "Why have you done this, Gunther, mighty king, to me, an
exile? What had I done to you? Of all my comfort I am left here alone.
{2330} It did not seem enough to you, in that great extremity, when you
killed the hero Rüdiger; now you have robbed me of every one of my men.
Truly, I never did you heroes such a wrong. {2331} Think of yourselves
and of your own grief, the death of your kinsmen and all the hardship —
does it not weigh on the hearts of you good warriors at all? Oh, how
heavily Rüdiger's death falls on me! {2332} Nothing so painful ever
happened to any man in this world. You gave little thought to my
suffering or to your own. Whatever kinsmen I had, you have killed them.
Truly, I can never mourn my kinsmen enough."

{2333} "We are not as guilty as that," said Hagen. "Your warriors came
to this house fully armed, in a great company. I think the story has not
been told to you correctly."

{2334} "What else am I to believe? Hildebrand told me that when my
warriors from the Amelung land asked you to give Rüdiger out of the
hall, you offered the bold heroes nothing but mockery from up there on
the stairs."

{2335} The king of the Rhine said: "They said they wanted to carry
Rüdiger away. I ordered that refused — to spite Etzel, not your men —
until Wolfhart began to hurl insults about it."

{2336} The hero of Bern said: "Then it had to be so. Gunther, noble
king, by your good breeding, make up to me for the wrongs you have done
me, and make such amends, bold knight, that I can give you credit for
it. {2337} Give yourself up to me as a hostage, you and your man, and I
will protect you as best I can, so that no one here among the Huns does
you any harm. You will find nothing in me but loyalty and good faith."

{2338} "God in heaven forbid," said Hagen, "that two warriors should
surrender to you who still stand armed and ready to fight you, and still
walk so freely in front of their enemies."

{2339} "You should not refuse it," Dietrich said. "Gunther and Hagen,
you have both so burdened my heart and my spirit that it would be only
right for you to make it up to me. {2340} I give you my loyalty, and my
hand on it: I will ride home with you to your own land. I will lead you
there with honor, or lie dead myself, and for your sakes I will forget
my own great suffering."

{2341} "Ask it no more," said Hagen. "It would not be a good story to
tell of us, that two such bold men surrendered to you. And now there is
no one to be seen beside you but Hildebrand alone."

{2342} Master Hildebrand said: "God knows, Sir Hagen, the hour is
coming when you might be glad to take peace from a man who offers to
keep it with you. You could well let my lord's terms content you."

{2343} "Yes, I would take the terms," said Hagen, "before I would run so
shamefully out of a hall, Master Hildebrand, as you did here. I thought
you could stand up to enemies better than that."

{2344} Hildebrand answered: "Why throw that at me? Who was it sat on his
shield in front of the Waskenstein, in the Vosges, while Walther of
Spain killed so many of his kinsmen? You have plenty to show on your own
account."

{2345} Lord Dietrich said: "It does not suit heroes to bicker like old
women. I forbid you, Hildebrand, to say another word. I am an exile, and
great sorrows press on me. {2346} Let me hear, warrior Hagen," said
Dietrich, "what you two swift fighters were saying when you saw me
coming toward you armed. You said that you alone would stand against me
in a fight."

{2347} "No one will deny you that," said Hagen the warrior. "I am
willing to try it here with hard blows, unless Nibelung's sword breaks
in my hand. It makes me angry that we two have been asked for as
hostages."

{2348} When Dietrich heard Hagen's grim mood, the swift good warrior
quickly snatched up his shield. How fast Hagen sprang at him from the
stairs! Nibelung's good sword rang out loudly on Dietrich. {2349} Then
Lord Dietrich knew well that the bold man was in a grim mood; the lord
of Bern began to guard himself against the fearful blows. He knew Hagen
well, that splendid warrior, {2350} and he feared Balmung too, a weapon
strong enough. From time to time Dietrich struck back with cunning,
until at last he overcame Hagen in the fight. He gave him a wound that
was deep and long.

{2351} Then Lord Dietrich thought: "You are worn out by your ordeal. I
would have little honor if you lay dead before me. I will try instead
whether I can force you to be my hostage." That was done at his peril.
{2352} He let his shield fall — his strength was great — and locked
Hagen of Tronje in his arms. So the bold man was overpowered by him.
Noble Gunther began to grieve at that.

{2353} Dietrich bound Hagen and led him to where he found the noble
queen, and gave into her hand the boldest warrior who ever carried a
sword. After her great sorrow she was glad enough. {2354} Etzel's wife
bowed to the warrior for joy. "May your heart and body be forever
blessed. You have made up to me for all my distress. I will serve you
for it always, unless death prevents me."

{2355} Lord Dietrich said: "You must let him live, noble queen; and it
may yet come about that he makes up to you for what he has done to you.
He must not suffer for it that you see him standing here bound."

{2356} Then she had Hagen taken to a prison where he lay locked up and
no one saw him. Gunther, the noble king, began to shout: "Where has the
hero of Bern gone? He has done me wrong."

{2357} Lord Dietrich went out to meet him. Gunther's courage was worthy
of praise: he waited no longer but ran out in front of the hall, and a
tremendous clash rose from the swords of the two of them. {2358} However
long Lord Dietrich had been praised, Gunther was so angry and so wild —
after his great sorrow he was his deadly enemy — that it is still told
as a wonder that Lord Dietrich came out of it alive. {2359} Both their
courage and their strength were great. The great hall and the towers
rang with the blows as they hewed with their swords at the good helmets.
King Gunther had a lordly spirit. {2360} But the man of Bern overcame
him, as had happened to Hagen before. The hero's blood could be seen
flowing through the rings of his mail, from a sharp sword that Dietrich
carried. Lord Gunther, for all his weariness, had defended himself with
honor.

{2361} The lord was bound by Dietrich's hand, though kings should never
suffer such bonds. Dietrich thought that if he let the king and his man
go free, everyone they met would have to die at their hands. {2362} Dietrich
of Bern took him by the hand and led him bound to where he
found Kriemhild. With his suffering, much of her sorrow was over. She
said: "Welcome, Gunther from the land of Burgundy."

{2363} He said: "I would bow to you, my dear sister, if your greeting
could be kinder. I know you, queen, to be so angry in your heart that
you give me and Hagen a very poor welcome."

{2364} The hero of Bern said: "Most noble king's wife, never were such
good knights given as hostages as I have given you here, high lady. Now,
for my sake, treat the exiles well." {2365} She said she would do so
gladly. Then Lord Dietrich went away from the praiseworthy heroes with
tears in his eyes. Later Etzel's wife took grim revenge: she took the
lives of both those chosen warriors.

{2366} To make their captivity worse she kept them apart, so that
neither saw the other again until she carried her brother's head to
Hagen. Kriemhild's revenge was enough on both of them.

{2367} The queen went to where she saw Hagen. How full of hatred she was
when she spoke to the hero: "If you will give me back what you have
taken from me, you may still go home alive to the Burgundians."

{2368} Grim Hagen said: "You are wasting your words, most noble queen. I
have sworn that I will never show the treasure as long as any of my
lords is alive; so I will give it to no one."

{2369} "Then I will bring it to an end," said the noble woman. She
ordered her brother's life taken. They struck off his head, and she
carried it by the hair before the hero of Tronje. It was grief enough to
him.

{2370} When the unhappy man saw his lord's head, the warrior said to
Kriemhild: "You have brought it to an end exactly as you wanted, and it
has turned out just as I thought it would. {2371} Now the noble king of
Burgundy is dead, and young Giselher, and Lord Gernot too. No one knows
where the treasure is now but God and me — and from you, she-devil, it
will stay hidden forever."

{2372} She said: "Then you have paid me badly. So I will keep
Siegfried's sword at least. My dear love carried it when I last saw
him — the man whose loss broke my heart, and the fault was yours."

{2373} She drew it from the scabbard; he could not stop her. She meant
to take the warrior's life. She lifted it with her hands and struck off
his head. King Etzel saw it, and it was grief enough to him.

{2374} "Alas!" said the prince. "Now the very best warrior who ever went
into battle or carried a shield lies dead at a woman's hands. However
much I was his enemy, it grieves me deeply."

{2375} Old Hildebrand said: "She shall gain nothing by daring to kill
him. Whatever comes to me of it — and though he himself brought me into
deadly peril — still I will avenge the bold man of Tronje's death."

{2376} Hildebrand sprang at Kriemhild in a rage. He struck the queen a
heavy swing of his sword. Her fear of Hildebrand gave her pain; what
could it help her that she screamed so terribly? {2377} Now all those
who were doomed lay dead there. The noble woman was hewn to pieces.
Dietrich and Etzel began to weep; they mourned from the heart, both
kinsmen and vassals.

{2378} Great honor lay there dead. All the people had grief and
distress. The king's festival had ended in sorrow, as joy always turns
to sorrow in the end. {2379} I cannot tell you what happened there after
that, except that knights and ladies could be seen weeping, and the
noble squires too, for the deaths of their dear kinsmen. Here the story
ends: this is the fall of the Nibelungs.
