Chapter 20: How King Etzel Sent to Worms for Kriemhild

{1143} It was in the days after Lady Helche died, when King Etzel was
looking for another wife, that his kinsmen urged him toward a proud
widow in the land of Burgundy. Her name was Lady Kriemhild. {1144} Now
that beautiful Helche was gone, they said: "If you ever want to win a
noble wife, the highest and the best a king ever had, then take this
same lady. Strong Siegfried was her husband."

{1145} The mighty king said: "How could that ever happen? I am a pagan,
and I have never been baptized, and the lady is a Christian. She would
never consent. It would take a miracle for it ever to come about."

{1146} But the quick-witted men said: "What if she does it anyway, for
the sake of your great name and your great wealth? It is worth
putting the question to so noble a woman. You could love her beautiful
self very gladly."

{1147} The noble king asked: "Which of you knows the people and the
land by the Rhine?" Then good Rüdiger of Pöchlarn spoke up: "I have
known those noble kings since I was a child: {1148} Gunther and Gernot,
fine and noble knights, and the third is called Giselher. Each of them
does everything that honor and good breeding can ask of a man, and
their forefathers always did the same."

{1149} Etzel said again: "Friend, you must tell me whether she is
worthy to wear a crown in my land. And if she is as beautiful as I
have been told, my closest kinsmen will never have cause to regret it."

{1150} "In beauty she is the equal of my own dear lady, mighty Helche.
Truly, there could be no king's wife in all this world more beautiful.
The man she chooses as her husband can take real comfort in it."

{1151} The king said: "Then go and win her, Rüdiger, as you love me.
And if I ever lie beside Kriemhild, I will reward you for it as well as
I know how, for you will have done my will to the very fullest.
{1152} From my own treasury I will have you given horses and clothes
and everything you want, so that you and your companions can live in
good cheer. I will have plenty of it made ready for your embassy."

{1153} To that Rüdiger, the mighty margrave — the lord who guarded
Etzel's western border — answered: "To ask for your goods would do me
no credit. I will gladly be your messenger to the Rhine at my own
expense, with what I already have from your hand."

{1154} The mighty king said: "Now, when will you set out after the
lovely woman? May God keep you on the journey in all honor, and my
lady too. And may good luck help me, so that she looks kindly on us."

{1155} Rüdiger said: "Before we leave this land, we must first make
ready weapons and clothing, so that we can stand before princes with
honor. I will take five hundred fine men to the Rhine, {1156} so that
wherever they see me and mine in Burgundy, every one of them will say
of you that no king ever sent so many men so far in such fine style as
you have sent to the Rhine. {1157} And, mighty king, if you will not
give it up on this account: she was bound in noble love to Siegfried,
Siegmund's son. You have seen him here yourself. In all truth, one had
to grant him real honor."

{1158} The noble king said: "If she was that warrior's wife, then the
noble prince was precious enough that I should not disdain the queen.
Her great beauty pleases me well."

{1159} The margrave said: "Then I will tell you this: we set out from
here in twenty-four days. I will send word to Gotelind, my dear wife,
that I myself mean to be the messenger to Kriemhild."

{1160} Rüdiger sent his message to Pöchlarn, and the margrave's wife
grew both sad and proud. He told her that he was to win a wife for the
king, and she thought lovingly of beautiful Helche. {1161} When the
margravine heard the message, part of her grieved, and weeping became
her, as she wondered whether she would ever have a lady like the one
before. Whenever she thought of Helche, it pained her to the heart.

{1162} Rüdiger rode out of Hungary seven days later. King Etzel was
glad and in high spirits at that. There in the city of Vienna they
made their clothes ready for him; then he could put off his journey no
longer. {1163} At Pöchlarn Gotelind was waiting for him, and the young
margravine, Rüdiger's daughter, was glad to see her father and his
men. Beautiful girls were waiting for them with love. {1164} Before
noble Rüdiger rode to Pöchlarn out of the city of Vienna, all their
clothes had been loaded in full on the pack-horses. They traveled so
well guarded that hardly anything was taken from them.

{1165} When they came into the town of Pöchlarn, the host very kindly
had his traveling companions lodged and made them comfortable, and
mighty Gotelind was very glad to see the host come home. {1166} So was
his dear daughter, the young margravine; nothing could have pleased
her more than his coming. And how glad she was to see the heroes from
the land of the Huns! With a laughing heart the noble young lady said:
{1167} "A great welcome to you, my father and his men." Then many a
good knight gave the young margravine warm and careful thanks. Gotelind
knew Lord Rüdiger's mind very well.

{1168} That night, as she lay close beside Rüdiger, how kindly the
margravine asked him where the king of the Huns had sent him. He said:
"My lady Gotelind, I will gladly tell you. {1169} I am to win another
wife for my lord, now that beautiful Helche is dead. I am going to
ride to the Rhine for Kriemhild. She is to be a mighty queen here among
the Huns."

{1170} "God grant it," said Gotelind, "and may it come about, since we
hear people speak of so much honor in her. Perhaps she can take our
lady's place for us in our old age. We would gladly let her wear the
crown among the Huns."

{1171} The margrave said: "My dearest, to the men who are to ride with
me from here to the Rhine you must offer your goods with a loving hand.
When heroes travel richly equipped, they are in high spirits."

{1172} She said: "There is not one of them who will gladly take from
me whom I will not give whatever suits him, before you and your men
leave this place." The margrave said: "That is a kindness to me."

{1173} Ah, what rich samite was carried out of her chambers! The noble
warriors were given a generous share of it, and clothed in it with
care from the neck to the spurs. Rüdiger had picked out the men who
pleased him for this.

{1174} On the seventh morning the host rode out of Pöchlarn with his
warriors. They took weapons and clothes in plenty through the land of
Bavaria, and on the road they were seldom set upon by robbers.
{1175} Within twelve days they reached the Rhine. There the news could
not be kept hidden. The king and his men were told that foreign guests
had come, and the host began to ask {1176} whether anyone knew them;
if so, he should say. People saw their pack-horses carrying such heavy
loads that it was plain they were very rich. Lodgings were found for
them at once in the wide city.

{1177} When the strangers had settled in, the same lords were closely
watched. People wondered where these warriors could have ridden to the
Rhine from. The host sent for Hagen, in case he might know them.

{1178} The hero of Tronje said: "I have not seen them. But once we
have a look at them, I can tell you for certain where they have
ridden from into this land. They must be very foreign indeed if I do
not know them soon."

{1179} The guests had now been given lodgings. The envoy had come in
very rich clothes, and so had his companions in arms, and they rode to
court wearing fine garments, cut with great skill. {1180} Quick Hagen
said: "As far as I can judge — for I have not seen the lord for a
long time — they ride exactly as if it were Rüdiger from the land of
the Huns, that bold and noble warrior."

{1181} "How am I to believe," said the king at once, "that the lord of
Pöchlarn has come into this land?" As King Gunther finished speaking,
bold Hagen caught sight of good Rüdiger. {1182} He and his kinsmen all
ran out, and five hundred knights were seen getting down from their
horses. The men from the land of the Huns were well received; no
messengers ever wore such splendid clothes.

{1183} Hagen of Tronje called out in a loud voice: "Now welcome, in
God's name, to these warriors, the lord of Pöchlarn and all his men!"
The quick Huns were received with full honor. {1184} The king's closest
kinsmen went to where they stood, and Ortwin of Metz said to Rüdiger:
"In all our days we have never seen guests here so gladly. I say that
in all truth."

{1185} The warriors thanked them all around for their greeting. With
their followers they went into the hall, where they found the king
among many splendid men. The lord rose from his seat; that was done
out of great courtesy. {1186} How courteously he went to meet the
envoys! Gunther and Giselher received the guest and his men with great
warmth, as was only proper. He took good Rüdiger by the hand {1187} and
led him to the seat where he himself sat. The guests were served drink,
and gladly: the very best mead, and the finest wine to be found
anywhere in the land along the Rhine. {1188} Giselher and Gere had both
come in, and Dankwart and Volker had heard about the guests too. They
were in good spirits, and they greeted the fine, noble knights in the
king's presence.

{1189} Hagen of Tronje said to his lord: "These warriors ought to repay
forever what the margrave once did for our sake. Beautiful Gotelind's
husband deserves a reward for it."

{1190} King Gunther said: "I cannot hold back the question. You must
tell me how they both are, Etzel and Helche in the land of the Huns."
The margrave said: "I will gladly tell you." {1191} He rose from his
seat with all his men and said to the king: "If it may be so, and you
give me leave, prince, then I will hold nothing back. I will gladly
tell you the news I bring."

{1192} The king said: "Whatever news has been sent to us through you, I
give you leave to tell it without taking counsel with my kinsmen. Let
me and my men hear it, for I begrudge you no honor you may win here."

{1193} The worthy envoy said: "My great lord sends to you on the Rhine
his loyal service, and to all the kinsmen you may have. This embassy
is made in great loyalty. {1194} The noble king asks you to share his
sorrow. His people are without joy. My lady is dead: mighty Helche, my
lord's wife. By her death many a young lady has been orphaned,
{1195} daughters of noble princes whom she had raised. The land is in
a pitiful state because of it. There is no one now, alas, to care for
them faithfully, and so I think the king's grief will be slow to
settle."

{1196} "God reward him," said Gunther, "for sending his service so
willingly to me and to my kinsmen. I have gladly heard his greeting
here, and both my kinsmen and my men will gladly repay it."

{1197} Gernot, the warrior of Burgundy, said: "The world will always
mourn beautiful Helche's death, for the many virtues she knew how to
practice." Hagen agreed with what he said, and so did many another
warrior.

{1198} Then Rüdiger, the noble, lordly envoy, spoke again: "Since you
give me leave, king, I shall tell you more of what my dear lord has
sent to say to you, for since Helche died his life has gone very
hard. {1199} My lord has been told that Kriemhild is without a husband,
that Lord Siegfried is dead. If that is how things stand, and if you
will allow it, she is to wear a crown before Etzel's warriors. That is
what my lord told me to say."

{1200} The mighty king said, and his manner was very courtly: "She
shall hear my wish, if she is willing. I will let you know her answer
within three days. Why should I refuse Etzel before I have learned her
mind?"

{1201} Meanwhile the guests were made comfortable, and they were served
so well that Rüdiger admitted he had friends among Gunther's men.
Hagen served him gladly; Rüdiger had once done the same for him.

{1202} So Gunther let the matter rest until the third day. Then the
king sent for counsel — he acted very wisely — to see whether his
kinsmen thought it a good thing that Kriemhild should take King Etzel
as her husband. {1203} All of them advised it together, all but
Hagen. He said to Gunther the warrior: "If you have any sense, you will
take good care that you never do it, even if she were willing to go
along with you."

{1204} "Why," said Gunther, "should I not go along with it? Whatever
happiness comes to the queen, I ought to be glad to grant her; she is
my sister. We ought to be arranging it ourselves, if it can bring her
honor."

{1205} Hagen said: "Drop this talk. If you knew Etzel as I know him,
and if she is to love him, as I hear you say, then you will have
reason to worry for the first time, and with good cause."

{1206} "Why?" said Gunther. "I will take very good care never to come
so near him that I have to suffer any hatred from him, even if she
were his wife." But Hagen said: "I will never advise it."

{1207} They sent for Gernot and for Giselher, to see whether the two
lords thought it a good thing that Kriemhild should love the mighty,
noble king. Hagen still argued against it, and no one else.

{1208} Giselher of Burgundy, the warrior, said: "Friend Hagen, you can
still show some loyalty. Make up to her the wrongs you have done her.
Whatever good comes to her, you ought to leave it unopposed.
{1209} Truly, you have done my sister so many wrongs," bold Giselher
went on, "that she would have every reason to be angry with you. No
man ever robbed a woman of more joy."

{1210} Hagen answered: "I will tell you plainly what I know for
certain: if she takes Etzel, and lives to see the day, she will still
do us great harm, however she manages it. Truly, many a fine man will
be in her service."

{1211} Bold Gernot answered Hagen: "It can stay that way until they
are both dead — that we never ride into Etzel's land. Let us be loyal
to her; that serves our honor."

{1212} Hagen said again: "No one can tell me otherwise. If noble
Kriemhild is to wear Helche's crown, she will do us great harm yet,
however she brings it about. You should let it be; that suits you
warriors far better."

{1213} Giselher, beautiful Ute's son, said angrily: "We need not all
act like traitors. Whatever honor comes to her, we ought to be glad of
it. Whatever you say, Hagen, I will serve her, out of my loyalty."

{1214} When Hagen heard that, it put him in a dark mood. Gernot and
Giselher, the proud, good knights, and mighty Gunther decided in the
end that if Kriemhild consented, they would let it happen without any
ill will.

{1215} Prince Gere said: "I will tell the lady that she should look
kindly on King Etzel. So many warriors are his subjects and stand in
great fear of him. He can well make up to her whatever wrong has been
done her."

{1216} The quick warrior went to where he found Kriemhild. She
received him kindly, and how quickly he spoke: "You may well greet me
gladly and give me the messenger's reward. Good luck is about to part
you from all your troubles, and very soon. {1217} For the sake of your
love, lady, one of the very best men who ever won a king's land with
full honor, or who ever ought to wear a crown, has sent here to us.
Noble knights have come wooing; your brother told me to tell you so."

{1218} The grief-stricken woman said: "God forbid that you and all my
kinsmen should mock a poor wretch like me. What could I be to any man
who had ever known heartfelt love from a good wife?"

{1219} She argued hard against it. But then her brother Gernot came,
and young Giselher, and they lovingly begged her to comfort her
heart: if she took the king, it would truly be good for her.
{1220} But no one could bring the woman round to loving any man at
all. Then the warriors begged her: "If you do nothing else, at least
let it be seen that you are willing to receive the envoy."

{1221} "That one thing I will not refuse," said the noble woman. "I
would gladly see Rüdiger, for his many virtues. If it were not he who
had been sent here, whoever else the envoy was, he would never have
met me at all. {1222} Tomorrow," she said, "tell him to come here to
my chamber. I will let him hear my wishes in full; I will tell him
myself." And her great lamenting broke out all over again.

{1223} Noble Rüdiger wanted nothing more than to see the high-born
queen. He knew himself to be so persuasive that, if it could ever be
done at all, she would have to let the warrior talk her round.

{1224} Early the next morning, when Mass had been sung, the noble
envoys came. There was a great crowd of those who wanted to go to
court with Rüdiger, and many a splendid man was seen in full dress.
{1225} High-born Kriemhild, very heavy at heart, waited for Rüdiger,
the good and noble envoy. He found her in the clothes she wore every
day, while her household around her wore rich clothes in plenty.
{1226} She went to the door to meet him and received the noble man
very kindly. He came in to her as one of twelve. He was shown great
respect; no higher envoys had ever come to her.

{1227} The lord and his men were asked to sit. The two margraves,
Eckewart and Gere, fine and noble warriors, were seen standing before
her. Out of regard for the lady of the house, they saw no one there in
good spirits. {1228} They saw many beautiful women seated before her,
but Kriemhild herself knew nothing but grief. The dress over her
breast was wet with hot tears, and the noble margrave saw it on
Kriemhild plainly.

{1229} The lordly envoy said: "Most noble king's daughter, will you
allow me and the companions who came here with me to stand before you
and tell you the news we have ridden here to bring?"

{1230} "You have my leave," said the queen. "Whatever you wish to say,
I am of a mind to hear it gladly. You are a good envoy." But the
others could hear her unwilling heart plainly enough.

{1231} Lord Rüdiger of Pöchlarn said: "Etzel, a noble king, has sent
you, lady, great love in all loyalty, here into this land. He has sent
many good warriors here to woo for your love. {1232} He offers you
with all his heart love without sorrow, and he is ready to give you
the same lasting friendship he once gave Lady Helche, who lay close to
his heart. Truly, since he lost her virtues he has had many a joyless
day."

{1233} The queen said: "Margrave Rüdiger, if there were anyone who
knew my bitter pain, he would not ask me to love any man. Truly, I
lost one of the best husbands a woman ever had."

{1234} "What can make up for grief," said the very bold man, "but
loving companionship? Whoever can bring that about, and then chooses
the one who is rightly suited to him — nothing does so much good
against heartfelt sorrow. {1235} And if you are willing to love my
noble lord, you shall hold power over twelve mighty crowns. On top of
that my lord will give you the lands of a full thirty princes, every
one of which his brave hand has conquered. {1236} You shall also be
lady over many worthy men who were subjects of my lady Helche, and
over many ladies of high princely blood who were under her rule,"
said the bold, brave warrior. {1237} "And besides, my lord says to
tell you that if you consent to wear the crown beside the king, he
will give you the very highest power Helche ever held. You shall hold
it with full authority over Etzel's men."

{1238} The queen said: "How could I ever again want to be a hero's
wife? Death has done me such bitter wrong through one man that I must
live without joy to the end of my days."

{1239} The Huns said: "Mighty queen, your life with Etzel will be so
glorious that it will delight you forever, if it comes to pass, for
the mighty king has many a splendid warrior. {1240} If Helche's young
ladies and your maidens were to be one household together, warriors
would take heart at the sight of them. Let yourself be persuaded,
lady; it will truly be good for you."

{1241} She said, with all her courtesy: "Let the matter rest now until
early tomorrow, when you are to come here again. Then I will give you
my answer to what you have in mind." The bold, good warriors had to
accept that.

{1242} When they had all gone back to their lodgings, the noble lady
sent for Giselher and for her mother as well. She told them both that
weeping was what became her, and nothing else.

{1243} Her brother Giselher said: "Sister, I have been told, and I am
ready to believe it, that King Etzel would wipe away all your sorrows,
if you take him as your husband. Whatever anyone else advises, I think
it well done. {1244} He can well make up your loss to you," Giselher
went on. "From the Rhône to the Rhine, from the Elbe down to the sea,
there is no king so powerful. You may rejoice right away, if he makes
you his wife."

{1245} She said: "My dearest brother, why do you advise me this?
Mourning and weeping would always suit me better. How could I go to
court before warriors? If I was ever beautiful, that is gone from me
now."

{1246} Lady Ute said to her dear daughter: "Whatever your brothers
advise, dear child, do it. Follow your kinsmen and good things may come
to you. I have watched you in great grief for far too long."

{1247} Then Kriemhild prayed to God many times to grant her the means
to have gold, silver and clothes to give, as she had once at her
husband's side, when he was still alive and well. But she never again
lived through so happy an hour.

{1248} She thought to herself: "If I give myself to a pagan — and I
am a Christian woman — I will carry shame in the world forever. If he
gave me every kingdom there is, I could never do it." {1249} And there
she let it rest. All night until daybreak the lady lay in her bed
deep in thought, and her bright eyes never dried, until in the morning
she went to matins again.

{1250} Just at the hour of Mass the kings came, and again they took
their sister in hand. Truly, they urged her to love the king of the
land of the Huns, and not one of them found the lady anything but
sad.

{1251} Then they sent for Etzel's men, who by now would gladly have
taken their leave and gone, won or lost, however it turned out.
Rüdiger came to court, and the heroes agreed among themselves
{1252} that they should learn the noble prince's true mind, and learn
it soon; that seemed the best course to all of them, for the road
home to their own land was long. Rüdiger was brought to where he found
Kriemhild.

{1253} The warrior began to beg the noble queen, very lovingly, to let
him hear what message she wished to send to Etzel's land. I think he
found nothing in her but refusal: {1254} she would never love any man
again. The margrave said: "That would be a wrong. Why would you let such
beauty waste away? You can still, with honor, become a good
man's wife."

{1255} All their begging did no good, until Rüdiger spoke to the noble
queen in private: he would make good to her whatever had ever
happened to her. At that her great misery began, a little, to ease.

{1256} He said to the queen: "Let your weeping be. If you had no one
among the Huns but me alone, and my loyal kinsmen, and my men, anyone
who had ever done you any wrong would pay dearly for it."

{1257} That lightened the lady's heart a good deal. She said: "Then
swear me an oath that whatever anyone does to me, you will be the
first to right my wrongs." The margrave said: "Lady, I am ready to do
that." {1258} Rüdiger swore to her then, with all his men, to serve
her always in loyalty, and that the noble warriors from Etzel's land
would never refuse her anything that could bring her honor. Rüdiger's
hand gave her that pledge.

{1259} Then the loyal woman thought: "Now that I have won so many
friends, I will let people say whatever they like about me, a woman
full of grief. What if my dear husband's death might still be
avenged?"

{1260} She thought: "Since Etzel has so many warriors, if I
command them, I can do whatever I want. And he is so rich, too, that I
will have something to give. Hateful Hagen has stripped me of
everything I had."

{1261} She said to Rüdiger: "If I had heard that he was not a pagan, I
would gladly go wherever he wished, and take him as my husband." The
margrave said: "Let that thought go, lady. {1262} He has so many
warriors of the Christian faith that you will never be unhappy at the
king's side. And what if you managed to bring him to be baptized? For
that alone you might gladly become King Etzel's wife."

{1263} Her brothers said again: "Now promise it, sister. You must let
your unhappiness go." They begged her for so long that at last, sad as
she was, she promised before the heroes that she would be Etzel's
wife. {1264} She said: "I will do as you say — poor queen that I am —
and go to the Huns as soon as it can be managed, once I have the
kinsmen who will take me to his land." And beautiful Kriemhild gave
her hand on it before the heroes.

{1265} The margrave said: "If you have two men, I have more than that.
It will be done well: we will bring you across the Rhine with full
honor. You are not to stay here in Burgundy any longer, lady. {1266} I
have five hundred men, and my kinsmen besides. They shall serve you
here, and at home too, lady, whatever you command, and I will do the
same myself; whenever you remind me of this promise, I will never be
ashamed of it. {1267} Now have your riding gear made ready — Rüdiger's
advice will never bring you sorrow — and tell the maidens you mean to
take with you. Truly, many a chosen hero will come to meet us on the
road."

{1268} She still had the harness they had ridden with before, in
Siegfried's time, so that she could take many a maiden along with
honor whenever she wanted to leave. Ah, what good saddles they found
for the beautiful women! {1269} If they had ever worn rich clothes
before, many more were made ready now for their journey, for they had
been told so much about the king. They unlocked the chests that had
stood well locked until then. {1270} For a full four and a half days
they were hard at work, bringing out of the presses all that lay
inside. Then Kriemhild began to open up her whole treasury: she wanted
to make all of Rüdiger's men rich.

{1271} She still had gold from the Nibelung land, and she meant to
share it out among the Huns with her own hand — so much that a
hundred horses could not have carried it all. Hagen heard the news of
what Kriemhild was doing. {1272} He said: "Since Kriemhild will never
be my friend, Siegfried's gold must stay here too. Why should I leave
my enemies such great wealth? I know very well what Kriemhild would
do with this treasure. {1273} If she took it away from here, I am
convinced it would be handed out to buy hatred against me. Besides,
they have no horses that could carry it. Hagen means to keep it; tell
Kriemhild that."

{1274} When she heard this news, it was a bitter wrong to her. All
three kings were told of it too, and they would gladly have changed
it, but when that did not happen, noble Rüdiger spoke very cheerfully:
{1275} "Mighty queen, why do you grieve over the gold? King Etzel
loves you so much that once his eyes have seen you, he will give you
more than you could ever spend. That I will swear to you, lady."

{1276} The queen said: "Most noble Rüdiger, no king's daughter ever
had greater wealth than what Hagen has stripped from me." Then her
brother Gernot went to her chamber. {1277} With the king's authority
he thrust the key into the door, and Kriemhild's gold was handed out:
thirty thousand marks' worth, or more. He told the guests to take it,
and Gunther was glad of that.

{1278} Gotelind's husband, the lord of Pöchlarn, said: "If my lady
Kriemhild had all of it, every bit that was ever carried out of the
Nibelung land, little of it would ever touch my hand or the queen's.
{1279} Have them keep it, for I want none of it. Truly, I brought so
much of my own from home that we will do very well on the road, and
our provision for the journey is in splendid order."

{1280} Before this, in all that time, her maidens had filled twelve
chests with the very best gold to be found anywhere. That they took
with them, and many of the women's ornaments that they were to have on
the journey. {1281} The power of grim Hagen seemed to her too great.
She still had a thousand marks of her gold for offerings, and she gave
it out for the soul of her dear husband. Rüdiger thought that done in
great loyalty.

{1282} The grieving lady said: "Where are my friends, those who are
willing to go into exile for my sake? Those who will ride with me into
the land of the Huns, let them take my treasure and buy horses and
clothes."

{1283} Margrave Eckewart said to the queen: "From the first day I
became one of your household I have served you loyally," said the
warrior, "and I will go on doing the same for you to the end of my
days. {1284} I will take five hundred of my own men with me too, and
give them to your service in true loyalty. Nothing will ever part us,
unless death does it." Kriemhild bowed to him for those words; truly,
she had need of them.

{1285} Then the horses were led out, for they wanted to set off. There
was great weeping among her kinsmen. Mighty Ute and many a beautiful
girl showed how much Lady Kriemhild's going grieved them. {1286} She
took a hundred high-born maidens with her, dressed as well as suited
them. The tears fell from their bright eyes. But later they also
lived to see much joy with Etzel.

{1287} Then Lord Giselher came, and Gernot too, with their followers,
as their good breeding demanded. They wanted to escort their dear
sister on her way, and they brought a full thousand fine warriors of
their own. {1288} Quick Gere came, and Ortwin, and Rumold, master of
the kitchen, had to be there too. They arranged night quarters all the
way to the Danube. Gunther himself rode no farther than a little way
out of the city.

{1289} Before they left the Rhine they had sent their messengers ahead,
riding fast, into the land of the Huns, to tell the king that Rüdiger
had won the noble, high-born queen to be his wife.
