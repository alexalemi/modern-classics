Chapter 14: How the Queens Quarreled

{814} One day, before vespers, a great commotion broke out in the
courtyard, where many warriors were riding at knightly games to pass
the time, and crowds of women and men ran out to watch. {815} The two
mighty queens were sitting together, and their thoughts were on two
warriors who were well worth praising. Then beautiful Kriemhild said:
"I have a husband who ought by rights to hold all these kingdoms in his
hands."

{816} Lady Brunhild said: "How could that be? If no one were alive but
him and you, then the kingdoms might well be subject to him. But as
long as Gunther lives, it can never happen."

{817} Kriemhild answered: "Just look at him standing there. Look how
royally he walks ahead of the warriors, like the bright moon in front
of the stars. I have every reason to be glad."

{818} Lady Brunhild said: "However handsome your husband is, however
noble and however fine, you must still put Gunther ahead of him, the
warrior, your noble brother. Know this for certain: he must rank above
all kings."

{819} Then Lady Kriemhild said: "My husband is so worthy that I have
not praised him without cause. His honor is great in a great many
things. Believe me, Brunhild, he is easily Gunther's equal."

{820} "You must not take it badly of me, Kriemhild, for I have not said
this without cause. I heard them both declare it, when I first saw
them, when the king got the better of me and won my love in such knightly
fashion: {821} there Siegfried himself said that he was the king's
vassal. And so I have counted him my bondsman ever since I heard him
say it."

Beautiful Kriemhild said: "Then I would have been badly served.
{822} How could my noble brothers have arranged things so that I should be a
bondsman's wife? So I ask you, Brunhild, as a friend: for my sake, be
kind and let this talk drop."

{823} "I cannot let it drop," said the king's wife. "Why should I give
up so many knights who, along with that warrior, owe us their service?"

Beautiful Kriemhild grew very angry. {824} "You will have to give him
up, and any idea that he will ever do you a day's service. He is
worthier than my brother Gunther, noble man that he is. You must spare
me any more of what I have just heard from you. {825} And it always
surprises me, since he is your bondsman and you have such power over
the two of us, that he has been allowed to leave his tribute unpaid for
so long. By rights I should be spared your arrogance."

{826} "You set yourself too high," said the king's wife. "Now I will
gladly see whether people hold you in as much honor as they hold me."

The two ladies were both in a fury. {827} Lady Kriemhild said: "Then
this is what must happen. Since you have declared my husband your
bondsman, the men of both kings shall see today whether I dare walk
into the cathedral ahead of the king's wife. {828} You will see today
that I am noble and freeborn, and that my husband is worthier than
yours. I will not be insulted with that. And you shall see tonight how
your bondswoman {829} walks to court ahead of the warriors of Burgundy.
I mean to be held worthier myself than anyone ever knew a queen to be
who ever wore a crown." So a great deal of hatred rose up between the
two ladies.

{830} Then Brunhild said: "If you will not be a bondswoman, then you and
your ladies must keep apart from my household when we go to the
cathedral." And Kriemhild answered: "Truly, that will be done."

{831} "Dress yourselves, my maids," said Siegfried's wife. "I must come
out of this without shame. Let it be seen whether you have rich
clothes. Brunhild may yet be glad to take back what she has said."
{832} They did not need much urging; they went looking for rich
clothes, and many a lady and maiden was handsomely turned out. Then the
noble king's wife went out with her household, and beautiful
Kriemhild's women were handsomely turned out too. {833} She had
brought forty-three maidens with her to the Rhine, and they wore bright
silks woven in Arabia. So the lovely maidens came to the cathedral, and
all Siegfried's men were waiting for them in front of the house.

{834} The people wondered what had happened, that the queens were seen
so separated, no longer walking together as they used to. Later many a
warrior suffered bitterly for it. {835} Here, in front of the
cathedral, stood Gunther's wife, and many a knight was passing the time
pleasantly with the lovely ladies he was watching. Then Lady Kriemhild
came with a large and splendid company. {836} Whatever clothes the
children of noble knights had ever worn, beside her household they were
so much wind. She was so rich in goods that the wives of thirty kings
together could not have produced what Kriemhild did. {837} Wish as you
might, you could not say that such rich clothes had ever been seen
before as her lovely maidens wore at that hour. Kriemhild would have
let it be, except to hurt Brunhild.

{838} They met in front of the wide cathedral. And the mistress of the
house, out of sheer hatred, told Kriemhild rudely to stand still: "A
bondswoman shall never walk in ahead of a king's wife."

{839} Beautiful Kriemhild answered, and her heart was full of anger:
"You should have kept your mouth shut; that would have been better for
you. You have shamed your own lovely body yourself. How could a
vassal's concubine ever become a king's wife?"

{840} "Whom are you calling a concubine here?" said the king's wife.

"I am calling you one," Kriemhild said. "It was Siegfried, my dear
husband, who was the first to have your lovely body. It was not my
brother who took your virginity. {841} Where were your wits? It was a
wicked trick. Why did you let him love you, if he is your bondsman? I
hear you complaining," said Kriemhild, "without any cause at all."

"Truly," said Brunhild, "I will tell Gunther this."

{842} "What is that to me? Your arrogance has betrayed you. With your
own words you have claimed me for your service. Know this, on my true
loyalty: it will grieve me as long as I live, and I will never again be
a loyal friend to your secrets."

{843} Brunhild wept. Kriemhild waited no longer: she walked into the
cathedral with her household ahead of the king's wife. That was the
start of a great hatred, and bright eyes were dimmed and wet because of
it.

{844} However much was sung and done there in God's service, the time
seemed far too long to Brunhild, for her body and her spirit were
heavy. Many a bold, good hero would pay for that later. {845} Brunhild
went out with her ladies and stood in front of the cathedral. She
thought: "Kriemhild must tell me more about what that sharp-tongued
woman is accusing me of so loudly. If he has boasted of it, it will
cost Siegfried his life."

{846} Now noble Kriemhild came out with many a bold man, and Lady
Brunhild said: "Stand still a moment. You said I was a concubine; you
must let me see your proof. Your words have hurt me, I want you to know
that."

{847} Lady Kriemhild said: "You had better let me pass. I prove it
with the gold I wear on my hand: my lover brought it to me when he
first lay with me." Brunhild never lived through a worse day.
{848} She said: "This fine gold was stolen from me, and it has been wickedly
hidden from me a very long time. I will get to the bottom of who took
it from me." Both the ladies were now in a great passion.

{849} Kriemhild said again: "I will not be made out a thief. You
should have thought of that, if your honor was dear to you. I prove
with the belt I wear here around my waist that I am not lying: truly,
my Siegfried was your man." {850} The belt she wore was silk from
Nineveh, set with precious stones, and truly it was fine enough. When
Lady Brunhild saw it, she began to weep. Now Gunther and all the men of
Burgundy would have to hear of this.

{851} The queen said: "Send here for the prince of the Rhine. I want
him to hear how his sister has shamed me. She says openly here that I
am Siegfried's woman."

{852} The king came with his warriors, and he saw his dear wife
weeping. How gently he spoke: "Tell me, dear lady, who has done
anything to you?" She said to the king: "I stand here in misery.
{853} Your sister would gladly strip me of all my honor. I make my complaint
to you: she says her husband Siegfried has had me as his concubine."

King Gunther said: "Then she has done wrong."

{854} "She is wearing my belt here, which I lost, and my red gold. I
bitterly regret that I was ever born unless you clear me, King, of this
great shame. If you do, I will serve you for it forever."

{855} King Gunther said: "He shall come forward. If he has boasted of
it, he must let us hear it, or else he must deny it, the hero from the
Netherlands." And Kriemhild's beloved was sent for at once.

{856} When Lord Siegfried saw the two distressed women, he had no idea
of the story, and how quickly he spoke: "Why are these ladies weeping?
I would like to know that, and what reason the king has for sending for
me."

{857} King Gunther said: "It grieves me badly. My lady Brunhild has
told me a story here: that you boasted you were the first to have her
lovely body. Your wife Kriemhild says so."

{858} Strong Siegfried said: "If she has said that, she will be sorry
for it before I am done. And I will clear myself before all your men
with solemn oaths that I never said it."

{859} The king of the Rhine said: "Then let it be seen. The oath you
offer, let it be sworn here and now, and I will hold you clear of every
false charge." The proud Burgundians were told to stand in a ring.
{860} Bold Siegfried held out his hand for the oath. Then the mighty
king said: "Your great innocence is so well known to me that I release
you. What my sister accuses you of, you never did."

{861} Siegfried said: "If my wife gets any good out of having made
Brunhild unhappy, then truly I am sorry for it beyond all measure." And
the good, handsome knights looked at one another.

{862} "Women should be brought up," said Siegfried the warrior, "to
leave idle talk alone. Forbid it to your wife, and I will do the same
to mine. Truly, I am ashamed of her gross bad manners."

{863} Many fair women were parted by those words. And Brunhild grieved
so bitterly that it moved Gunther's men to pity. Then Hagen of Tronje
came to his lady. {864} He found her weeping and asked what was wrong.
She told him the story, and on the spot he promised her that Kriemhild's
husband would pay for it, or he himself would never be happy again.

{865} Ortwin and Gernot joined the talk, as the heroes were planning
Siegfried's death. Giselher came too, noble Ute's son, and when he
heard what they were saying, he spoke like a loyal man: {866} "You
good warriors, why are you doing this? Siegfried never earned such
hatred that he should lose his life for it. Truly, women get angry over
very little."

{867} "Are we to raise cuckolds?" Hagen said. "Good warriors like us
would have little honor from that. Because he has boasted of my dear
lady, I will die for it myself unless it costs him his life."

{868} Then the king himself said: "He has done us nothing but good and
brought us nothing but honor. Let him live. What would it gain me to
turn against the warrior now? He was always loyal to us, and he was
loyal gladly."

{869} Ortwin of Metz, the warrior, said: "His great strength will not
help him. If my lord allows it, I will do him harm." So the heroes had
declared themselves his enemies without any cause. {870} No one took
it further, except that Hagen kept telling Gunther, every chance he
got, that if Siegfried were no longer alive, a great many kings' lands
would be subject to him. The hero began to brood on it.

{871} They let it rest. The games went on, and oh, what stout shafts
were splintered in front of the cathedral, before Siegfried's wife, all
the way to the hall! But all Gunther's men were in a black mood.
{872} The king said: "Let this murderous anger go. He was born to be
our good fortune and our honor. Besides, the man is so wonderfully bold
and so terribly strong that if he ever found out, no one would dare
face him."

{873} "No," said Hagen. "You can keep quiet about it. I trust myself to
arrange it so secretly that he will be sorry for Brunhild's tears.
Truly, Hagen is his enemy from this day on."

{874} King Gunther said: "How can that be done?" And Hagen answered:
"I will tell you. We have messengers ride into this land to us, men no
one here knows, and declare war on us openly. {875} Then you announce
in front of the guests that you and your men are going to war. Once
that is done, he will promise to serve you in it, and that is how he
will lose his life: I will get the secret out of the bold warrior's
wife."

{876} The king followed Hagen, his vassal, and it was a bad thing he
did. The chosen knights began to plot a great treachery before anyone
could find it out. Because two women quarreled, many a hero lost his
life.
