Chapter 19: How the Nibelung Treasure Was Brought to Worms

{1101} When noble Kriemhild was left a widow like this, Count Eckewart
stayed on in the land with his men and served her every day; and often
he helped his lady mourn his lord. {1102} At Worms, beside the
cathedral, they built a house for her, wide and very large, rich and
grand, and there she lived from then on with her household, without
joy. She loved to go to church, and she went faithfully. {1103} To the
place where her beloved lay buried she went all the time, with a heavy
heart; how seldom she missed a day. She asked the good God to care for
his soul, and she wept for the warrior many times over, with great
loyalty.

{1104} Ute and her household comforted her at every hour, but her
heart was so terribly wounded that nothing could help her, whatever
comfort they offered. She had the greatest longing for her dear
husband {1105} that any woman ever had for the man she loved; you
could see her great goodness in it. She mourned until her end, as long
as she lived. Later, bold Siegfried's wife took her revenge, and took
it with courage.

{1106} So she sat with her grief after her husband's death, it is all
true, for a good three and a half years, and in all that time she
never spoke a word to Gunther and never once set eyes on her enemy
Hagen.

{1107} Then the hero of Tronje said: "If you could manage to make a
friend of your sister again, then the Nibelung gold would come to
these lands. You could win a great deal of it, if the queen were
well-disposed toward us."

{1108} "Let us try it," said Gunther. "My brothers are with her. We
will ask them to bring it about that she is our friend, if we can win
her over to the point that she is glad to see it."

"I do not believe," said Hagen, "that it will ever happen."

{1109} Then he had Ortwin go to court, and the margrave Gere too. When
that was done, they brought Gernot as well, and young Giselher. Then
they tried it with Lady Kriemhild in a friendly way. {1110} Bold
Gernot of Burgundy said: "Lady, you have mourned Siegfried's death too
long. The king will give you proof that he did not kill him. People
hear you lamenting so terribly, all the time."

{1111} She said: "No one accuses him of it. Hagen's hand struck him
down, at the place where he could be wounded. When he found that out
from me, how could I have believed that he hated him? I would have
taken good care," said the queen, {1112} "never to betray his life.
Then I could stop my weeping now, poor woman that I am. I will never
be a friend to the ones who did it." Then Giselher, that very handsome
man, began to plead with her.

{1113} "I will greet the king." When she had granted him that, Gunther
was seen standing before her with his closest kinsmen. But Hagen did
not dare come into her presence; he knew his guilt well enough. He had
done her wrong. {1114} Since she was willing to give up her hatred of
Gunther, and he was to kiss her, it would have suited him far better
if the wrong had never been done to her on his advice; then he could
have gone to Kriemhild with nothing on his conscience. {1115} Never
was peace made among kinsmen with so many tears. Her loss still hurt
her. She forgave them all, except the one man: no one would ever have
killed Siegfried if Hagen had not done it.

{1116} Not long after that they arranged for Lady Kriemhild to have
the great treasure brought from the Nibelung land to the Rhine. It was
her morning gift, the gift a husband gives his bride the morning after
the wedding, and it was hers by right. {1117} Giselher and Gernot went
to fetch it. Kriemhild ordered eight thousand men to bring it from
where it lay hidden, where the warrior Alberich guarded it with his
closest kinsmen.

{1118} When they saw the men from the Rhine coming for the treasure,
bold Alberich said to his kinsmen: "We dare not keep back any of the
treasure from her, since the noble queen claims it as her morning
gift. {1119} And yet it would never happen at all," said Alberich, "if
we had not lost, along with Siegfried, the good cloak of invisibility
— and lost it so badly. Fair Kriemhild's beloved wore it all the time.
{1120} Now it has turned out badly for Siegfried, worse luck, that the
hero took that cloak from us and that all this land had to serve him."
Then the keeper went to where the keys were kept.

{1121} Kriemhild's men stood before the mountain, and some of her
kinsmen with them. They had the treasure carried down to the sea and
into the ships, and it was carried on the waves upriver to the Rhine.
{1122} Now you can hear wonders told of that treasure. As much as
twelve wagons could carry at the most, they hauled it away from the
mountain in four days and nights, and each wagon had to make three
trips a day. {1123} It was nothing but gems and gold, and if the wages
of the whole world had been paid out of it, it would not have been a
single mark the poorer. Truly, Hagen had not wanted it for nothing.
{1124} The best of it all lay buried in it: a little rod of gold, and
whoever learned its secret could be master over every single man in
all the world. Many of Alberich's kinsmen came away with Gernot.

{1125} When they had stored the treasure in Gunther's land and the
queen first took charge of it, chambers and towers were filled with
it. No one ever heard such wonders told of wealth. {1126} And had
there been a thousand times as much, if only Lord Siegfried were alive
and well, Kriemhild would gladly have stood beside him with empty
hands. No hero ever won a more loyal wife.

{1127} Now that she had the treasure, she brought many warriors no one
knew into the land. Yes, the lady's hand gave so freely that such
generosity was never seen again. She practiced many good virtues;
people granted the queen that. {1128} She began to give now to the
poor and the rich alike, so freely that Hagen said if she lived much
longer she would win so many men into her service that it would end in
sorrow for them.

{1129} Then King Gunther said: "Her life and her goods are her own.
How am I to stop whatever she does with them? I had a hard enough time
winning her goodwill back. Let us not care where she shares out her
silver and her gold."

{1130} Hagen said to the king: "A sensible man would not leave any of
that treasure to any woman. With her gifts she will bring on the day
that the bold Burgundians may bitterly regret."

{1131} King Gunther said: "I swore her an oath that I would never do
her any wrong again, and I will keep it from now on. She is my
sister."

Then Hagen said: "Let me be the guilty one."

{1132} Some of their oaths were not kept. They took the mighty
treasure from the widow, and Hagen got hold of all the keys. That made
her brother Gernot angry when he learned the truth of it. {1133} Lord
Giselher said: "Hagen has done my sister a great wrong. I ought to put
a stop to it. If he were not my kinsman, it would cost him his life."
Siegfried's wife wept all over again.

{1134} Lord Gernot said: "Before this gold troubles us forever, we
should have the whole of it sunk in the Rhine; then it would belong to
no one." Very pitifully she went and stood before her brother
Giselher. {1135} "Dear brother," she said, "you must think of me. You
must be my guardian, for my life and my goods both." He said to the
lady: "It shall be done when we come back again. We are planning to
ride."

{1136} The king and his kinsmen then left the land, the very best men
among them that could be found anywhere — all but Hagen alone. He
stayed behind out of the hatred he bore Kriemhild, and he stayed
behind on purpose. {1137} Before the mighty king came home again,
Hagen had taken the whole treasure. He poured it all into the Rhine at
Loche. He thought he would make use of it himself; that was never to
be. {1138} The princes came back, and many men with them. Kriemhild
began to lament her great loss, with her maidens and her ladies; it
grieved them bitterly. Giselher would gladly have stood by her with
all his loyalty. {1139} They all said together: "He has done wrong."
Hagen kept out of the princes' anger until he had won back their
favor, and they let him live. But Kriemhild could never have been more
his enemy than she was now.

{1140} Before Hagen of Tronje hid the treasure like this, they had
bound themselves with oaths so strong that it should stay hidden as
long as any one of them was alive. Afterwards they could not give it
to themselves or to anyone else. {1141} Her heart was weighed down
with fresh sorrow, for her husband's death and because they had taken
her wealth from her so completely, and her lament never stopped as
long as she lived, down to her last day. {1142} After Siegfried's
death, it is the plain truth, she lived in great pain for thirteen
years, and she could not forget the warrior's death. She was loyal to
him; most people grant her that.
