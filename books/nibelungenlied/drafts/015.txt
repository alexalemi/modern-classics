Chapter 16: How Siegfried Was Murdered

{916} Gunther and Hagen, those bold warriors, treacherously proclaimed
a hunt in the forest. With their sharp spears they meant to hunt boars
and bears and bison. What could be braver? {917} Siegfried rode with
them in lordly style, and food of every kind was carried along. At a
clear spring he would later lose his life, and it was Brunhild, King
Gunther's wife, who had counseled it.

{918} The bold warrior went to find Kriemhild. His fine hunting
clothes and his companions' were already loaded on the pack-horses;
they were going to cross the Rhine. Kriemhild could never have been
sadder. {919} He kissed his beloved on the mouth. "God grant that I
see you again in health, lady, and that your eyes see me. Pass the
time with your kinsmen who love you; I cannot stay at home."

{920} Then she thought of what she had told Hagen—she dared not say a
word of it—and the noble queen began to lament that she had ever been
born. Lord Siegfried's wife wept without measure. {921} She said to
the warrior: "Let your hunting be. Last night I dreamed that two wild
boars chased you across the heath, and the flowers turned red. I have
good reason to weep like this, believe me. {922} I am terribly afraid
of some plot, that we may have wronged someone who could turn hatred
against us. Stay here, dear lord. I advise it in true loyalty."

{923} "My beloved," he said, "I will be back in a few days. I know of
no one here who bears me any hatred. All your kinsmen are my friends
alike, and I have deserved nothing else from the warriors here."

{924} "No, Lord Siegfried, truly I fear your fall. Last night I had a
terrible dream: two mountains fell down on top of you, and I never saw
you again. If you leave me, it hurts me to the heart."

{925} He took the good woman in his arms and caressed her lovely body
with tender kisses, and in a little while he took his leave and went.
Sadly, she never saw him in health again.

{926} Then they rode away into a deep forest for the sport of it. Many
a bold knight followed Gunther and his men, but Gernot and Giselher
had stayed at home. {927} Many loaded horses had crossed the Rhine
ahead of them, carrying for the hunting party bread and wine, meat and
fish, and plenty of other provisions, such as a king so rich has every
right to have. {928} The proud, bold hunters had camp made at the edge
of the green forest, where the game would break cover, on a wide
stretch of flat land by the river where they meant to hunt. Siegfried
had arrived there too, and the king was told of it.

{929} The hunting party now manned the watch-posts on every side. Then
the bold man, Siegfried the strong, said: "Who is going to guide us
into the forest after the game, you bold and daring heroes?"

{930} "Shall we split up," said Hagen, "before we begin the hunt? That
way my lords and I can tell who the best hunters on this forest trip
are. {931} We will divide the men and the hounds between us, and then
let each man go wherever he likes. Whoever hunts best shall have our
thanks." After that the hunters did not stay together long.

{932} Lord Siegfried said: "I have no need of hounds, except for one
tracker so well trained that it can follow an animal's trail through
the pines. We will find game enough," said Kriemhild's husband.

{933} Then an old huntsman took a good tracking hound, and in a short
while he had brought the lord to where they found animals in plenty.
Whatever rose from its lair the companions hunted down, as good
hunters still do. {934} Whatever the hound started, bold Siegfried,
the hero from the Netherlands, killed with his own hand. His horse ran
so fast that nothing got away from him, and he won the praise of them
all at the hunt.

{935} He was more than capable in everything. The first animal he
killed with his hand was a strong young boar, and soon after that he
found a monstrous lion. {936} When the hound started it, he shot it
with his bow; he had fitted a strong arrow to the string, and after
the shot the lion ran only three bounds. His hunting companions
thanked Siegfried. {937} After that he quickly killed a bison and an
elk, four strong aurochs and a savage schelch, a beast of the old
forests. His horse carried him so fast that nothing escaped him; hart
or hind could hardly get away from him.

{938} The tracking hound found a great boar. When it tried to run, the
master of that hunt was there at once and stood in its path. The boar
charged the hero in a rage, {939} and Kriemhild's husband killed it
with his sword. Another hunter would not have managed that so easily.
When he had felled it, they leashed the tracking hound, and the
Burgundians soon knew all about his rich hunting.

{940} Then his hunters said: "If it can be done without offense, Lord
Siegfried, leave a few of the animals alive for us. You are emptying
the mountain and the forest for us today." At that the bold, daring
warrior began to smile.

{941} Now on every side they heard clamor and din. The noise of men
and hounds was so great that the mountain and the pine woods echoed it
back. The hunters had loosed twenty-four packs, {942} and many an
animal had to lose its life. Each of them thought he would be given
the prize of the hunt, but that could not happen, once strong
Siegfried was seen standing by the fire. {943} The hunt was over, and
yet not entirely. Those who were going to the fire brought with them
hides of many animals and game in plenty. What a load of it the king's
household carried to the kitchen!

{944} Then the king had word sent to the chosen hunters that he wanted
to eat. A horn was blown once, very loud, and by that they knew the
noble prince was to be found at the camp. {945} One of Siegfried's
huntsmen said: "My lord, I hear from a horn's blast that we are to
come to the camp now. I will answer it." And with much blowing of
horns the companions were called in.

{946} Lord Siegfried said: "Now let us leave the pine woods too." His
horse carried him at an easy pace, and they hurried along with him.
With their noise they started a fearsome animal: it was a wild bear.
The warrior said over his shoulder: {947} "I am going to give us
companions some good entertainment. Let the hound loose; I see a bear.
It is coming with us to the camp. Unless it runs very fast, it will
never get away."

{948} The hound was loosed and the bear bounded off. Kriemhild's
husband wanted to ride it down, but it got into a thicket where that
could not be done, and the strong animal thought itself safe from the
hunter. {949} Then the proud, good knight sprang from his horse and
ran after it on foot. The animal was off its guard; it could not
escape him, and he caught it at once. Without giving it a single wound
the hero quickly bound it, {950} so that it could neither scratch nor
bite anyone. He tied it to his saddle and the swift man mounted. In
his high spirits the warrior, bold and good, brought it to the fire as
a bit of fun.

{951} How splendidly he rode to the camp! His spear was huge, strong
and broad. A handsome sword hung down to his spurs, and the lord
carried a beautiful horn of red gold. {952} I never heard tell of
better hunting clothes. He was seen wearing a coat of black silk and a
hat of sable, rich enough, and what costly bands he had on his quiver!
{953} A panther's skin was drawn over it, for the sweet smell. He also
carried a bow that anyone else who meant to draw it would have had to
draw with a winch; he did it himself. {954} His whole outfit was of
otter skin from head to foot, and scattered over the bright fur were
strips of gold, shining on both sides of the bold master of the hunt.
{955} He carried Balmung, a fine broad weapon, so sharp that it never
failed wherever it was struck on helmets; its edges were good. The
splendid hunter was in high spirits. {956} Since I am to tell you the
whole story: his noble quiver was full of good arrows with golden
sockets, their blades a good hand's breadth across. Whatever he cut
with those had to die very soon.

{957} So the noble knight rode off in his hunter's gear. Gunther's men
saw him coming; they ran to meet him and took his horse, and there
beside the saddle he led a big, strong bear. {958} When he had
dismounted he loosed the bonds from its feet and its muzzle. At once
the whole pack set up a howl, every hound that saw the bear. The
animal wanted to get to the forest, and the people were in trouble.
{959} Frightened by the noise, the bear blundered into the kitchen.
How many kitchen boys it scattered from the fire! Many a kettle was
knocked over and many a burning brand scattered, and what good food
was found lying in the ashes!

{960} The lords and their men sprang up from their seats. The bear
grew angry, and the king ordered the whole pack loosed from the
leashes where it lay. If it had all ended well, they would have had a
merry day. {961} With bows and spears, and no more delay, the quick
men ran after the bear. There were so many hounds about it that no one
could shoot, and the whole mountain rang with the people's shouting.
{962} The bear began to run from the hounds, and no one could keep up
with it except Kriemhild's husband. He ran it down with his sword and
struck it dead, and then the bear was carried back to the fire.
{963} Those who saw it said he was a powerful man.

The proud hunting party was called to the tables, and a good many of
them sat down on a beautiful meadow. What rich food was carried to
those noble hunters! {964} But the cupbearers who should have brought
the wine were slow in coming. Otherwise heroes could never have been
better served. If there had not been such false hearts among them, the
warriors would have been safe from every disgrace.

{965} Lord Siegfried said: "It amazes me, when they send us so much
from the kitchen, that the cupbearers do not bring us the wine. If
hunters are not looked after better than this, I will not be a hunting
companion. {966} I have earned better treatment than this."

The king spoke from his table, falsely: "We will gladly make good
whatever is lacking. It is Hagen's fault; he wants to let us die of
thirst."

{967} Hagen of Tronje said: "My dear lord, I thought today's hunt was
to be in the Spessart forest, and I sent the wine there. If we go
thirsty today, I will take good care it never happens again."

{968} Lord Siegfried said: "Bad luck to you for it. Seven pack-loads
of mead and clear wine should have been brought here for me, and if
that could not be done, we should have been seated nearer the Rhine."

{969} Hagen of Tronje said: "You noble, bold knights, I know a cold
spring near here. Do not be angry; let us go there." That advice would
bring many a warrior great sorrow. {970} Thirst was tormenting
Siegfried the warrior, and he ordered the table cleared away all the
sooner; he wanted to go past the hills to the spring. The advice had
been given by the warriors with evil in it. {971} The animals
Siegfried's hand had killed were ordered loaded on wagons and sent
home, and everyone who saw them gave him great honor. Hagen broke his
loyalty to Siegfried, and broke it badly.

{972} When they were about to set off for the broad linden tree, Hagen
of Tronje said: "I have often been told that no one can keep up with
Kriemhild's husband when he wants to run. If only he would show us!"

{973} Bold Siegfried of the Netherlands said: "You can easily test
that, if you will race me to the spring. When it is done, the prize
goes to whoever is seen to win."

{974} "Then let us try it," said Hagen the warrior.

Swift Siegfried said: "Then I will lie down in the grass at your
feet." When Gunther heard that, how glad it made him! {975} The bold
warrior went on: "I will tell you more. I will carry all my gear with
me, my spear and my shield and all my hunting clothes." He quickly
strapped on his quiver and his sword.

{976} Then the other two pulled their clothes off, and the two of them
were seen standing in their white shirts. Like two wild panthers they
ran through the clover, but it was bold Siegfried who was seen at the
spring first. {977} He carried off the prize in everything, ahead of
many a man. He quickly unbuckled his sword and laid the quiver aside,
and he leaned his strong spear against a branch of the linden. The
splendid guest stood beside the running spring. {978} Siegfried's
courtesy was very great. He laid his shield down where the water
flowed, and however badly he thirsted, the hero did not drink until
the king had drunk. He got poor thanks for it.

{979} The spring was cool, clear and good. Gunther bent down to the
water, and when he had drunk he straightened up again. Bold Siegfried
would gladly have done the same, {980} and now he paid for his good
manners. Hagen carried his bow and his sword away out of his reach,
then sprang back to where he had left the spear, and looked for the
mark on the bold man's clothing. {981} As Lord Siegfried bent over the
spring and drank, Hagen shot him through the cross, so that the blood
from his heart spurted out of the wound and splashed hard on Hagen's
clothes. No hero will ever commit so great a crime again. {982} He
left the spear sticking in him near the heart. Never in the world did
Hagen run so desperately from any man as he fled then.

When Lord Siegfried felt the great wound, {983} the lord sprang up
from the spring in a fury, with a long spear-shaft standing out from
his heart. The prince thought to find his bow or his sword, and if he
had, Hagen would have been paid what his service deserved. {984} When
the sorely wounded man found no sword, he had nothing left but the rim
of his shield. He snatched it up from the spring and ran at Hagen, and
King Gunther's man could not get away from him. {985} Wounded to death
as he was, he struck so powerfully that a shower of precious stones
flew from the shield and the shield itself burst to pieces. The
splendid guest would gladly have had his revenge. {986} Hagen was
knocked to the ground by his hand, and the flat by the river rang
loudly with the force of the blow. If he had had the sword in his
hand, it would have been Hagen's death, so terribly did the wounded
man rage. He had good reason to.

{987} His color had drained away; he could not stand. All the strength
of his body was leaving him, for he carried death's mark in his pale
face. Later many beautiful women wept for him. {988} Then Kriemhild's
husband fell among the flowers. The blood was seen running hard from
his wound. In his great extremity he began to curse those who had
plotted the treacherous death against him.

{989} The mortally wounded man said: "You wretched cowards. What good
are my services, now that you have killed me? I was always loyal to
you, and this is what it has cost me. You have done a wicked thing to
your own kinsmen. {990} Everyone born of your blood from this day on
is disgraced by it. You have avenged your anger far too hard on my
body. In shame you shall be set apart from all good warriors."

{991} The knights all ran to where he lay slain. For many of them it
was a joyless day, and those who had any loyalty in them mourned him.
The bold and gallant knight had well deserved it. {992} The king of
Burgundy lamented his death. The dying man said: "There is no need for
the man who did the harm to weep over it. He earns great blame for it.
It would have been better left undone."

{993} Grim Hagen said: "I do not know what he is lamenting. All our
fear and all our sorrow are over now. We will find very few who dare
to stand against us. Good for me, that I have put an end to his rule."

{994} "You may easily boast," said Siegfried. "If I had recognized
your murderous nature, I would have kept my life safe from you.
Nothing grieves me so much as Lady Kriemhild, my wife. {995} Now God
have mercy that I ever had a son, who will be reproached in time to
come because his own kinsmen murdered a man. If I could," said
Siegfried, "I would have every right to lament that."

{996} Then the mortally wounded man said pitifully: "Noble king, if
you ever mean to keep loyalty to anyone in this world, let my dear
beloved be commended to your grace. {997} Let it count for her that
she is your sister. By the virtue of all princes, stand by her
loyally. My father and my men will have a long wait for me. Never was
a woman so wronged in a beloved husband."

{998} The flowers all around were wet with blood. Then he struggled
with death, but not for long, for death's weapon always cuts too deep.
Then the bold and gallant warrior could speak no more.

{999} When the lords saw that the hero was dead, they laid him on a
shield of red gold and took counsel how to arrange it so that no one
would know Hagen had done it. {1000} Many of them said: "This is an
evil thing that has happened to us. You must all hide it and all tell
the same story: that Kriemhild's husband rode out hunting alone, and
robbers killed him as he went through the pine woods."

{1001} Hagen of Tronje said: "I will bring him home myself. I do not
care in the least if she finds out, the woman who has so troubled
Brunhild's heart. It matters very little to me how much she weeps."
