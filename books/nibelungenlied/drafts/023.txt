Chapter 24: How the Envoys Came to the Rhine

{1422} When Etzel sent his envoys to the Rhine, the news flew from land
to land. By messengers riding hard he asked, and commanded too, that
people come to his festival. Many a man found his death there.

{1423} The envoys rode out of the land of the Huns toward Burgundy. They
had been sent for three noble kings and for their men, who were to come
to Etzel; and so they made haste.

{1424} They came riding to Pöchlarn, where people were glad to serve
them; nothing was left undone. Rüdiger and Gotelind sent their service
with them to the Rhine, and so did the dear child the two of them
shared. {1425} Nor did they let the envoys leave without gifts, so that
Etzel's men would travel the better for it. To Ute and her children
Rüdiger sent word that there was no margrave anywhere who wished them
better than he did. {1426} To Brunhild too they sent their service and
good wishes, unfailing loyalty and a willing heart. When the envoys had
heard these messages and were ready to ride, the margravine asked God in
heaven to keep them safe.

{1427} Before the envoys had come all the way through Bavaria, quick
Werbel sought out the good bishop. What word he sent to his kinsmen on
the Rhine I do not know; only that he gave the envoys his red gold
{1428} as a token of friendship, and let them ride on. Then Bishop
Pilgrim said: "If I could see them here, my sister's sons, it would do
my heart good, for I can very seldom get to them on the Rhine."

{1429} Which roads they took through the lands to the Rhine I cannot
tell you. No one robbed them of their silver or their clothes: people
feared their master's anger. Truly, that noble, high-born king was a
powerful man.

{1430} Within twelve days Werbel and Swemmel came to the Rhine, to Worms
and its country. The news was told to the kings and their men that
strange messengers had arrived, and Gunther began asking about them.
{1431} The lord of the Rhine said: "Who can tell us where these
strangers riding into our land have come from?" No one knew, until Hagen
of Tronje saw them. Then he said to Gunther: {1432} "There is fresh news
for us, I promise you that. I have just seen Etzel's fiddlers here. Your
sister has sent them to the Rhine, and for their master's sake we must
make them very welcome."

{1433} They were already riding up in front of the great hall. No
prince's minstrels ever traveled in finer style. The king's household
received them at once; they were given good lodgings and their baggage
was taken into keeping. {1434} Their traveling clothes were rich and so
well made that they could have gone before the king in them with honor.
But they did not want to wear them any longer at court, and the envoys
asked whether anyone there might care to have them. {1435} Sure enough,
people were found who were glad to take the clothes, and the clothes
were sent to them. Then the guests put on far better garments, the kind
it is fitting for a king's envoys to wear in state.

{1436} Then, with leave, Etzel's men went to where the king was sitting,
and people were glad to see it. Hagen sprang up courteously to meet the
envoys and greeted them warmly, and the squires thanked him for it.
{1437} Wanting to hear what news they brought, he began asking how Etzel
was faring, and his men. The fiddler said: "The land never stood better,
nor the people so happy. You may be sure of that."

{1438} They went in to the host. The great hall was full. There the
guests were received as is right and proper when such friendly greetings
are sent into another king's land. Werbel found a great many warriors
there at Gunther's side. {1439} The king greeted them courteously:
"Welcome, both of you, you fiddlers of the Huns, and your companions
with you. Has mighty Etzel sent you here to the land of the
Burgundians?"

{1440} They bowed to the king. Then Werbel said: "My dear lord sends you
his devoted service into this land, and so does Kriemhild, your sister.
They have sent us to you warriors in good faith."

{1441} The mighty king said: "I am glad of this news. How is Etzel? And
Kriemhild, my sister, in the land of the Huns?"

The fiddler said: "I will tell you the news. {1442} No people ever fared
better than the two of them are faring now, you may be sure of that, and
all their household with them, their kinsmen and their men. They were
delighted with our journey when we left them."

{1443} The king said: "My thanks for his service, which he has sent me,
and for my sister's, since it stands as you say, that the king and his
men live in happiness. For I asked the news with a worried heart."

{1444} The two young kings had now arrived too; they had only just heard
the news. For love of his sister, young Giselher was glad to see the
envoys, and he spoke to them warmly: {1445} "Envoys, you would be very
welcome to us if you cared to ride to the Rhine more often. You would
find friends here you would be glad to see. Very little harm would come
to you in this land."

{1446} "We trust you to do us every honor," said Swemmel. "I could not
make you understand, with all the wit I have, how lovingly Etzel sends
his greetings, and your noble sister too, whose affairs stand in high
honor. {1447} The king's wife reminds you of your love and your loyalty,
and that your heart and your whole self were always devoted to her. But
first and foremost we are sent to the king, to ask you to be so good as
to ride into Etzel's land. {1448} Mighty Etzel ordered us most strictly
to beg this of you, and he sends this message to you all: if you will
not let your sister see you, then he would at least like to know what he
has done to you, {1449} that you stay so distant from him and his lands.
Even if the queen had never been known to you, he would still like to
earn the favor of your coming to see him. If that should happen, it
would make him very glad."

{1450} King Gunther said: "In seven days' time I will tell you what I
have decided with my kinsmen. Meanwhile, go to your lodgings and take a
good rest."

{1451} Werbel spoke again: "Might it be possible for us to see my lady
first, the mighty Ute, before we settle in to rest?" Noble Giselher
answered very courteously: {1452} "No one shall stop you. If you want to
go to her, you will be doing exactly what my mother wishes. She will be
glad to see you, for the sake of my sister Lady Kriemhild. You will be
welcome to her."

{1453} Giselher brought them to where he found the lady. She was pleased
to see the envoys from the land of the Huns, and in her kindness she
greeted them warmly. Then the good and courtly envoys told her their
news. {1454} "My lady sends you her service and her loyalty, truly,"
said Swemmel. "If it could be that she saw you often, believe me, there
would be nothing in the world that could make her happier."

{1455} The queen said: "That cannot be. However much I would like to see
my dear daughter often, the noble king's wife lives too far from me, to
my sorrow. May she and Etzel be blessed forever. {1456} Let me know,
before you leave here, when you want to go back. I have not been so glad
to see messengers in a long time as I am to see you." The squires
promised her that they would.

{1457} The men from the land of the Huns rode to their lodgings.
Meanwhile the mighty king had sent for his kinsmen. Noble Gunther asked
his men how they liked the proposal, and many began to say {1458} that
he could well ride to Etzel's land. The best men he had there advised
him so — all but Hagen alone, and he was grimly against it. He said to
the king in private: "You are declaring war on yourself. {1459} You know
very well what we have done. We will always have reason to fear
Kriemhild, for I killed her husband with my own hand. How could we dare
ride into Etzel's land?"

{1460} The mighty king said: "My sister gave up her anger. She kissed us
lovingly and forgave us for everything we ever did to her, before she
rode away from here — unless, Hagen, the quarrel is with you alone."

{1461} "Whatever the envoys from the Huns tell you, do not let them fool
you," said Hagen. "If you want to see Kriemhild, you could well lose
your honor there, and your lives too. King Etzel's wife has a very long
memory for a wrong."

{1462} Then Prince Gernot said to the council: "Just because you have
good reason to fear death in the Hunnish kingdoms, should we give up
seeing our sister on that account? That would be very wrong of us."

{1463} Prince Giselher said to the warrior: "Since you know you are
guilty, friend Hagen, you should stay here and look after yourself, and
let those who dare come with us to my sister."

{1464} At that the warrior of Tronje grew angry. "I do not want you
taking anyone on this road who would dare to ride to that court more
boldly than I. Since you will not give it up, I will show you that
plainly enough."

{1465} Then Rumold the warrior, the master of the kitchen, spoke: "You
could have strangers and friends looked after here just as you please,
for you have plenty of everything. As far as I know, you are not Hagen's
hostage yet. {1466} If you will not take Hagen's advice, then take
Rumold's, for I am your loyal and devoted servant: stay here, for my
sake, and leave King Etzel over there with Kriemhild.

{1467} "How could you ever be more comfortable anywhere in the world?
Here you are perfectly safe from your enemies. Dress yourselves in fine
clothes, drink the best wine, and make love to lovely women. {1468} And
besides, you are served the finest food any king in the world ever had.
And even if that were not so, you ought to stay for your beautiful
wife's sake, before you go risking your life like a child. {1469} So I
advise you to stay. Your lands are rich; a man can redeem his pledges
better here at home than over there among the Huns, where who knows how
things stand. Stay at home, my lords. That is Rumold's advice."

{1470} "We will not stay," said Gernot, "since my sister and mighty
Etzel have invited us so lovingly. Why should we refuse? Anyone who does
not want to go can stay at home."

{1471} Hagen answered: "Do not take my words amiss, whatever comes of
it. I advise you on my loyalty: if you want to stay alive, ride to the
Huns well armed. {1472} Since you will not give it up, send for your
men, the best you can find or can get anywhere, and from among them all
I will choose a thousand good knights. Then Kriemhild's malice can do
you no harm."

{1473} "I will gladly follow that," the king said at once, and he sent
messengers riding far and wide through his lands. They brought in three
thousand heroes or more. Those men never dreamed they were riding to
earn such terrible sorrow.

{1474} They rode cheerfully into Gunther's land. Everyone who was to
ride out of Burgundy was given horses and clothes, and the king won the
goodwill of a great many men. {1475} Then Hagen of Tronje had his
brother Dankwart bring eighty of their own warriors to the Rhine. They
came like knights; those quick men brought armor and clothing with them
into Gunther's land. {1476} Then bold Volker came, a noble minstrel,
with thirty of his men for the journey to court. They wore clothes a
king could have worn. He sent word to Gunther that he meant to go to the
Huns.

{1477} I will tell you who this Volker was. He was a noble lord, and
many good warriors in Burgundy were his vassals. Because he could play
the fiddle, he was called the minstrel. {1478} Hagen chose a thousand
men he knew well. He had seen for himself what their hands had done in
hard fighting, and everything else they had ever done. No one could say
anything of them but that they were brave.

{1479} Kriemhild's envoys were growing very weary of the delay, for they
were in great fear of their master. Every day they asked for leave to
go, and Hagen would not grant it. That was done on purpose. {1480} He
said to his lord: "We must take care not to let them ride away until we
ourselves are ready to set out for Etzel's land seven days after them.
If anyone means us harm, we will know it that much sooner. {1481} And
then Lady Kriemhild will have no time to arrange for anyone to harm us
on her advice. But if that is her plan, it may turn out badly for her,
for we are taking so many chosen men with us from here."

{1482} Shields and saddles and all the clothes they wanted to take into
Etzel's land were now fully ready for many a bold man. Then Kriemhild's
envoys were told to come before Gunther. {1483} When the envoys came in,
Gernot said: "The king will do as Etzel has asked us. We will gladly
come to his festival and see our sister. You need have no doubt of
that."

{1484} King Gunther said: "Can you tell us when the festival is to be,
and on what days we should arrive?"

Swemmel said: "At next midsummer, that is the truth."

{1485} The king gave them leave — something that had not happened before
now — to go before Lady Brunhild with his blessing, if they wanted to
see her. Volker stepped in to prevent it, and that was done out of
kindness to her. {1486} "My lady Brunhild is not in such good spirits
just now that you could see her," the good knight said. "Wait until
tomorrow, and then they will let you see her." But when they thought
they would see her, it could not be managed.

{1487} Then the mighty prince, who was fond of the envoys, had his gold
carried out on the broad shields, out of his own generosity; he had
plenty of it. His kinsmen gave them rich gifts as well. {1488} Giselher
and Gernot, Gere and Ortwin showed plainly that they too were
open-handed. They offered the envoys gifts so rich that, for fear of
their master, they did not dare accept them.

{1489} Then the envoy Werbel said to the king: "Sir King, let your gifts
stay here in your own land. We cannot carry them away. My lord forbade
us to accept any gifts at all; and besides, we have very little need of
them."

{1490} The lord of the Rhine was much put out that they wanted to refuse
the goods of so mighty a king. In the end they had to accept his gold
and his clothes, and they took them home to Etzel's land.

{1491} They wanted to see Ute before they left, so quick Giselher
brought the minstrels before his mother Ute. The lady sent this message:
whatever honors her daughter enjoyed, that was a joy to her. {1492} Then
the queen had her embroidered braid and her gold given to these same
minstrels for Kriemhild's sake, because she loved her, and for King
Etzel's. They could gladly accept it: it was given in loyalty.

{1493} Now the envoys had taken their leave of the women and the men
there, and they rode away happily into Swabia. Gernot had his heroes
escort them that far, so that no one would do them harm. {1494} When the
men who were charged with their care had parted from them, Etzel's power
kept the peace for them on every road. No one took horse or clothing
from them, and they hurried on at full speed into Etzel's land.

{1495} Wherever they knew of any kinsmen of the king, they let them know
that the Burgundians would very shortly be coming from the Rhine into
the land of the Huns. Bishop Pilgrim was told the news too. {1496} As
they rode down the road past Pöchlarn, the news was told to Rüdiger —
that was not left undone — and to Gotelind, the margrave's wife. That
she was going to see them made her very happy.

{1497} The minstrels were seen hurrying on with their news. They found
Etzel in the city of Esztergom. They told the king of greeting after
greeting that had been sent to him, and he flushed red with joy.
{1498} When the queen learned for certain that her brothers were coming
into the land, she was glad at heart. She rewarded the minstrels with
very great gifts; that was done for her honor.

{1499} She said: "Now tell me, both of you, Werbel and Swemmel, which of
my kinsmen mean to be at the festival — the best of those we invited
here into this land? And tell me, what did Hagen say when he heard the
news?"

{1500} He said: "He came to the council early one morning, and he had
little good to say about it. When the others agreed to the journey here
to the land of the Huns, to grim Hagen it was the same as a sentence of
death. {1501} Your brothers are coming, all three kings, in high
spirits. Who else is coming with them I cannot tell you for certain.
Volker, the bold minstrel, promised to ride with them."

{1502} "I could easily do without ever seeing Volker," said the king's
wife. "But I am fond of Hagen. He is a very fine hero. That we are going
to see him here lifts my heart."

{1503} Then the queen went to where she saw the king. How lovingly
Kriemhild spoke to him! "How do you like this news, my dear lord? What
my heart has always desired is going to come about now."

{1504} "Your wish is my joy," the king said. "I was never so glad to see
my own kinsmen coming into my land as I am for your family's sake. Now
that they are coming, my worries are gone."

{1505} The king's officers had the great hall and the hall of state
everywhere fitted out with seats for the dear guests who were on their
way to them. Later those guests would bring the king much bitter
weeping.
