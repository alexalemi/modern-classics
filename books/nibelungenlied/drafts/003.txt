Chapter 4: How Siegfried Fought the Saxons

{139} Now strange news came to Gunther's land, carried by messengers
sent from far away by warriors no one there knew, men who hated them.
When the Burgundians heard the message, truly, it grieved them.
{140} I will name those warriors for you. One was Liudeger of Saxony, a
mighty and proud prince, and the other was Liudegast, the king of
Denmark, and for their campaign they had gathered many a splendid
foreign fighter.

{141} So their messengers had come into Gunther's land, sent there by
his enemies. People asked the strangers what news they brought, and
they were told to go quickly to court and stand before the king.
{142} He greeted them courteously. "Welcome," he said. "Who sent you
here I have not yet heard. You should let us hear it," said the good
king. But they were badly afraid of Gunther's grim temper.

{143} "If you will permit us, King, to tell you the news we bring, we
will not hold it back. We name to you the lords who sent us here:
Liudegast and Liudeger. They mean to come looking for you in this land.
{144} You have earned their anger. Indeed, we heard it plainly that
both lords bear you great hatred. They mean to march on Worms on the
Rhine, and many warriors are helping them; you may take my word for
that. {145} Within twelve weeks the campaign must begin. If you have any
good friends who will help you defend your castles and your land, show
it quickly, for many a helmet and shield-rim will be hacked to pieces
here by their hands. {146} Or if you would rather negotiate with them,
send word to them, and then the many companies of your strong enemies
will not ride so close to you, bringing you heartfelt sorrow, the kind
that costs many good and gallant knights their lives."

{147} "Now wait a while," said the good king, "until I have thought it
over better; then I will let you know my mind. If I have any loyal men,
I ought not to keep this from them. Such hard news I must lay before my
friends."

{148} Mighty Gunther was grieved enough. He carried the message
secretly in his heart. He had Hagen fetched, and others of his men, and
he sent in great haste to bring Gernot to court as well. {149} Then
came the best men to be found there, and he said: "They mean to come
looking for us here in our own land with strong armies. Take it to
heart." Gernot, a bold and gallant knight, answered him:

{150} "We will ward that off with swords," said Gernot. "Only those
whose time has come will die there; let them lie dead. I cannot forget
my honor over this. Our enemies shall be welcome here."

{151} Then Hagen of Tronje said: "That does not seem good to me.
Liudegast and Liudeger are arrogant men, and we cannot call up our
forces in so few days." Then the bold warrior added: "Why don't you
tell Siegfried?"

{152} The messengers were given lodging in the town. However hostile
they were, mighty Gunther ordered them well looked after, and that was
well done, until he found out from his friends who would stand by him.
{153} In his worry the king was still deeply troubled. A gallant
knight saw him brooding, a man who could not know what had happened to
him, and he asked King Gunther to tell him what the matter was.

{154} "It amazes me," said Siegfried, "how completely you have changed
the cheerful ways you have long kept with us." Gunther, that handsome
warrior, answered him: {155} "I cannot tell everyone the trouble I must
carry secretly in my heart. A man should lament his heart's distress
only to steadfast friends." Siegfried's color went pale, then red.
{156} He said to the king: "I have never refused you anything. I will
help you turn aside every sorrow you have. If you are looking for
friends, I will be one of them, and I trust I can see it through with
honor to the end of my days."

{157} "God reward you, Lord Siegfried. I like what you say. And even if
your courage never comes to my aid, I am glad to hear that you are so
devoted to me, and if I live any time at all, you will be well repaid
for it. {158} I will let you hear why I stand here so sad. From my
enemies' messengers I have learned that they mean to come looking for
me here with armies. No warriors ever did that to us in these lands
before."

{159} "Make light of it," said Siegfried, "and calm your mind. Do what
I ask you: let me win you honor and advantage, and ask your warriors to
come to your aid as well. {160} Even if your strong enemies could call
on thirty thousand warriors to help them, I would take them on if I
had no more than a thousand. Leave that to me." Then King Gunther said:
"For that I will serve you as long as I live."

{161} "Then have a thousand of your men brought to me, since I have
none of my own here except twelve warriors, and I will defend your
land. Siegfried's hand will serve you loyally forever. {162} Hagen
shall help us in this, and Ortwin too, Dankwart and Sindold, your dear
warriors. And Volker, that bold man, shall ride with us and carry the
banner; there is no one I would rather give it to. {163} And let the
messengers ride home to their lords' land. Let them know that they will
be seeing us soon, so that our castles can be left in peace." Then the
king sent for his kinsmen and his vassals.

{164} Liudeger's messengers came to court, and they were very glad to
be going home. Gunther the good king offered them rich gifts and gave
them safe-conduct, and their spirits rose at that. {165} "Now tell my
strong enemies," said Gunther, "that they may as well stay home with
their campaign. But if they want to come looking for me here in my
lands, then unless my friends fail me, they will learn what hardship
is." {166} Rich gifts were brought out for the messengers; Gunther had
more than enough to give, and Liudeger's men did not dare refuse them.
When they took their leave, they went away in high spirits.

{167} When the messengers reached Denmark and King Liudegast heard how
they had come away from the Rhine, as it was told to him, their bold
defiance truly grieved him. {168} They said the Burgundians had a great
many brave men, and that they had seen among them a warrior called
Siegfried, a hero from the Netherlands. Liudegast was sorry when he
understood that news properly. {169} When the Danes heard this they
hurried all the more to gather their friends, until Lord Liudegast had
twenty thousand of his brave warriors for the campaign. {170} King
Liudeger of Saxony sent out his summons too, until between them they
had forty thousand men and more, with whom they meant to ride into
Burgundy.

Meanwhile, back at home, King Gunther had gathered {171} his kinsmen,
his brothers' vassals, and Hagen's warriors as well, the men they
meant to lead to war. The heroes had need of them. Later, warriors
would have to meet their deaths for it.

{172} They threw themselves into getting ready, since they wanted to be
gone. Volker the bold was to carry the banner as they rode out from
Worms across the Rhine, and Hagen of Tronje was to command the troop.
{173} Sindold rode with them, and Hunold, men who knew well how to earn
Gunther's gold, and Dankwart, Hagen's brother, and Ortwin too, men who
could serve with honor on the campaign.

{174} "Sir King, stay here at home," said Siegfried, "since your
warriors are willing to follow me. Stay with the ladies and keep your
spirits high. I trust I can guard your honor and your goods both.
{175} As for the men who wanted to come looking for you at Worms on the
Rhine, I will see to it that they wish they had stayed home. We will
ride so deep into their land that their arrogance turns to worry."

{176} From the Rhine they rode through Hesse with their heroes toward
Saxony, where the fighting would later be. With plunder and burning
they laid the land waste, and both princes learned of it, to their
grief. {177} When they came to the border, the squires moved on ahead,
and Siegfried the strong began asking: "Who is to guard the camp for us
here?" Truly, no ride ever did the Saxons more harm. {178} They said:
"Let bold Dankwart guard the young ones on the road; he is a swift
warrior. That way we will lose fewer to Liudeger's men. Let him and
Ortwin have the rearguard."

{179} "Then I will ride out myself," said Siegfried the warrior, "and
keep the watch against the enemy until I find out exactly where their
warriors are." Fair Sieglinde's son was quickly armed. {180} When he
set out he left the troops in the charge of Hagen and of bold Gernot,
and then he rode alone into the land of the Saxons. Many a helmet-strap
he cut through that day.

{181} He saw the great army that lay camped on the plain, far more than
the force he had with him: a good forty thousand, or more. Siegfried,
in high spirits, looked on it gladly. {182} Now a warrior on the other
side had also gone up on watch against the enemy, fully armed. Lord
Siegfried saw him, and the bold man saw Siegfried, and each began to
watch the other with hatred. {183} I will tell you who it was that kept
the watch: a bright shield of gold lay before his hand. It was King
Liudegast, guarding his own troops. The noble stranger came galloping
at him splendidly.

{184} Now Lord Liudegast too had marked him out as an enemy. They both
drove the spurs into their horses' flanks and lowered their spears
against the shields with all their strength, and the mighty king was
suddenly in great trouble. {185} After the thrust, the horses carried
the two royal princes past each other as if a wind had blown them. With
their bridles they wheeled round, like true knights, and the two grim
men tried it with swords. {186} Lord Siegfried struck so hard that the
whole field rang. Out of the helmet flew fire-red sparks, as from great
torches, struck by the hero's hand. Each had found his match in the
other. {187} Lord Liudegast struck many a grim blow too, and each man's
full strength landed on the shields. Thirty of his men were standing
watch nearby, but before they could reach him, Siegfried had won the
victory {188} with three deep wounds he dealt the king through his
bright coat of mail, good as it was. The sword's edges brought blood
out of the wounds, and King Liudegast was left in a sorry state.

{189} He begged for his life and offered Siegfried his lands, and told
him his name was Liudegast. Then his warriors came up; they had seen
plainly what had happened between the two of them on the watch.
{190} Siegfried wanted to lead him away, but thirty of Liudegast's men
attacked him. The hero's hand defended his royal hostage with huge
blows, and after that the handsome warrior did worse harm still.
{191} Fighting for his life, he killed all thirty of them. One he left
alive, and that man rode off fast and carried the news of what had
happened here; the truth of it could be read on his reddened helmet.
{192} The Danes were bitterly grieved when they were told their lord
was taken. It was told to his brother, and he began to rage with
uncontrollable anger, for a wrong had been done him.

{193} Liudegast the warrior was led away by Siegfried's might to
Gunther's men, and Siegfried handed him over to Hagen. When they were
told it was the king himself, their grief was not overwhelming.
{194} The Burgundians were ordered to tie on their banners. "Up!" said
Siegfried. "More will be done here before the day is over, if I keep my
life. Many a lovely woman in Saxony will grieve for it.
{195} Heroes from the Rhine, listen to me: I can lead you straight to
Liudeger's army. There you will see helmets hewn by good heroes' hands.
Before we turn for home, they will know what sorrow is."

{196} Gernot and his men ran for their horses. The strong minstrel,
Lord Volker, snatched up the banner and rode at the head of the troop,
and the whole company was splendidly ready for battle. {197} Still,
they had no more than a thousand men, and the twelve warriors besides.
The dust rose from the roads as they rode across the land, and many a
splendid shield could be seen shining among them.

{198} The Saxons had come too, with their companies and with
well-sharpened swords, as I have since heard. Those swords cut hard in
the heroes' hands, for they meant to defend their castles and their
land against the strangers. {199} The lords' commander led the troops
forward. Siegfried had come up too, with the men he had brought with
him from the Netherlands. In the storm of that day's fighting many a
hand turned bloody. {200} Sindold and Hunold and Gernot killed many a
hero in the fight before the enemy learned how bold they really were.
Many a noble woman would weep for it later.

{201} Volker and Hagen and Ortwin dimmed the shine of many a helmet with
flowing blood, storm-bold men that they were, and Dankwart did great
wonders. {202} The Danes gave a good account of themselves; the crash
of shields rang out from the charges and from the sharp swords that
were swung there in such numbers, and the battle-hardened Saxons did
damage enough. {203} But when the Burgundians pressed into the fight,
they hacked open many a wide wound, and the blood could be seen running
down over the saddles. That is how those bold, good knights went after
honor.

{204} The sharp weapons were heard ringing loud in the heroes' hands as
the men from the Netherlands pressed after their lord into the thick of
the enemy ranks. They came in bravely alongside Siegfried. {205} Not
one of the men from the Rhine could be seen following him. You could
see the bloody stream running out through the bright helmets, drawn by
Siegfried's hand, until he found Liudeger in front of his comrades.
{206} Three times now he had cut his way clean through the army and
back. Now Hagen had come up, and he helped Siegfried do all that his
heart desired in that battle. Many a good knight had to die before them
that day.

{207} When strong Liudeger found Siegfried, and saw how high he carried
good Balmung in his hand and how many he was killing with it, the lord
grew angry and grim enough. {208} Then there was a great surge and a
great clanging of swords as their men pressed in against each other,
and the two warriors tested themselves all the harder. The ranks began
to give way, and bitter hatred rose there. {209} The ruler of the
Saxons had already been told that his brother was captured, and it
grieved him hard. He knew well enough that Sieglinde's son had done it;
people were blaming Gernot, but he learned the truth of it later.

{210} Liudeger's blows were so strong that Siegfried's horse staggered
under the saddle. When the horse recovered itself, bold Siegfried took
on a terrible manner in that fight. {211} Hagen helped him well, and
Gernot, Dankwart and Volker, and many lay dead because of it. Sindold
and Hunold and Ortwin the warrior knew how to cut many a man down to
his death in that battle. {212} The noble princes stayed side by side
in the storm of it. Many a spear was seen flying over the helmets and
through the bright shields, thrown by heroes' hands, and many a
splendid shield was seen stained the color of blood. {213} In that hard
fight many a man got down from his horse. Siegfried the bold and
Liudeger ran at each other on foot; shafts were seen flying, and many
a sharp spear. {214} Then the shield-fittings flew off under Siegfried's
hand. The hero from the Netherlands meant to win the victory over the
bold Saxons, of whom so many wonders were seen that day. Oh, how many
bright mail-rings bold Dankwart broke!

{215} Then Lord Liudeger made out a crown painted on the shield in
Siegfried's hand. He knew well now that this was the mighty man, and
the hero began shouting to his friends: {216} "Give up the fight, all
my men! I have seen Siegmund's son here. I know him: it is strong
Siegfried. The evil devil has sent him here to the Saxons."

{217} He had the banners lowered in the middle of the fighting. He
asked for peace, and later it was granted him, but he had to go to
Gunther's land as a hostage. Bold Siegfried's hand had forced that on
him. {218} By common agreement they broke off the fight. They laid
down out of their hands all the battered helmets and broad shields
that could be found, and every one of them bore the color of blood,
from the Burgundians' hands. {219} They took captive whoever they
chose, since they had the power to. Gernot and Hagen, those bold
warriors, had the wounded laid on biers, and they led five hundred
fighting men away captive to the Rhine.

{220} The beaten warriors rode home to Denmark. Nor had the Saxons
fought so well that anyone would give them praise, and that grieved
those heroes. And the dead were sorely mourned by their friends.
{221} The Burgundians had the weapons loaded onto pack-horses again
for the Rhine. Siegfried the warrior had fought splendidly with his
heroes; he had done well, and every one of Gunther's men had to admit
it.

{222} Lord Gernot sent messengers to Worms. He let his kinsmen at home
know how he and his men had fared, and that these brave warriors had
won great honor. {223} The pages ran, and the story spread. Those who
had been grieving before now rejoiced for joy at the good news that
had come to them, and the noble ladies were heard asking question after
question {224} about how the mighty king's men had fared. One of the
messengers was told to go to Kriemhild. That was done very secretly,
for she did not dare do it openly, since among those men was her own
dear beloved.

{225} When she saw the messenger coming to her chamber, beautiful
Kriemhild spoke to him kindly: "Now tell me good news, and I will give
you my gold. If you tell it without lying, I will always be your
friend. {226} How did my brother Gernot come out of the fighting, and
the rest of my kinsmen? Are many of our men dead? And who did best
there? You must tell me." The messenger answered at once: "We had no
cowards anywhere. {227} In earnest fighting no one rode so well, most
noble princess, since I must tell it to you, as the noble guest from
the Netherlands. Bold Siegfried's hand worked great wonders there.

{228} "Whatever all the warriors did in the fight, Dankwart and Hagen
and the rest of the king's men, however they fought for honor, it is so
much wind beside Siegfried alone, King Siegmund's son. {229} They cut
down many a hero in the storm of battle, but no one could ever tell you
the wonders Siegfried worked whenever he rode into the fight. Through
their kinsmen he brought the women terrible grief. {230} Many a woman's
beloved had to stay behind there. His blows were heard landing so loud
on the helmets that they brought the blood streaming from the wounds.
In every virtue he is a bold and good knight.

{231} "Whatever Ortwin of Metz did there, whoever he could reach with
his sword was left wounded, or more often dead. But it was your brother
who did the very greatest harm {232} that could ever be done in
battle. One must tell the truth about those chosen men: the proud
Burgundians have carried themselves so well that they can keep their
honor clear of every shame. {233} Many a saddle was seen emptied by
their hands, and the field rang loud with their bright swords. The
warriors from the Rhine rode so hard that their enemies would have
done better to avoid them.

{234} "The bold men of Tronje did great harm too, when the armies met
with their full strength. Bold Hagen's hand left many a man dead; there
would be plenty to tell of it here in Burgundy. {235} Sindold and
Hunold, Gernot's men, and bold Rumold have done so much that Liudeger
may regret forever that he ever declared war on your kinsmen at the
Rhine. {236} But the very finest fighting that was done anywhere there,
first or last, that anyone saw, Siegfried's hand did gladly. He is
bringing noble hostages into Gunther's land. {237} That handsome man
overpowered them by his courage, and King Liudegast must bear the loss
of it, and so must his brother, Liudeger of Saxony. Now hear my news,
noble princess: {238} Siegfried's hand has captured them both. Never
have so many hostages been brought into this land as are now coming to
the Rhine through his doing. No news could ever be dearer to you.
{239} They are bringing five hundred or more of the unhurt, and of the
mortally wounded, my lady, you should know, some eighty red biers into
our land, most of them hacked down by strong Siegfried's hand.
{240} The men who in their arrogance sent their defiance to the Rhine
must now be Gunther's prisoners, and they are being brought into this
land with rejoicing."

Her bright color bloomed when she heard that news properly. {241} Her
lovely face turned rose-red, now that the handsome warrior, young
Siegfried, had come out of that great peril in joy. She rejoiced for
her kinsmen too, and rightly so. {242} Then the lovely girl said: "You
have told me well. For it you shall have rich clothes as your reward,
and ten marks of gold, which I will have brought to you." That is why
men are so glad to carry such news to rich ladies.

{243} He was given his reward, the gold and the clothes. Then many a
beautiful girl went to the windows and watched the roads, and there
they saw them riding, many high-spirited men, into Burgundy.
{244} The unhurt came, and the wounded came too, and they could hear
the greetings of their friends without any shame. The host rode out
joyfully to meet his guests; his great sorrow had ended in joy.
{245} He received his own men well, and the strangers just the same,
for nothing else would have been fitting for a mighty king than to
thank those who had come back to him kindly, because they had won the
victory with honor in the storm of battle.

{246} Gunther asked for news of his kinsmen, who had been killed on
the campaign. He had lost no one but sixty men, and they had to be
mourned, as heroes always are. {247} The unhurt
brought many a hacked shield and many a battered helmet into Gunther's
land. The troops dismounted in front of the king's hall, and a joyful
clamor of welcome was heard. {248} Then the warriors were given lodging
in the town. The king asked that his guests be well looked after; he
had the wounded tended and made comfortable. His goodness showed itself
plainly in how he treated his enemies.

{249} He said to Liudegast: "Now you are welcome here. I have suffered
great damage through your fault; that will be repaid me now, if I have
any luck. God reward my friends; they have given me joy."

{250} "You may well thank them," said Liudeger. "No king ever won such
noble hostages before. For fair treatment we will give great wealth, so
that you deal mercifully with your enemies."

{251} "I will let you both go free," said Gunther, "on condition that
my enemies stay here with me. For that I want sureties, that they will
not leave my lands without my leave." Liudeger gave his hand on it.

{252} They were taken to rest and made comfortable. The wounded were
bedded down kindly, and the unhurt were served mead and good wine, and
the household could not have been merrier. {253} Their hacked shields
were carried off for safekeeping. Of bloody saddles there were plenty,
and these were ordered hidden away so that the women would not weep.
Many a good knight came home worn out from the war. {254} The king
looked after his guests wonderfully well; the land was full of
strangers and of his own people. He asked that the badly wounded be
cared for kindly. As for the enemies' arrogance, it had been laid very
low. {255} Those who knew the healing arts were offered rich pay,
silver unweighed and bright gold besides, to heal the heroes after the
ordeal of the battle, and on top of that the king offered his guests
great gifts.

{256} Those who had a mind to go home were asked to stay longer, as one
does with friends. Then the king took counsel on how he should reward
his men, for they had done his will with great honor. {257} Lord Gernot
said: "Let them ride home. But let them know that in six weeks they are
to come back for a festival. By then many a man who now lies badly
wounded will be healed."

{258} Siegfried of the Netherlands asked for leave too, but when King
Gunther learned what he wanted, he begged him lovingly to stay a while
longer. If it had not been for the king's beautiful sister, Siegfried
would never have agreed. {259} Besides, Siegfried was too rich to take any
pay, though he had well deserved it. The king was fond of him, and so
were the king's kinsmen; they had seen what his strength had done in
that battle. {260} For the sake of the beautiful girl, he thought he
would stay, in case he might get to see her. Later that happened: he
came to know the girl just as he had wished, and later he rode home
glad to Siegmund's land.

{261} The host had knightly sports held at all hours, and many a young
warrior joined in gladly. Meanwhile he had seats built on the river
sand outside Worms for the guests who were to come to Burgundy.
{262} Right at the time when they were due to arrive, beautiful
Kriemhild heard the news that the king meant to hold a festival for the
sake of his dear friends. Then the beautiful women set to work in
earnest {263} on the dresses and headbands they were to wear. Ute, the
great queen, heard the news of the proud warriors who were coming, and
many rich gowns were taken out of the chests. {264} For love of her
children she had clothes made ready, and with them many a lady and
many a girl were adorned, and many young warriors of Burgundy as well.
And for many of the strangers, too, she had splendid garments made.
