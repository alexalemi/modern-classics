Chapter 37: How Rüdiger Was Killed

{2135} The strangers had done well toward morning. Gotelind's husband
came to court, and when he saw the grievous losses on both sides, good
Rüdiger wept from his heart. {2136} "How wretched I am," said the
warrior, "that I was ever born, since no one can stop this great misery.
However much I want to make peace, the king will not do it, for he sees
the sufferings of his people growing greater and greater."

{2137} Good Rüdiger sent to Dietrich to ask whether they could still
turn the fate of the noble kings. The lord of Bern sent back his answer:
"Who could stop it? King Etzel will let no one part the fighting."

{2138} A Hun warrior saw Rüdiger standing there with weeping eyes — he
had shed many tears — and said to the queen: "Now look how he stands
there, the man who holds the greatest power here at Etzel's side,
{2139} the man whom people and lands all serve. How is it that so many
castles have been turned over to Rüdiger, when he holds so many of them
from the king? In all this fighting he has not struck one praiseworthy
blow. {2140} It seems to me he does not care how things go here, since
he has all he wants. People say he is braver than anyone could be. That
has shown itself badly in this trouble."

{2141} In sorrow the loyal man looked at the one he heard say this. He
thought: "You will pay for that. You say I am a coward; you have told
your tale too loudly at court." {2142} He clenched his fist and ran at
him and struck the Hun so hard that he lay dead at his feet in an
instant. And so King Etzel's distress was greater again.

{2143} "Away with you, you notorious coward," said Rüdiger. "I have
grief and pain enough already. Why do you reproach me for not fighting
here? Truly I have good cause to hate the guests, {2144} and I would
have done everything I could against them — except that I am the one who
brought these warriors here. I was their escort into my lord's land, and
so this wretched hand of mine shall not fight them."

{2145} Then King Etzel said to the margrave: "A fine help you have been
to us, noble Rüdiger! We have so many doomed men in this land already
that we had no need of more. You have done very wrong."

{2146} The noble knight answered: "He insulted me, and threw in my face
the honor and the goods I have taken in such plenty from your hands.
That turned out a little unlucky for the liar."

{2147} Then the queen came; she too had seen what the hero's anger had
done to the Hun. She lamented it beyond measure, and her eyes grew wet.
She said to Rüdiger: "How have we deserved this, {2148} that you add to
the sorrows of the king and me? Until now, noble Rüdiger, you have
always told us you would risk your honor and your life for us. I have
heard many warriors give you the highest praise. {2149} I remind you of
the favor you promised me, and of what you swore to me, chosen knight,
when you counseled me to take Etzel: that you would serve me until one
of us died. Never has a poor woman been in such need of it as I am now."

{2150} The margrave answered: "There is no denying it. I swore to you,
noble lady, that I would risk my honor and my life for your sake. That I
would lose my soul, I never swore. It was I who invited the highborn
princes to this feast."

{2151} She said: "Remember, Rüdiger, your great loyalty, your constancy
and your oaths: that you would always be the first to right my wrongs,
and avenge every grief of mine." The margrave said: "I have seldom
refused you anything."

{2152} Mighty Etzel began to plead as well, and the two of them fell at
the warrior's feet. The noble margrave stood there in anguish, and the
loyal warrior spoke in the bitterest misery: {2153} "Oh, God help me,
wretched man that I am, that I ever lived to see this! I must give up
all my honor, and the loyalty and the good breeding that God commanded
of me. Oh, God in heaven, that death does not come and turn this from
me! {2154} Whichever I leave undone and whichever I take up, I shall
have acted badly and wickedly; and if I leave them both, the whole world
will curse me. May he who gave me life show me what to do."

{2155} The king and his wife kept begging him, hard. Because of it,
warriors would soon lose their lives at Rüdiger's hands, and there the
hero died himself. You will hear now how wretchedly he went about it.
{2156} He knew he would win nothing but harm and monstrous grief. He
would gladly have refused the king, and the queen too; he feared
bitterly that if he killed a single one of them, the whole world would
hate him.

{2157} Then the brave man said to the king: "Sir King, take back
everything I hold from you — the land and the castles. Let none of it
remain to me. I will go on foot into exile."

{2158} King Etzel said: "Who would help me then? The land and the people
— I give it all to you outright, so that you avenge me on my enemies,
Rüdiger. You shall be a mighty king beside Etzel."

{2159} Rüdiger answered: "How can I set about it? I invited them home to
my own house; I offered them food and drink as a friend, and I gave them
my gifts. How can I plot their deaths? {2160} People may easily think I
am a coward. I have never refused the noble princes and their men any
service of mine; and now the friendship I made with them grieves me.
{2161} I gave my daughter to the warrior Giselher. She could not have
been better bestowed in all this world — for breeding and for honor, for
loyalty and for wealth. I never saw so young a king so nobly minded."

{2162} Kriemhild said again: "Most noble Rüdiger, take pity on the
suffering of us both, mine and the king's. Think well on this: no host
ever had such deadly guests."

{2163} The margrave answered the noble woman: "Today Rüdiger's life must
pay for whatever kindness you and my lord have ever shown me. For that I
must die; it can be put off no longer. {2164} I know well that this very
day my castles and my lands will fall back to you by the hand of one of
those men. I commend my wife and my children to your mercy, and the
strangers too, the exiles who are at Pöchlarn."

{2165} "Now God reward you, Rüdiger," said the king, and he and the
queen were both glad. "Your people shall be well cared for by us. And I
trust my own good fortune that you yourself will come through alive."

{2166} So he laid soul and body in the balance. Etzel's wife began to
weep. He said: "I must keep my word to you as I promised. Alas for my
friends, whom I attack so unwillingly!"

{2167} He was seen going from the king in deep sorrow. He found his
warriors standing close by, and said: "Arm yourselves, all my men. To my
grief, I must fight the bold Burgundians."

{2168} They had the squires run at once to where their weapons were
kept; helmet or shield-rim, the household brought it all out to them.
Soon the proud exiles heard sorrowful news. {2169} Rüdiger was armed
now, with five hundred men, and besides them he won twelve warriors to
his help, who wanted to earn fame in the peril of battle. They did not
know that death was so close to them.

{2170} Then Rüdiger was seen going under his helmet. Rüdiger's men
carried sharp swords, and in front of them their bright broad shields.
The fiddler saw it, and it grieved him deeply.

{2171} Young Giselher saw his father-in-law coming with his helmet laced
on. How could he understand what he meant by it, except that he meant
all good? The noble king's heart was glad. {2172} "What friends we have
won on this journey," said the warrior Giselher. "My wife's family will
do us a great deal of good here. On my loyalty, I am glad the match was
ever made."

{2173} "I do not know where you find your comfort," said the minstrel.
"When did you ever see so many heroes come for the sake of peace with
their helmets laced on and swords in their hands? Rüdiger means to earn
his castles and his lands at our expense."

{2174} Before the fiddler had finished speaking, noble Rüdiger was seen
in front of the hall. He set his good shield down in front of his feet;
now he had to refuse his friends both service and greeting. {2175} The
noble margrave called into the hall: "Bold Nibelungs, now defend
yourselves on every side. You should have profited by me; now you pay
for me. Once we were friends. I want to be free of that loyalty."

{2176} The hard-pressed men were dismayed at this news; none of them
found any joy in it that the man they loved now meant to fight them.
They had already endured great hardship from their enemies. {2177} "God
in heaven forbid," said the warrior Gunther, "that you should turn from
your kindness to us, and from the great loyalty that we truly counted
on. I trust you will never do it."

{2178} "I cannot leave it undone," said the brave man. "I must fight
you, for I have given my word. Now defend yourselves, bold heroes, as
you love your lives. King Etzel's wife would not release me."

{2179} "You turn against us too late," said the noble king. "May God
reward you, noble Rüdiger, for the loyalty and the love you have shown
us — if only you would act with kindness to the end. {2180} We should
always be in your debt, I and my kinsmen, for what you gave us, if you
let us live: the splendid gifts you brought us here, in loyalty, into
Etzel's land. Think of that, noble Rüdiger."

{2181} "How gladly I would grant you," said the warrior Rüdiger, "that I
might give you my gifts in full measure, as freely as I once hoped to —
if only I would never be blamed for it."

{2182} "Turn back, noble Rüdiger," said Gernot. "No host ever treated
his guests as lovingly as you treated us. You will profit from it well,
if we stay alive."

{2183} "Would to God, most noble Gernot," said Rüdiger, "that you were
on the Rhine and I were dead with some honor, since I must fight you. No
friends ever did worse by heroes."

{2184} "God reward you, Lord Rüdiger," Gernot answered, "for your rich
gifts. Your death grieves me, if such goodness as yours is to perish.
Here I carry your weapon, the one you gave me, good hero. {2185} It has
never failed me in all this peril; under its edges lies many a knight
dead. It is bright and true, splendid and good. I believe no warrior
will ever give so rich a gift again. {2186} And if you will not turn
back, if you mean to come at us, and you kill any of the friends I still
have in here, then with your own sword I will take your life. Then I
will be sorry for you, Rüdiger, and for your splendid wife."

{2187} "Would to God, Lord Gernot, that it could happen: that your whole
will were done here, and your friends came out of it alive! Then both my
daughter and my wife could trust you well."

{2188} Then young Giselher, beautiful Ute's son, spoke for Burgundy:
"Why do you do this, Lord Rüdiger? Those who came with me all wish you
well. You are setting about this badly; you want to make your beautiful
daughter a widow too soon. {2189} When you and your warriors attack me
in battle, how unfriendly you make it look — I who trust you above all
other men, and that is why I took your daughter for my wife."

{2190} "Remember your loyalty, most noble king, and if God lets you
leave this place," said Rüdiger, "do not let the young lady pay for me.
For your own goodness' sake, be gracious to her."

{2191} "That I would do, and rightly," said young Giselher. "But if my
high kinsmen who are still in here are to die at your hands, then my
steadfast friendship for you, and for your daughter too, must be at an
end."

{2192} "Now may God have mercy on us," said the brave man. Then they
raised their shields, as if to go and fight the guests in Kriemhild's
hall. But Hagen called down loudly from the top of the stairs:
{2193} "Wait a while, most noble Rüdiger," said Hagen. "I and my lords
want to say more, as our extremity forces us to. What good can the death
of us exiles do Etzel? {2194} I stand in great trouble," Hagen went on.
"The shield Lady Gotelind gave me to carry has been hacked to pieces in
my hand by the Huns. I brought it in friendship into Etzel's land.
{2195} If only God in heaven would grant that I might still carry a
shield as good as the one in your hand, most noble Rüdiger! Then I would
need no coat of mail in the fighting."

{2196} "Gladly would I serve you with my shield, if I dared offer it to
you in front of Kriemhild. But take it, Hagen, and carry it on your arm.
Ah, if only you could carry it home to the land of Burgundy!"

{2197} When he offered to give the shield so willingly, many eyes grew
red with hot tears. It was the last gift that Rüdiger of Pöchlarn ever
gave to any warrior. {2198} However grim Hagen was, and however hard his
temper, the gift the good hero had made so near his last hour moved him.
Many a noble knight began to grieve with him.

{2199} "God in heaven reward you, most noble Rüdiger. There will never
be another like you, to give exiled warriors such splendid gifts. God
grant that your goodness lives forever." {2200} "Alas for this news,"
said Hagen again. "We had so many other burdens to carry; must we fight
with friends too? God be told of it." The margrave said: "It grieves me
from my heart."

{2201} "God reward you for the gift, most noble Rüdiger. Whatever these
proud warriors do toward you, my hand shall never touch you in this
battle, even if you kill every one of them from the land of Burgundy."

{2202} Good Rüdiger bowed to him courteously. They wept on every side,
that no one could part this heartbreak; it was a great misery. In
Rüdiger the father of many virtues was about to die.

{2203} Then Volker the minstrel spoke from the hall: "Since my comrade
Hagen has made peace with you, you shall have it just as steadfastly
from my hand. You earned it well when we came into this land.
{2204} Noble margrave, you shall be my messenger. The margravine gave me
these red arm-rings, to wear here at the feast. See them for yourself,
so that you can be my witness to it."

{2205} "Would to God in heaven," said Rüdiger, "that the margravine
could give you more! I will gladly tell my dear love the news, if I see
her alive again. Of that you may have no doubt."

{2206} When he had promised him that, Rüdiger raised his shield. His
heart raged; he waited no longer. He ran at the guests like a true
warrior, and the mighty margrave struck many a swift blow. {2207} The
two of them, Volker and Hagen, stepped higher up the stairs, for the
bold warriors had promised him that. But he still found men bold enough
standing by the tower, so that Rüdiger began the fight with heavy care.

{2208} Bent on deadly revenge, Gunther and Gernot let him in; they had
heroes' hearts. Giselher stood back higher up, and truly it grieved him.
He still hoped to live, and so he kept clear of Rüdiger.

{2209} The margrave's men sprang at their enemies; they were seen
following their lord like true warriors. They carried their cutting
swords in their hands, and many helmets and many a splendid shield-rim
broke under them. {2210} The weary men struck the men of Pöchlarn many a
hard blow that cut clean and deep through the bright mail rings, right
down to the life. They did splendid work in that storm of battle.

{2211} The noble company had now come all the way in. Volker and Hagen
sprang into it at once; they gave quarter to no one but the one man.
Blood ran down out of the helmets from the hands of both of them.
{2212} How grimly the swords rang in there! The shield-plates burst from
their fastenings under the blows, and the jewels hewn from the shields
fell into the blood. They fought so grimly that no one will ever do the
like again.

{2213} The lord of Pöchlarn went back and forth, as a man does who knows
how to work with courage in battle. That day Rüdiger showed himself
every inch a warrior, bold and worthy of praise. {2214} Here stood the
warriors Gunther and Gernot; they struck many a hero dead in the fight.
Giselher and Dankwart, the two of them, made light of it; they sent many
a man to his last day.

{2215} Rüdiger showed well that he was strong enough, bold and well
armed. Hey, what heroes he killed! A Burgundian saw it, and anger drove
him; and from that, noble Rüdiger's death began to draw near.

{2216} Strong Gernot called out to the hero. He said to the margrave:
"You will not leave me any of my men alive, most noble Rüdiger. That
grieves me beyond measure; I can watch it no longer. {2217} Now your
gift may well do you harm, since you have taken so many of my friends
from me. Turn this way, noble, brave man: your gift shall be paid for as
best I can."

{2218} Before the margrave could reach him, bright mail rings had to
lose their color. Then the two men, hungry for honor, sprang at each
other, and each began to guard himself against deep wounds. {2219} Their
swords were so sharp that nothing could stand against them. Then the
warrior Rüdiger struck Gernot through his flint-hard helmet, so that the
blood flowed down. The bold good knight soon paid him back. {2220} He
swung Rüdiger's gift high in his hand, and wounded to death as he was,
he struck him one blow through his good shield, down to the helmet
strap. From that, beautiful Gotelind's husband had to die.

{2221} Truly, so rich a gift was never worse repaid. They fell, both
killed, Gernot and Rüdiger, alike in the fight, each by the other's
hand. Only now did Hagen grow angry, when he saw the great loss.

{2222} The hero of Tronje said: "This has gone badly for us. In these
two men we have suffered a loss so great that their people and their
lands will never get over it. Rüdiger's heroes are forfeit to us exiles.
{2223} Alas for my brother, who has been killed here! What grievous news
comes to me all the time! And noble Rüdiger too I must mourn forever.
The loss and the grievous wounds are on both sides."

{2224} When Lord Giselher saw his father-in-law dead, those still inside
had to suffer for it. Death went hunting hard through Rüdiger's company;
not one of the men of Pöchlarn was left alive.

{2225} Then Gunther and Giselher, and Hagen too, Dankwart and Volker,
the good warriors, went to where they found the two men lying, and there
the heroes wept in grief. {2226} "Death is robbing us cruelly," said
young Giselher. "Now stop your weeping and let us go out to the air, so
that the wind cools the mail on us battle-weary men. Truly, I think God
in heaven does not grant us much longer to live."

{2227} Here one warrior sat and there another leaned, many of them; they
were idle again, for Rüdiger's heroes lay dead. The din had died away.
The silence lasted so long that it troubled Etzel.

{2228} "Alas for such service," said the king's wife. "It is not loyal
enough to make our enemies pay with their lives at Rüdiger's hand. He
means to bring them back to the land of Burgundy. {2229} What use is it,
King Etzel, that we shared with him whatever he wanted? The hero has
done wrong. The man who should avenge us is making peace." Volker, the
fine warrior, answered her:

{2230} "The truth is not so, alas, most noble queen. If I dared to call
so noble a lady a liar, I would say you have lied like a devil against
Rüdiger. He and his warriors are quite cheated of any peace. {2231} He
did what the king commanded so completely that he and his household lie
dead here. Now look around you, Kriemhild, for someone else to command.
The hero Rüdiger has served you to the end. {2232} If you will not
believe it, we will let you see." To her heart's grief it was done: they
carried the hewn hero to where the king could see him. Nothing so
sorrowful had ever happened to Etzel's warriors.

{2233} When they saw the margrave carried in dead, no scribe could write
or tell of the frenzied grief of women and men that began to show itself
there, from the sorrow of their hearts. {2234} Etzel's grief grew so
great that the mighty king's voice rang out like a lion's roar in
heartbroken lament; and so did his wife's. Beyond all measure, they
mourned good Rüdiger.
