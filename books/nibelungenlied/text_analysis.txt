THE NIBELUNGENLIED -- TEXT ANALYSIS AND TRANSLATION STRATEGY
(written at setup, 2026-10-10. A scholarly modernization of a canonical
medieval epic, from the Middle High German.)

Sections: 1 source and edition; 2 the work and its shape; 3 the governing
decision (prose); 4 voice; 5 locked vocabulary and names; 6 the narrator's
formulas; 7 famous passages; 8 sensitive content; 9 headings and format;
10 ratio and checks; 11 reception (for the introduction, NOT the text);
12 open questions for Alex.


1. SOURCE AND EDITION

THE ROADMAP WAS WRONG ABOUT THE SOURCE. Gutenberg #14915 is not Middle High
German: it is Karl Simrock's New High German verse translation (his last
edition), and it follows the C version -- it ends "das ist das
Nibelungenlied" (C's "daz ist der Nibelunge liet"), not B's "daz ist der
Nibelunge nôt". It is kept in _src/ and was consulted once (strophe 524).

THE TEXT WE TRANSLATE: Karl Bartsch's edition of the B version, "Der
Nibelunge Nôt" (Leipzig: Brockhaus, 1870-80), in the raw form of the Oxford
Text Archive's text 0202, as published by Bibliotheca Augustana (Ulrich
Harsch). This is the standard reading text: Bartsch's numbering, revised
by de Boor, is the standard numbering modern editions and translations
cite (which ones: verify before the introduction). 39 Aventiuren, 2,379 strophes. MANUSCRIPT
TRADITION: B (St. Gallen, Cod. Sang. 857, c. 1250), the "nôt" version,
the older of the two redactions; not C (the "liet" version, about twenty
years younger and more courtly) and not Lachmann's A.

The OTA typing is lower-case and keeps manuscript-like spellings: v for u
(vns, chvene = küene), ch for k (chvnech = künec), c for z (ecel = Etzel,
ze/ce), and no length marks (maere = mære). A few late Aventiuren switch to
u-spellings (uil, uon). Read through the spelling; the crib settles sense.

Strophes Harsch supplied in italics where the OTA typing lacked them: 1, 3,
18, 389, 523, 524. All are Bartsch's text. STROPHE 1 ("Uns ist in alten
mæren wunders vil geseit") IS NOT IN MANUSCRIPT B: Bartsch, like every
reading edition, prints it from A/C. It is the poem's most famous strophe
and the edition keeps it (the introduction should say so in a sentence).

prep.py repairs three strophes the OTA typing gives with three lines, from
Reichert's diplomatic transcription of B (the second witness): 524 (B's
line, which Bartsch moves here from 523), 1555, 2288. Each is asserted.

CRIB: Daniel B. Shumway, The Nibelungenlied (Houghton Mifflin, 1909),
Gutenberg #1151 -- a COMPLETE, LITERAL PROSE translation of Bartsch's B
text, the same text we translate. It runs 1.05 English words per MHG word
(1.01-1.16 per Aventiure), which is what complete-and-literal looks like.
reference/NNN.txt holds his Adventure with note markers and endnotes
removed. No strophe numbers, so alignment is per Aventiure.
THE CRIB'S COSTUME: Shumway's preface says he made "the language to some
extent archaic, especially in dialogue, in order to give the impression of
age": ye, thou, spake, quoth, hight, wot, fain, an', "passing bold",
"worship" (for êre), "hightide", "lordings", "thereto". None of it is the
poet's (whose-archaism-is-it): MHG was the living language of its
audience. check.py sweeps for all of it. Use him for what a strophe MEANS.

NOT USED, and why (all measured, _src/):
  Armour 1897 (#3636), prose, 0.94 of the MHG -- complete in its 39
    Adventures but condensing (Shumway's preface names the habit:
    prose translations that "retell the story instead of translating it").
  Horton 1898 (#59831, also SE), Needler 1904 (#7321): verse; a verse
    crib bends the sense to the rhyme. Lettsom 1850 (#38468): verse, and
    Lachmann's A, a different text.
  Simrock (#14915): C version, NHG verse, different numbering (~2,495).

COPYRIGHT: Bartsch (d. 1888) and Shumway (1909) are public domain. The OTA
typing and Harsch's supplements add no copyrightable expression to a
public-domain text under US law (the project's existing ruling for
transcriptions); OTA texts sometimes carry a non-commercial licence of
their own, which Alex may want to look at (section 12). Reichert's
diplomatic B is used only as a checking witness and is never reproduced.


2. THE WORK AND ITS SHAPE

An anonymous Austrian poet, working around 1200 (the scholarly consensus
places him at or near the court of Bishop Wolfger of Passau; verify the
phrasing before the introduction), gathered centuries-old heroic legend --
the destruction of the Burgundian kingdom by the Huns in 436-37, Attila's
death in 453, the Siegfried story the Norse also told -- into one long
poem in four-line rhymed strophes, set in the courtly world of his own
day. Two halves, which this edition marks with word-form Part dividers:

  Part One: Siegfried            Aventiuren 1-19   (files 000-018)
    Siegfried comes to Worms to win Kriemhild; helps Gunther win Brunhild
    by deceit (twice); the queens' quarrel exposes the deceit; Hagen
    murders Siegfried; Hagen sinks the Nibelung treasure in the Rhine.
  Part Two: Kriemhild's Revenge  Aventiuren 20-39  (files 019-038)
    Kriemhild marries Etzel, king of the Huns; after thirteen years she
    invites her brothers; the Burgundians ride east knowing they will not
    return; the feast becomes a massacre; the hall burns; Rüdiger dies
    between two loyalties; Dietrich takes Gunther and Hagen; Kriemhild
    kills them both; Hildebrand kills her.

The division is the poem's: the first nineteen Aventiuren were the "first
Rhenish part" for Simrock's Vorrede, and Armour prints Book I/Book II at the
same seam. The dividers are "Part One: Siegfried" and "Part Two:
Kriemhild's Revenge" (word form: assemble.PART_LINE would DELETE a Roman
numeral). Chapters number 1-39 continuously across both Parts, as every
edition numbers the Aventiuren: "Chapter 39" is the 39th Aventiure.

ONE FILE PER AVENTIURE, no splits: the largest (20, 147 strophes, 5,020
MHG words) projects to about 6,000-6,500 English, under the ~7k cap. The
smallest (34, 19 strophes; 1, 19 strophes) stay whole: an Aventiure is the
poem's own unit and every reader's reference.


3. THE GOVERNING DECISION: PROSE

THE ODYSSEY CASE, NOT THE INFERNO CASE. inferno/ kept verse, tercet for
tercet, because the tercet is Dante's unit of thought and the line count
can be held exact. odyssey/ went to prose (Alex's ruling) because the
hexameter has no English shape that is not padding and 12,000 lines of it
is a novel-length story a reader wants as prose. The Nibelungenstrophe is
Homer's case: a long-line rhymed quatrain whose rhymes are carried by
filler ("daz ist wâr", "der recke küene", "sît"), 9,500 long lines, a story
told for its plot and its people. So: VIVID MODERN PROSE, every strophe's
content present and in order, the poet's machinery kept instead of his
metre (sections 4 and 6). Say in the front matter that it is prose.

WHAT THE STROPHE STILL GIVES US: the strophe is the unit the reader can
check, so every agent marks where each one begins ({N}, Bartsch's number)
in a draft, and check.py proves all 2,379 present, once, in order (section
10). The markers never reach the page.


4. VOICE

THE NARRATOR is a performer in front of an audience. He says "I", he
addresses "you" ("now you can hear wonders told"), he tells you what he
has heard and what he cannot tell, and above all he TELLS YOU THE ENDING
IN ADVANCE, again and again (section 6). Keep his first person and his
address to the listener; keep every intrusion. His register is plain,
grave, sometimes dry, occasionally grimly funny (Volker's "fiddle bow",
Hagen's toast). It is NOT courtly-precious and NOT Wagnerian.

READABILITY IS THE DELIVERABLE (Alex, 2026-09-02): every sentence one a
bright fourteen-year-old could read aloud and follow. Concretely:
- Join the strophes into real paragraphs (typically two to five strophes
  a paragraph, breaking at a change of scene or speaker); dialogue in
  quotation marks with modern paragraphing.
- Ceremony is plot here: who greets whom first, who rises, who rides
  beside whom, who gives what gift, who stands at the stirrup. Keep all
  of it, lucidly; the queens' quarrel and Hagen's refusal to rise are
  about precisely this. Clothes, horses, gold and silk in their lists:
  text, not decoration -- the poet's audience loved them; keep every item.
- The rhyme-fillers: render each epithet at least as a word ("bold
  Gernot"), never as a doubled phrase ("the bold, the brave warrior"
  where the MHG has one idea); "daz ist wâr" and "daz sage ich iu" become
  the narrator's own "truly", "believe me", "I tell you" -- kept as a
  voice, not mechanically every time, never cut as a habit.
- Fold what a reader needs into the sentence (the Verne rule; NO notes):
  "the margrave -- the lord who guarded Etzel's western border --" once,
  where it first matters; never a footnote, never a bracket.

CHARACTERS (keep them distinct; the poem's power is in them):
  Kriemhild: in Part One a young, proud, loving princess -- and the one
    who unwittingly betrays Siegfried's weak spot. In Part Two grief made
    iron: patient, calculating, still grieving; the poem calls her both
    faithful and a she-devil. Render both; take no side the poem doesn't.
  Siegfried: dazzling, generous, impulsive, boastful at first; loyal to
    Gunther to the point of deceit; his dying speech is bitter and kind.
  Gunther: the king, decent, weak, dependent on others' strength.
  Hagen: the poem's most complex figure -- the murderer, the most loyal
    vassal, the clear-eyed realist who knows they are riding to their
    deaths and goes, sardonic, fearless, contemptuous. Never a cartoon
    villain; never a hero the poem doesn't make him.
  Brunhild: a warrior-queen of superhuman strength in her own land, a
    humiliated wife at Worms. Proud, wronged, then nearly silent.
  Volker: the knight-fiddler, Hagen's friend; wit and ferocity.
  Rüdiger: the generous margrave, the poem's best man, destroyed by
    oaths that cannot all be kept. His crisis (37) is the moral centre.
  Dietrich: the exiled king among the Huns, grave, wise, reluctant;
    Hildebrand his old armsmaster. Etzel: a mighty, generous, outwitted
    king, not a monster. Giselher: the youngest, warm, innocent.


5. LOCKED VOCABULARY AND NAMES

NAMES (check.py fails the unlocked forms and warns on a missing locked
one): Siegfried; Kriemhild; Gunther, Gernot, Giselher ("young Giselher");
Ute (their mother); Dankrat (their father); Hagen of Tronje; Dankwart
(Hagen's brother); Ortwin of Metz; Volker of Alzey, "Volker the fiddler";
Gere; Eckewart; Rumold (master of the kitchen); Sindold; Hunold; Siegmund
and Sieglinde (Siegfried's parents); Brunhild; Liudeger (of Saxony) and
Liudegast (of Denmark); Alberich (the dwarf); Schilbung and Nibelung
(the princes whose treasure Siegfried took); Balmung (Siegfried's sword,
later Hagen's); Etzel (king of the Huns -- NEVER "Attila" in the text;
the introduction makes the connection); Helche (Etzel's late queen);
Ortlieb (Etzel and Kriemhild's son); Blödel (Etzel's brother); Werbel and
Swemmel (Etzel's fiddlers, his envoys); Rüdiger of Pöchlarn ("the
margrave"); Gotelind (his wife); Dietrich of Bern; Hildebrand; Wolfhart,
Helfrich, Helmnot, Wolfwin, Wolfbrand, Sigestab, Ritschart, Gerbart,
Wichart (Dietrich's men, "the Amelungs"); Iring of Denmark; Irnfried of
Thuringia; Hawart of Denmark; Bishop Pilgrim of Passau; Else and Gelfrat
(Bavarian lords); Ramung, Gibeche, Hornboge (Etzel's vassal princes).
Umlauts kept: Rüdiger, Blödel, Pöchlarn.

PEOPLES AND PLACES:
  the Burgundians (Burgonden) -- the Nibelungs (see the RULE below) --
  the Huns (Hiunen); the land of the Huns -- the Saxons, the Danes --
  Worms on the Rhine; Xanten (Siegfried's home, in the Netherlands);
  Isenland and its castle Isenstein (NEVER "Iceland": the poem's Isenland
  is not the modern island, and the word would hand the reader a false
  fact); Etzel's castle (Etzelnburg) -- route of the journey east, by
  their modern names: Lorsch, the Danube, Passau, the Inn, Enns, Eferding,
  Pöchlarn, Melk, Mautern, Traismauer, Tulln, Vienna, Wieselburg,
  Esztergom (Gran) -- Bern (Dietrich's city: Verona. The poem first names
  it in Aventiure 28 (file 027); give it there once, in the sentence --
  "Hildebrand of Bern, the Italian city we call Verona" -- because "Bern"
  alone hands a modern reader Switzerland), Metz, Alzey, Tronje, Spessart, the Vosges (Waskenwald).
  RULE -- WHO ARE "THE NIBELUNGS"? In Part One the Nibelungs are
  Siegfried's treasure-people and land; from Aventiure 25 on, the poem
  calls the Burgundians "Nibelungs" (they now hold the treasure). Use
  "Nibelungs" exactly where the poem does, "Burgundians" where it does;
  never "correct" either. Where the shift first happens (Aventiure 25),
  one clause in the sentence may say "the Burgundians -- the Nibelungs,
  as they were called now --" if the sentence needs it.

TITLES AND RANKS: künec / küneginne = king / queen; vogt = lord (or
"ruler"); marcgrâve = margrave (glossed once in the sentence, 20); vürste
= prince; herre = lord; vrouwe = lady (as address, "my lady"); magedîn /
maget / juncvrouwe = girl / young lady / maiden (sparingly); ritter =
knight; recke / degen / helt = warrior / fighter / hero (vary freely; not
always "knight"); knehte = squires / young men; ingesinde = household,
retinue; man (someone's "man") = vassal or "his men"; eigen man /
eigenholt = BONDSMAN: a man who is another's property-in-service, owing
tribute and duty -- this is the queens' quarrel (13-14) and it is locked;
mâge / vriunde = kinsmen / family (vriunt is usually kin, not "friend").

THE MORAL VOCABULARY (the poem turns on these; keep each in its lane):
  triuwe        loyalty (faithfulness to a sworn bond: vassal to lord,
                wife to husband, host to guest). The adjective getriuwe =
                loyal, faithful. untriuwe / ungetriuwe = treachery,
                treacherous, faithless. Never "troth", never "fealty".
  êre           honour (also: honours = splendour, gifts, rank).
                NEVER Shumway's "worship".
  lop / prîs    renown / fame / praise.
  ellen         courage.  küene = bold, brave.  snel = swift, quick.
  übermuot      arrogance (Brunhild's, Hagen's, the Burgundians').
  hôher muot    high spirits / pride (good sense).
  leit          grief, sorrow; also the WRONG suffered ("Kriemhild's
                wrong" -- in 23 "how she planned to avenge her wrongs").
  liebe ... leit   joy ... sorrow; the poem's master pair (section 7).
  nît / haz     hatred, malice, envy.  zorn = anger, rage.
  râche         revenge.  mort = murder (Siegfried's death is "murder",
                and the word is the poem's: ermort, mortlîch).
  minne         love (also the memorial cup in 32; see section 7).
  arebeit       hardship, struggle, ordeal (NOT "work").
  nôt           distress, peril, extremity, doom; in the poem's last
                words, "der Nibelunge nôt" = "the fall of the Nibelungs".
  sælde         good fortune, blessing.  vâlant / vâlandinne = devil /
                she-devil.  tiuvel = devil.
  heiden / kristen  pagan / Christian (never "heathen" as a noun of
                contempt; "pagan" is the plain word).

OBJECTS AND CUSTOMS:
  tarnkappe / tarnhût = the cloak of invisibility (gloss once, in the
    sentence, when Hagen tells the story in 3 and when Siegfried uses it
    in 6-7: it makes him invisible and gives him the strength of twelve
    men); afterwards "the cloak", "his magic cloak". Never "Tarnkappe".
  hort / schaz der Nibelunge = the Nibelung treasure (or "the treasure",
    "the hoard" sparingly).  Balmung = the sword (named).
  hôchgezît = festival / feast / celebration; the wedding where it is
    one. NEVER "hightide" (Armour's calque).
  bûhurt / buhurt = mock battle on horseback, a tournament melee (gloss
    in the sentence the first time). tjost = joust. ros = horse/warhorse.
  palas = the great hall.  kemenâte = the women's chambers, a lady's room.
  münster = the cathedral (Worms, 14) / church.
  videlære = fiddler; videlboge = fiddle bow (Volker's grim joke: his
    sword is his bow; keep it, it is the poet's).
  Units: a rast (484) is a day's stage of travel; render the distance as
    "more than a hundred days' journey" (the sense) -- the poem's
    exaggeration stays exaggerated. Money and measures are given as the
    poem gives them ("twelve wagons", "a hundred pack-horses' load").

ADDRESS: du / ir: the MHG switch between intimate du and formal ir
carries tone in the queens' quarrel and in Hagen's insults; English
"you" can't show it, so let the word choice carry it (curt, contemptuous,
tender), never "thou". Kriemhild to Hagen in Part Two: icy formality.


6. THE NARRATOR'S FORMULAS

THE FORESHADOWING IS THE POEM'S MACHINE. Dozens of strophes end with the
narrator telling you what it will cost: "sît" (later), "daz wart in sît
leit", "des muosen sît engelten", "durch sîn eines sterben starp vil
maneger muoter kint" (19). These are not decoration and a retelling that
trims them has removed the book's tragic structure. RULES:
- Every one is kept, in its strophe, at the end of its paragraph where
  the MHG puts it at the end of a strophe.
- A small stable core of words: "later", "in the end", "sorrow",
  "cost ... their lives", "would regret it". Vary the sentence, keep the
  core. Short, plain, ominous: "Later it cost many a mother's son his
  life." "He would come to regret that bitterly."
- Never comic, never melodramatic ("little did they know...!" is banned).
The other formulas: "Now you can hear..." (the narrator's announcement);
"as I have heard tell" (his source); "I cannot tell you..." (his limits);
"there was great weeping" / lament (kept, varied, not multiplied). The
greeting and arming formulas are kept at full length: they are scenes.

8. SENSITIVE CONTENT -- RULINGS

A canonical medieval epic about loyalty, deceit, murder and annihilation.
Measured register throughout: render the poet's world as his, never
sensationalize, never bowdlerize, never annotate, never add a modern
judgement the poem does not make (the Verne rule; the herodotus/odyssey
violence rule: plain words, nothing cut, nothing relished).

1. THE VIOLENCE. The second half is a massacre: Hagen's beheading of the
   ferryman (1562) and his attempt to drown the chaplain (1575-81); the
   killing of the child Ortlieb at the table (1961); severed heads and
   hands; the hall burned with the Burgundians inside (36); the survivors
   drinking the blood of the dead to quench their thirst (2114-17); the
   bodies thrown down the stairs (34); Gunther's head carried in by the
   hair (2369); Kriemhild beheading the bound Hagen with Siegfried's sword
   (2373); Hildebrand hewing Kriemhild to pieces (2376-77). The poet
   narrates all of it at the same even pace. So do we: short sentences,
   exact, no adjectives he did not use, no gore he did not write, no
   softening ("killed" where he says beheaded is softening).
2. THE BRIDAL NIGHTS (10). Brunhild ties Gunther hand and foot with her
   belt and hangs him on a nail all night (637); the next night Siegfried,
   invisible, overpowers her in the dark for Gunther, takes her ring and
   belt, and withdraws; Gunther then sleeps with her, and with her
   virginity her strength is gone (675-82). The poet plays the first
   night half as farce and the second as a violent struggle. It is a
   rape by proxy in all but name and the poem does not call it that.
   Render it exactly as written: the farce as farce (Gunther's humiliation
   is the joke, not Brunhild's), the struggle as a struggle, her loss of
   strength as the poem states it, nothing explicit beyond the MHG, no
   wink, no modern comment. The theft of ring and belt is plot: keep it
   precise.
3. THE QUARREL (14). Kriemhild calls Brunhild "mannes kebse": a vassal's
   CONCUBINE (locked; the insult is exact and the word is clear), and
   says (840) Siegfried was the first to sleep with her ("den minnet
   êrste Sîvrit ... der dir den magetuom an gewan" -- "it wasn't my
   brother who took your virginity"). Plain and adult, not coarse: these
   are queens shouting on the cathedral steps. "Bondsman" is the other
   locked insult (section 5).
4. A WIFE BEATEN. Siegfried beats Kriemhild for having talked (894: "he
   has beaten me black and blue for it"). One plain sentence, as written.
5. RELIGION. Etzel is a pagan; Kriemhild's reluctance to marry a pagan
   (1248, 1261) and her having Ortlieb baptized (1388); Christians and
   pagans at Etzel's court (1335); the chaplain; Mass at Worms and at
   Etzel's court. Plain, the poet's terms: "pagan", "Christian".
6. THE HUNS. A people in the poem, not a slur; the poet is sometimes
   contemptuous of their fighting and that is his narrator's view,
   rendered as his. Etzel is not demonized and must not be.
7. "SHE-DEVIL" (2371) and "the devil" (Gunther of Brunhild, 649):
   Hagen's word and Gunther's, kept.
8. LOYALTY. The poem shows triuwe destroying everyone who keeps it.
   Render it neither as a virtue to admire nor as a pathology to
   diagnose: as the poem's characters' iron obligation (see 11).


9. HEADINGS AND FORMAT

Headings (manifest "title"; agents copy them exactly), from the poem's own
rubrics (manifest "mhg_title"), "How ..." kept as the rubric's form:
  Chapter 1: The Nibelungs ... Chapter 39: How Lord Dietrich Overcame
  Gunther and Hagen. part_before: 000 "Part One: Siegfried", 019 "Part
  Two: Kriemhild's Revenge".
Body: prose paragraphs, one blank line between; dialogue in straight
double quotes, single inside; real em dashes (—); straight apostrophes.
No tabs, no indentation, no markup, no notes, no part lines.
DRAFTS: agents write drafts/NNN.txt with {N} strophe markers; check.py
writes modern_chapters/ (section 10).


10. RATIO AND CHECKS

Ratio = English words / MHG words (the tabbed verse lines only). Shumway's
complete literal prose is 1.05 overall, 1.01-1.16 per file. A readable
retelling that joins strophes into sentences, resolves the filler and
folds in what a reader needs lands ABOVE a literal crib: expect about
1.1-1.35. PROVISIONAL check.py band 1.0-1.6; re-derive it from file 000
(the dogen rule) and record the result in running_notes. BELOW SHUMWAY'S
RATIO FOR THE FILE IS A RED FLAG FOR COMPRESSION; above ~1.45 means
explaining or padding.
verify.py counts chapters/ including the "[N]" lines (2,379 tokens, about
3% of the denominator), so its figures run slightly under check.py's:
run `python3 verify.py nibelungenlied --min-ratio 0.95 --max-ratio 1.55`
(provisional, re-derive with the band). Expected total: about 90-110k
English words from 81,430 MHG.

check.py (see its docstring): source structure against witnesses prep did
not produce (Reichert's B, divided into Aventiuren independently;
Shumway's and Armour's 39; the 2,379 total); per draft, every strophe
marked once and in order; heading, prose form, renderer, quotes, Shumway
archaism sweep, unlocked name forms; locked names warned when missing.


11. RECEPTION -- FOR THE INTRODUCTION, NEVER THE TEXT

The poem was lost from view for two centuries and rediscovered in 1755
(manuscript C at Hohenems). The nineteenth century made it "the German
Iliad", a national epic, and the twentieth weaponized its central value:
"Nibelungentreue" (Chancellor von Bülow, 1909, of Germany's loyalty to
Austria-Hungary); Göring's speech of 30 January 1943 comparing the Sixth
Army at Stalingrad to the Burgundians fighting on in the burning hall;
Nazi schoolbook use. Wagner's Ring is a different work drawn mostly from
the Norse sources (the Volsunga saga, the Edda), not this poem. None of
this belongs in the retelling, which must neither glorify the loyalty
(the reception's error) nor lecture against it (an anachronism the poem
does not make). It belongs in the editor's introduction, along with: the
