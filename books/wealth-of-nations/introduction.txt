Adam Smith published this in March 1776, at fifty-two. He was a moral philosopher by trade — he had held the chair of moral philosophy at Glasgow for twelve years — and the book he was known for at the time was The Theory of Moral Sentiments, published in 1759, which is about sympathy and conscience and has nothing to do with commerce. He left the university to tutor a young duke, spent two years travelling in France, where he met Quesnay and the economists around him, went home to Kirkcaldy, and worked on this for about a decade.

It is quoted more than it is read, and the quoting has given it a reputation that does not survive contact with the pages. The invisible hand appears once. It is in Book Four, inside a narrow argument about why a merchant who could invest his money abroad usually keeps it at home, and Smith builds nothing further on the phrase. What he does build on is whatever he could count. The first chapter is about a pin factory. After that come the wages of London bricklayers and Scottish country labourers, the profit on capital, the rent of land, the price of cattle and herring and corn, the silver mines of Peru, the Bank of Amsterdam, the practice of hiring apprentices, the tax on windows, and the cost of holding colonies, which he thinks Britain cannot afford and ought to give up.

He is also, repeatedly and by name, hostile to businessmen. People of the same trade seldom meet together, he writes, even for fun and socializing, without the conversation ending in a conspiracy against the public. Masters combine against their workers constantly and quietly, and the law lets them. Any new trade regulation proposed by merchants should be examined, he says, with the most suspicious attention, because it comes from people whose interest is never the same as the public's and who have deceived and oppressed the public many times before. Whatever he was for, it was not business getting what it asks for.

It is long: five Books, from the division of labour through capital, economic history, the attack on mercantilism, and finally taxation and public debt. It also contains real data. The chapter on rent ends in a table of English wheat prices year by year from 1202 onward, several hundred rows of it, which an earlier state of this edition summarized in a note and which is printed here in full.

Two books written against parts of this one are also in this collection: Malthus's Essay on the Principle of Population of 1798, and Henry George's Progress and Poverty of 1879.
