The plague reached Florence in the spring of 1348 and killed a large part of the city inside a few months. Giovanni Boccaccio lived through it, and the book opens with a long account of what it did: the swellings in the groin and the armpit, the houses standing open, the bodies put out on the doorstep in the morning, the neighbours who stopped visiting each other and the families who stopped burying their own. He says more than a hundred thousand died within the walls. Then, in the church of Santa Maria Novella, seven young women meet one Tuesday morning, are joined by three young men, and decide to leave.

They go out to a villa in the hills with servants and provisions, and to fill the days they make a rule. Each of them tells one story a day for ten days, which comes to a hundred. A different member of the company is crowned king or queen each morning and sets that day's subject — people who come through disaster to happiness, people who get what they want by cleverness, love affairs that end badly, quick answers that save the speaker, tricks wives play on their husbands. Dioneo always tells last, and has bargained for the right to ignore the theme. Between the days they eat, walk, sing and argue about the stories, and that frame is not decoration: it is a group of people building an orderly life on purpose while a city dies behind them.

The stories themselves are worldly, fast, and frequently filthy. Priests, friars and nuns come off worst of anyone, and the collection opens with a professional liar making a false confession on his deathbed and being canonised for it. A great many of the tales turn on a woman outmanoeuvring a husband, a suitor or a confessor, and Boccaccio says outright in his Proem that he is writing for women, who have fewer ways than men to get out of their own heads. He knew what he would be accused of and answered it in advance: the last thing in the book is his own reply to critics who found it indecent. This edition does not soften any of it.

Chaucer read him and took from him, and so did most of the storytellers who came after. The hundred stories are all here, along with the frame, the ten days' introductions and conclusions, and the songs. The translation is made from John Payne's 1886 English rather than from Boccaccio's Italian. The last story in the book, about the peasant wife Griselda and the trials her husband puts her through, was the most famous of them for centuries and is the hardest to read now; it is here as he told it, and the man telling it signs off by saying the husband deserved worse.
