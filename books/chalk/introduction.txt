Thomas Henry Huxley (1825–1895) trained as a surgeon, spent four years as assistant surgeon on HMS Rattlesnake surveying the waters off Australia and New Guinea, and made his name as a zoologist studying the soft-bodied animals the ship's nets brought up. By the 1860s he was professor at the Royal School of Mines in London and the loudest public defender of Darwin's Origin of Species, which had appeared in 1859. He gave lectures to working men on principle: he thought science belonged to them as much as to anyone.

In August 1868 the British Association for the Advancement of Science met at Norwich, and Huxley gave this lecture to the working men of the city. He took a piece of chalk, the kind a carpenter keeps in his pocket, and asked what it could tell them. It was printed in Macmillan's Magazine the next month, collected in his Lay Sermons in 1870, and revised by him for the eighth volume of his Collected Essays, Discourses Biological and Geological, in 1894.

The argument runs like a detective story. Under a microscope the chalk turns out to be made almost entirely of tiny shells. The same shells were being dredged up from the floor of the Atlantic, where ships sounding the bottom for the new telegraph cable had brought up a fine mud made of them from great depths. So, he argues, the chalk that covers south-eastern England was once the bed of a sea. From there Huxley reads the fossils in it, the layers above and below it, and the changes in its animals, and ends on evolution.

A few things are worth knowing in advance. His "Globigerinae" are foraminifera, single-celled animals with chalky shells, and his "coccoliths" are the plates of microscopic algae. His central claim still stands: the chalk is the hardened ooze of an ancient sea floor. Two details have changed. Most of the chalk's bulk turned out to be coccoliths rather than Globigerina shells, and the Chalk sea is now thought to have been a few hundred metres deep, not an ocean abyss like the Atlantic floor he compares it with. The deep-sea soundings he describes were new in 1868. Where he says "an immense lapse of time", modern dating puts the English chalk at roughly 100 to 66 million years old. His four footnotes are kept, set after the paragraphs that cite them.
