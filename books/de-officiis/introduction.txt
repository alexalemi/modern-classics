Cicero wrote this in the last months of 44 BC. Caesar had been stabbed to death in March, the Republic was breaking apart around him, and Cicero, born in 106 BC and now sixty-two, had just thrown himself back into politics with a series of speeches attacking Mark Antony. He lost. Late in 43 BC, with his name on a list of men to be killed, soldiers caught him near his villa and cut his throat. On Duties was finished about a year before that, and it is the last of his philosophical books; only the later Philippics, which are speeches, came after it.

It is a letter to his son. Marcus was twenty-one and in Athens, studying philosophy under Cratippus and, his father plainly suspects, not studying very hard. The opening pages tell him to work, to read his father's books as well as his teacher's, and to keep up his Latin. What follows is an ethics for a young man about to enter public life, built in three parts: Book One on what is right, Book Two on what is advantageous, Book Three on what to do when the two appear to collide. The answer to the third is that they never really do, and the whole book is the argument for it.

The argument runs on hard cases, which are the reason to read it. A grain merchant sails into famine-struck Rhodes knowing that more ships are close behind: must he say so before he sells? A man selling a house knows about the defects the buyer cannot see. A ring makes its wearer invisible, so that nothing he does can be found out. Regulus, released by the Carthaginians on his promise to return, goes back to be tortured to death. Cicero takes the Greek framework here from a lost book by Panaetius, but the examples and the pressure applied to them are his.

Two of his terms are doing technical work. Officium is duty, meaning whatever a person in a given situation is obliged to do, not a job. Honestum is the right, what is morally good in itself, and it is set throughout against utile, the useful or advantageous. The famous sentence is that we are not born for ourselves alone.

The book had an unusually long career as a school text. It was one of the first books printed in Europe after the Gutenberg Bible, and it was standard reading for the generation that founded the United States. John Adams quoted it for most of his life.

This edition is translated from Cicero's Latin, complete. His positions are left as he held them: Caesar is a tyrant and his killers are praised, Cicero defends his own execution of the Catilinarian conspirators, and slavery appears as an ordinary institution with rules attached. His other book from these same months, the Tusculan Disputations, is in this collection as a separate volume.
