The Sleepwalker’s Song

I

Meanwhile, however, one after another had stepped outside, into the open air and the cool, thoughtful night; but Zarathustra himself led the ugliest man by the hand, to show him his night-world and the great round moon and the silver waterfalls near his cave. There at last they stood still beside one another, nothing but old people, but with comforted, brave hearts, and wondering to themselves that they felt so well on earth; and the secrecy of the night came closer and closer to their hearts. And once again Zarathustra thought to himself: “Oh, how well I like them now, these higher men!” — but he did not say it aloud, for he honored their happiness and their silence. —

But then there happened the thing that was the most astonishing of that long, astonishing day: the ugliest man began once more, and for the last time, to gurgle and to snort, and when he had brought it as far as words, look, a question sprang round and clean from his mouth, a good, deep, clear question, which stirred the heart in the body of everyone who heard him.

“My friends, all of you,” said the ugliest man, “what do you think? For the sake of this day — *I* am content for the first time to have lived my whole life.

And that I bear witness to this much is still not enough for me. It is worth it to live on the earth: one day, one festival with Zarathustra has taught me to love the earth.

‘Was *that* life?’ I will say to death. ‘Well then! Once more!’

My friends, what do you think? Will you not say to death, as I do: Was that life? For Zarathustra’s sake, well then! Once more!” —

Thus spoke the ugliest man; but it was not long before midnight. And what do you suppose happened then? As soon as the higher men heard his question, they became aware all at once of their transformation and their recovery, and of who had given it to them: then they sprang toward Zarathustra, thanking him, revering him, caressing him, kissing his hands, each in the way that was his own: so that some laughed and some wept. But the old soothsayer danced with pleasure; and even if, as some storytellers think, he was full of sweet wine at the time, he was certainly fuller still of sweet life, and had renounced all weariness. There are even some who tell that the donkey danced then: for it was not for nothing that the ugliest man had given it wine to drink beforehand. This may have been so, or it may have been otherwise; and if in truth the donkey did not dance that evening, still greater and stranger wonders happened then than the dancing of a donkey would be. In short, as Zarathustra’s saying goes: “What does it matter!”

II

But Zarathustra, while this was happening with the ugliest man, stood there like a drunken man: his gaze went dim, his tongue stammered, his feet swayed. And who could guess what thoughts were running over Zarathustra’s soul just then? But plainly his spirit drew back and fled ahead and was in far distances and, as it were, “on a high ridge, as it is written, between two seas,

— wandering between the past and the future like a heavy cloud.” Gradually, however, while the higher men held him in their arms, he came back to himself a little, and with his hands he fended off the throng of those who revered him and worried about him; but he did not speak. All at once, however, he turned his head quickly, for he seemed to hear something: then he laid his finger to his lips and said: “Come!”

And at once all around it became still and secret; but from the depths the sound of a bell came slowly up. Zarathustra listened for it, as did the higher men; but then he laid his finger to his lips a second time and said again: “Come! Come! It is getting close to midnight!” — and his voice had changed. But still he did not stir from the spot: then it became stiller and more secret still, and everything listened, even the donkey, and Zarathustra’s animals of honor, the eagle and the serpent, and likewise Zarathustra’s cave and the great cool moon and the night itself. But Zarathustra laid his hand to his lips a third time and said:

Come! Come! Come! Let us wander now! It is the hour: let us wander into the night!

III

You higher men, it is getting close to midnight: so I want to say something into your ears, as that old bell says it into my ear, —

— as secretly, as terribly, as heartily as that midnight bell speaks it to me, which has lived through more than any one man:

— which already counted out the heartache-heartbeats of your fathers — ah! ah! how she sighs! how she laughs in her dream! the old, deep, deep midnight!

Hush! Hush! Then many things are heard that may not grow loud by day; but now, in the cool air, when all the noise of your hearts has grown still as well, —

— now it speaks, now it is heard, now it creeps into nocturnal, overwakeful souls: ah! ah! how she sighs! how she laughs in her dream!

— do you not hear how she speaks secretly, terribly, heartily to *you*, the old, deep, deep midnight? Oh man, attend!

IV

Woe is me! Where has the time gone? Did I not sink into deep wells? The world is asleep —

Ah! Ah! The dog howls, the moon shines. I would sooner die, die, than tell you what my midnight heart is thinking just now.

Now I have died already. It is over. Spider, what are you spinning around me? Do you want blood? Ah! Ah! The dew is falling, the hour is coming —

— the hour when I shiver and freeze, the hour that asks and asks and asks: “Who has heart enough for it?

— who shall be lord of the earth? Who will say: *this* is how you shall run, you great rivers and small!”

— the hour draws near: oh man, you higher man, attend! this speech is for fine ears, for your ears — what does the deep midnight portend?

V

It carries me away, my soul is dancing. Day’s work! Day’s work! Who shall be lord of the earth?

The moon is cool, the wind is silent. Ah! Ah! Have you flown high enough yet? You danced: but a leg is still no wing.

You good dancers, now all joy is over, wine has turned to dregs, every cup has grown brittle, the graves are stammering.

You did not fly high enough: now the graves stammer, “Redeem the dead, then! Why is the night so long? Does the moon not make us drunk?”

You higher men, redeem the graves, then, wake the corpses! Ah, what is the worm still digging for? It is near, the hour is drawing near, —

— the bell is booming, the heart is still rattling, the woodworm is still digging, the heart-worm. Ah! Ah! The world is deep!

VI

Sweet lyre! Sweet lyre! I love your tone, your drunken toad-call tone! — from how long ago, from how far away your tone comes to me, from far away, from the ponds of love!

You old bell, you sweet lyre! Every pain has torn into your heart, father-pain, fathers’-pain, forefathers’-pain; your speech has grown ripe, —

— ripe like golden autumn and afternoon, like my hermit’s heart — now you speak: the world itself has grown ripe, the grape is turning brown,

— now it wants to die, to die of happiness. You higher men, do you not smell it? A scent is secretly welling up,

— a fragrance and scent of eternity, a rose-blissful, brown, gold-wine scent of old happiness,

of drunken midnight-dying-happiness, which sings: the world is deep, and deeper than the day could comprehend!

VII

Leave me! Leave me! I am too pure for you. Do not touch me! Has my world not just now become perfect?

My skin is too pure for your hands. Leave me, you stupid, oafish, stuffy day! Is the midnight not brighter?

The purest shall be lords of the earth, the most unknown, the strongest, the midnight souls, who are brighter and deeper than any day.

Oh day, you grope for me? You fumble for my happiness? To you I am rich, solitary, a treasure pit, a chamber of gold?

Oh world, you want *me*? Am I worldly to you? Am I spiritual to you? Am I godlike to you? But day and world, you are too clumsy, —

— have shrewder hands, reach for deeper happiness, for deeper misfortune, reach for some god or other, do not reach for me:

— my misfortune, my happiness is deep, you strange day, but still I am no god, no god’s hell: deep is its woe.

VIII

God’s woe is deeper, you strange world! Reach for God’s woe, not for me! What am I! A drunken sweet lyre, —

a midnight lyre, a bell-toad that no one understands, but that *must* speak, before the deaf, you higher men! For you do not understand me!

Gone! Gone! Oh youth! Oh noon! Oh afternoon! Now evening has come, and night and midnight, — the dog howls, the wind:

— is the wind not a dog? It whimpers, it yaps, it howls. Ah! Ah! how she sighs! how she laughs, how she rattles and gasps, the midnight!

How soberly she speaks just now, this drunken poetess! has she drunk herself past her drunkenness? has she grown overwakeful? is she chewing the cud?

— her woe she chews over again, in her dream, the old, deep midnight, and her joy still more. For joy, even if woe is deep: joy is deeper still than misery.

IX

You vine! Why do you praise me? Did I not cut you! I am cruel, you are bleeding —: what does your praise of my drunken cruelty want?

“What has become perfect, everything ripe — wants to die!” so you speak. Blessed, blessed be the vintner’s knife! But everything unripe wants to live: woe!

Woe says: “Go! Away with you, woe!” But everything that suffers wants to live, so that it may become ripe and joyful and full of longing,

— longing for what is farther, higher, brighter. “I want heirs,” so speaks everything that suffers, “I want children, I do not want *myself*,” —

But joy does not want heirs, nor children, — joy wants itself, wants eternity, wants recurrence, wants everything-eternally-the-same.

Woe says: “Break, bleed, heart! Walk, leg! Wing, fly! Onward! Upward! Pain!” Well then! Up, then! Oh my old heart: woe says: “Go!”

X

You higher men, what do you think? Am I a soothsayer? A dreamer? A drunkard? An interpreter of dreams? A midnight bell?

A drop of dew? A haze and fragrance of eternity? Do you not hear it? Do you not smell it? Just now my world became perfect, midnight is also noon, —

pain is also a joy, a curse is also a blessing, night is also a sun, — go away, or you will learn: a wise man is also a fool.

Did you ever say Yes to a single joy? Oh, my friends, then you said Yes to *all* woe as well. All things are linked, laced, in love, —

— if you ever wanted one time twice, if you ever said: “You please me, happiness! Quick! Moment, remain!” then you wanted *everything* back again!

— everything anew, everything eternally, everything linked, laced, in love, oh, that is how you *loved* the world, —

— you eternal ones, love it eternally and for all time: and to woe, too, you say: go, but come back! For all joy wants — eternity!

XI

All joy wants the eternity of all things, wants honey, wants dregs, wants drunken midnight, wants graves, wants the comfort of tears at the grave, wants gilded sunset —

— *what* does joy not want! it is thirstier, heartier, hungrier, more terrible, more secret than all woe, it wants *itself*, it bites into *itself*, the ring’s will wrestles in it, —

— it wants love, it wants hatred, it is overrich, it gives, throws away, begs someone to take it, thanks the one who takes, it would gladly be hated, —

— so rich is joy that it thirsts for woe, for hell, for hatred, for disgrace, for the cripple, for *world*, — for this world, oh, you know it well enough!

You higher men, it is for you that it longs, joy, the unruly, the blissful, — for your woe, you who have turned out badly! All eternal joy longs for what has turned out badly.

For all joy wants itself, and so it wants misery too! Oh happiness, oh pain! Oh break, heart! You higher men, learn it, then: joy wants eternity,

— joy wants the eternity of *all* things, wants deep, deep eternity!

XII

Have you learned my song now? Have you guessed what it wants? Well then! Up, then! You higher men, now sing me my round!

Sing me the song yourselves now, the song whose name is “Once More,” whose meaning is “to all eternity!” — sing, you higher men, Zarathustra’s round!

	Oh man, attend!
	What does the deep midnight portend?
	“I slept, I slept —,
	From a deep dream I woke, at its end: —
	The world is deep,
	And deeper than the day could comprehend.
	Deep is its woe —,
	Joy — deeper still than misery:
	Woe says: Go!
	But all joy wants eternity
	wants deep, deep eternity!”
