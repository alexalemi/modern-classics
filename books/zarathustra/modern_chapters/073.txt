The Shadow

But scarcely had the voluntary beggar run off and Zarathustra was alone with himself again, when he heard a new voice behind him, calling, “Stop! Zarathustra! Wait for me! It’s me, oh Zarathustra, me, your shadow!” But Zarathustra did not wait, for a sudden annoyance came over him at all the crowding and thronging in his mountains. “Where has my solitude gone?” he said.

Truly, it is getting to be too much for me; these mountains are swarming, my kingdom is no longer of *this* world, I need new mountains.

My shadow is calling me? What does my shadow matter! Let him run after me! I — will run away from him.” —

Thus spoke Zarathustra to his heart, and ran away. But the one who was behind him followed after him: so that soon there were three runners one behind the other, namely the voluntary beggar in front, then Zarathustra, and third and hindmost his shadow. They had not run like this for long before Zarathustra came to his senses about his folly and with one jerk shook off all his annoyance and weariness.

“What!” he said, “have not the most ridiculous things always happened to us old hermits and saints?

Truly, my folly has grown tall in the mountains! Now I hear six old fools’ legs clattering along one behind the other!

But may Zarathustra really be afraid of a shadow? Besides, it seems to me, when all is said and done, that he has longer legs than I do.”

Thus spoke Zarathustra, laughing with his eyes and his bowels; he stopped and turned around quickly — and look, in doing so he almost threw his follower and shadow to the ground: so closely was the shadow already following on his heels, and so weak was he, too. For when Zarathustra looked him over with his eyes, he was startled as if by a sudden ghost: so thin, swarthy, hollow, and worn-out did this follower look.

“Who are you?” asked Zarathustra fiercely. “What are you doing here? And why do you call yourself my shadow? I do not like you.”

“Forgive me,” answered the shadow, “that it is I; and if you do not like me, well then, oh Zarathustra! in that I praise you and your good taste.

A wanderer I am, who has already walked a great deal behind your heels: always on the way, but without a goal, without a home too: so that truly I lack little of being the eternal Wandering Jew, except that I am not eternal, and not a Jew either.

What? Must I always be on the way? Whirled about by every wind, restless, driven on? Oh earth, you have grown too round for me!

I have already sat on every surface; like tired dust I have fallen asleep on mirrors and windowpanes: everything takes from me, nothing gives, I am growing thin, — I almost resemble a shadow.

But it was you, oh Zarathustra, whom I flew and trailed after longest; and though I hid from you, still I was your best shadow: wherever you sat, I sat too.

With you I haunted the farthest, coldest worlds, like a ghost that runs of its own free will over winter roofs and snow.

With you I strove into everything forbidden, everything worst, everything farthest: and if anything in me is virtue, it is that I had no fear of any prohibition.

With you I broke whatever my heart ever revered, I overturned all boundary stones and images, I ran after the most dangerous wishes, — truly, I once ran right over every crime there is.

With you I unlearned my faith in words and values and great names. When the devil sheds his skin, does his name not fall off too? For that too is skin. The devil himself is perhaps — skin.

“Nothing is true, everything is permitted”: so I told myself. I plunged into the coldest waters, head and heart. Ah, how often did I stand there afterward naked, red as a lobster!

Ah, where did all goodness go, and all shame, and all faith in the good! Ah, where is that lying innocence I once had, the innocence of the good and of their noble lies!

Too often, truly, I followed close on the heels of truth: then it kicked me in the head. Sometimes I thought I was lying, and look! only then did I hit upon — the truth.

Too much has become clear to me: now none of it concerns me anymore. Nothing lives anymore that I love, — how should I still love myself?

“To live as I please, or not to live at all”: that is how I want it, that is how the holiest man wants it too. But woe! what is there that still — pleases *me*?

Do *I* — still have a goal? A harbor toward which *my* sail is running?

A good wind? Ah, only the one who knows *where* he is sailing also knows which wind is good and is his fair wind.

What is left to me now? A heart weary and insolent; a restless will; fluttering wings; a broken backbone.

This hunting for *my* home: oh Zarathustra, do you know, this home-hunting was *my* haunting, it is eating me up.

“Where is — *my* home?” I ask and search and have searched for it, and I have not found it. Oh eternal everywhere, oh eternal nowhere, oh eternal — in vain!”

Thus spoke the shadow, and Zarathustra’s face grew long at his words. “You are my shadow!” he said at last, with sadness.

Your danger is no small one, you free spirit and wanderer! You have had a bad day: see to it that an even worse evening does not come to you!

To restless ones like you, in the end even a prison seems blissful. Have you ever seen how captured criminals sleep? They sleep peacefully, they enjoy their new security.

Beware that in the end a narrow faith does not capture you, a hard, strict delusion! For from now on anything that is narrow and firm seduces and tempts you.

You have lost your goal: woe, how will you laugh off and live down this loss? With it — you have lost your way as well!

You poor roamer and rover, you tired butterfly! Do you want a rest and a home for this evening? Then go up to my cave!

That way leads the path to my cave. And now I want to run away from you again, quickly. Already something lies on me like a shadow.

I want to run alone, so that it grows bright around me again. For that I must stay merrily on my legs a long while yet. But this evening at my place there will be — dancing!” —

Thus spoke Zarathustra.
