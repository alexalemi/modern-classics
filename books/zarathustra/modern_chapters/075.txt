The Welcome

It was not until late in the afternoon that Zarathustra, after long and useless searching and roaming about, came home to his cave again. But when he stood facing it, no more than twenty paces away, there happened what he now least expected: once again he heard the great *cry of distress*. And, astonishing! this time it came from his own cave. But it was a long, manifold, strange cry, and Zarathustra could clearly make out that it was made up of many voices: even though, heard from far away, it might sound like the cry from a single mouth.

Then Zarathustra sprang toward his cave, and look! what a sight for the eyes awaited him after that treat for the ears! For there they sat all together, the ones he had passed by during the day: the king on the right and the king on the left, the old magician, the pope, the voluntary beggar, the shadow, the conscientious one of the spirit, the sad soothsayer, and the donkey; but the ugliest man had put on a crown and wound two purple sashes around himself, — for, like all ugly people, he loved to dress up and play the beauty. But in the middle of this gloomy company stood Zarathustra’s eagle, ruffled and restless, for he was supposed to answer too many things for which his pride had no answer; and the shrewd serpent hung around his neck.

All this Zarathustra looked at in great amazement; then he examined each one of his guests with friendly curiosity, read their souls, and wondered again. Meanwhile the assembled company had risen from their seats and were waiting in reverence for Zarathustra to speak. But Zarathustra spoke like this:

“You despairing ones! You strange ones! So it was *your* cry of distress I heard? And now I also know where to look for the one I looked for in vain today: the higher man —:

— in my own cave he sits, the higher man! But why am I surprised! Did I not lure him to me myself, with honey sacrifices and the cunning calls of my happiness?

But it seems to me that you are poor company for one another, you make each other’s hearts sour, you who cry out in distress, when you sit here together? Someone has to come first,

— someone who will make you laugh again, a good merry clown, a dancer and a wind and a wild thing, some old fool: — what do you think?

But forgive me, you despairing ones, for talking to you in such small words, unworthy, truly!, of such guests! But you do not guess *what* makes my heart so mischievous: —

— you yourselves do it, and the sight of you, forgive me! For everyone grows brave who looks at a despairing man. To talk courage into a despairing man — everyone thinks himself strong enough for that.

You gave this strength to me myself, — a good gift, my high guests! A proper guest-gift! Well then, do not be angry now if I offer you something of mine too.

This here is my kingdom and my dominion: but what is mine shall be yours for this evening and this night. My animals shall serve you: let my cave be your resting place!

At home with me no one shall despair; in my territory I protect everyone from his wild beasts. And that is the first thing I offer you: safety!

But the second is: my little finger. And once you have *that*, go ahead and take the whole hand too! and the heart along with it! Welcome here, welcome, my guests and friends!”

Thus spoke Zarathustra, and laughed with love and malice. After this welcome his guests bowed once more and were silent in reverence; but the king on the right answered him in their name.

“By the way you offered us your hand and your greeting, oh Zarathustra, we recognize you as Zarathustra. You humbled yourself before us; you almost hurt our reverence —:

— but who could humble himself as you do, with such pride? *That* lifts us up ourselves; it is a refreshment to our eyes and hearts.

Just to see this, we would gladly climb higher mountains than this mountain. For we came as sightseers, we wanted to see what makes dim eyes bright.

And look, already all our cries of distress are over. Already our minds and hearts stand open and are enchanted. Little is missing: and our courage will turn to mischief.

Nothing more delightful grows on earth, oh Zarathustra, than a high, strong will: it is the earth’s most beautiful growth. A whole landscape is refreshed by one such tree.

I compare to the pine whoever grows up like you, oh Zarathustra: tall, silent, hard, alone, of the best and most supple wood, magnificent, —

— but in the end reaching out with strong green branches for *its* dominion, asking strong questions of the winds and the storms and whatever else is at home on the heights,

— answering more strongly, a commander, a victor: oh, who would not climb high mountains to see such growths?

By your tree here, oh Zarathustra, even the gloomy one, the failure, is refreshed; at the sight of you even the restless one grows steady and heals his heart.

And truly, many eyes today are turned toward your mountain and your tree; a great longing has set out, and some have learned to ask: who is Zarathustra?

And all into whose ears you ever dripped your song and your honey: all the hidden ones, those who live alone and those who live alone together, said all at once to their hearts:

‘Is Zarathustra still alive? It is no longer worth living, all is the same, all is in vain: or else — we must live with Zarathustra!’

‘Why does he not come, he who announced himself so long ago? so many ask; did solitude swallow him up? Or should we perhaps come to him?’

Now it is happening that solitude itself grows brittle and breaks open, like a grave that breaks open and can no longer hold its dead. Everywhere one sees the risen.

Now the waves are rising and rising around your mountain, oh Zarathustra. And however high your height may be, many must come up to you; your boat shall not sit on dry land much longer.

And that we despairing ones have now come into your cave and already despair no longer: it is only a token and an omen that better ones are on their way to you, —

— for he himself is on his way to you, the last remnant of God among men, that is: all the men of the great longing, of the great disgust, of the great weariness,

— all who do not want to live unless they learn to *hope* again — unless they learn from you, oh Zarathustra, the *great* hope!”

Thus spoke the king on the right, and took Zarathustra’s hand to kiss it; but Zarathustra fended off his veneration and stepped back in alarm, silent and sudden, as if fleeing into far distances. But after a little while he was back with his guests again, looked at them with clear, searching eyes, and said:

My guests, you higher men, I want to speak to you in plain German, and plainly. It was not for *you* that I was waiting here in these mountains.

(“Plain German, and plainly? God have mercy!” said the king on the left at this point, aside; “you can tell he does not know the dear Germans, this wise man from the East!

But he means ‘German, and gruffly’ — well then! These days that is not yet the worst taste!”)

“You may all be higher men, truly,” Zarathustra went on: “but for me — you are not high and strong enough.

For me, that means: for the inexorable thing in me that is silent, but will not always be silent. And if you do belong to me, it is still not as my right arm.

For whoever stands on sick and delicate legs himself, as you do, wants above all, whether he knows it or hides it from himself: to be *spared*.

But my arms and my legs I do not spare, I do not spare my warriors: how could you be any use in *my* war?

With you I would spoil every victory of mine. And some of you would fall over just from hearing the loud roll of my drums.

Nor are you beautiful enough for me, or well-born enough. I need pure, smooth mirrors for my teachings; on your surface even my own image is distorted.

Many a burden presses on your shoulders, many a memory; many a nasty dwarf crouches in your corners. There is a hidden mob in you too.

And even if you are high and of a higher kind: much in you is crooked and misshapen. There is no smith in the world who could hammer you right and straight for me.

You are only bridges: may higher ones stride across over you! You stand for steps: so do not be angry with the one who climbs over you into *his* height!

From your seed a true son and perfect heir may one day grow for me too: but that is far off. You yourselves are not the ones to whom my inheritance and name belong.

It is not for you that I am waiting here in these mountains, it is not with you that I may go down for the last time. You came to me only as an omen that higher ones are already on their way to me, —

— *not* the men of the great longing, of the great disgust, of the great weariness, and what you called the remains of God.

— No! No! Three times no! It is for *others* that I am waiting here in these mountains, and I will not lift my foot from here without them,

— for higher ones, stronger ones, more victorious ones, more cheerful ones, ones who are built square in body and soul: *laughing lions* must come!

Oh my guests and friends, you strange ones, — have you heard nothing yet of my children? And that they are on their way to me?

Do speak to me of my gardens, of my Blessed Isles, of my new, beautiful kind, — why do you not speak to me of them?

This guest-gift I ask of your love, that you speak to me of my children. For this I am rich, for this I became poor: what have I not given away,

— what would I not give away to have one thing: *these* children, *this* living plantation, *these* trees of life of my will and of my highest hope!”

Thus spoke Zarathustra, and suddenly broke off his speech: for his longing came over him, and he closed his eyes and his mouth against the turmoil of his heart. And all his guests too were silent and stood still and dismayed: except that the old soothsayer made signs with his hands and gestures.
