The Child with the Mirror

After this Zarathustra went back into the mountains and into the solitude of his cave, and withdrew from people, waiting like a sower who has scattered his seed. But his soul grew full of impatience and of longing for those he loved: for he still had much to give them. For this is the hardest thing of all: to close the open hand out of love, and to keep a sense of shame as a giver.

So months and years passed for the solitary one; but his wisdom grew, and it caused him pain with its fullness.

But one morning he woke even before the dawn, lay thinking for a long time on his bed, and at last spoke to his heart:

Why was I so frightened in my dream that I woke up? Did not a child come to me, carrying a mirror?

“Oh Zarathustra,” the child said to me, “look at yourself in the mirror!”

But when I looked into the mirror, I cried out, and my heart was shaken: for it was not myself I saw there, but a devil’s grimace and sneering laughter.

Truly, I understand the dream’s sign and warning all too well: my *teaching* is in danger; weeds want to be called wheat!

My enemies have grown powerful and have distorted the image of my teaching, so that those dearest to me must be ashamed of the gifts I gave them.

I have lost my friends; the hour has come for me to seek my lost ones! —

With these words Zarathustra sprang up, but not like a frightened man gasping for air — rather like a seer and a singer whom the spirit seizes. His eagle and his serpent looked at him in wonder: for a coming happiness lay on his face like the dawn.

What has happened to me, my animals? — said Zarathustra. Am I not transformed? Did bliss not come to me like a whirlwind?

My happiness is foolish, and it will say foolish things: it is still too young — so be patient with it!

I am wounded by my happiness: let all who suffer be my physicians!

I may go down to my friends again, and to my enemies too! Zarathustra may speak and give again, and do the dearest thing for those he loves!

My impatient love overflows in torrents, downward, toward the sunrise and the sunset. Out of silent mountains and thunderstorms of pain my soul rushes roaring into the valleys.

Too long I longed and gazed into the distance. Too long I belonged to solitude: so I have forgotten how to be silent.

I have become mouth through and through, and the roar of a brook from high cliffs: I want to hurl my speech down into the valleys.

And let my torrent of love plunge where there is no path! How could a torrent not find its way to the sea at last!

There is a lake in me, it is true, a hermit lake, sufficient to itself; but my torrent of love sweeps it along with it, down — to the sea!

I walk new ways, a new speech comes to me; like all creators, I have grown tired of the old tongues. My spirit no longer wants to walk on worn-out soles.

All speaking runs too slowly for me: — into your chariot I leap, storm! And even you I will whip with my malice!

Like a cry and a shout of joy I will sail over wide seas, until I find the Blessed Isles where my friends dwell: —

And my enemies among them! How I love everyone now to whom I may simply speak! My enemies too belong to my bliss.

And when I want to mount my wildest horse, it is always my spear that helps me up best: it is my foot’s ever-ready servant: —

The spear that I hurl at my enemies! How grateful I am to my enemies that at last I may hurl it!

The tension in my cloud was too great: between the laughter of the lightning I will throw showers of hail into the depths.

Mightily my chest will heave then, mightily it will blow its storm over the mountains: so it will find relief.

Truly, my happiness and my freedom come like a storm! But my enemies shall believe that *the evil one* is raging over their heads.

Yes, you too, my friends, will be frightened by my wild wisdom; and perhaps you will run away from it, along with my enemies.

Ah, if only I knew how to lure you back with shepherds’ flutes! Ah, if only my lioness Wisdom would learn to roar tenderly! And we have already learned much together!

My wild wisdom grew pregnant on lonely mountains; on rough stones she gave birth to her young, her youngest.

Now she runs foolishly through the hard desert, and seeks and seeks for soft grass — my old wild wisdom!

On the soft grass of your hearts, my friends! — on your love she would like to bed her dearest one!

Thus spoke Zarathustra.
