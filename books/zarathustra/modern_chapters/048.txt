On the Vision and the Riddle

I

When word spread among the sailors that Zarathustra was on the ship — for a man who came from the Blessed Isles had come on board with him — there arose a great curiosity and expectation. But Zarathustra kept silent for two days and was cold and deaf with sadness, so that he answered neither looks nor questions. On the evening of the second day, however, he opened his ears again, though he still kept silent: for there was much that was strange and dangerous to listen to on this ship, which came from far away and wanted to go farther still. And Zarathustra was a friend of all those who make long voyages and do not like to live without danger. And look! at last, as he listened, his own tongue was loosened, and the ice of his heart broke: — then he began to speak like this:

To you, the bold seekers and tempters, and whoever has ever set out with cunning sails on terrible seas, —

to you who are drunk on riddles, who delight in twilight, whose souls are lured by flutes into every maze and gulf:

— for you do not want to grope along a thread with a cowardly hand; and where you can *guess*, you hate to *deduce* —

to you alone I tell the riddle that I *saw*, — the vision of the most solitary one. —

Gloomily I walked not long ago through the corpse-colored twilight, — gloomily and hard, with pressed lips. Not only one sun had gone under for me.

A path that climbed defiantly through the scree, a spiteful, solitary path, cheered on by neither herb nor bush any longer: a mountain path crunched under the defiance of my foot.

Striding silently over the mocking clatter of pebbles, crushing the stone that made it slip: so my foot forced its way upward.

Upward: — in spite of the spirit that pulled it downward, pulled it toward the abyss, the spirit of gravity, my devil and archenemy.

Upward: — although he sat on me, half dwarf, half mole; lame; laming; dripping lead into my ear, leaden-drop thoughts into my brain.

“Oh Zarathustra,” he whispered mockingly, syllable by syllable, “you stone of wisdom! You threw yourself high, but every stone that is thrown must — fall!

Oh Zarathustra, you stone of wisdom, you sling-stone, you star-smasher! You threw yourself so high, — but every stone that is thrown — must fall!

Condemned to yourself and to your own stoning: oh Zarathustra, far indeed you threw the stone, — but it will fall back on *you*!”

Then the dwarf fell silent; and it lasted a long time. But his silence weighed on me; and to be two in this way is, truly, lonelier than to be one!

I climbed, I climbed, I dreamed, I thought, — but everything weighed on me. I was like a sick man worn out by his cruel torment, who is woken again from his falling asleep by a still crueler dream. —

But there is something in me that I call courage: so far it has struck dead every discouragement in me. This courage at last bade me stand still and say: “Dwarf! You! Or I!” —

For courage is the best killer, — courage that *attacks*: for in every attack there is the sound of drums and trumpets.

But man is the most courageous animal: with that he has overcome every animal. With the sound of drums and trumpets he has overcome every pain as well; but human pain is the deepest pain.

Courage strikes dead even dizziness at the edge of abysses: and where does man not stand at the edge of abysses! Is seeing itself not — seeing abysses?

Courage is the best killer: courage strikes dead even pity. But pity is the deepest abyss: as deeply as man sees into life, so deeply does he see into suffering.

But courage is the best killer, courage that attacks: it strikes dead even death, for it says: “Was *that* life? Well then! Once more!”

In such a saying, though, there is much sound of drums and trumpets. Whoever has ears, let him hear. —

II

“Stop! Dwarf!” I said. “I! Or you! But I am the stronger of us two —: you do not know my abysmal thought! *That* — you could not bear!” —

Then something happened that made me lighter: for the dwarf jumped down from my shoulder, the curious creature! And he squatted on a stone in front of me. But just there, where we had stopped, stood a gateway.

“Look at this gateway, dwarf!” I went on. “It has two faces. Two paths come together here: no one has ever yet walked either of them to its end.

This long lane back here: it lasts an eternity. And that long lane out there — that is another eternity.

They contradict each other, these paths; they run head-on into each other: — and it is here, at this gateway, that they come together. The gateway’s name is written above it: ‘Moment.’

But if someone were to walk on along one of them — and on and on, ever farther: do you believe, dwarf, that these paths contradict each other eternally?” —

“Everything straight lies,” the dwarf muttered contemptuously. “All truth is crooked; time itself is a circle.”

“You spirit of gravity!” I said angrily, “do not take this too lightly! Or I will leave you squatting where you squat, lamefoot, — and I carried you *high*!

Look,” I went on, “at this moment! From this gateway, Moment, a long eternal lane runs *backward*: behind us lies an eternity.

Must not whatever of all things *can* run have already run along this lane once before? Must not whatever of all things *can* happen have already happened once before, been done, run past?

And if everything has already been here before: what do you think, dwarf, of this moment? Must not this gateway too have — been here before?

And are not all things knotted together so tightly that this moment draws *all* coming things after it? *And so* — itself as well?

For whatever of all things *can* run: in this long lane *out there* too — it *must* run once more! —

And this slow spider that creeps in the moonlight, and this moonlight itself, and I and you in the gateway, whispering together, whispering of eternal things — must we not all have been here before?

— and come again and run in that other lane, out there, before us, in this long, eerie lane — must we not come again eternally?” —

So I spoke, more and more softly: for I was afraid of my own thoughts, and of the thoughts behind my thoughts. Then, suddenly, I heard a dog *howl* nearby.

Had I ever heard a dog howl like that? My thoughts ran back. Yes! When I was a child, in my most distant childhood:

— then I heard a dog howl like that. And I saw it too, its hair bristling, its head raised, trembling, in the stillest midnight, when even dogs believe in ghosts:

— so that I felt sorry for it. For just then the full moon, silent as death, was passing over the house; just then it stood still, a round glow, — still on the flat roof, as if on someone else’s property: —

that was what had terrified the dog then: for dogs believe in thieves and ghosts. And when I heard such howling again, I felt sorry for it once more.

Where now was the dwarf? And the gateway? And the spider? And all the whispering? Was I dreaming, then? Was I waking up? All at once I stood among wild cliffs, alone, desolate, in the most desolate moonlight.

But there lay a man! And there! The dog, leaping, bristling, whining, — now it saw me coming — then it howled again, then it *screamed*: — had I ever heard a dog scream for help like that?

And truly, what I saw, I had never seen the like of. I saw a young shepherd, writhing, choking, twitching, his face distorted, and a heavy black serpent was hanging out of his mouth.

Had I ever seen so much disgust and pale horror on one face? He must have been asleep? Then the serpent crawled into his throat — and there it bit fast.

My hand pulled at the serpent and pulled: — in vain! it could not pull the serpent out of his throat. Then it cried out of me: “Bite! Bite down!

Its head off! Bite!” — so it cried out of me, my horror, my hatred, my disgust, my mercy, all my good and my bad cried out of me in a single cry. —

You bold ones around me! You seekers and tempters, and whoever of you has set out with cunning sails on unexplored seas! You who delight in riddles!

Then guess me the riddle that I saw back then, interpret for me the vision of the most solitary one!

For it was a vision and a foreseeing: — *what* did I see back then in a parable? And *who* is it that must one day still come?

*Who* is the shepherd into whose throat the serpent crawled like this? *Who* is the man into whose throat everything heaviest, blackest will crawl like this?

— But the shepherd bit, as my cry had advised him; he bit with a good bite! Far away he spat the serpent’s head —: and sprang up. —

No longer shepherd, no longer man, — one transformed, surrounded with light, who *laughed*! Never yet on earth has a man laughed as *he* laughed!

Oh my brothers, I heard a laughter that was no human laughter, — and now a thirst gnaws at me, a longing that is never stilled.

My longing for this laughter gnaws at me: oh, how can I bear to go on living! And how could I bear to die now! —

Thus spoke Zarathustra.
