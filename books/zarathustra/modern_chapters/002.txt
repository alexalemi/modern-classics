On the Professors of Virtue

Zarathustra heard people praise a wise man who was said to speak well about sleep and about virtue: he was greatly honored and rewarded for it, and all the young men sat before his professor’s chair. Zarathustra went to him, and he sat before his chair with all the young men. And the wise man spoke like this:

Honor sleep, and be bashful before it! That is the first thing! And stay out of the way of all who sleep badly and lie awake at night!

Even the thief is bashful before sleep: he always steals softly through the night. But the night watchman is shameless; shamelessly he carries his horn.

Sleeping is no small art: you have to stay awake all day for it.

Ten times a day you must overcome yourself: that makes for a good tiredness, and it is poppy for the soul.

Ten times you must reconcile yourself with yourself again; for overcoming is bitterness, and the unreconciled sleep badly.

Ten truths you must find in a day: otherwise you will still be searching for truth at night, and your soul will have stayed hungry.

Ten times a day you must laugh and be cheerful: otherwise your stomach, that father of gloom, will disturb you in the night.

Few people know it: but you must have all the virtues in order to sleep well. Shall I bear false witness? Shall I commit adultery?

Shall I covet my neighbor’s maidservant? None of that would go well with good sleep.

And even when you have all the virtues, there is still one more thing you must know how to do: send the virtues themselves to sleep at the right time.

So that they do not quarrel with one another, the nice little women! And over you, you unhappy wretch!

Peace with God and with your neighbor: that is what good sleep wants. And peace even with your neighbor’s devil! Otherwise he will haunt you at night.

Honor the authorities and obey them, even the crooked authorities! That is what good sleep wants. How can I help it if power likes to walk on crooked legs?

I will always call him the best shepherd who leads his sheep to the greenest pasture: that is what goes well with good sleep.

I do not want many honors, or great treasures: that inflames the spleen. But it is hard to sleep well without a good name and a little treasure.

A little company is more welcome to me than bad company: but it must come and go at the right time. That is what goes well with good sleep.

I am also very fond of the poor in spirit: they promote sleep. Blessed are they, especially if you always tell them they are right.

That is how the day passes for the virtuous man. And when night comes, I take good care not to summon sleep! Sleep, who is the master of the virtues, does not want to be summoned!

Instead I think over what I did and thought during the day. Chewing it over, patient as a cow, I ask myself: what, then, were your ten overcomings?

And what were the ten reconciliations, and the ten truths, and the ten laughs with which my heart treated itself?

As I weigh such things, rocked by forty thoughts, sleep suddenly overtakes me, the unsummoned one, the master of the virtues.

Sleep taps on my eyes: and they grow heavy. Sleep touches my mouth: and it stays open.

Truly, on soft soles he comes to me, the dearest of thieves, and steals my thoughts from me: I stand there as stupid as this chair.

But I do not stand there for long: I am already lying down. —

When Zarathustra heard the wise man speak like this, he laughed in his heart: for a light had dawned on him. And he spoke to his heart like this:

This wise man with his forty thoughts is a fool to me: but I do believe he knows all about sleeping.

Happy already is anyone who lives near this wise man! Sleep like that is catching; it is catching even through a thick wall.

There is a magic even in his professor’s chair. And it was not for nothing that the young men sat before this preacher of virtue.

His wisdom says: stay awake in order to sleep well. And truly, if life had no meaning and I had to choose nonsense, this would be the nonsense most worth choosing for me too.

Now I understand clearly what people once looked for above all when they looked for teachers of virtue. Good sleep is what they looked for, and poppy-flowered virtues to go with it!

To all these much-praised wise men of the lecture chairs, wisdom was sleep without dreams: they knew no better meaning of life.

Even today there may well be a few like this preacher of virtue, and not always so honest: but their time is up. And they will not stand for much longer: they are already lying down.

Blessed are the sleepy ones: for they shall soon nod off. —

Thus spoke Zarathustra.
