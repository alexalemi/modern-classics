The Voluntary Beggar

When Zarathustra had left the ugliest man, he was cold, and he felt lonely: for much that was cold and lonely went through his senses, so that his limbs too grew colder. But as he climbed on and on, up, down, now past green pastures, but also over wild, stony beds where an impatient brook had probably once gone to bed: — then all at once he felt warmer and more cheerful at heart again.

“What has happened to me?” he asked himself. “Something warm and living refreshes me; it must be near me.

Already I am less alone; unwitting companions and brothers roam around me, their warm breath touches my soul.”

But when he peered about and searched for the comforters of his solitude: look, they were cows, standing together on a hilltop; their nearness and their smell had warmed his heart. These cows, though, seemed to be listening eagerly to someone speaking, and paid no attention to the one who was coming near. But when Zarathustra was quite close to them, he heard clearly that a human voice was speaking from the middle of the cows; and plainly they had all turned their heads toward the speaker.

Then Zarathustra sprang eagerly up the slope and pushed the animals apart, for he feared that some harm had come to someone here, which the pity of cows could hardly help. But in this he was mistaken; for look, there sat a man on the ground, who seemed to be urging the animals not to be afraid of him, a peaceable man and preacher on the mount, out of whose eyes goodness itself was preaching. “What are you looking for here?” cried Zarathustra in surprise.

“What am I looking for here?” he answered. “The same thing you are looking for, you troublemaker! Namely, happiness on earth.

But for that I want to learn from these cows. For, you know, I have been talking to them for half a morning already, and they were just about to give me their answer. Why do you have to disturb them?

Unless we turn and become like cows, we will never enter the kingdom of heaven. For there is one thing we ought to learn from them: chewing the cud.

And truly, if a man gained the whole world and did not learn this one thing, chewing the cud: what good would it do him! He would not be rid of his gloom

— his great gloom: but today it is called *disgust*. Who today does not have his heart, mouth, and eyes full of disgust? You too! You too! But just look at these cows!” —

Thus spoke the preacher on the mount, and then turned his own gaze on Zarathustra — for until then it had clung lovingly to the cows —: but then he was transformed. “Who is this I am talking to?” he cried in alarm, and sprang up from the ground.

This is the man without disgust, this is Zarathustra himself, the overcomer of the great disgust, this is the eye, this is the mouth, this is the heart of Zarathustra himself.”

And as he spoke like this, he kissed the hands of the one he was speaking to, with overflowing eyes, and behaved exactly like someone on whom a precious gift and jewel has fallen unexpectedly from heaven. But the cows looked on at all of this and wondered.

“Do not speak of me, you strange one! you lovely one!” said Zarathustra, warding off his tenderness, “speak to me first of yourself! Are you not the voluntary beggar who once threw away great riches, —

— who was ashamed of his riches and of the rich, and fled to the poorest, to give them his abundance and his heart? But they did not accept him.”

“But they did not accept me,” said the voluntary beggar, “you know that already. So in the end I went to the animals and to these cows.”

“There you learned,” Zarathustra interrupted the speaker, “how it is harder to give rightly than to take rightly, and that giving well is an *art*, and the last and most cunning master-art of goodness.”

“Especially nowadays,” answered the voluntary beggar: “today, that is, when everything low has become rebellious and shy and haughty in its own way: in the way of the mob, that is.

For the hour has come, you know it already, for the great, grim, long, slow uprising of the mob and the slaves: it grows and grows!

Now all charity and petty giving-away outrages the lowly; and the overrich had better be on their guard!

Whoever today drips like a big-bellied bottle out of a far too narrow neck: — people today like nothing better than to break the necks of such bottles.

Lustful greed, bilious envy, sullen vengefulness, mob pride: all of it leaped into my face. It is no longer true that the poor are blessed. But the kingdom of heaven is among the cows.”

“And why is it not among the rich?” asked Zarathustra, to tempt him, while he warded off the cows, which were snuffling trustingly at the peaceable one.

“Why do you tempt me?” the other answered. “You know it yourself even better than I do. What drove me to the poorest, oh Zarathustra? Was it not disgust at our richest?

— at the convicts of riches, who pick their profit out of every rubbish heap, with cold eyes and lecherous thoughts, at this rabble that stinks to high heaven,

— at this gilded, falsified mob, whose fathers were pickpockets, or carrion birds, or ragpickers, with wives who were willing, lustful, forgetful: — for none of them has far to go to be a whore —

Mob above, mob below! What do “poor” and “rich” mean anymore today! I unlearned that difference, — and so I fled, farther, ever farther, until I came to these cows.”

Thus spoke the peaceable one, and he himself snorted and sweated as he spoke: so that the cows wondered all over again. But Zarathustra kept looking him in the face with a smile while he talked so harshly, and silently shook his head.

“You are doing yourself violence, you preacher on the mount, when you use such hard words. Your mouth did not grow for such hardness, nor your eye.

Nor, it seems to me, your stomach itself: *it* turns against all such raging and hating and frothing over. Your stomach wants gentler things: you are no butcher.

Rather, you seem to me a plant-eater and a root-man. Perhaps you grind grain. But surely you are averse to the pleasures of the flesh, and you love honey.”

“You have guessed me well,” answered the voluntary beggar, with a lighter heart. “I love honey, I grind grain too, for I sought what tastes sweet and makes the breath pure:

— and also what takes a long time, a day’s work and a jaw’s work for gentle idlers and day-wasters.

These cows, of course, have gone furthest of all: they invented chewing the cud and lying in the sun for themselves. And they abstain from all heavy thoughts, which bloat the heart.”

“— Well then!” said Zarathustra: “you ought to see *my* animals too, my eagle and my serpent, — there are none like them on earth today.

Look, that way leads the path to my cave: be its guest tonight. And talk with my animals about the happiness of animals, —

— until I come home myself. For now a cry of distress calls me away from you in haste. You will also find new honey at my place, ice-fresh golden honey in the comb: eat it!

But now take leave of your cows, quickly, you strange one! you lovely one! however hard it may be for you. For they are your warmest friends and teachers!” —

“— All but one, whom I love even more,” answered the voluntary beggar. “You yourself are good, and better even than a cow, oh Zarathustra!”

“Away, away with you! you wicked flatterer!” cried Zarathustra with malice, “why do you spoil me with such praise and flattery-honey?”

“Away, away from me!” he cried once more, and swung his stick at the tender beggar: but the beggar ran nimbly away.
