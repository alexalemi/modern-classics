The Wanderer

It was midnight when Zarathustra set out over the ridge of the island, so that he would reach the other shore by early morning: for there he meant to board a ship. There was a good harbor there, where foreign ships too liked to drop anchor; and they took along many who wanted to cross the sea from the Blessed Isles. Now as Zarathustra climbed the mountain like this, he thought along the way of all his solitary wandering since his youth, and of how many mountains and ridges and summits he had already climbed.

I am a wanderer and a mountain climber, he said to his heart; I do not love the plains, and it seems I cannot sit still for long.

And whatever may still come to me as fate and experience — there will be wandering in it, and mountain climbing: in the end one experiences only oneself.

The time has run out when accidents were still allowed to befall me; and what *could* still fall to me now that was not already my own!

It only returns, it finally comes home to me — my own Self, and whatever of it has long been abroad and scattered among all things and accidents.

And I know one thing more: I am standing now before my last summit, and before what has been saved up for me the longest. Ah, I must climb my hardest path! Ah, I have begun my most solitary wandering!

But whoever is of my kind does not escape such an hour: the hour that says to him: “Only now are you going your way of greatness! Summit and abyss — they are now joined into one!

You are going your way of greatness: what until now was called your last danger has now become your last refuge!

You are going your way of greatness: now this must be your best courage, that there is no longer any path behind you!

You are going your way of greatness; here no one shall sneak after you! Your own foot has wiped out the path behind you, and above it is written: Impossibility.

And if from now on you have no ladders left, then you must know how to climb up on your own head: how else would you climb upward?

Up on your own head and away over your own heart! Now the gentlest thing in you must become the hardest.

Whoever has always spared himself much grows sick at last from all his sparing. Praised be what makes hard! I do not praise the land where butter and honey — flow!

Learning to *look away* from oneself is necessary in order to see *much*: — every mountain climber needs this hardness.

But whoever, as a seeker of knowledge, is pushy with his eyes — how could he see more of any thing than its foreground!

But you, oh Zarathustra, wanted to see the ground of all things, and their background: so you must climb up over yourself — upward, higher, until even your stars are *under* you!

Yes! To look down on myself and even on my stars: that alone would I call my *summit*, that has remained for me as my *last* summit! —”

Thus spoke Zarathustra to himself as he climbed, comforting his heart with hard little sayings: for he was sore at heart as never before. And when he reached the top of the ridge, look, there the other sea lay spread out before him: and he stood still and was silent for a long time. But the night was cold at this height, and clear, and bright with stars.

I recognize my lot, he said at last, with sadness. Well then! I am ready. Now my last solitude has begun.

Ah, this black, sad sea beneath me! Ah, this pregnant, nighttime sullenness! Ah, fate and sea! To you I must now climb *down*!

I stand before my highest mountain and before my longest wandering: that is why I must first go down deeper than I ever climbed:

— deeper down into pain than I ever climbed, right into its blackest flood! So my fate wills it: Well then! I am ready.

Where do the highest mountains come from? I once asked. Then I learned that they come out of the sea.

This testimony is written in their rock and in the walls of their summits. It is out of the deepest that the highest must come to its height. —

Thus spoke Zarathustra on the peak of the mountain, where it was cold; but when he came near the sea and at last stood alone among the cliffs, he had grown weary on the way, and more full of longing than ever before.

Everything is still asleep now, he said; even the sea is asleep. Drunk with sleep and strange, its eye gazes at me.

But its breath is warm, I can feel that. And I feel too that it is dreaming. It writhes, dreaming, on hard pillows.

Listen! Listen! How it groans with evil memories! Or with evil expectations?

Ah, I am sad along with you, you dark monster, and angry with myself on your account.

Ah, that my hand does not have strength enough! Gladly, truly, would I redeem you from evil dreams! —

And as Zarathustra spoke like this, he laughed at himself with melancholy and bitterness. “What! Zarathustra!” he said, “do you even want to sing comfort to the sea?

Ah, you loving fool Zarathustra, you who are overblissful with trust! But you were always like this: you always came trustingly up to everything terrible.

Every monster you still wanted to stroke. A breath of warm air, a little soft shagginess on the paw —: and at once you were ready to love it and to coax it.

*Love* is the danger of the most solitary one, love for everything, if only it is alive! Truly, my folly and my modesty in love are laughable!” —

Thus spoke Zarathustra, and laughed a second time as he did; but then he thought of the friends he had left behind —, and as if he had wronged them with his thoughts, he was angry with himself for his thoughts. And soon it happened that the one who laughed was weeping: — with anger and longing Zarathustra wept bitterly.
