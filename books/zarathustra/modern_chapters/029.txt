On the Rabble

Life is a fountain of joy; but where the rabble drinks too, all wells are poisoned.

I am fond of everything clean; but I do not like to see the grinning mouths and the thirst of the unclean.

They cast their eye down into the well: now their repulsive smile gleams up at me out of the well.

They have poisoned the holy water with their lechery; and when they called their dirty dreams joy, they poisoned even the words.

The flame turns sullen when they lay their damp hearts on the fire; the spirit itself bubbles and smokes wherever the rabble steps up to the fire.

In their hands the fruit turns sickly sweet and overripe: their gaze makes the fruit tree windfallen and withered at the crown.

And many a man who turned away from life turned away only from the rabble: he did not want to share well and flame and fruit with the rabble.

And many a man who went into the desert and suffered thirst among beasts of prey only did not want to sit around the cistern with filthy camel drivers.

And many a man who came along like a destroyer, and like a hailstorm to every field of grain, only wanted to set his foot in the rabble’s jaws and so stop up its throat.

And the mouthful that choked me most was not knowing that life itself needs enmity and dying and crosses of torture: —

But rather, I once asked, and almost suffocated on my question: what? Does life *need* the rabble too?

Are poisoned wells needed, and stinking fires, and soiled dreams, and maggots in the bread of life?

Not my hatred but my disgust gnawed hungrily at my life! Ah, I often grew weary of the spirit when I found even the rabble rich in spirit!

And I turned my back on the rulers when I saw what they now call ruling: haggling and bargaining for power — with the rabble!

I lived among peoples with a foreign tongue, with my ears shut: so that the tongue of their haggling and their bargaining for power would stay foreign to me.

And holding my nose, I walked in ill humor through all of yesterday and today: truly, all of yesterday and today stinks of the scribbling rabble!

Like a cripple who has gone deaf and blind and dumb: that is how I lived for a long time, so as not to live with the power-rabble and the scribble-rabble and the pleasure-rabble.

My spirit climbed stairs with effort, and with caution; alms of joy were its refreshment; life crept along on a stick for the blind man.

What was it, then, that happened to me? How did I redeem myself from disgust? Who made my eye young again? How did I fly up to the height where no rabble sits at the well anymore?

Did my disgust itself create wings for me, and powers that can divine where springs are hidden? Truly, I had to fly to the very highest to find the fountain of joy again!

Oh, I have found it, my brothers! Here in the highest the fountain of joy wells up for me! And there is a life the rabble does not drink from too!

You flow almost too violently for me, spring of joy! And often you empty the cup again by wanting to fill it!

And I must still learn to approach you more modestly: my heart still flows toward you all too violently: —

My heart, on which my summer burns, my short, hot, melancholy, overblissful summer: how my summer heart longs for your coolness!

Gone, the lingering gloom of my spring! Over, the malice of my snowflakes in June! I have become summer entirely, and summer noon!

A summer in the highest, with cold springs and blissful stillness: oh come, my friends, so that the stillness may become more blissful still! For this is *our* height and our home: we live here too high and too steep for all the unclean and their thirst. Just cast your pure eyes into the fountain of my joy, you friends! How could that make it cloudy? It shall laugh back at you with *its* purity.

On the tree called Future we build our nest; eagles shall bring food in their beaks to us solitary ones!

Truly, no food that the unclean would be allowed to share! They would think they were eating fire, and burn their mouths!

Truly, we keep no homes ready here for the unclean! Our happiness would be an ice cave to their bodies and to their spirits!

And like strong winds we want to live above them, neighbors to the eagles, neighbors to the snow, neighbors to the sun: that is how strong winds live.

And like a wind I will one day blow among them, and with my spirit take the breath from their spirit: that is what my future wills.

Truly, Zarathustra is a strong wind to all lowlands; and this advice he advises to his enemies and to everything that spits and spews: beware of spitting *against* the wind!

Thus spoke Zarathustra.
