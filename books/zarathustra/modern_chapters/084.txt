The Sign

But on the morning after this night Zarathustra sprang up from his bed, girded his loins, and came out of his cave, glowing and strong, like a morning sun that comes out of dark mountains.

“You great star,” he said, as he had once spoken before, “you deep eye of happiness, what would all your happiness be, if you did not have *those* you shine for!

And if they stayed in their rooms while you are already awake and come and give and hand out: how your proud shame would be angered at that!

Well then! they are still asleep, these higher men, while *I* am awake: *these* are not my true companions! It is not for them that I am waiting here in my mountains.

I want to go to my work, to my day: but they do not understand what the signs of my morning are; my step — is no wake-up call for them.

They are still asleep in my cave, their dream is still chewing on my midnights. The ear that hears *me*, — the *heeding* ear is missing from their limbs.”

— Zarathustra had spoken this to his heart as the sun rose: then he looked up questioningly, for above him he heard the sharp cry of his eagle. “Well then!” he called up, “so it pleases me and so it befits me. My animals are awake, for I am awake.

My eagle is awake and, like me, honors the sun. With eagle’s talons he reaches for the new light. You are my true animals; I love you.

But I still lack my true men!” —

Thus spoke Zarathustra; but then it happened that he suddenly heard himself swarmed and fluttered around as if by countless birds, — but the whirring of so many wings and the crowding around his head was so great that he shut his eyes. And truly, it fell upon him like a cloud, like a cloud of arrows that pours itself out over a new enemy. But look, here it was a cloud of love, and over a new friend.

“What is happening to me?” thought Zarathustra in his astonished heart, and he slowly sat down on the great stone that lay beside the mouth of his cave. But as he reached with his hands around him and above him and below him, fending off the tender birds, look, something stranger still happened to him: for in doing so he reached, without noticing, into a thick, warm, shaggy mane; and at the same moment a roar rang out in front of him, — a soft, long lion’s roar.

“The sign is coming,” said Zarathustra, and his heart was transformed. And in truth, when it grew light before him, there lay at his feet a mighty yellow beast, and it pressed its head against his knees and would not leave him, out of love, and acted like a dog that finds its old master again. But the doves were no less eager with their love than the lion; and every time a dove flitted across the lion’s nose, the lion shook its head and wondered and laughed at it.

To all this Zarathustra said only one word: “My children are near, my children” —, then he fell completely silent. But his heart was loosened, and tears dropped from his eyes and fell on his hands. And he paid no more heed to anything, and sat there motionless, no longer fending off the animals. Then the doves flew to and fro and perched on his shoulder and caressed his white hair and did not tire of their tenderness and rejoicing. But the strong lion kept licking the tears that fell on Zarathustra’s hands, and roared and growled shyly as he did. That is how these animals carried on. —

All this lasted a long time, or a short time: for, properly speaking, there is *no* time on earth for such things —. Meanwhile, however, the higher men in Zarathustra’s cave had woken up and were forming themselves into a procession, to go out to meet Zarathustra and offer him their morning greeting: for when they woke they had found that he was no longer among them. But when they reached the door of the cave, and the sound of their steps ran ahead of them, the lion started violently, turned all at once away from Zarathustra, and leaped, roaring wildly, at the cave; and the higher men, when they heard it roar, all cried out as with one mouth, and fled back, and in an instant they were gone.

But Zarathustra himself, dazed and estranged, rose from his seat, looked around him, stood there in amazement, questioned his heart, came to himself, and was alone. “What was it I heard?” he said at last, slowly. “What just happened to me?”

And already the memory came back to him, and he grasped in a single glance everything that had taken place between yesterday and today. “Here is the stone,” he said, and stroked his beard, “on *this* one I sat yesterday morning; and here the soothsayer came up to me, and here I first heard the cry that I heard just now, the great cry of distress.

Oh you higher men, it was *your* distress that the old soothsayer foretold to me yesterday morning, —

— to your distress he wanted to seduce and tempt me: oh Zarathustra, he said to me, I come to seduce you to your last sin.

To my last sin? cried Zarathustra, and laughed angrily at his own word: *what* was it that was saved up for me as my last sin?”

— And once more Zarathustra sank into himself, and sat down again on the great stone, and pondered. Suddenly he sprang up, —

“Pity! Pity for the higher man!” he cried out, and his face turned to bronze. “Well then! *That* — has had its time!

My suffering and my pity — what does it matter! Am I striving for *happiness*? I am striving for my *work*!

Well then! The lion has come, my children are near, Zarathustra has grown ripe, my hour has come: —

This is *my* morning, *my* day is beginning: rise up now, rise up, you great noon!” —

Thus spoke Zarathustra, and he left his cave, glowing and strong, like a morning sun that comes out of dark mountains.
