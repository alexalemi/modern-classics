On the Mount of Olives

Winter, a bad guest, is sitting at home with me; my hands are blue from the handshake of his friendship.

I honor him, this bad guest, but I like to leave him sitting alone. I like to run away from him; and if one runs *well*, one gets away from him!

With warm feet and warm thoughts I run to where the wind stands still — to the sunny corner of my Mount of Olives.

There I laugh at my stern guest and am still fond of him, because at home he catches the flies for me and silences much small noise.

For he will not put up with a mosquito wanting to sing, let alone two; he makes even the street so lonely that the moonlight is afraid in it at night.

He is a hard guest — but I honor him, and I do not pray, like the pampered ones, to the potbellied fire-idol.

Better to chatter one’s teeth a little than to worship idols! — that is how my nature wants it. And I bear a special grudge against all fire-idols, the ardent, the steaming, the stuffy.

Whom I love, I love better in winter than in summer; I mock my enemies better and more heartily now that winter sits in my house.

Heartily, truly, even when I *crawl* into bed —: even then my huddled happiness laughs and makes mischief; even my lying dream laughs.

I — a crawler? Never in my life have I crawled before the mighty; and if I ever lied, I lied out of love. That is why I am glad even in my winter bed.

A humble bed warms me more than a rich one, for I am jealous of my poverty. And in winter it is most faithful to me.

I begin each day with a piece of malice: I mock the winter with a cold bath: and at that my stern housemate grumbles.

I also like to tickle him with a little wax candle: so that at last he lets the sky out of the ash-gray dusk for me.

For I am especially malicious in the morning: at the early hour, when the bucket rattles at the well and the horses whinny warmly through gray streets: —

Impatiently I wait then for the bright sky to rise for me at last, the snow-bearded winter sky, the old man and white-head, —

— the winter sky, the silent one, which often keeps silent even about its sun!

Did I learn the long bright silence from him? Or did he learn it from me? Or did each of us invent it himself?

The origin of all good things is thousandfold — all good, mischievous things leap into existence for joy: how could they ever do that only — once!

A good, mischievous thing is the long silence too, and to look out, like the winter sky, from a bright, round-eyed face: —

— like it, to keep silent about one’s sun and one’s unbending sun-will: truly, this art and this winter mischief I learned *well*!

It is my favorite malice and art that my silence has learned not to betray itself by being silent.

Rattling words and dice, I outwit the solemn watchers: my will and purpose shall slip away from all these stern spies.

So that no one may see down into my ground and my last will — for that I invented the long bright silence.

Many a clever one I found who veiled his face and muddied his water, so that no one might see through him and down into him.

But it was to him of all people that the cleverer distrusters and nutcrackers came: it was from him of all people that they fished out his most hidden fish!

No, the clear, the brave, the transparent ones — those are to me the cleverest of the silent: their ground is so *deep* that even the clearest water does not — betray it. —

You snow-bearded, silent winter sky, you round-eyed white-head above me! Oh you heavenly parable of my soul and its mischief!

And *must* I not hide, like one who has swallowed gold — so that they do not slit my soul open?

*Must* I not wear stilts, so that they *overlook* my long legs — all these envy-mongers and misery-mongers around me?

These smoky, stove-warmed, worn-out, mildewed, moping souls — how *could* their envy bear my happiness!

So I show them only the ice and the winter on my peaks — and *not* that my mountain still winds all the belts of the sun around itself!

They hear only my winter storms whistling: and *not* that I also sail over warm seas, like longing, heavy, hot south winds.

They even take mercy on my mishaps and accidents: — but *my* word is: “Let accident come to me: it is innocent, like a little child!”

How *could* they bear my happiness, if I did not wrap mishaps and winter hardships and polar-bear caps and cloaks of snowy sky around my happiness!

— if I did not myself take mercy on their *pity* — the pity of these envy-mongers and misery-mongers!

— if I did not myself sigh and shiver with frost in front of them and patiently *let* myself be wrapped in their pity!

This is the wise mischief and goodwill of my soul, that it *does not hide* its winter and its frost storms; nor does it hide its chilblains.

One man’s solitude is the flight of the sick; another’s solitude is the flight *from* the sick.

Let them *hear* me chattering and sighing from the winter cold, all these poor squint-eyed scoundrels around me! With such sighing and chattering I still flee from their heated rooms.

Let them pity me and sigh their pity over my chilblains: “He will *freeze* to death on us yet, on the ice of knowledge!” — so they lament.

Meanwhile I run with warm feet this way and that on my Mount of Olives: in the sunny corner of my Mount of Olives I sing and mock at all pity. —

Thus sang Zarathustra.
