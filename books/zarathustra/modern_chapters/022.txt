On the Gift-Giving Virtue

I

When Zarathustra had taken leave of the town his heart had grown fond of, whose name is “the Motley Cow” — many who called themselves his disciples followed him and kept him company on his way. So they came to a crossroads: there Zarathustra told them that from now on he wanted to go alone; for he was a friend of going alone. But at parting his disciples handed him a staff, on whose golden handle a serpent coiled around the sun. Zarathustra was glad of the staff and leaned on it; then he spoke to his disciples like this:

Tell me, then: how did gold come to have the highest value? Because it is uncommon and useless and gleaming and gentle in its luster; it always gives itself away.

Only as an image of the highest virtue did gold come to have the highest value. The glance of the giver gleams like gold. The gleam of gold makes peace between moon and sun.

The highest virtue is uncommon and useless, it is gleaming and gentle in its luster: a gift-giving virtue is the highest virtue.

Truly, I can guess what you are, my disciples: like me, you strive for the gift-giving virtue. What would you have in common with cats and wolves?

This is your thirst, to become sacrifices and gifts yourselves: and that is why you have the thirst to heap up all riches in your soul.

Your soul strives insatiably for treasures and jewels, because your virtue is insatiable in its wanting to give away.

You force all things to come to you and into you, so that they may flow back out of your well as the gifts of your love.

Truly, such gift-giving love must become a robber of all values; but whole and holy, I call this selfishness.

There is another selfishness, an all-too-poor one, a hungry one, which always wants to steal: that selfishness of the sick, the sick selfishness.

With the eye of a thief it looks at everything that gleams; with the greed of hunger it sizes up the one who has plenty to eat; and it is always creeping around the table of the givers.

Sickness speaks out of such craving, and an invisible degeneration; the thieving greed of this selfishness speaks of an ailing body.

Tell me, my brothers: what do we count as bad, and the worst of all? Is it not *degeneration*? — And we always suspect degeneration where the gift-giving soul is missing.

Our way goes upward, from our kind over to an overkind. But a horror to us is the degenerate mind that says: “Everything for me.”

Our mind flies upward: so it is a parable of our body, a parable of an elevation. The names of the virtues are parables of such elevations.

So the body goes through history, a becoming and a fighting thing. And the spirit — what is it to the body? The herald of its fights and victories, their companion and their echo.

All the names of good and evil are parables: they do not speak out, they only hint. He is a fool who wants knowledge from them!

Pay attention, my brothers, to every hour when your spirit wants to speak in parables: that is where your virtue has its origin.

Then your body is exalted and risen; with its delight it enraptures the spirit, so that the spirit becomes a creator and a valuer and a lover and a benefactor of all things.

When your heart surges broad and full like the river, a blessing and a danger to those who live along its banks: that is where your virtue has its origin.

When you are lifted above praise and blame, and your will wants to command all things, as the will of one who loves: that is where your virtue has its origin.

When you despise what is comfortable and the soft bed, and cannot make your bed far enough away from the soft: that is where your virtue has its origin.

When you are the willers of a single will, and this turning point of all need is what you call your necessity: that is where your virtue has its origin.

Truly, it is a new good and evil! Truly, a new deep rushing, and the voice of a new spring!

It is power, this new virtue; it is a ruling thought, and around it a shrewd soul: a golden sun, and around it the serpent of knowledge.

II

Here Zarathustra fell silent for a while and looked lovingly at his disciples. Then he went on speaking like this: — and his voice had changed.

Remain faithful to the earth, my brothers, with the power of your virtue! Let your gift-giving love and your knowledge serve the meaning of the earth! So I beg and implore you.

Do not let it fly away from the earthly and beat its wings against eternal walls! Ah, there has always been so much virtue that flew away!

Lead the virtue that flew away back to the earth, as I do — yes, back to body and life: so that it may give the earth its meaning, a human meaning!

A hundred times so far both spirit and virtue have flown astray and grasped wrong. Ah, all this delusion and blundering still lives in our body now: there it has become body and will.

A hundred times so far both spirit and virtue have tried and strayed. Yes, man was an experiment. Ah, much ignorance and error has become body in us!

Not only the reason of millennia — their madness, too, breaks out in us. It is dangerous to be an heir.

We are still fighting step by step with the giant Chance, and over all of humanity until now nonsense has ruled — the meaningless.

Let your spirit and your virtue serve the meaning of the earth, my brothers: and let the value of all things be set anew by you! That is why you shall be fighters! That is why you shall be creators!

Through knowing, the body purifies itself; experimenting with knowledge, it elevates itself; to the seeker of knowledge all drives become holy; to the one who is elevated the soul becomes joyful.

Physician, heal yourself: in that way you will heal your patient too. Let this be his best help, that he sees with his own eyes the one who makes himself whole.

There are a thousand paths that have never yet been walked; a thousand healths and hidden islands of life. Man and man’s earth are still unexhausted and undiscovered.

Keep watch and listen, you solitary ones! From the future come winds with secret beating of wings; and good news goes out to fine ears.

You solitary ones of today, you who withdraw, you shall one day be a people: from you who have chosen yourselves a chosen people shall grow: — and from it the overman.

Truly, the earth shall yet become a place of healing! And already a new scent lies around it, a scent that brings salvation — and a new hope!

III

When Zarathustra had said these words, he fell silent, like one who has not said his last word; for a long time he weighed the staff doubtfully in his hand. At last he spoke like this: — and his voice had changed.

I go alone now, my disciples! You too go away now, and alone! This is how I want it.

Truly, I advise you: go away from me and guard yourselves against Zarathustra! And better still: be ashamed of him! Perhaps he deceived you.

The man of knowledge must be able not only to love his enemies but also to hate his friends.

One repays a teacher badly if one always remains only a pupil. And why will you not pluck at my wreath?

You revere me; but what if one day your reverence topples over? Take care that a statue does not strike you dead!

You say you believe in Zarathustra? But what does Zarathustra matter! You are my believers: but what do any believers matter!

You had not yet sought yourselves: then you found me. That is what all believers do; that is why all faith amounts to so little.

Now I bid you lose me and find yourselves; and only when you have all denied me will I return to you.

Truly, with other eyes, my brothers, I will then seek my lost ones; with another love I will then love you.

And one day you shall have become my friends, and children of one hope: then I will be with you for the third time, to celebrate the great noon with you.

And this is the great noon: when man stands at the middle of his course between beast and overman, and celebrates his way to the evening as his highest hope: for it is the way to a new morning.

Then the one who goes under will bless himself for being one who goes over; and the sun of his knowledge will stand at noon for him.

“Dead are all gods: now we want the overman to live.” — let this one day, at the great noon, be our last will! —

Thus spoke Zarathustra.
