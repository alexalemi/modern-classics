On Scholars

As I lay asleep, a sheep ate at the ivy wreath on my head — ate, and said as it ate: “Zarathustra is no longer a scholar.”

It said this and went off, stiff-legged and proud. A child told me about it.

I like to lie here, where the children play, by the broken wall, among thistles and red poppies.

To the children I am still a scholar, and to the thistles and red poppies too. They are innocent, even in their mischief.

But to the sheep I am one no longer: so my lot wills it — blessed be it!

For this is the truth: I have moved out of the house of the scholars, and slammed the door behind me as well.

Too long my soul sat hungry at their table; I am not trained, as they are, to knowing as if to nutcracking.

I love freedom and the air over fresh earth; I would rather sleep on ox hides than on their honors and respectabilities.

I am too hot and scorched by my own thoughts: often they nearly take my breath away. Then I have to go out into the open, away from all dusty rooms.

But they sit cool in the cool shade: in everything they want only to be spectators, and they take care not to sit where the sun burns on the steps.

Like those who stand in the street and gape at the people going by: so they too wait, and gape at thoughts that others have thought.

If you take hold of them with your hands, they send up dust all around like flour sacks, and without meaning to; but who would guess that their dust comes from grain, and from the yellow delight of the summer fields?

When they act wise, their little sayings and truths make me shiver: there is often a smell about their wisdom, as if it came from the swamp: and truly, I have even heard the frog croaking out of it!

They are skillful, they have clever fingers: what is *my* simplicity next to their multiplicity! Their fingers understand all threading and knotting and weaving: so they knit the stockings of the spirit!

They are good clockworks: only take care to wind them up correctly! Then they tell the hour without fail and make a modest noise about it.

They work like mills and stamping machines: just throw them your seed grain! — they know well how to grind grain small and make white dust out of it.

They keep a close eye on one another’s fingers and do not trust each other the best. Inventive in little cunnings, they wait for those whose knowledge walks on lame feet — like spiders they wait.

I always saw them prepare poison with care; and every time they put glass gloves on their fingers to do it.

They know how to play with loaded dice too; and I found them playing so eagerly that they sweated over it.

We are strangers to one another, and their virtues go against my taste even more than their falsehoods and their loaded dice.

And when I lived among them, I lived above them. They held that against me.

They want to hear nothing of anyone walking over their heads; and so they put wood and earth and filth between me and their heads.

So they muffled the sound of my steps: and until now I have been heard worst of all by the most learned.

All of mankind’s faults and weakness they laid between themselves and me: — a “false floor” is what they call that in their houses.

But in spite of it I walk with my thoughts *over* their heads; and even if I wanted to walk on my own faults, I would still be above them and their heads.

For men are *not* equal: so speaks justice. And what I will, *they* may not will!

Thus spoke Zarathustra.
