The Awakening

I

After the song of the wanderer and shadow, the cave was all at once full of noise and laughter; and since the assembled guests were all talking at the same time, and even the donkey, with such encouragement, no longer kept quiet, a little aversion and mockery toward his visitors came over Zarathustra: even though he rejoiced at their gladness. For it seemed to him a sign of convalescence. So he slipped out into the open and spoke to his animals.

“Where has their distress gone now?” he said, and already he himself breathed more freely after his little weariness, — “with me, it seems to me, they have unlearned their cries of distress!

— though not yet, alas, their crying.” And Zarathustra held his ears shut, for just then the donkey’s Yeah-aw was mingling strangely with the jubilant noise of these higher men.

“They are merry,” he began again, “and who knows? perhaps at their host’s expense; and if they learned to laugh from me, still it is not *my* laughter that they learned.

But what does it matter! They are old people: they recover in their own way, they laugh in their own way; my ears have put up with worse already and did not grow sullen.

This day is a victory: he is already giving way, he is fleeing, *the spirit of gravity*, my old archenemy! How well this day will end, which began so badly and so heavily!

And end it *will*. Already the evening is coming: it rides here over the sea, the good rider! How it sways, the blessed one, the homecoming one, in its purple saddles!

The sky looks on with clear eyes, the world lies deep: oh all you strange ones who came to me, it is already worth it to live with me!”

Thus spoke Zarathustra. And again the shouting and laughter of the higher men came out of the cave: then he began anew.

“They are biting, my bait is working, their enemy is giving way for them too, the spirit of gravity. Already they are learning to laugh at themselves: do I hear right?

My man’s food is working, my sayings full of sap and strength: and truly, I did not feed them on vegetables that bloat! But on warrior’s food, on conqueror’s food: I awakened new desires.

New hopes are in their arms and legs, their hearts are stretching out. They are finding new words, soon their spirit will breathe mischief.

Such food may not be for children, of course, nor for longing little women, old and young. Their bowels are persuaded in other ways; I am not their doctor and teacher.

*Disgust* is leaving these higher men: well then! that is my victory. In my kingdom they become secure, all stupid shame runs away, they pour themselves out.

They pour out their hearts, good hours come back to them, they celebrate and chew the cud, — they become *grateful*.

*That* I take as the best sign: they become grateful. Before long they will be thinking up festivals and setting up memorials to their old joys.

They are *convalescents*!” Thus spoke Zarathustra joyfully to his heart and looked out; but his animals pressed close to him and honored his happiness and his silence.

II

But suddenly Zarathustra’s ear took fright: for the cave, which until then had been full of noise and laughter, all at once went deathly still; — but his nose smelled a sweet-smelling smoke and incense, as if from burning pine cones.

“What is happening? What are they doing?” he asked himself, and crept up to the entrance, so that he could watch his guests unnoticed. But wonder upon wonder! what did he have to see there with his own eyes!

“They have all become *pious* again, they are *praying*, they are mad!” — he said, and was astonished beyond measure. And truly! all these higher men, the two kings, the retired pope, the wicked magician, the voluntary beggar, the wanderer and shadow, the old soothsayer, the conscientious one of the spirit, and the ugliest man: they were all lying on their knees like children and credulous little old women, worshipping the donkey. And just then the ugliest man began to gurgle and snort, as if something unspeakable wanted to come out of him; but when he had really brought it as far as words, look, it was a pious, strange litany in praise of the donkey they worshipped and burned incense to. And this litany went like this:

Amen! And blessing and glory and wisdom and thanksgiving and honor and might be to our God, forever and ever!

— But the donkey brayed in response: Yeah-aw.

He carries our burden, he took on the form of a servant, he is patient of heart and never says No; and whoever loves his God chastises him.

— But the donkey brayed in response: Yeah-aw.

He does not speak: except that he always says Yes to the world he created: so he praises his world. It is his cunning that does not speak: that way he is seldom in the wrong.

— But the donkey brayed in response: Yeah-aw.

Unnoticed he goes through the world. Gray is the coat in which he wraps his virtue. If he has spirit, he hides it; but everyone believes in his long ears.

— But the donkey brayed in response: Yeah-aw.

What hidden wisdom it is, that he wears long ears and says only Yes and never No! Did he not create the world in his own image, that is, as stupid as possible?

— But the donkey brayed in response: Yeah-aw.

You walk straight paths and crooked ones; it troubles you little what seems straight or crooked to us human beings. Beyond good and evil is your kingdom. It is your innocence not to know what innocence is.

— But the donkey brayed in response: Yeah-aw.

Look how you push no one away from you, not the beggars, nor the kings. You let the little children come to you, and when evil boys entice you, you say, in all simplicity, Yeah-aw.

— But the donkey brayed in response: Yeah-aw.

You love she-donkeys and fresh figs, you are no despiser of good food. A thistle tickles your heart when you happen to be hungry. In that lies the wisdom of a god.

— But the donkey brayed in response: Yeah-aw.
