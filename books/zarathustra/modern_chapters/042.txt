The Soothsayer

“— and I saw a great sadness come over mankind. The best of them grew weary of their works.

A doctrine went out, and a faith ran alongside it: ‘All is empty, all is the same, all has been!’

And from every hill it echoed back: ‘All is empty, all is the same, all has been!’

We have harvested, it is true: but why did all our fruits turn rotten and brown on us? What fell down from the evil moon last night?

All our work was in vain, our wine has turned to poison, the evil eye has scorched our fields and our hearts yellow.

We have all become dry; and if fire falls on us, we scatter like ash: — yes, we have made fire itself weary.

All our wells have dried up, even the sea has drawn back. All the ground wants to split open, but the depths will not swallow!

‘Ah, where is there still a sea one could drown in’: so goes our lament — out across the shallow swamps.

Truly, we have grown too weary even to die; now we stay awake and live on — in burial vaults!” —

So Zarathustra heard a soothsayer speak; and his prophecy went to Zarathustra’s heart and changed him. He went about sad and weary; and he became like those of whom the soothsayer had spoken.

Truly, he said to his disciples, it will not be long before this long twilight comes. Ah, how am I to save my light and carry it across!

If only it does not suffocate in this sadness! It is meant to be a light to farther worlds, and to the farthest nights!

Troubled like this in his heart, Zarathustra went about; and for three days he took neither food nor drink, had no rest, and lost his speech. At last it happened that he fell into a deep sleep. But his disciples sat around him through long night watches, waiting anxiously to see whether he would wake and speak again and be cured of his gloom.

And this is the discourse that Zarathustra gave when he woke; but his voice came to his disciples as if from far away.

Listen to the dream I dreamed, my friends, and help me guess its meaning!

It is still a riddle to me, this dream; its meaning is hidden inside it and trapped there, and does not yet fly out over it on free wings.

I had renounced all life, so I dreamed. I had become a night watchman and a guardian of graves, up there in the lonely mountain castle of death.

Up there I guarded his coffins: the musty vaults stood full of these trophies of victory. Overcome life gazed at me out of glass coffins.

I breathed the smell of dusty eternities: my soul lay sultry and dusty. And who could have aired out his soul in such a place!

The brightness of midnight was always around me, solitude crouched beside it; and, making three, a death-rattle silence, the worst of my companions.

I carried keys, the rustiest of all keys; and I knew how to open the creakiest of all gates with them.

Like a bitter, evil croaking the sound ran through the long corridors whenever the wings of the gate swung up: this bird screeched spitefully, it did not like being woken.

But more frightening still, and more heart-wringing, was when it fell silent again and all around grew still, and I sat alone in that treacherous silence.

So time passed for me and crept along, if there was still such a thing as time: what do I know of it! But at last the thing happened that woke me.

Three times blows struck the gate, like thunder, and three times the vaults echoed and howled back: then I went to the gate.

Alpa! I cried, who is carrying his ashes up the mountain? Alpa! Alpa! Who is carrying his ashes up the mountain?

And I pressed the key and heaved at the gate and strained. But it was still not open a finger’s breadth:

Then a roaring wind tore its wings apart: whistling, shrilling, and cutting, it hurled a black coffin toward me:

And amid the roaring and whistling and shrilling the coffin burst open and spewed out a thousandfold laughter.

And out of a thousand grimacing faces of children, angels, owls, fools, and butterflies as big as children, something laughed and jeered and roared at me.

I was horribly frightened by it: it threw me to the ground. And I screamed in horror, as I had never screamed before.

But my own scream woke me up: — and I came to myself. —

So Zarathustra told his dream, and then fell silent: for he did not yet know the interpretation of his dream. But the disciple he loved most rose quickly, took Zarathustra’s hand, and said:

“Your life itself interprets this dream for us, oh Zarathustra!

Are you not yourself the wind with its shrill whistling, which tears open the gates of the castles of death?

Are you not yourself the coffin full of life’s many-colored mischief and angel-grimaces?

Truly, like a thousandfold children’s laughter Zarathustra comes into every death chamber, laughing at those night watchmen and guardians of graves, and at whoever else goes rattling gloomy keys.

You will frighten them and knock them over with your laughter; fainting and waking will prove your power over them.

And even when the long twilight comes, and the weariness of death, you will not go under in our sky, you advocate of life!

You have let us see new stars and new splendors of the night; truly, you have spread laughter itself over us like a many-colored tent.

Now children’s laughter will always well up out of coffins; now a strong wind will always come, victorious over all weariness of death: of that you yourself are our guarantee and our soothsayer!

Truly, *you dreamed them themselves*, your enemies: that was your hardest dream!

But just as you woke from them and came to yourself, so they shall wake from themselves — and come to you!” —

So spoke the disciple; and all the others now crowded around Zarathustra and took him by the hands and tried to persuade him to leave his bed and his sadness and come back to them. But Zarathustra sat upright on his bed, with a strange look in his eyes. Like someone coming home from a long time in foreign lands, he looked at his disciples and searched their faces; and still he did not recognize them. But when they lifted him and set him on his feet, look, all at once his eye changed; he understood everything that had happened, stroked his beard, and said in a strong voice:

“Well then! All this has its time; but see to it, my disciples, that we have a good meal, and soon! That is how I mean to do penance for bad dreams!

But the soothsayer shall eat and drink at my side: and truly, I will yet show him a sea he can drown in!”

Thus spoke Zarathustra. But then he looked for a long time into the face of the disciple who had played the interpreter of dreams, and shook his head as he did so. —
