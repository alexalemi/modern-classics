Retired

But not long after Zarathustra had freed himself from the magician, he again saw someone sitting by the path he was walking, namely a tall man in black with a gaunt, pasty face: *he* annoyed him enormously. “Woe,” he said to his heart, “there sits gloom in disguise; that looks to me like the priestly kind: what do *they* want in my kingdom?

What! I have hardly escaped that magician, and must another black artist run across my path again —

— some sorcerer with a laying-on of hands, a dark miracle-worker by the grace of God, an anointed world-slanderer, may the devil take him!

But the devil is never in his place when he would be in place: he always comes too late, this accursed dwarf and clubfoot!” —

Thus Zarathustra cursed impatiently in his heart, and thought how he might slip past the black man with his eyes turned away: but look, it turned out otherwise. For in the same moment the sitting man had already caught sight of him; and not unlike someone to whom an unexpected piece of luck has happened, he jumped up and went straight for Zarathustra.

“Whoever you are, you wanderer,” he said, “help a man who has lost his way, a seeker, an old man who could easily come to harm here!

This world here is strange and far from me, and I heard wild animals howling, too; and the one who could have offered me protection — he himself is no more.

I was looking for the last pious man, a saint and hermit, who alone in his forest had not yet heard anything of what all the world knows today.”

“*What* does all the world know today?” asked Zarathustra. “This, perhaps: that the old God no longer lives, the God all the world once believed in?”

“You have said it,” answered the old man sorrowfully. “And I served this old God until his last hour.

But now I am retired, without a master, and still not free, and never merry for a single hour anymore, except in memories.

That is why I climbed into these mountains: so that I might at last hold a festival again for myself, as befits an old pope and father of the church — for know this, I am the last pope! — a festival of pious memories and divine services.

But now he himself is dead, the most pious man, that saint in the forest who constantly praised his God with singing and growling.

Him I no longer found when I found his hut — but I did find two wolves in it, howling over his death — for all the animals loved him. Then I ran away.

Had I come into these forests and mountains for nothing, then? Then my heart resolved that I should seek another, the most pious of all those who do not believe in God — that I should seek Zarathustra!”

Thus spoke the old man, and looked with a sharp eye at the one who stood before him; but Zarathustra took the old pope’s hand and gazed at it a long time in admiration.

“Look at that, you venerable man,” he said then, “what a beautiful, long hand! It is the hand of one who has always handed out blessings. But now it holds fast the one you are looking for: me, Zarathustra.

It is I, Zarathustra the godless, who says: who is more godless than I, that I may delight in his instruction?” —

Thus spoke Zarathustra, and with his gaze he pierced the thoughts and the second thoughts of the old pope. At last the pope began:

“Whoever loved and possessed him most has now also lost him most —:

— look, of the two of us, am I not now probably the more godless one? But who could be glad of that!” —

“You served him to the end,” asked Zarathustra thoughtfully, after a deep silence, “do you know *how* he died? Is it true, what people say, that pity strangled him,

— that he saw how *man* hung on the cross, and could not bear it that his love of man became his hell and at last his death?” —

But the old pope did not answer; he looked shyly aside, with a pained and gloomy expression.

“Let him go,” said Zarathustra after long reflection, still looking the old man straight in the eye.

“Let him go, he is gone. And though it honors you that you speak only good of this dead one, you know as well as I do *who* he was; and that he went strange ways.”

“Speaking between the three of our eyes,” said the old pope, cheered (for he was blind in one eye), “in matters of God I am more enlightened than Zarathustra himself — and I may well be.

My love served him for long years, my will followed all his will. But a good servant knows everything, including many things his master hides from himself.

He was a hidden god, full of secrecy. Truly, even to a son he came no other way than by sneaking paths. At the door of his faith stands adultery.

Whoever praises him as a god of love does not think highly enough of love itself. Did this god not want to be judge as well? But the one who loves, loves beyond reward and retribution.

When he was young, this god from the East, he was hard and vengeful, and built himself a hell for the delight of his favorites.

But at last he grew old and soft and crumbly and pitying, more like a grandfather than a father, but most of all like a wobbly old grandmother.

There he sat, withered, in his corner by the stove, fretting over his weak legs, weary of the world, weary of willing, and one day he choked on his all-too-great pity.” —

“You old pope,” Zarathustra broke in here, “did you see *that* with your own eyes? It could well have happened that way: that way, *and* some other way too. When gods die, they always die many kinds of death.

But well then! This way or that, this way and that — he is gone! He went against the taste of my ears and eyes; I would not want to say anything worse of him than that.

I love everything that looks clearly and speaks honestly. But he — you know it, you old priest, there was something of your kind about him, of the priestly kind — he had many meanings.

He was unclear, too. How angry he got with us over it, this snorter of wrath, because we understood him badly! But why did he not speak more plainly?

And if it was the fault of our ears, why did he give us ears that heard him badly? If there was mud in our ears, well then! who put it there?

Too much went wrong for him, this potter who had never finished his apprenticeship! But that he took revenge on his pots and his creatures because they turned out badly for him — that was a sin against *good taste*.

There is good taste in piety too: and it said at last, ‘Away with *such* a god! Better no god at all, better to make one’s own destiny on one’s own account, better to be a fool, better to be a god oneself!’”

— “What do I hear!” said the old pope here, pricking up his ears. “Oh Zarathustra, you are more pious than you believe, with an unbelief like that! Some god in you converted you to your godlessness.

Is it not your piety itself that no longer lets you believe in a god? And your overgreat honesty will yet carry you off beyond good and evil as well!

Look, then, what has been kept in store for you? You have eyes and hand and mouth, and they have been destined for blessing from all eternity. One does not bless with the hand alone.

Near you, though you want to be the most godless of all, I scent a secret fragrance of incense and well-being from long blessings: it makes me feel both well and woeful.

Let me be your guest, oh Zarathustra, for a single night! Nowhere on earth do I feel better now than with you!” —

“Amen! So be it!” said Zarathustra in great astonishment. “Up there the way leads on; there lies Zarathustra’s cave.

Truly, I would gladly lead you there myself, you venerable man, for I love all pious people. But now a cry of distress calls me away from you in haste.

In my domain no one shall come to harm; my cave is a good harbor. And most of all I would like to set every sad person back on firm land and firm legs.

But who could take *your* melancholy off your shoulders? For that I am too weak. Truly, we might wait a long time before someone wakes your God again.

For this old God no longer lives: he is thoroughly dead.” —

Thus spoke Zarathustra.
