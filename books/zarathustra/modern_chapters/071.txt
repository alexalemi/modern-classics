The Ugliest Man

— And again Zarathustra’s feet ran through mountains and forests, and his eyes searched and searched, but nowhere was the one to be seen whom they wanted to see, the one in great distress who had cried out in distress. But all along the way he rejoiced in his heart and was grateful. “What good things,” he said, “this day has given me, to make up for its bad beginning! What strange people I have found to talk with!

Now I will chew on their words for a long time, as on good grains; my teeth shall grind and crush them small, until they flow into my soul like milk!” —

But when the path bent around a rock again, all at once the landscape changed, and Zarathustra stepped into a realm of death. Here black and red cliffs jutted upward: no grass, no tree, no bird’s voice. For it was a valley that all animals avoided, even the beasts of prey; only a kind of ugly, thick, green serpent came here to die when they grew old. That is why the shepherds called this valley: Serpents’ Death.

But Zarathustra sank into a black memory, for it seemed to him that he had stood in this valley once before. And much that was heavy weighed on his mind: so that he walked slowly, and more and more slowly, and at last stood still. But then, when he opened his eyes, he saw something sitting by the wayside, shaped like a man and hardly like a man, something unspeakable. And all at once a great shame fell on Zarathustra, that he had looked at such a thing with his eyes: blushing up to the roots of his white hair, he turned his gaze away and lifted his foot to leave this bad place. But then the dead wasteland grew loud: for up from the ground there welled a gurgling and a rattling, the way water gurgles and rattles at night through clogged water pipes; and at last it turned into a human voice and human speech: — and it went like this.

“Zarathustra! Zarathustra! Guess my riddle! Speak, speak! What is *revenge on the witness*?

I am luring you back, here is slippery ice! Watch out, watch out that your pride does not break its legs here!

You think yourself wise, you proud Zarathustra! Then guess the riddle, you hard nutcracker — the riddle that I am! Then speak — who am *I*!”

— But when Zarathustra had heard these words — what do you suppose happened then in his soul? Pity fell upon him; and all at once he sank to the ground, like an oak that has long withstood many woodcutters — heavily, suddenly, to the terror even of those who wanted to fell it. But already he was rising from the ground again, and his face grew hard.

“I know you well,” he said, in a voice of bronze: “you are the murderer of God! Let me go.

You could not *bear* the one who saw *you* — who saw you always and through and through, you ugliest man! You took revenge on this witness!”

Thus spoke Zarathustra, and he wanted to get away; but the unspeakable one grabbed at a corner of his robe and began again to gurgle and search for words. “Stay!” he said at last —

— “stay! Do not pass by! I guessed what axe struck you to the ground: hail to you, oh Zarathustra, that you are standing again!

You guessed, I know it well, how the one feels who killed him — the murderer of God. Stay! Sit down here beside me; it is not for nothing.

To whom would I go, if not to you? Stay, sit down! But do not look at me! Honor in this way — my ugliness!

They are persecuting me: now *you* are my last refuge. *Not* with their hatred, *not* with their bailiffs: — oh, I would mock such persecution, and be proud and glad!

Has not all success so far been with the well-persecuted? And whoever persecutes well easily learns to *follow*: — after all, he is already — behind! But it is their *pity* —

— it is their pity that I am fleeing, fleeing to you for refuge. Oh Zarathustra, protect me, you my last refuge, you the only one who guessed me:

— you guessed how the one feels who killed *him*. Stay! And if you want to go, you impatient one: do not go the way I came. *That* way is bad.

Are you angry with me that I have been talk-tangle-mangling too long already? That I am already giving you advice? But know that it is I, the ugliest man,

— who also has the biggest, heaviest feet. Where *I* have walked, the way is bad. I trample every way to death and to ruin.

But that you passed me by, in silence; that you blushed, I saw it well: by that I knew you for Zarathustra.

Anyone else would have tossed me his alms, his pity, with a glance and a word. But for that — I am not beggar enough, you guessed that —

— for that I am too *rich*, rich in what is great, in what is terrible, in what is ugliest, in what is most unspeakable! Your shame, oh Zarathustra, *honored* me!

With difficulty I got out of the crush of the pitying, — to find the only one who teaches today that “pity is pushy” — you, oh Zarathustra!

— whether it is a god’s pity or the pity of men: pity goes against shame. And not-wanting-to-help can be nobler than that virtue which leaps to help.

But *that* is what all the little people today call virtue itself, pity: — they have no reverence for great misfortune, for great ugliness, for great failure.

I look away over all of these, as a dog looks away over the backs of teeming flocks of sheep. They are little, well-meaning, well-willing gray people.

As a heron looks away in contempt over shallow ponds, with its head thrown back: so I look away over the swarm of gray little waves and wills and souls.

Too long they have been told they are right, these little people: *so* in the end they were given power too — now they teach: “good is only what little people call good.”

And “truth” today is what the preacher spoke who came from among them himself, that strange saint and advocate of the little people, who testified of himself, “I — am the truth.”

For a long time now this immodest man has been making the little people puff up their crests — he who taught no small error when he taught, “I — am the truth.”

Was an immodest man ever answered more politely? — But you, oh Zarathustra, passed him by and said: “No! No! Three times no!”

You warned against his error, you were the first to warn against pity — not all, not none, but yourself and your kind.

You are ashamed at the shame of the great sufferer; and truly, when you say, “from pity comes a great cloud, beware, you people!”

— when you teach, “all creators are hard, all great love is above its pity”: oh Zarathustra, how well schooled in the signs of the weather you seem to me!

But you yourself — warn yourself too against *your* pity! For many are on their way to you, many who are suffering, doubting, despairing, drowning, freezing —

I warn you against me too. You guessed my best, my worst riddle, myself and what I did. I know the axe that fells you.

But he — *had to* die: he saw with eyes that saw *everything*, — he saw man’s depths and grounds, all his hidden disgrace and ugliness.

His pity knew no shame: he crept into my filthiest corners. This most curious, over-pushy, over-pitying one had to die.

He always saw *me*: on such a witness I wanted to have revenge — or not live myself.

The God who saw everything, *even man*: this God had to die! Man cannot *bear* that such a witness should live.”

Thus spoke the ugliest man. But Zarathustra rose and made ready to go: for he was chilled to his very bowels.

“You unspeakable one,” he said, “you warned me against your way. In thanks for that, I commend my own to you. Look, up there lies Zarathustra’s cave.

My cave is large and deep and has many corners; there the most hidden one finds his hiding place. And close by it are a hundred holes and hideaways for creeping, fluttering, and hopping creatures.

You outcast, who cast yourself out, you do not want to live among men and the pity of men? Well then, do as I do! So you will learn from me too; only the doer learns.

And talk first and foremost with my animals! The proudest animal and the shrewdest animal — they may well be the right counselors for the two of us!” —

Thus spoke Zarathustra, and went his way, more thoughtful and slower even than before: for he asked himself many things and did not easily know the answer.

“How poor man is, after all!” he thought in his heart, “how ugly, how rattling, how full of hidden shame!

I am told that man loves himself: ah, how great this self-love must be! How much contempt it has against it!

This one too loved himself even as he despised himself, — a great lover he is to me, and a great despiser.

I have found no one yet who despised himself more deeply: even *that* is height. Woe, was *he* perhaps the higher man whose cry I heard?

I love the great despisers. But man is something that must be overcome.” —
