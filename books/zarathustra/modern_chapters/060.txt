The Convalescent

I

One morning, not long after his return to the cave, Zarathustra sprang up from his bed like a madman, cried out in a terrible voice, and acted as if someone else were still lying on the bed who would not get up; and Zarathustra’s voice rang out so loudly that his animals came running to him in fright, and out of all the caves and hiding places that neighbored Zarathustra’s cave, every creature scurried away — flying, fluttering, crawling, leaping, each according to the kind of foot or wing it had been given. But Zarathustra spoke these words:

Up, abysmal thought, out of my depths! I am your rooster and your morning twilight, you sleepy worm: up! up! My voice shall crow you awake!

Untie the fetters from your ears: listen! For I want to hear you! Up! Up! There is thunder enough here to teach even graves to listen!

And wipe the sleep and all the dullness and blindness from your eyes! Hear me with your eyes as well: my voice is a medicine even for those born blind.

And once you are awake, you shall stay awake for me forever. It is not *my* way to wake great-grandmothers from their sleep only to tell them — go back to sleep!

You stir, you stretch, you rattle in your throat? Up! Up! Not rattle — you shall speak to me! Zarathustra calls you, Zarathustra the godless!

I, Zarathustra, the advocate of life, the advocate of suffering, the advocate of the circle — I call you, my most abysmal thought!

Hail to me! You are coming — I hear you! My abyss *speaks*, I have turned my last depth inside out into the light!

Hail to me! Come here! Give me your hand — ha! let go! Haha! — disgust, disgust, disgust — woe is me!

II

But Zarathustra had hardly spoken these words when he fell down like a dead man and lay for a long time like a dead man. And when he came to himself again, he was pale and trembling, and he stayed lying down, and for a long time he would neither eat nor drink. This went on for seven days; but his animals did not leave him by day or by night, except that the eagle flew out to fetch food. And whatever it fetched and plundered, it laid on Zarathustra’s bed: so that at last Zarathustra lay among yellow and red berries, grapes, rose apples, sweet-smelling herbs, and pine cones. But at his feet lay two lambs, which the eagle had stolen with great effort from their shepherds.

At last, after seven days, Zarathustra sat up on his bed, took a rose apple in his hand, smelled it, and found its smell lovely. Then his animals believed that the time had come to speak with him.

“Oh Zarathustra,” they said, “you have been lying like this for seven days now, with heavy eyes: will you not stand on your feet again at last?

Step out of your cave: the world is waiting for you like a garden. The wind is playing with heavy fragrances that want to come to you; and all the brooks would like to run after you.

All things are longing for you, while you have stayed alone for seven days — step out of your cave! All things want to be your physicians!

Did some new knowledge come to you, perhaps, a sour and heavy one? You lay like leavened dough; your soul rose and swelled over all its rims. —”

— Oh my animals, answered Zarathustra, go on chattering like this and let me listen! It refreshes me so to hear you chatter: where there is chatter, the world already lies before me like a garden.

How lovely it is that there are words and sounds: are not words and sounds rainbows and illusory bridges between things eternally apart?

To every soul belongs another world; for every soul, every other soul is a world behind the world.

It is between the things most alike that illusion lies most beautifully; for the smallest gap is the hardest to bridge.

For me — how could there be anything outside me? There is no outside! But we forget that with every sound; how lovely it is that we forget!

Have not names and sounds been given to things so that man may refresh himself with things? Speaking is a beautiful folly: with it man dances over all things.

How lovely is all talking, and all the lying of sounds! With sounds our love dances on many-colored rainbows. —

— “Oh Zarathustra,” the animals said then, “to those who think as we do, all things dance by themselves: they come and offer each other their hands and laugh and run away — and come back.

Everything goes, everything comes back; the wheel of being rolls eternally. Everything dies, everything blossoms again; the year of being runs eternally.

Everything breaks, everything is joined anew; the same house of being builds itself eternally. Everything parts, everything greets itself again; the ring of being stays eternally true to itself.

In every instant being begins; around every Here rolls the sphere There. The center is everywhere. Crooked is the path of eternity.” —

— Oh you rascally fools and barrel organs! answered Zarathustra, and smiled again. How well you know what had to be fulfilled in seven days: —

— and how that monster crawled down my throat and choked me! But I bit its head off and spat it away from me.

And you — you have already made a barrel-organ song out of it? But now I lie here, still tired from that biting and spitting away, still sick from my own redemption.

And you watched all this? Oh my animals, are you cruel too? Did you want to watch my great pain, the way human beings do? For man is the cruelest animal.

At tragedies, bullfights, and crucifixions he has so far felt most at home on earth; and when he invented hell for himself, look, that was his heaven on earth.

When the great man cries out —: quick, the little man runs up; and his tongue hangs out of his mouth with lust. But he calls it his “pity.”

The little man, especially the poet — how eagerly he accuses life in words! Listen to him, but do not fail to hear the pleasure that is in all accusing!

Such accusers of life: life overcomes them with a blink of the eye. “You love me?” says the shameless one. “Wait a little longer, I have no time for you yet.”

Man is the cruelest animal toward himself; and in everyone who calls himself a “sinner” and a “cross-bearer” and a “penitent,” do not fail to hear the sensual pleasure in this complaining and accusing!

And I myself — do I want to be man’s accuser by saying this? Ah, my animals, this alone have I learned so far: that man needs what is most evil in him for what is best in him —

— that everything most evil is his best *power*, and the hardest stone for the highest creator; and that man must become better *and* more evil: —

It was not to *this* stake of torture that I was nailed — to knowing that man is evil — but I cried out as no one has yet cried out:

“Ah, that his most evil is so very small! Ah, that his best is so very small!”

The great weariness of man — *that* choked me and had crawled down my throat: and what the soothsayer foretold: “All is the same, nothing is worth it, knowledge chokes.”

A long twilight limped along in front of me, a sadness tired to death and drunk with death, which spoke with a yawning mouth.

“Eternally he comes back, the man you are tired of, the little man” — so yawned my sadness, and dragged its foot and could not fall asleep.

The human earth turned into a cave for me, its breast sank in, everything living became human rot and bones and crumbling past to me.

My sighing sat on all the graves of man and could no longer get up; my sighing and questioning croaked and choked and gnawed and wailed by day and by night:

— “Ah, man comes back eternally! The little man comes back eternally!” —

I had once seen them both naked, the greatest man and the smallest man: all too like each other — all too human, even the greatest!

All too small, the greatest! — that was my weariness of man! And eternal recurrence even of the smallest! — that was my weariness of all existence!

Ah, disgust! Disgust! Disgust! — — Thus spoke Zarathustra, and sighed and shuddered; for he remembered his sickness. But then his animals would not let him go on speaking.

“Speak no further, you convalescent!” — so his animals answered him — “but go out, where the world is waiting for you like a garden.

Go out to the roses and the bees and the flocks of doves! But above all to the songbirds: so that you may learn *singing* from them!

For singing is for the convalescent; the healthy may talk. And even when the healthy man wants songs, he wants other songs than the convalescent.”

— “Oh you rascally fools and barrel organs, do be quiet!” answered Zarathustra, and smiled at his animals. “How well you know what comfort I invented for myself in seven days!

That I would have to sing again — *that* comfort I invented for myself, and *this* convalescence: do you want to make a barrel-organ song out of that right away, too?”

— “Speak no further,” his animals answered him once more; “rather, you convalescent, first make yourself a lyre, a new lyre!

For look, oh Zarathustra! Your new songs need new lyres.

Sing and overflow, oh Zarathustra, heal your soul with new songs: so that you may bear your great destiny, which has never yet been any man’s destiny!

For your animals know well, oh Zarathustra, who you are and must become: look, you are the teacher of the eternal recurrence — that is now *your* destiny!

That you must be the first to teach this teaching — how could this great destiny not also be your greatest danger and sickness!

Look, we know what you teach: that all things recur eternally, and we ourselves with them, and that we have already been here an eternal number of times, and all things with us.

You teach that there is a great year of becoming, a monster of a great year: like an hourglass, it must turn itself over again and again, so that it may run down and run out anew: —

— so that all these years are the same as themselves, in the greatest and in the smallest as well — so that we ourselves are the same as ourselves in every great year, in the greatest and in the smallest as well.

And if you wanted to die now, oh Zarathustra: look, we know too how you would speak to yourself then: — but your animals beg you not to die yet!

You would speak, and without trembling, but rather breathing a sigh of bliss: for a great weight and a great sultriness would be lifted from you, you most patient one! —

‘Now I die and vanish,’ you would say, ‘and in an instant I am nothing. Souls are as mortal as bodies.

But the knot of causes in which I am entangled comes back — it will create me again! I myself belong to the causes of the eternal recurrence.

I come again, with this sun, with this earth, with this eagle, with this serpent — *not* to a new life or a better life or a similar life:

— I come again eternally to this same, selfsame life, in the greatest and in the smallest as well, to teach again the eternal recurrence of all things —

— to speak again the word of the great noon of earth and man, to proclaim the overman to mankind again.

I spoke my word, I break on my word: so my eternal lot wills it — as a proclaimer I perish!

The hour has now come for the one who goes under to bless himself. Thus *ends* Zarathustra’s going under.’” —

When the animals had spoken these words, they fell silent and waited for Zarathustra to say something to them: but Zarathustra did not hear that they were silent. Instead he lay still, with his eyes closed, like someone sleeping, although he was not asleep: for he was just then conversing with his soul. But the serpent and the eagle, when they found him so silent, honored the great stillness around him and carefully slipped away.
