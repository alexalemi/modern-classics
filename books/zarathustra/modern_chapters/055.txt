The Homecoming

Oh solitude! You, my *home*, solitude! Too long have I lived wild in wild foreign lands to come home to you without tears!

Now just wag your finger at me, as mothers do; no, smile at me, as mothers smile; now just say: “And who was it that once stormed away from me like a storm wind? —

— who cried as he left: too long have I sat with solitude, and there I unlearned silence! *That* — I suppose you have learned it now?

Oh Zarathustra, I know everything: and that among the many you were more *forsaken*, you single one, than you ever were with me!

Forsakenness is one thing, solitude another: *that* — you have learned now! And that among men you will always be wild and strange:

— wild and strange even when they love you: for above all else they want to be *spared*!

But here you are at home in your own house; here you can speak everything out and pour out all your reasons; nothing here is ashamed of hidden, hardened feelings.

Here all things come caressing to your speech and flatter you: for they want to ride on your back. On every parable you ride here to every truth.

Upright and forthright you may speak here to all things: and truly, it sounds like praise in their ears, that someone speaks to all things — straight!

But being forsaken is another thing. For do you still remember, oh Zarathustra? That time your bird screamed above you, when you stood in the forest, undecided, where to go? not knowing, near a corpse: —

— when you said: May my animals lead me! I found it more dangerous among men than among animals: — *That* was forsakenness!

And do you still remember, oh Zarathustra? When you sat on your island, a well of wine among empty buckets, giving and giving away, bestowing and pouring out among the thirsty:

— until at last you sat alone and thirsty among the drunk and lamented at night: ‘Is taking not more blessed than giving? And stealing more blessed still than taking?’ — *That* was forsakenness!

And do you still remember, oh Zarathustra? When your stillest hour came and drove you away from yourself, when it said with an evil whisper: ‘Speak and break!’ —

— when it made you sick of all your waiting and silence and discouraged your humble courage: *That* was forsakenness!” —

Oh solitude! You, my home, solitude! How blissfully and tenderly your voice speaks to me!

We do not question each other, we do not complain to each other, we walk openly together through open doors.

For with you everything is open and bright; and even the hours run here on lighter feet. For in the dark, time weighs more heavily on us than in the light.

Here the words and word-shrines of all being spring open for me: here all being wants to become word, here all becoming wants to learn from me how to speak.

But down there — down there all speech is in vain! There, forgetting and passing by are the best wisdom: *that* — I have learned now!

Whoever wanted to grasp everything about people would have to handle everything. But my hands are too clean for that.

I do not even like to breathe in their breath; ah, that I lived so long among their noise and bad breath!

Oh blissful stillness around me! Oh pure scents around me! Oh, how this stillness draws pure breath from a deep chest! Oh, how it listens, this blissful stillness!

But down there — there everything talks, there everything goes unheard. You may ring in your wisdom with bells: the shopkeepers in the marketplace will out-jingle it with pennies!

Everything among them talks; no one knows how to understand anymore. Everything falls into the water; nothing falls into deep wells anymore.

Everything among them talks; nothing turns out well and comes to an end anymore. Everything cackles, but who still wants to sit quietly on the nest and hatch eggs?

Everything among them talks; everything is talked to pieces. And what yesterday was still too hard for time itself and its tooth: today it hangs, scraped and gnawed, out of the mouths of the people of today.

Everything among them talks; everything is betrayed. And what was once called the secret and the secrecy of deep souls belongs today to the street trumpeters and other butterflies.

Oh humankind, you strange thing! You noise in dark alleys! Now you lie behind me again: — my greatest danger lies behind me!

My greatest danger always lay in sparing and pitying; and all humankind wants to be spared and suffered.

With truths held back, with a fool’s hand and a fool-fond heart, and rich in the little lies of pity: — that is how I always lived among men.

I sat among them in disguise, ready to mistake *myself* so that I could bear *them*, and gladly talking myself into it: “You fool, you do not know people!”

One unlearns people when one lives among people: there is too much foreground in all people — what use are far-seeing, far-craving eyes there!

And when they mistook me: I, the fool, spared them for it more than myself: used to being hard on myself, and often even taking revenge on myself for this sparing.

Stung all over by poisonous flies and hollowed out, like a stone, by many drops of malice: that is how I sat among them, and still talked myself into it: “Everything small is innocent of its smallness!”

Especially those who call themselves “the good” I found to be the most poisonous flies: they sting in all innocence, they lie in all innocence; how *could* they manage — to be just to me!

Whoever lives among the good is taught by pity to lie. Pity makes the air stuffy for all free souls. For the stupidity of the good is unfathomable.

To hide myself and my riches — *that* is what I learned down there: for I found everyone still poor in spirit. That was the lie of my pity, that with everyone I knew,

— that with everyone I saw and smelled what was *enough* spirit for him and what was already *too much* spirit for him!

Their stiff wise men: I called them wise, not stiff — so I learned to swallow words. Their gravediggers: I called them researchers and examiners — so I learned to swap words.

The gravediggers dig themselves into diseases. Foul vapors rest under old rubble. One should not stir up the swamp. One should live on mountains.

With blissful nostrils I breathe mountain freedom again! Redeemed at last is my nose from the smell of all humankind!

Tickled by sharp breezes, as if by sparkling wines, my soul *sneezes* — sneezes and cheers itself: Gesundheit!

Thus spoke Zarathustra.
