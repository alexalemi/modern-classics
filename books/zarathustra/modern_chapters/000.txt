Zarathustra’s Prologue

I

When Zarathustra was thirty years old, he left his home and the lake of his home and went up into the mountains. There he enjoyed his spirit and his solitude, and for ten years he did not grow tired of it. But at last his heart changed — and one morning he rose with the dawn, stepped out before the sun, and spoke to it like this:

“You great star! What would your happiness be, if you did not have those you shine for?

For ten years you have come up here to my cave: you would have grown sick of your light and of this road, if not for me, my eagle, and my serpent.

But every morning we waited for you, took your overflow from you, and blessed you for it.

Look! I am weary of my wisdom, like a bee that has gathered too much honey. I need hands that reach out for it.

I want to give it away and hand it out, until the wise among men are glad once again of their folly, and the poor once again of their riches.

For that I must go down into the depths, as you do in the evening when you sink behind the sea and bring your light even to the underworld, you overrich star!

Like you, I must *go under*, as men call it, the men to whom I want to go down.

So bless me, then, you calm eye, which can look without envy even on a happiness that is all too great!

Bless the cup that wants to overflow, so that the water may flow from it golden and carry the reflection of your delight everywhere!

Look! This cup wants to be empty again, and Zarathustra wants to be a man again.”

— Thus began Zarathustra’s going under.

II

Zarathustra came down the mountain alone, and no one met him. But when he came into the forest, there suddenly stood before him an old man, who had left his holy hut to look for roots in the woods. And the old man spoke to Zarathustra like this:

This wanderer is no stranger to me: many years ago he passed by here. Zarathustra was his name; but he has changed. Back then you carried your ashes up the mountain: will you carry your fire down into the valleys today? Are you not afraid of the punishment for arsonists?

Yes, I recognize Zarathustra. His eye is clear, and no disgust lurks around his mouth. Does he not walk like a dancer?

Zarathustra has changed, Zarathustra has become a child, Zarathustra is awake: what do you want now among the sleepers?

You lived in your solitude as in the sea, and the sea carried you. Woe, do you want to climb out onto the land? Woe, do you want to drag your own body around again?

Zarathustra answered: “I love mankind.”

Why, said the saint, did I go into the forest and the wilderness at all? Was it not because I loved mankind all too much?

Now I love God: mankind I do not love. Man is too imperfect a thing for me. Love of man would kill me.

Zarathustra answered: “Who said anything about love! I am bringing mankind a gift.”

Give them nothing, said the saint. Better to take something off their shoulders and carry it with them — that will do them the most good, as long as it does you good!

And if you want to give to them, give them no more than alms, and make them beg for it, too!

“No,” answered Zarathustra, “I give no alms. I am not poor enough for that.”

The saint laughed at Zarathustra and said: Then see to it that they accept your treasures! They are suspicious of hermits and do not believe that we come to give gifts.

Our footsteps sound too lonely to them as they echo through the streets. And just as when they lie in bed at night and hear a man walking about, long before the sun is up, they probably ask themselves: where is that thief going?

Do not go to men, and stay in the forest! Go to the animals instead! Why will you not be as I am — a bear among bears, a bird among birds?

“And what does the saint do in the forest?” asked Zarathustra.

The saint answered: I make songs and sing them, and when I make songs, I laugh, I weep, and I growl: that is how I praise God.

With singing, weeping, laughing, and growling I praise the God who is my god. But what do you bring us as a gift?

When Zarathustra had heard these words, he saluted the saint and said: “What could I have to give you! But let me go quickly, before I take something from you!” — And so they parted from each other, the old man and the man, laughing, just as two boys laugh.

But when Zarathustra was alone, he spoke to his heart: “Could it be possible! This old saint in his forest has not yet heard that *God is dead*!” —

III

When Zarathustra came into the nearest town, which lies at the edge of the forest, he found a great crowd of people gathered in the marketplace: for it had been announced that there would be a tightrope walker to see. And Zarathustra spoke to the people like this:

I teach you the overman. Man is something that must be overcome. What have you done to overcome him?

All beings so far have created something beyond themselves: and you want to be the ebb of this great flood, and would rather go back to the beasts than overcome man?

What is the ape to man? A laughingstock, or a painful shame. And that is exactly what man shall be to the overman: a laughingstock, or a painful shame.

You have made your way from worm to man, and much in you is still worm. Once you were apes, and even now man is more of an ape than any ape.

But even the wisest among you is only a discord, a hybrid of plant and ghost. But do I tell you to become ghosts or plants?

Look, I teach you the overman!

The overman is the meaning of the earth. Let your will say: the overman *shall be* the meaning of the earth!

I implore you, my brothers, *remain faithful to the earth*, and do not believe those who speak to you of hopes beyond the earth! They are poisoners, whether they know it or not.

They are despisers of life, dying off and poisoned themselves, and the earth is weary of them: so let them go!

Once, blasphemy against God was the greatest blasphemy; but God died, and these blasphemers died with him. To blaspheme against the earth is now the most terrible thing — and to honor the bowels of the unfathomable more highly than the meaning of the earth!

Once the soul looked on the body with contempt: and in those days that contempt was the highest thing — the soul wanted the body thin, hideous, starved. In that way it thought it could slip away from the body and from the earth.

Oh, that soul was itself still thin, hideous, and starved: and cruelty was that soul’s lust!

But you too, my brothers, tell me: what does your body say about your soul? Is your soul not poverty and filth and a wretched contentment?

Truly, man is a polluted river. One must be a sea to take in a polluted river without becoming unclean.

Look, I teach you the overman: he is this sea; in him your great contempt can go under.

What is the greatest thing you can experience? It is the hour of the great contempt. The hour in which even your happiness turns to disgust for you, and your reason and your virtue as well.

The hour when you say: “What does my happiness matter! It is poverty and filth and a wretched contentment. But my happiness ought to justify existence itself!”

The hour when you say: “What does my reason matter! Does it crave knowledge as the lion craves its food? It is poverty and filth and a wretched contentment!”

The hour when you say: “What does my virtue matter! It has never yet made me rage. How tired I am of my good and my evil! All of that is poverty and filth and a wretched contentment!”

The hour when you say: “What does my justice matter! I do not see that I am fire and fuel. But the just man is fire and fuel!”

The hour when you say: “What does my pity matter! Is pity not the cross to which he is nailed who loves mankind? But my pity is no crucifixion.”

Have you ever spoken like this? Have you ever cried out like this? Ah, if only I had heard you cry out like this!

Not your sin — your easy satisfaction cries out to heaven; your stinginess even in your sinning cries out to heaven!

Where, then, is the lightning that would lick you with its tongue? Where is the madness you ought to be inoculated with?

Look, I teach you the overman: he is this lightning, he is this madness! —

When Zarathustra had spoken like this, someone in the crowd shouted: “We’ve heard enough about the tightrope walker; now let’s see him too!” And all the people laughed at Zarathustra. But the tightrope walker, who thought the words were meant for him, set to work.

IV

But Zarathustra looked at the people and wondered. Then he spoke like this:

Man is a rope, tied between beast and overman — a rope over an abyss.

A dangerous going-over, a dangerous on-the-way, a dangerous looking-back, a dangerous shuddering and standing-still.

What is great in man is that he is a bridge and not a goal: what can be loved in man is that he is a *going over* and a *going under*.

I love those who do not know how to live except by going under, for they are the ones who go over.

I love the great despisers, because they are the great reverers, and arrows of longing for the other shore.

I love those who do not first look behind the stars for a reason to go under and to be a sacrifice, but who sacrifice themselves to the earth, so that the earth may one day belong to the overmen.

I love the one who lives in order to know, and who wants to know so that one day the overman may live. And so he wants his going under.

I love the one who works and invents so that he may build a house for the overman and make ready earth, animal, and plant for him: for that is how he wants his going under.

I love the one who loves his virtue: for virtue is the will to go under, and an arrow of longing.

I love the one who does not hold back a single drop of spirit for himself, but wants to be wholly the spirit of his virtue: so he strides over the bridge as spirit.

I love the one who makes his virtue his bent and his fate: so for the sake of his virtue he wants to live on, and to live no longer.

I love the one who does not want too many virtues. One virtue is more virtue than two, because it is more of a knot for fate to hang on.

I love the one whose soul squanders itself, who wants no thanks and gives nothing back: for he always gives and does not want to keep himself.

I love the one who is ashamed when the dice fall in his favor, and who then asks: am I a cheat, then? — for he wants to perish.

I love the one who throws golden words out ahead of his deeds and always does still more than he promises: for he wants his going under.

I love the one who justifies the people of the future and redeems the people of the past: for he wants to perish of the people of the present.

I love the one who chastises his god because he loves his god: for he must perish of his god’s wrath.

I love the one whose soul is deep even in its wounds, and who can perish of a small experience: so he gladly goes over the bridge.

I love the one whose soul is so overfull that he forgets himself, and all things are in him: so all things become his going under.

I love the one who is free in spirit and free in heart: so his head is only the bowels of his heart, but his heart drives him to his going under.

I love all those who are heavy drops, falling one by one from the dark cloud that hangs over mankind: they announce that the lightning is coming, and they perish as its heralds.

Look, I am a herald of the lightning and a heavy drop from the cloud: but this lightning is called overman. —

V

When Zarathustra had spoken these words, he looked at the people again and fell silent. “There they stand,” he said to his heart, “there they laugh: they do not understand me; I am not the mouth for these ears.

Must one first smash their ears, so that they learn to hear with their eyes? Must one rattle like kettledrums and preachers of repentance? Or do they believe only a man who stammers?

They have something they are proud of. What is it they call the thing that makes them proud? They call it culture; it sets them apart from the goatherds.

That is why they do not like to hear the word ‘contempt’ spoken of themselves. So I will speak to their pride.

So I will speak to them of the most contemptible thing of all: and that is the *last man*.”

And Zarathustra spoke to the people like this:

It is time for man to set himself his goal. It is time for man to plant the seed of his highest hope.

His soil is still rich enough for it. But one day this soil will be poor and tame, and no tall tree will be able to grow from it anymore.

Woe! The time is coming when man will no longer shoot the arrow of his longing out beyond man, and the string of his bow will have forgotten how to whir!

I tell you: you must still have chaos in you to give birth to a dancing star. I tell you: you still have chaos in you.

Woe! The time is coming when man will give birth to no more stars. Woe! The time of the most contemptible man is coming, the man who can no longer hold himself in contempt.

Look! I show you the *last man*.

“What is love? What is creation? What is longing? What is a star?” asks the last man, and blinks.

The earth has grown small by then, and on it hops the last man, who makes everything small. His kind is as impossible to wipe out as the earth flea; the last man lives longest.

“We have invented happiness,” say the last men, and they blink.

They have left the places where it was hard to live: for they need warmth. They still love their neighbor and rub up against him: for they need warmth.

Getting sick and being suspicious count as sins to them: they go about carefully. What a fool, whoever still stumbles over stones or people!

A little poison now and then: that makes for pleasant dreams. And a lot of poison at the end, for a pleasant death.

They still work, for work is entertainment. But they take care that the entertainment does not tire them out.

No one becomes poor or rich anymore: both are too much bother. Who still wants to rule? Who still wants to obey? Both are too much bother.

No shepherd and one herd! Everyone wants the same thing, everyone is the same: whoever feels differently goes into the madhouse of his own accord.

“In the old days the whole world was mad,” say the most refined of them, and they blink.

They are clever and know everything that has ever happened: so there is no end to their mockery. They still quarrel, but they soon make up — otherwise it upsets the stomach.

They have their little pleasure for the day and their little pleasure for the night: but they have great respect for health.

“We have invented happiness,” say the last men, and they blink —

And here ended the first speech of Zarathustra, which is also called “the Prologue”: for at this point the shouting and glee of the crowd interrupted him. “Give us this last man, oh Zarathustra,” they cried, “make us into these last men! Then we will make you a present of the overman!” And all the people cheered and smacked their lips. But Zarathustra grew sad and said to his heart:

They do not understand me: I am not the mouth for these ears.

I must have lived too long in the mountains; I listened too much to brooks and trees: now I talk to them as if they were goatherds.

My soul is unmoved and bright as the mountains in the morning. But they think I am cold, a mocker who makes terrible jokes.

And now they look at me and laugh: and as they laugh, they hate me too. There is ice in their laughter.

VI

But then something happened that struck every mouth dumb and fixed every eye in a stare. For in the meantime the tightrope walker had begun his performance: he had stepped out of a little door and was walking along the rope, which was stretched between two towers so that it hung over the marketplace and the people. Just as he reached the middle of his way, the little door opened once more, and a fellow in motley, dressed like a jester, sprang out and went after the first man with quick steps. “Forward, lame-foot!” cried his terrible voice. “Forward, lazybones, sneak, pasty-face! Move, or I’ll tickle you with my heel! What are you doing here between towers? The tower is where you belong — they ought to lock you up in it! You’re blocking the open road for a better man than you!” — And with every word he came closer and closer to him: but when he was only a single step behind him, the terrible thing happened that struck every mouth dumb and fixed every eye in a stare: — he let out a shriek like a devil and leaped over the man who stood in his way. But the other, when he saw his rival win like this, lost his head and the rope; he threw away his pole and shot down into the depths faster than it, a whirlwind of arms and legs. The marketplace and the people were like the sea when a storm drives into it: everyone fled apart and over one another, and most of all where the body was bound to come crashing down.

But Zarathustra stood still, and the body fell right beside him, badly smashed and broken, but not yet dead. After a while consciousness returned to the shattered man, and he saw Zarathustra kneeling beside him. “What are you doing there?” he said at last. “I knew long ago that the devil would trip me up. Now he is dragging me off to hell: will you try to stop him?”

“On my honor, friend,” answered Zarathustra, “none of the things you speak of exist: there is no devil and no hell. Your soul will be dead even sooner than your body: so fear nothing anymore!”

The man looked up at him with distrust. “If you are telling the truth,” he said then, “then I lose nothing when I lose my life. I am not much more than an animal that has been taught to dance with blows and scraps of food.”

“Not so,” said Zarathustra. “You made danger your calling, and there is nothing in that to despise. Now you perish by your calling: for that I will bury you with my own hands.”

When Zarathustra had said this, the dying man answered no more; but he moved his hand, as if he were reaching for Zarathustra’s hand to thank him. —

VII

Meanwhile evening came, and the marketplace hid itself in darkness: then the people drifted away, for even curiosity and terror grow tired. But Zarathustra sat on the ground beside the dead man and was lost in thought: so he forgot the time. But at last night came, and a cold wind blew over the lonely man. Then Zarathustra rose and said to his heart:

Truly, a fine catch Zarathustra made today! He caught no man, but he did catch a corpse.

Human existence is uncanny, and still without meaning: a jester can become its fate.

I want to teach people the meaning of their being: which is the overman, the lightning out of the dark cloud that is man.

But I am still far from them, and my sense does not speak to their senses. To people I am still something halfway between a fool and a corpse.

Dark is the night, dark are the ways of Zarathustra. Come, you cold and stiff companion! I will carry you to where I will bury you with my own hands.

VIII

When Zarathustra had said this to his heart, he loaded the corpse onto his back and set off on his way. And he had not yet gone a hundred steps when a man crept up to him and whispered in his ear — and look! The one who spoke was the jester from the tower. “Get away from this town, oh Zarathustra,” he said; “too many people here hate you. The good and the just hate you, and they call you their enemy and despiser; the believers of the true faith hate you, and they call you a danger to the crowd. It was your good luck that they laughed at you: and truly, you talked like a jester. It was your good luck that you made friends with the dead dog; by lowering yourself like that you saved yourself, for today. But go away from this town — or tomorrow I will leap over you, a living man over a dead one.” And when he had said this, the man disappeared; but Zarathustra walked on through the dark streets.

At the town gate the gravediggers met him: they held their torch up to his face, recognized Zarathustra, and mocked him a great deal. “Zarathustra is carrying off the dead dog: good for Zarathustra, he’s turned gravedigger! For our hands are too clean for this roast. Does Zarathustra mean to steal the devil’s mouthful? Well, go ahead! And enjoy your meal! If only the devil isn’t a better thief than Zarathustra! — he’ll steal them both, he’ll gobble them both up!” And they laughed together and put their heads together.

Zarathustra said not a word to this and went on his way. When he had walked for two hours, past forests and swamps, he had heard too much of the hungry howling of the wolves, and he grew hungry himself. So he stopped at a lonely house in which a light was burning.

Hunger falls on me like a robber, said Zarathustra. In forests and swamps my hunger falls on me, and in the deep of the night.

My hunger has strange moods. Often it comes to me only after a meal, and today it did not come all day: where has it been?

And with that Zarathustra knocked at the door of the house. An old man appeared; he carried the light and asked: “Who comes to me and to my bad sleep?”

“A living man and a dead one,” said Zarathustra. “Give me something to eat and drink; I forgot to during the day. He who feeds the hungry refreshes his own soul: so says wisdom.”

The old man went away, but came back at once and offered Zarathustra bread and wine. “This is a bad place for the hungry,” he said; “that is why I live here. Animals and people come to me, the hermit. But tell your companion to eat and drink too; he is more tired than you are.” Zarathustra answered: “My companion is dead; I doubt I can talk him into it.” “That’s no concern of mine,” said the old man grumpily; “whoever knocks at my house must take what I offer him. Eat, and fare well!” —

Then Zarathustra walked for another two hours and trusted to the path and to the light of the stars: for he was used to walking at night and loved to look everything that sleeps in the face. But when the morning grayed, Zarathustra found himself in a deep forest, and no path showed itself to him anymore. Then he laid the dead man in a hollow tree above where his head would lie — for he wanted to protect him from the wolves — and laid himself down on the ground and the moss. And at once he fell asleep, tired in body but with an unmoved soul.

IX

Zarathustra slept a long time, and not only the dawn passed over his face but the morning as well. But at last his eye opened: in wonder Zarathustra looked into the forest and the stillness, in wonder he looked into himself. Then he rose quickly, like a seafarer who all at once sees land, and shouted for joy: for he saw a new truth. And he spoke to his heart like this:

A light has dawned on me: I need companions, and living ones — not dead companions and corpses that I carry with me wherever I want.

Rather, I need living companions who follow me because they want to follow themselves — and to go where I want.

A light has dawned on me: let Zarathustra speak not to the people, but to companions! Zarathustra shall not become the shepherd and the dog of a herd!

To lure many away from the herd — that is what I came for. The people and the herd shall be angry with me: Zarathustra wants the shepherds to call him a robber.

Shepherds, I say, but they call themselves the good and the just. Shepherds, I say: but they call themselves the believers of the true faith.

Look at the good and the just! Whom do they hate most? The one who breaks their tablets of values, the breaker, the lawbreaker: — but he is the creator.

Look at the believers of every faith! Whom do they hate most? The one who breaks their tablets of values, the breaker, the lawbreaker: — but he is the creator.

The creator seeks companions, not corpses, and not herds and believers either. The creator seeks fellow creators, those who write new values on new tablets.

The creator seeks companions, and fellow harvesters: for everything around him stands ripe for the harvest. But he lacks the hundred sickles: so he tears off the ears of grain by hand and is annoyed.

The creator seeks companions, and those who know how to whet their sickles. They will be called destroyers and despisers of good and evil. But they are the harvesters and the celebrators.

Zarathustra seeks fellow creators; Zarathustra seeks fellow harvesters and fellow celebrators: what has he to do with herds and shepherds and corpses!

And you, my first companion, farewell! I buried you well in your hollow tree; I hid you well from the wolves.

But I am leaving you; the time is up. Between dawn and dawn a new truth came to me.

I am not to be a shepherd, nor a gravedigger. I will not even speak with the people again; for the last time I have spoken to a dead man.

I will join the creators, the harvesters, the celebrators: I will show them the rainbow and all the stairways of the overman.

I will sing my song to those who live alone and to those who live alone together; and whoever still has ears for the unheard-of, I will make his heart heavy with my happiness.

I am bound for my goal, I go my own way; over the hesitant and the dawdlers I will leap. So let my going be their going under!

X

Zarathustra had said this to his heart when the sun stood at noon: then he looked up questioningly — for above him he heard the sharp cry of a bird. And look! An eagle was sweeping through the air in wide circles, and a serpent hung from it, not like prey but like a friend: for she kept herself coiled around his neck.

“They are my animals!” said Zarathustra, and his heart was glad.

“The proudest animal under the sun and the shrewdest animal under the sun — they have gone out scouting.

They want to find out whether Zarathustra is still alive. Truly, am I still alive?

I found it more dangerous among men than among animals; Zarathustra walks dangerous paths. May my animals lead me!”

When Zarathustra had said this, he remembered the words of the saint in the forest, sighed, and spoke to his heart like this:

If only I were shrewder! If only I were shrewd to the very core, like my serpent!

But I am asking for the impossible: so I ask my pride instead to go always with my shrewdness!

And if one day my shrewdness leaves me — ah, it loves to fly away! — then may my pride still fly with my folly!

— Thus began Zarathustra’s going under.
