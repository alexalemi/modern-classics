On the Believers in Worlds Behind

Once Zarathustra too cast his delusion beyond man, like all the believers in worlds behind. Back then the world seemed to me the work of a suffering and tormented god.

Back then the world seemed to me a dream, and the poem of a god; colored smoke before the eyes of a divinely dissatisfied one.

Good and evil and pleasure and pain and I and you — colored smoke, it seemed to me, before creative eyes. The creator wanted to look away from himself — so he created the world.

It is a drunken pleasure for the sufferer to look away from his suffering and to lose himself. Drunken pleasure and losing-oneself: that is what the world once seemed to me.

This world, the eternally imperfect one, the image of an eternal contradiction, and an imperfect image — a drunken pleasure for its imperfect creator: — that is what the world once seemed to me.

So I too once cast my delusion beyond man, like all the believers in worlds behind. Beyond man in truth?

Ah, brothers, this god whom I created was man’s work and man’s madness, like all gods!

He was man, and only a poor piece of man and of the “I”: out of my own ashes and embers this ghost came to me, and truly! It did not come to me from beyond!

What happened, my brothers? I overcame myself, the sufferer; I carried my own ashes up the mountain; I invented a brighter flame for myself. And look! Then the ghost *fled* from me!

It would be suffering for me now, and torment for the one who has recovered, to believe in such ghosts: it would be suffering for me now, and humiliation. This is how I speak to the believers in worlds behind.

It was suffering and incapacity — that is what created all worlds behind; and that brief madness of happiness that only the one who suffers most experiences.

Weariness, which wants to reach the ultimate in one leap, in one death-leap; a poor, ignorant weariness that no longer even wants to want: that is what created all gods and worlds behind.

Believe me, my brothers! It was the body that despaired of the body — it groped at the ultimate walls with the fingers of a deluded spirit.

Believe me, my brothers! It was the body that despaired of the earth — it heard the belly of being speaking to it.

And then it wanted to push its head through the ultimate walls, and not only its head — over into “that other world.”

But “that other world” is well hidden from man, that dehumanized, inhuman world which is a heavenly nothing; and the belly of being does not speak to man at all, except as man.

Truly, all being is hard to prove and hard to get to speak. Tell me, brothers, is not the strangest of all things still the best proved?

Yes, this “I,” and the contradiction and confusion of the “I,” still speak most honestly of its being — this creating, willing, valuing “I,” which is the measure and value of things.

And this most honest being, the “I” — it speaks of the body, and it still wants the body, even when it makes poems and raves and flutters about on broken wings.

It learns to speak more and more honestly, the “I”: and the more it learns, the more words and honors it finds for the body and the earth.

My “I” taught me a new pride, and I teach it to mankind: no longer to bury your head in the sand of heavenly things, but to carry it freely, an earthly head, which creates the meaning of the earth!

I teach mankind a new will: to want this way that man has walked blindly, and to call it good, and no longer to slink off to the side of it, like the sick and the dying!

It was the sick and the dying who despised the body and the earth and invented the heavenly realm and the redeeming drops of blood: but even these sweet and gloomy poisons they took from the body and the earth!

They wanted to escape their misery, and the stars were too far away for them. So they sighed: “Oh, if only there were heavenly paths by which to sneak into another being and another happiness!” — so they invented their sneaking ways for themselves, and their bloody little potions!

Now they imagined themselves carried away from their body and this earth, these ingrates. Yet to whom did they owe the spasm and the bliss of their rapture? To their body and this earth.

Zarathustra is gentle with the sick. Truly, he is not angry at their kinds of comfort and ingratitude. May they become convalescents and overcomers, and create a higher body for themselves!

Nor is Zarathustra angry with the convalescent when he looks tenderly back at his delusion and creeps around the grave of his god at midnight: but to me even his tears are still sickness and a sick body.

There have always been many sickly people among those who make poems and crave God; they furiously hate the seeker of knowledge and that youngest of the virtues, which is called: honesty.

They always look back toward dark ages: then, of course, delusion and faith were a different thing; the raving of reason was likeness to God, and doubt was sin.

I know these godlike ones all too well: they want people to believe in them, and doubt to be sin. All too well I also know what they themselves believe in most.

Truly not in worlds behind and redeeming drops of blood: rather, they too believe most in the body, and their own body is their thing in itself.

But to them it is a sickly thing: and they would gladly jump out of their skin. That is why they listen to the preachers of death, and themselves preach worlds behind.

Listen instead, my brothers, to the voice of the healthy body: it is a more honest and purer voice.

More honestly and more purely speaks the healthy body, the perfect body, built square and true: and it speaks of the meaning of the earth.

Thus spoke Zarathustra.
