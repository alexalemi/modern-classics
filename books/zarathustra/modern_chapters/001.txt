On the Three Metamorphoses

Three metamorphoses of the spirit I name for you: how the spirit becomes a camel, the camel a lion, and the lion at last a child.

There are many heavy things for the spirit, for the strong, load-bearing spirit in which reverence dwells: its strength longs for the heavy and the heaviest.

What is heavy? asks the load-bearing spirit; and so it kneels down, like the camel, and wants to be well loaded.

What is the heaviest thing, you heroes? asks the load-bearing spirit, so that I may take it upon myself and rejoice in my strength.

Is it not this: to humble yourself, in order to wound your pride? To let your folly shine out, in order to mock your wisdom?

Or is it this: to part from our cause just when it celebrates its victory? To climb high mountains in order to tempt the tempter?

Or is it this: to feed on the acorns and grass of knowledge, and for the sake of truth to suffer hunger in the soul?

Or is it this: to be sick, and send the comforters home, and make friends with the deaf, who never hear what you want?

Or is it this: to step into dirty water, if it is the water of truth, and not to push away cold frogs and hot toads?

Or is it this: to love those who despise us, and to hold out a hand to the ghost when it wants to frighten us?

All these heaviest things the load-bearing spirit takes upon itself: like the camel that hurries into the desert with its load, so it hurries into its desert.

But in the loneliest desert the second metamorphosis takes place: here the spirit becomes a lion; it wants to seize freedom as its prey and be master in its own desert.

Here it seeks out its last master: it wants to become his enemy and the enemy of its last god; it wants to wrestle for victory with the great dragon.

What is the great dragon that the spirit no longer wants to call master and god? “You-shall” is the name of the great dragon. But the spirit of the lion says “I will.”

“You-shall” lies in its path, glittering with gold, a scaly beast, and on every scale “You-shall!” shines in gold.

Values a thousand years old shine on these scales, and so speaks the mightiest of all dragons: “All the value of things — it shines on me.”

“All value has already been created, and all created value — that is me. Truly, there shall be no more ‘I will’!” So speaks the dragon.

My brothers, why is the lion needed in the spirit? Why is the beast of burden not enough, the one that renounces and is reverent?

To create new values — even the lion cannot yet do that: but to create freedom for itself for new creating — that the lion’s might can do.

To create freedom for itself, and a holy No even to duty: for that, my brothers, the lion is needed.

To take for itself the right to new values — that is the most terrible taking for a load-bearing and reverent spirit. Truly, to such a spirit it is robbery, and the business of a beast of prey.

Once it loved the “You-shall” as its holiest thing: now it must find delusion and caprice even in the holiest, so that it may steal its freedom away from its love: it takes a lion for this robbery.

But tell me, my brothers, what can the child still do that even the lion could not? Why must the preying lion still become a child?

The child is innocence and forgetting, a new beginning, a game, a wheel rolling out of itself, a first movement, a holy Yes.

Yes, for the game of creating, my brothers, a holy Yes is needed: the spirit now wills *its own* will; the one who was lost to the world now wins *his own* world.

Three metamorphoses of the spirit I have named for you: how the spirit became a camel, the camel a lion, and the lion at last a child. —

Thus spoke Zarathustra. And at that time he was staying in the town that is called: the Motley Cow.
