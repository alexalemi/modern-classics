On Child and Marriage

I have a question for you alone, my brother: like a sounding lead I cast this question into your soul, so that I may know how deep it is.

You are young and you wish for a child and marriage. But I ask you: are you a man who is *allowed* to wish for a child?

Are you the victorious one, the self-conqueror, the commander of your senses, the master of your virtues? That is what I ask you.

Or is it the animal and need that speak out of your wish? Or loneliness? Or discord with yourself?

I want your victory and your freedom to long for a child. You shall build living monuments to your victory and your liberation.

You shall build beyond yourself. But first you must be built yourself, square and true in body and soul.

You shall not only plant yourself onward, but upward! May the garden of marriage help you to do it!

You shall create a higher body, a first motion, a wheel rolling out of itself — you shall create a creator.

Marriage: that is what I call the will of two to create the one that is more than those who created it. Reverence for each other, as for those who will such a will — that is what I call marriage.

Let this be the meaning and the truth of your marriage. But what the many-too-many call marriage, these superfluous ones — ah, what shall I call that?

Ah, this poverty of the soul for two! Ah, this filth of the soul for two! Ah, this wretched contentment for two!

Marriage, they call all this; and they say their marriages are made in heaven.

Well, I do not like it, this heaven of the superfluous! No, I do not like them, these animals tangled together in heaven’s net!

And let the god keep far from me, too, who comes limping up to bless what he did not join together!

Do not laugh at such marriages! What child would not have reason to weep over its parents?

This man seemed worthy to me, and ripe for the meaning of the earth: but when I saw his wife, the earth seemed to me a madhouse, a home for the senseless.

Yes, I wish the earth would shake in convulsions when a saint and a goose mate with each other.

This one set out like a hero in search of truths, and in the end he carried off as his prize a little dressed-up lie. His marriage, he calls it.

That one was standoffish with people and choosy in his choosing. But all at once he spoiled his company once and for all: his marriage, he calls it.

That one looked for a maid with the virtues of an angel. But all at once he became the maid of a woman, and now he would need to become an angel on top of it.

I have found all buyers careful, and all of them have cunning eyes. But even the most cunning of them still buys his wife in a sack, sight unseen.

Many short follies — that is what you call love. And your marriage puts an end to many short follies, as one long stupidity.

Your love for woman, and woman’s love for man: ah, if only it were pity for suffering and veiled gods! But mostly two animals sniff each other out.

But even your best love is only an ecstatic parable and a painful fire. It is a torch that should light your way to higher paths.

One day you shall love beyond yourselves! So first *learn* to love! And that is why you had to drink the bitter cup of your love.

There is bitterness in the cup of even the best love: that is how it stirs longing for the overman, that is how it stirs thirst in you, the creator!

Thirst in the creator, arrow and longing for the overman: tell me, my brother, is this your will to marriage?

Holy, I call such a will and such a marriage. —

Thus spoke Zarathustra.
