On the Virtuous

One must speak with thunder and heavenly fireworks to slack and sleeping senses.

But the voice of beauty speaks softly: it steals only into the most awakened souls.

Softly my shield trembled and laughed for me today; that is the holy laughing and trembling of beauty.

It was at you, you virtuous ones, that my beauty laughed today. And its voice came to me like this: “They still want — to be paid!”

You still want to be paid, you virtuous ones! You want reward for virtue, and heaven for earth, and the eternal for your today?

And now you are angry with me because I teach that there is no rewarder and no paymaster? And truly, I do not even teach that virtue is its own reward.

Ah, this is my sorrow: reward and punishment have been lied into the ground of things — and now into the ground of your souls as well, you virtuous ones!

But like the snout of the boar my word shall tear open the ground of your souls; I want to be a plowshare to you.

All the secrets of your ground shall come to light; and when you lie rooted up and broken in the sun, your lie too will have been sorted out from your truth.

For this is your truth: you are *too clean* for the filth of the words: revenge, punishment, reward, retribution.

You love your virtue as a mother loves her child; but when did anyone hear of a mother who wanted to be paid for her love?

Your virtue is your dearest Self. The ring’s thirst is in you: every ring struggles and turns to reach itself again.

And every work of your virtue is like a star that dies out: its light is always still on its way and wandering — and when will it no longer be on its way?

So the light of your virtue is still on its way, even when the work is done. Let the work be forgotten and dead: its ray of light still lives and wanders.

That your virtue should be your Self and not something foreign, a skin, a cloak: that is the truth from the ground of your soul, you virtuous ones! —

But there are, to be sure, those for whom virtue means the cramp under a whip: and you have listened too much to their crying!

And there are others who call it virtue when their vices grow lazy; and when their hatred and their jealousy stretch their limbs for once, their “justice” wakes up and rubs its sleepy eyes.

And there are others who are dragged downward: their devils drag them. But the deeper they sink, the more fiercely their eye glows, and their craving for their god.

Ah, their crying too has reached your ears, you virtuous ones: “What I am *not*, that, that is God and virtue to me!”

And there are others who come along heavy and creaking, like wagons carrying stones downhill: they talk a great deal of dignity and virtue — they call their brake virtue!

And there are others who are like everyday clocks that have been wound up; they go tick-tock and want tick-tock to be called — virtue.

Truly, these give me my delight: wherever I find such clocks, I will wind them up with my mockery; and they shall purr for me while I do it!

And others are proud of their handful of justice, and for its sake commit outrages against all things: so that the world is drowned in their injustice.

Ah, how badly the word “virtue” dribbles out of their mouths! And when they say, “I am just,” it always sounds like, “I am just getting even!”

With their virtue they want to scratch out their enemies’ eyes; and they lift themselves up only to bring others down.

And then again there are those who sit in their swamp and speak out of the reeds like this: “Virtue — that means sitting still in the swamp.

We bite no one, and we get out of the way of anyone who wants to bite; and in all things we hold the opinion that is given to us.”

And then again there are those who love gestures and think: virtue is a kind of gesture.

Their knees are always worshiping, and their hands are hymns of praise to virtue, but their heart knows nothing of it.

And then again there are those who think it is virtue to say: “Virtue is necessary”; but at bottom they believe only that the police are necessary.

And many a man who cannot see what is high in people calls it virtue that he sees what is low in them all too closely: so he calls his evil eye virtue.

And some want to be edified and set upright, and call it virtue; and others want to be knocked over — and call that virtue too.

And in this way almost everyone believes he has a share in virtue; and at the very least, each one wants to be an expert on “good” and “evil.”

But Zarathustra did not come to say to all these liars and fools: “What do *you* know of virtue! What *could* you know of virtue!” —

But rather so that you, my friends, might grow weary of the old words you have learned from the fools and liars:

Grow weary of the words “reward,” “retribution,” “punishment,” “revenge in justice” —

Grow weary of saying: “What makes an action good is that it is selfless.”

Ah, my friends! That *your* Self be in the action as the mother is in the child: let that be *your* word for virtue!

Truly, I have taken from you a hundred words or so, and your virtue’s dearest toys; and now you are angry with me, the way children are angry.

They were playing by the sea — then the wave came and swept their toys away into the deep: now they cry.

But the same wave shall bring them new toys and pour out new colorful shells before them!

So they will be comforted; and like them, you too, my friends, shall have your comforts — and new colorful shells! —

Thus spoke Zarathustra.
