Friedrich Nietzsche (1844–1900) had been a professor of classical philology at Basel at twenty-four and had retired with bad health at thirty-four. By the early 1880s he was living on a small pension, moving between boarding houses in Switzerland and Italy, half-blind and often ill, and writing books that hardly sold. In August 1881, walking by a lake near Sils-Maria in the Swiss Alps, he had the idea he called the eternal recurrence: the thought that everything that happens will happen again, exactly the same, forever. Thus Spoke Zarathustra grew out of it. He wrote the first part in about ten days in early 1883 and published the book in four parts between 1883 and 1885. The fourth part was printed at his own expense in a few dozen copies, most of which he kept.

Zarathustra is the Persian prophet the Greeks called Zoroaster. Nietzsche chose him because, as he later explained, Zarathustra was the first to make the struggle between good and evil the engine of the world, and so he should be the first to see past it. The book is not an argument laid out in steps. It is a story told in short speeches, songs and parables: a prophet comes down from his mountain after ten years alone, tells a crowd that God is dead and that man is something that must be overcome, is laughed at, gathers a few disciples, sends them away, and climbs back up, several times, as he works toward the hardest of his ideas.

The style imitates Luther's German Bible on purpose: "Thus spoke Zarathustra" closes most of the speeches the way a refrain closes a psalm, and there are parables, beatitudes, a last supper and a donkey. The parody is the point, and much of the book is funny. Some of its terms need a word. The overman is Nietzsche's Übermensch, the higher type he says humanity should be a bridge toward; the word is kept as "overman" because the book plays it against "going under" from its first page. The will to power, the last man and the eternal recurrence are his own phrases, and each is explained by the book itself.

Nietzsche collapsed in Turin in January 1889 and spent his last eleven years unable to work. His sister, Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche, controlled his papers and shaped his reputation, later presenting him as a prophet of German nationalism; he had broken with her husband over his antisemitism. This edition is translated from Nietzsche's German, with Thomas Common's 1909 English translation consulted for sense only. Common wrote in the language of the King James Bible; the German is plainer than that, and so is this.
