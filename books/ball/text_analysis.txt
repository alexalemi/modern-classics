STAR-LAND — Sir Robert S. Ball, 1889
Translation strategy

WHAT THIS IS
The Royal Institution Christmas courses of 1881 and 1887, given by the
Royal Astronomer of Ireland to an audience of children and worked up
into a book. It is the fifth Royal Institution Christmas Lecture volume
in this collection, and by some distance the warmest. Read the
soap-bubbles/, candle/, forces/ and fleming/ analyses first: the
register is the same family, but Ball is the funniest of the five and
the only one who is openly delighted by his own subject.

Six lectures — the Sun, the Moon, the inner planets, the giant planets,
comets and shooting stars, the stars — plus a closing chapter that
teaches the reader to actually find the constellations. ~98k words,
36 files, 94 plates.

VOICE
Ball talks TO children, never down to them. The distinguishing marks:

- He is constantly doing something. A burning glass, a noonday gun, a
  ball thrown in the air, two eyes and a finger held at arm's length,
  playing cards laid on a table to make a base-line. Keep every
  demonstration concrete and in the present tense.
- He is funny, and the jokes are load-bearing. His figure captions are
  often the punchline ("This is what we wanted the Cards for", "Two
  Eyes are better than One"). PRESERVE THE HUMOUR. A flattened Ball is
  a failed translation, more so than a slightly long one.
- He uses vast comparisons to make numbers mean something — an express
  train that would take so many years to reach the sun, a cannon ball
  fired at the moon. These are the heart of the book. Render them with
  their arithmetic intact and their theatre intact.
- Direct address is constant: "you will see", "I want you to notice",
  "suppose that you and I". Keep all of it. Keep "Star-land" itself,
  which is his coinage and the book's whole conceit.
- He is Irish and writing for a British audience in 1889; the diction
  is Victorian but the sentences are already short by Victorian
  standards. Much of this book needs light work, not heavy rebuilding.

THE VERNE RULE APPLIES THROUGHOUT
This is 1889 astronomy and a great deal of it has since been overturned
or refined. Render every figure and every claim as HIS, unhedged, with
no editorial correction and no wink to the reader:
  - the sun's distance, size and temperature as he gives them
  - Vulcan, the intra-Mercurial planet, discussed as an open question
  - the nature and origin of the lunar craters
  - speculation about life in the moon and on the planets, which he
    treats seriously and answers carefully
  - the nebulae, and what they are made of
He is often careful to say what is known versus what is guessed. Keep
that distinction exactly where he puts it — it is the best thing in the
book's method, and blurring it would misrepresent him.
Modernize dated WORDS freely (that is vocabulary, not a claim), but
never a dated measurement or conclusion. See fleming/ for the same
split applied to the aether.

NUMBERS
Every measured quantity keeps its digits exactly as printed. Do not
spell measured values out as words — that slip passes every mechanical
check and was only caught on fleming/ by a numeric-token diff. Run that
check per file (recipe in running_notes.txt).

FIGURES
Ball captioned his own plates, and the captions are in the source, so
they arrive in chapters/ as [Figure N: caption]. MODERNIZE them, do not
replace them — the wit is his. Where a caption is only a label
("A Pendulum") it can be lightly extended if the plate plainly shows
more, but never at the cost of his phrasing.
Three plates are irregular: the frontispiece (id "front", uncaptioned),
figures 29 and 30 which share one plate ("29-30" — the caption must
cover both, saying which is which), and figures 35 and 64, whose
captions carry the plate's own internal sub-labels.

CONTENT NEEDING CARE
Three passages, each a different call. All three are logged in
running_notes.txt when done.

1. Lecture V quotes an eyewitness account of the great Leonid meteor
   storm of 12-13 November 1833, from a South Carolina plantation:
   a white enslaver describing the terror of the people he held
   enslaved, who are called "the negroes" throughout. The astronomy in
   it is genuinely valuable — it is one of the best surviving
   descriptions of what that night looked like. Render the period term
   as "the enslaved people" / "the enslaved men and women". This is
   MORE explicit about what was happening, not less: a modern young
   reader would not otherwise know these were enslaved people at all.
   Do not add commentary, do not cut the passage, do not soften the
   fear it describes.
2. "some savages are able to produce fire by means of friction" —
   dated word, no claim attached. Render as people in some parts of
   the world.
3. "inhabited by painted savages, who waged incessant war with wild
   beasts" — he means pre-Roman Britons, in a lovely passage about
   light from distant stars showing us the past. Render as painted
   Britons / painted tribes. Keep the image; it is doing real work.

FAMOUS AND LOAD-BEARING PASSAGES (also in must_contain.txt)
- the burning glass and the noonday gun that opens Lecture I
- "Two eyes are better than one" and the base-line measurement
- the express train to the sun
- the phases of the moon and the old moon in the new moon's arms
- the great November meteor showers of 1833 and 1866
- the passage on how light from distant stars shows us the earth's own
  past — Magna Charta, and the forests before it
- the closing chapter's Great Bear as the key to the whole sky
