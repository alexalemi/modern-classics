Robert Stawell Ball was Royal Astronomer of Ireland, working at Dunsink Observatory outside Dublin. Twice, in 1881 and again in 1887, the Royal Institution gave him its Christmas course of lectures for children — the series Faraday had started in 1825, and the one that produced several other books in this collection. The second course was largely a repeat of the first, and Ball built this book out of his notes and recollections of both. It came out in 1889. The text here is his revised edition of ten years later, which is why a great sunspot of September 1898 turns up in the first lecture.

He is the funniest of the Royal Institution lecturers, and the jokes are doing the teaching. He sends you off on a journey towards the sun carrying a wax candle, a lead bullet, a penny, a poker and a flint, and you lose them one at a time in that order. He works out that an express train at forty miles an hour would need 265 years to reach the sun, so that a passenger who boarded in the reign of Charles I would not have arrived. He explains that the ancients had Vulcan row the sun round the north of the world each night in a boat, and objects that this was all work and no play for poor Vulcan. To measure a ball hanging over the lecture table he uses a boy, a girl, two playing cards and a pair of scissors, and the caption on the resulting diagram reads "This is what we wanted the cards for". Those captions are his own, and they are kept.

Six lectures go from the sun to the moon, out past the inner planets to Jupiter and Saturn, through the comets and the meteor showers, and finally to the stars and the nebulae, ending on a library fifty miles long that would still be too small to hold a description of the visible universe. A seventh chapter teaches you the constellations, starting from the Great Bear, and finishes by telling you not to wait for anybody: if it is clear tonight, go out and start. All ninety-four of the original plates are here.

This is the astronomy of 1889 and 1899 and a good deal of it has since been abandoned. The craters of the moon are volcanoes, there are canals on Mars, and a planet inside the orbit of Mercury is an open question. It is left as he gives it. Ball is unusually careful about the line between what is measured and what is guessed, and that grading has been kept exactly where he puts it.

One passage in the fifth lecture needs warning. To describe the great meteor storm of November 1833 he quotes an eyewitness on a South Carolina plantation: a man who held people in slavery, writing about their terror. It is quoted here in full, and the people in it are named as what they were.
