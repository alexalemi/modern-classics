Chapter Fourteen: The Mountain Years

From the fourth month of that year until the twelfth day of the
eleventh, an extraordinary fragrance came again and again, and drifted
through the monks' hall inside and out. There is a sheet in the
Founder's own hand about it.

Two sheets in his hand from about this time, addressed to the
storehouse, read:

	One. From this day forward the following is to be stopped.
	[two characters worn away]

	One. When a donor gives a meal to the monks and hands over
	money for the provisions [two characters worn away]: even if
	the common rice and the rest are drawn on, reckon up what has
	been spent, and buy rice promptly to make it good. The money is
	not to be put to any other use.

	One. What is spent is the common rice, and it is not to be laid
	out on sweetmeats and such against the coming years.

One. Rice is not to be taken and used to buy greens or other
	provisions.

	One. The common rice is not to be lent out to this person or
	that.

	One. The common rice is not to be spent on firewood and
	charcoal and the like.

	The five articles above are for the storehouse to know.

	The twenty-first day of the twelfth month, the second year of
	Hoji. Kigen, the monk who founded Eihei. [cipher]

That sheet is in the abbot's quarters at Eiheiji today.

Eiheiji hereby informs the officers. From this day forward, if a donor
offers a meal in the afternoon, it is to wait until the next day. As
for noodles, cakes, sweetmeats and the rest, they may still be served
late: they are the medicine-stone of an assembly of buddhas and
ancestors, and moreover it is the excellent custom of those who have
the way, throughout Great Song. The Tathagata once allowed the monk of
the Snow Mountains to fill his belly. This mountain likewise allows the
medicine-stone in snowy weather.

	Kigen, the monk who founded Eihei. [cipher]

That scroll is now in the abbot's quarters at Myooji at Imasu in Mino.
In the autumn of the year of the metal hare, in the Shotoku era, while
on pilgrimage, I asked the retired abbot Shozan, then in the chief
seat, to let me see it, and made a note of it. The note is entered
separately.

The third year of Hoji, the year of the cock.

On the first day of the first month there was a dharma assembly for the
offering to the arhats, and the Founder was asked to conduct it. The
arhats, both the wooden images and the painted ones, and the other
sages with them, gave out light and received the offering. In Great
Tang there were five hundred living arhats on Mount Tiantai; in our own
country, he wrote in his own hand, they are here on this mountain. The
original of that record is in the patron Yoshishige's document box.

A note of the auspicious signs shown at that time in the
	abbot's quarters at Eiheiji.

	Before the Buddha there appeared something particularly
	excellent and beautiful. The sixteen venerable ones of the
	wooden images all appeared, and the sixteen of the painted
	images all appeared. Of flowers of good omen appearing like
	this, the only instance is the stone bridge on Mount Tiantai in
	Taizhou in Great Song; on no other mountain has it ever been
	heard of. On this mountain it has now appeared several times.
	This is an auspicious sign of the highest kind. From it we may
	judge that the venerable ones look with pity on the people and
	the dharma of this mountain and this temple, and shelter them,
	and that this is why it is so.

	Kigen, the monk who founded this mountain. Presented on the day
	of the snake.

At that offering the sixteen venerable ones and all their retinue
appeared above the tall pine on the rock east of the temple, and that
old pine is called the Arhat Pine to this day. They say an arhat left
something behind on that occasion: a round fan of unusual make, still
kept secretly in the abbot's quarters. It is shaped like a palm-leaf
fan but larger, and there is nothing like it now.

Considering it: when the Founder was in Song, he met an old monk in
front of the arhat hall at Jingshan, and it was on that monk's
direction that he went to see Rujing. That must have been an arhat.
After he came home he held the offering to the arhats in the manner
described above, and he composed a liturgy for the arhats with his own
hand. At the present day

From the eleventh day of the first month of that year the Rules for the
Monks' Common Room began to be read aloud, and the practice has never
lapsed since. On the first, the eleventh and the twenty-first of every
month, junior and senior alike, the monks of the temple take it in turn
to prepare hot water, tea and sweets; the assembly gathers, they drink
tea and hot water together in the common room, and then the head monk
reads it out. The point of those rules is that he set down how monks
are to stand and sit and move and be still, and the courtesies between
host and guest, in such a way that they would go into the ear of even a
foolish man.

It is handed down to this day that the Founder made a vow not to leave
Mount Kichijo for five hundred years. On the tenth day of the ninth
month he addressed the assembly:

	From now to the very end of the future, this old man of Eihei
	will be constantly among human beings, and day and night will
	not leave the bounds of this mountain. Even should I receive a
	command from the sovereign, I vow that I will not go out from
	this mountain. And what is the meaning of that? Only that I
	wish to spend

day and night without a break in strenuous practice, and pile
	up merit and heap up virtue by walking the way. By that merit I
	would first carry across all living beings, so that they may
	see the Buddha and hear the dharma and come to rest inside the
	cave of the buddhas and ancestors. And at the very last this
	old man of Eihei will sit under the tree of awakening, break
	Mara the tempter, throw the great matter open, and complete the
	highest awakening. Wishing to say this over again, he put it in
	a verse:

	The old buddhas did most of their practice in the mountains;
	spring, autumn, winter and summer, they lived in the mountains.
	Eihei would follow in those old footprints:
	through all twelve hours of the day, always in the mountains.

The first year of Kencho, the year of the cock. The era changed on the
eighteenth day of the third month.

That mid-autumn, while he was watching the moon, a monk standing by
drew him from life as he looked up at it, and asked him for an
inscription. The painting with his inscription survives as a large
scroll, now at Hokyoji at Ono in Echizen. It reads:

	The air fresh and clear, the mountain grown old in autumn;
	we look at one another — the bright moon afloat in the well of
	the sky.
	Nothing to lean on, and the six do not contain it;
	let it go rolling on as it will.
	Gruel enough, rice enough, alive and leaping.
	Straight overhead: above the heavens and below the heavens,
	the clouds of themselves, the water as it goes.

	Written by himself on the day of the full moon, the year of the
	cock, Kencho: Kigen, the monk who founded Eiheiji, on Kamiyama
	at Yoshida in the district of Yoshida in Echizen.

The tenth book of the Extensive Record carries this inscription, with
some small differences of wording: where the Record has "the one does
not contain it" for "the six do not contain it," the Record is wrong.
Since it says the day of the full moon, it must certainly have been
mid-autumn, and the face in the portrait is looking up at the moon.

There was also a petition of his, in one sheet, sent up to the shogun
at Kamakura or else to the Rokuhara deputies. What seems to be the
draft of it survives in his own hand and is kept privately at Eigonji
at Tsunuga in Echizen: sixteen lines, a hundred and ninety-two
characters in all, written in a running hand. It reads:

	One. Cases like the thousand Shakya princes leaving home are
	not covered by these restrictions.

	One. The monks resident at this temple are to be excused
	attendance as chaplains in service anywhere.

	One. The monks resident at this temple are to be excused
	appearing at court in lawsuits, however good their case.

	One. This temple is to be excused appointments to the office of
	monastic superintendent by merit, and to posts of rank in other
	temples.

	One. The monks of this temple are not to accept the customary
	invitations of the great houses to preach on set occasions.

	One. The monks resident at this temple are not to be appointed
	to fund-raising posts in this or any other temple.

One. The monks resident at this temple are to be excused
	kneeling before the local steward, the constable's office or
	the administrative office to bring suit.

	One. The monks resident at this temple are not to accept
	invitations from practitioners of magical austerities.

	One. The monks resident at this temple are not to take part
	with monks of other places in conferring the tonsure.

	One. The monks resident at this temple are not to be appointed
	to serve as chantry monks or memorial monks at grave halls
	anywhere.

	The articles above are for the flourishing of the buddha-dharma,
	and I ask that an order be issued accordingly. So it stands.

	The eighteenth day of the tenth month, the first year of
	Kencho. From Eiheiji.

It is unmistakably in his hand, though there is no seal under the name;
it must be the draft.

The second year of Kencho, the year of the dog.

A letter came from the governor of Izumo, the patron who had founded
the temple, saying that he meant to have the whole of the Buddhist
canon copied out and installed at Eiheiji; and the Founder took the
high seat.

A monk once asked Touzi: is there anything remarkable in the
	whole canon of the teaching, or not? Touzi said: it is the
	canon being expounded.

	Since the old buddha Touzi spoke so, this mountain gate is
	fortunate indeed. And so there is a verse, spoken for the
	wandering monks:

	Expounding the whole canon of the teaching —
	know that in this world there must be arhats,
	and how should good and evil have no road of cause and effect?
	Great men, gods, sages and worthies,
	are fortunate to have got this charm to keep them safe.
	And just at such a moment, what then?

	After a long silence, he spoke.

When the copying was finished and the governor's letter came again with
the canon to be installed, he took the high seat once more:

	Vairocana's ocean-store, handed on from old to now;
	the wheel of the dharma turned three times over the great
	thousand worlds.
	A thousand peaks, ten thousand peaks, the colour of yellow
	leaves:
	all living beings come to the way, complete in a single moment.

The Founder made fifteen verses on living in the mountains at Kichijo.
The first three:

	How glad I am that living in the mountains is so very still.
	Because of it I read the Lotus Sutra constantly.
	Wholly given to it under the trees, what is there to hate or
	love?
	The colour of the moon to look at, the rain to listen to.

The ancestral way came from the west, and I have handed it on
	in the east:
	fishing the moon, ploughing the clouds, longing for the old
	manner.
	The red dust of the world does not blow as far as this —
	deep in the mountains, a snowy night, inside a grass hut.

	Sitting at night, the watches nearly gone, sleep not yet come:
	I know now in my bones that the way is to be worked out in
	mountains and woods.
	The sound of the valley stream in the ears, the moon reaching
	the eyes,
	and beyond that, what use is there for the mind at all?

The retired emperor Go-Saga, hearing of the Founder's reputation for
the way, granted him the purple robe. He declined it two and three
times over, and was not permitted to; and he accepted it but never
wore it. He made a verse and sent it up in thanks:

	Eihei's valley may be shallow,
	but the imperial commands come again and again.
	I am afraid the monkeys and the cranes
	will laugh at this one old man.

The third year of Kencho, the year of the boar.

A bell was constantly heard sounding deep in the mountain. The patron
wrote to ask about it, and this is the Founder's reply:

	Since you ask about it day after day, I write. It has happened
	again and again over these seven or eight years. This year, at
	the hour of the rat on the fifth day of the first month, while
	I was discussing the buddha-dharma with the lay-monk counsellor
	of the Kazan'in in the hermitage at Ryozan'in, a bell was heard,
	some two hundred strokes of it. The sound of it was deeper than
	the bell of Kiyomizudera in Higashiyama in the capital. We
	listened to it with joy, and it seemed to me solemn and holy
	beyond words. The counsellor too
	said that this is a marvellous and holy ground, and was full of
	joy at it, and it was for that that he took the tonsure.

	The lord Kaneyori, middle captain, was in the same room and did
	not hear it. Nor did his foster-brother, the lay-monk of the
	right guard, the poor priest Keihin. And of two or three women
	besides, and seven or eight retainers who were there, all say
	they heard nothing.
