What This Book Is

The founder of the Soto school of Zen in Japan, and of the great
monastery of Eiheiji in the mountains of Echizen, was a monk named
Dogen. He lived from 1200 to 1253. This is the oldest full account of
his life.

It was written in the fifteenth century by a monk called Kenzei, who
was the thirteenth abbot of Eiheiji, at the request of one of the
temple's lay patrons. Kenzei explains at the start why he wrote it the
way he did. The facts of Dogen's life, he says, were already set down
in the temple chronicle and in the collected record of Dogen's
teachings. But those were in Chinese, the learned language. Kenzei
wrote his in plain Japanese, in the simple syllabary, "so that the
children and the unlettered of this last age may read them easily."

Three and a half centuries later, in 1806, another abbot, Menzan of
Eifukuji, corrected Kenzei's text against every old document he could
find, and a group of monks had it printed from woodblocks with
seventy-one pictures. They also wrote a preface, and it says what they
were trying to do:

	Kenzei's record, written in the plain syllabary, was made in the
	first place for ordinary believers, and his kindness in it is
	complete. Beyond that, old Menzan corrected it and filled the
	gaps in it with the greatest care. And now we have gone further
	and dressed it out with pictures, setting them beside the text,
	so that people who cannot read may see it too. The whole
	intention is that ordinary men and women should have a way into
	it by this road: that they may be brought close to him, and hear
	the founder's deeds with their own ears.

	And if anyone should accuse us of peddling cheap talk, we hope
	only that it may make a strong connection with them, whether
	they take it well or ill.

This English version is made in the same spirit and for the same
reason. Everything Kenzei set down is here. The letters, the sworn
statements, the verses and the temple rules that he copied into the
record are here too, because they are the evidence the book is built
on, and because Dogen's own voice is in them. What has been left out is
Menzan's scholarly apparatus: his notes on sources, his arguments with
other historians, and his appendix. A short account of how the
illustrated edition came to be made is given at the end.

A word about names and dates. Japanese years were counted by the
reigning era name, so 1244 was "the second year of Kangen." Both are
given here. Ages are counted the old Japanese way, in which a child is
one at birth. The hours of the day were named after the animals of the
zodiac, and are given here as clock times. Dogen is called "the
Founder" throughout, as Kenzei calls him.
