Chapter Eleven: Eiheiji

On the nineteenth day of the second month of 1244 the ground for the
dharma hall of the Great Buddha Temple was levelled. On the
twenty-first day of the fourth month the foundation stones were laid
and the pillars raised, and the ridgepole went up the day after. The
lucky hour for the ceremony was worked out by Abe no Harumune, an
official of the court's Bureau of Divination.

On the eighteenth day of the seventh month the hall was opened and the
Founder preached in it for the first time. From this day, he said, let
this mountain be called Kichijozan, the Mountain of Good Fortune, and
the temple be called Daibutsuji, the Temple of the Great Buddha. And
he gave a verse:

	The great merit of all the buddhas,
	highest of all good fortunes, above which is nothing.
	All the buddhas come together and enter this place;
	therefore this ground is the most fortunate of all.

That day, while the sutras were being chanted, the dragon gods raised
clouds and brought down rain.

Until then the hill had been called Sanshoho, Parasol Pine Peak. A copy
of the lettering of the name-plaque, in the Founder's own hand, is now
in a scroll kept at Doshoan in the capital, and it reads:

	In this world, in Great Japan, in the province of Echizen, in
	the estate of Shibi in the district of Yoshida, Parasol Pine
	Peak is from this day named the Mountain of Good Fortune.
	The great merit of all the buddhas,
	highest of all good fortunes, above which is nothing.
	All the buddhas come together and enter this place;
	therefore this ground is the most fortunate of all.
	The first day of the eleventh month, the second year of Kangen.

So it stands, in eight lines. Since it says "from this day," the place
was still Parasol Pine Peak the day before, and the old record that
puts the naming in the seventh month was mistaken about the date.

Hatano Yoshishige, the patron, rejoiced with his whole heart. Among
those who came to that first assembly were the former governor of
Yamato, Kiyohara Sadahito; Minamoto no Kurando; the lay novice Jitsua
of Nojiri; and Anjo, a clerk of the guard.

After the discourse the Founder said to Hatano: look at this one piece
of ground. The main mountain to the north; Kozai along the flank to the
south; the eastern ridge running unbroken to the shrine of the mountain
god; and the stream drawing away westward towards the blue sea and the
dragon palace. When I was in China my late master at Tiantong gave me
more than ten articles on the essentials of sitting, and one of them
was this: do not build looking out on the open sea; look instead on the
water of a mountain valley. This ground answers to that. The woods and
the springs make a fine prospect, and there will always be good food in
good vessels here, and fragrant rice enough. Is this not a place for
excellent practice? It is above all a training ground for raising up
the teaching. I have been choosing among good sites from Kyushu to the
Kanto and in every quarter, north and south, and coming to this one I
have stopped.

It was named the Mountain of Good Fortune because Kichijo is a name of
the palace of the god Indra, and because when the Buddha came to
awakening he sat on a mat of kichijo grass. Now, where the ground has
been levelled and the buildings are going up, that too is good fortune.

On the first day of the ninth month the dharma hall was finished and
there was a ceremony of opening. More than a thousand men and women
came to it. From that time on the monastic rules were put into
practice.

On the seventh day of the same month an osmanthus tree arrived from
Koshoji at Uji, sent up by the senior monk Gijun. It is said to have
been planted in front of the Koun pavilion, where it stands today.

From about this time there survives a draft of a letter from the
Founder to Hatano Yoshishige. It carries no year, but it shows what
Hatano had founded the temple for:

	This temple having been founded for the salvation of the Lady of
	the Second Rank and of the Minister of the Right, and the
	fortnightly recitation of the precepts having now been begun
	here, I have respectfully received your wish that the merit be
	dedicated to them. As you are at present in the province, I
	shall see that it is done. The letter from the Rokuhara lord I
	have read with reverence, and it should be put into the temple's
	keeping at once.

	On the fifteenth of this month the recitation of the precepts
	was duly performed. With respect and awe,

	The twentieth day of the tenth month. Dogen.

The letter is at Eiheiji now, in the abbot's quarters. The Lady of the
Second Rank was Hojo Masako, the widow of the first shogun and the real
ruler of Japan in her day, and the Minister of the Right was her son
Sanetomo, the third shogun, who had been murdered on the steps of a
shrine. Hatano's house had served the Kamakura government for
generations and stood high in its favour, and it was for the salvation
of those two spirits that he founded the monastery.

On the third day of the eleventh month of that year the ridgepole of
the monks' hall was raised. This time too the dragon gods sent a little
rain and a little wind, which is the lucky sign. Hatano, who as a lay
monk took the Buddhist name Kakunen, had a deerskin spread under the
south eaves of the storehouse and watched from there, and his son
Fujiwara no Tokizumi, a lieutenant of the guard, watched from under the
west eaves. The Founder stood in front of the storehouse and saw the
ridgepole go up. The master carpenter raised the ceremonial wands, in
the five colours, and made three bows. He was given a horse. Every one
of the under-carpenters and the workmen was given a bolt of silk, and
the thousand and more men and women who had come to watch were given a
white rice-cake each.

Within one year both the dharma hall and the monks' hall were
finished. Even so, the full seven halls of a Zen monastery were never
built in the Founder's lifetime. The old record of his life says: the
timber was not yet ready, and there were barely two or three halls
standing.

In 1245, the third year of Kangen, on the sixth day of the third month,
he first set out in order the fifty-six essays of the Treasury of the
True Dharma Eye.

On the day the summer retreat opened that year, in the fourth month,
flowers came scattering down out of the sky before and after his
discourse in the hall, falling on the dharma seat and on the seated
monks and into the very tea bowls. Nothing like it, the record says,
had ever been known.

From the fifth month of that year there survives a paper in the
Founder's own hand, fifty-one characters with his cipher under the
name, written for Hatano Hironaga:

	Heaven and earth, the whole great mass, smashed into a hundred
	pieces. At the very bottom of it, no heaven and no earth.
	Turning back on myself I say: past mind cannot be got hold of;
	present mind cannot be got hold of; future mind cannot be got
	hold of.

	The fifth month of the year of the snake, Kangen. Dogen. Given
	to Hatano Hironaga.

In 1246, the fourth year of Kangen, from the discourse in the hall on
the fifteenth day of the sixth month, the temple's name was changed
from Daibutsuji to Eiheiji, the Temple of Eternal Peace. The name is
taken from the Yongping era of the Later Han dynasty, which in
Japanese is read Eihei: it was in those years that the Buddha's
teaching first came east into China. From the same day the Rules for
the Officers of the Monastery were put into practice.

A sheet in the Founder's hand from about this time, forty-six
characters in eight uneven lines with his cipher under the name, lays
down the roster for one of the temple's daily offerings:

	Concerning the monks who attend the offering of gruel before the
	Buddha at Eiheiji. First, the monk Ejo. Second, the monk
	Kakubutsu. And so on round again in turn, making the offering
	reverently.

	The tenth day of the seventh month, the fourth year of Kangen.
	Dogen, monk resident on this mountain.

On the sixth day of the eighth month of the same year he addressed the
assembly, with instructions for the kitchen. He began with the old
Chinese rule book.

	The Rules of the Zen Garden say: in the serving of a meal to
	monks, reverence is the essence. Far back, that carries on the
	way of India; nearer to hand, the way of China. In the Buddha's
	own lifetime and after his death, the gods offered the food of
	heaven to the Buddha and the monks, kings set their own dishes
	before them, and offerings came from the houses of the great and
	of ordinary people, from merchants and from labourers too. In
	every one of these offerings the reverence went into the
	smallest detail. They used the deepest bow known among gods or
	men. They kept to the most respectful forms of speech. And it was
	in that spirit that they prepared the rice and the dishes and
	all the vessels. The ways of the kitchen, and the very words used
	there, are what ought to be handed on correctly, and those who
	study the Buddha's teaching should do the same.

	So: the gruel is to be called the honourable gruel, and the
	midday meal the honourable meal. You may speak of the hour of
	the meal, but never simply of the hour. When you send word about
	the rice, say that the rice is to be washed clean and served,
	never that it is to be rinsed. Say that the honourable soup is
	to be made and served, never simply soup. Say that the honourable
	broth is to be made and served. Say of the gruel that it has been
	brought to the boil for them.

	When you are serving, never blow with your breath on the rice
	or the greens or anything else. Even if your sleeve is dry, do
	not let it touch the food. If your hand has touched your face
	and you have not washed it, do not put it on the bowls or on the
	food. From the picking over of the rice and greens, through to
	the making of the rice and broth and the serving of them, if at
	any point you have scratched an itch anywhere on your body, you
	must wash that hand.

	Where the meals are made ready, recite passages of the sutras
	and the words of the old teachers, and keep off worldly talk and
	all coarse and filthy speech. Of the rice, the greens, the salt,
	the bean paste and everything else, speak with respect: do not
	say only that there is rice, or greens. When you pass the place
	where the meal is set out, monks and lay workers alike should
	bow. Odd greens and odd grains of rice may be used, but only
	after the meals are done; nothing is to be taken from them while
	they are being served. The utensils used to make and serve the
	meals are for that only, and are to be kept carefully and used
	for nothing else. Anyone coming from a lay household whose hands
	are not yet purified must not touch the greens or the sweets.
	Only after water has been sprinkled, incense offered and the
	fire tended may the food be offered to the Three Treasures and
	the assembly. In the monasteries of China today, buns and
	milk-cakes and steamed cakes that come in from lay households
	are steamed a second time before they are set out. Steaming is
	a purifying. Until they have been steamed they are not offered.

	These instructions are a very few out of many. Take the gist of
	them and run the storehouse and the kitchen accordingly. Let
	nothing be done out of due form.

	This is the lifeline of the buddhas and ancestors, the very eye
	of a monk. Those outside the way do not know it. The demons of
	heaven cannot bear it. Only the children of the Buddha can hand
	it on. Officers of the storehouse, look to this clearly, and do
	not let it slip.

	The founding monk, Dogen.
