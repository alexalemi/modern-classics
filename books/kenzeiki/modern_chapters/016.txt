Chapter Sixteen: The Road to Kyoto

On the fifth day of the eighth month of 1253 the Founder went up to the
capital. Because of his illness, Hatano Yoshishige had pressed him
again and again to come, so that he could be seen by the physicians
there, and Ejo went with him.

The record of his last words says that on the sixth day of the eighth
month, at the lodging at Wakimoto on the road, Gikai was given leave to
go back to the temple, and took the chance to ask him a question. It is
hard, he said, that I should not attend you this time. What I really
want is to follow you back to the temple. But if there is any delay,
may I come up to the capital to see you? Will you allow it?

The master said: yes, certainly, that is quite right; there is nothing
to discuss. But it is because I am thinking of the temple that I am
leaving you behind. See to it, and look after the temple well. You are
a man of this province, and many people here know you, and there are
particular reasons, inside the temple and outside it, why you should
stay.

Gikai received this with awe. It was the last time he saw his master's
face, and the last instruction he had from him, and he never forgot
it.

The Founder made a verse on the journey:

	Ten years eating my rice on Eihei's ground;
	seven months now lying on a sickbed.
	To look for medicine I go out among men a while, over the
	ridge, to see the King of Physicians.

And a Japanese poem, on the pass over Mount Kinome, where the road
leaves Echizen:

	Setting out in a robe of grass
	over Kinome mountain,
	it feels to me
	as though there were a road through the clouds.

He was carried over the mountains in a litter, by the Tanba road. On
reaching the capital he lodged at the house of a lay disciple at
Takatsuji and Nishinotoin, in the middle of the city. His condition
neither improved nor got worse.

One day he walked slowly round the room, and as he walked he recited a
passage of the Lotus Sutra under his breath:

	Whether in a garden, or in a wood, or under a tree, or in a
	monk's quarters, or in a layman's house, or in a palace hall, or
	in a mountain valley or an empty moor: in every one of these
	places a stupa should be raised and offerings made. Why? Because
	you should know that this place is a place of the way. Here the
	buddhas attain the highest awakening. Here the buddhas turn the
	wheel of the teaching. Here the buddhas enter final nirvana.

When he had finished he wrote that passage on the pillar in front of
him, and beside it he wrote: the Hermitage of the Lotus Sutra. What he
meant by it was this. He was dying in a layman's house, in the middle
of a city, and the Buddha himself had said that even such a place is
a place of awakening.

At midnight on the twenty-eighth day of the eighth month of 1253, the
fifth year of Kencho, he wrote his own death verse and died. The verse:

	Fifty-four years
	lighting up the first heaven.
	With this one leap
	I shatter the thousandfold worlds.
	Ah!
	My whole body has nowhere to rest.
	Living, I plunge into the Yellow Springs.

He threw down the brush and passed away.

Hatano Yoshishige looked up at the sky and threw himself on the ground,
and there was nothing like his grief at a man dying so early, at
fifty-four. The monks and the lay people and the disciples he left
could not stop crying. Ejo was shattered, and for the better part of
an hour he was as one dead.

They carried the body first to a small hut in Naka no Koji, in the
Tenjin quarter of the city, and made the arrangements there. Then
Hatano looked for a suitable place, found a small temple at Akatsuji in
the eastern hills, and had the coffin carried there and the body
cremated according to the rites. The verse said at the lighting of the
pyre is preserved in the travel-record of a later monk, though nobody
now knows who composed it:

	He searched the dark road everywhere and hid nothing at all;
	the dream broke after fifty-four frosts and stars.
	The wood goes into the ashes; the tears fall like rain on
	blossom;
	and for one night the whole city smells of running water.

On the sixth day of the ninth month they gathered the relics and left
the capital, and on the tenth, in the evening, they reached the
Mountain of Good Fortune. On the twelfth, in the afternoon, the rites
of a buddha's passing were performed in the abbot's quarters. Tea and
sweets and fine food and candles were set out according to the rule,
the offerings were made, and the services and the observances of
mourning were all carried out as though he were present. His stupa is
at the north-west corner of the temple. It is the present Joyo-an.

Hatano afterwards went back to the house in the capital, took the
pillar on which the Founder had written the passage from the Lotus
Sutra, cut it free, and carried it down to Echizen. At the foot of
Mount Tsukio, on his own land, he raised a memorial stupa with that
pillar as its central post, and it is said he made offerings there
every day. It is still there, as a branch temple.

Of the Founder's Japanese poems, Kenzei lists these: the poems he made
on the night of the mid-autumn moon in the year he died; five on themes
from the Lotus Sutra; the poem on the first snow, from the ninth month
of 1244; the poems made at Kamakura at Tokiyori's request; and
thirty-four made in the grass hut at Eiheiji as they came to him. They
are collected in a separate book, and Kenzei does not copy them here.
