Chapter Nine: Koshoji at Fukakusa

At Fukakusa, in the district of Uji, there was the old site of a temple
called Gokurakuji. On it a vow was made to found a new one. It was
finished in the spring of 1233, the first year of Tenpuku, and named
Kosho Horinji. The dharma hall was built by a nun named Shogaku, and
the dharma seat inside it by the lord of Guseiin.

The monks' hall the Founder raised himself, by an appeal for
subscriptions in every direction. The appeal survives, and it is the
clearest statement anywhere of what he was trying to build.

	An appeal for subscriptions towards the monks' hall of
	Kannon-dori-in at Uji.

	Bowing my head, I respectfully address all the buddhas and
	bodhisattvas of the ten directions, the sages and the assembly
	of monks, gods and men, dragons and the eight kinds of beings,
	and all good men and good women, concerning the wish to found
	one place of practice out of the pure faith that gives a single
	coin.

	The Sutra of the Bodhisattva Precepts says: a child of the
	Buddha should always be teaching all living beings, and should
	found monks' quarters, mountains and woods and gardens and
	fields, and raise stupas, and set up places for the winter and
	summer retreats and for sitting zazen, and every place where the
	way is practised; and if he does not, he commits a minor
	offence.

	So a temple is a place of practice for all the buddhas. The
	temples of China copied the monasteries of India, and the
	training halls of Japan should learn from them in the same way.
	The merit of it is great and deep, and it is handed on from
	country to country and given out to one person after another.

	Since I came back from China I have carried the wish to found a
	temple, and the days have been long and the months deep with it.
	It was a thing my robe and bowl should have been laid down for.
	Now I have got one excellent site, out beyond Fukakusa, on the
	old ground of Gokurakuji, and it is called Kannon-dori-in. It is
	rank grass and no monastery yet. Here I mean to raise a temple
	of the first rank.

	The most necessary buildings of a temple are the buddha hall,
	the dharma hall and the monks' hall. Of these the buddha hall is
	here already. The dharma hall is not yet. And the monks' hall is
	the most needful of all, and it is this I now mean to build. Its
	form is this: a hall of seven bays, with no partitions inside
	it; long platforms set along the walls; the whole assembly of
	monks living there together, and the practice of the way not
	interrupted for a moment, day or night. In the middle the sacred
	monk is enshrined, and the assembly sits round him. The custom
	of honouring the three treasures in one hall has been kept up a
	long while. The merit of it is great, and the work of the Buddha
	will spread by it.

	One might look for a single rich donor to finish the whole
	thing. Instead I appeal widely in all ten directions, so that
	many may form a good connection with it. This is the excellent
	way of India and of China, and the manner of the monks' life in
	the age of the true teaching and the age of its likeness.

	I shall keep the donors' names in the heart of the sacred monk,
	as seed of the wisdom of the ten thousand characters, and as an
	adornment for myself and for others. If among them there should
	be one who goes ahead of the rest and attains the way, I shall
	make him the guide of this assembly. Would he not be a good
	friend indeed?

	And this does not call on men alone. It should move the heavens
	and the dragon palace too, and be heard of in the world of the
	immortals and in the halls of the dead. It is simply the wheel
	of the dharma that Shakyamuni turned, and it will reach within
	the dharma realm and beyond it. Respectfully set out.

	A day in the twelfth month of the first year of Katei. The
	chief solicitor, resident of Kannon-dori-in, a monk of the
	Shakya clan.

From that summer the retreat was formally opened.

From the same year, 1233, there survives a board with fifteen lines
cut into it, now at Myokakuji in Echizen. The brush strokes are
unmistakably the Founder's own. It is a prayer inviting the gods of
Japan to guard a temple:

	Respectfully inviting the deities of ten places as protectors of
	Myokakuji. First, Kumano. Second, Nissho. Third, Kinpusen.
	Fourth, Kamo. Fifth, Hachiman. Sixth, Gion. Seventh, Sekizan.
	Eighth, Hiyoshi. Ninth, Kifune. Tenth, Hakusan. The above are
	invited as set out. A day in the fourth month of the first year
	of Tenpuku.

There is also a wooden statue at that temple that is called a portrait
of the Founder, but it looks more like one of the patriarchs of the
Shingon school.

In 1234, the first year of Bunryaku, the monk Ejo came to see the
Founder at Fukakusa, and changed his robe. That is all the record says
of the arrival of the man who would become the Founder's closest
disciple, his heir, and the second abbot of Eiheiji.

In 1236, the second year of Katei, at the beginning of the winter
retreat, the Founder formally opened the hall at Koshoji. The assembly
was small, and he told them not to mind it.

	Do not be troubled that the assembly is small. Do not look back
	at the fact that you are a beginner. The master Fenyang had
	barely six or seven men. Yaoshan's assembly did not come to ten.
	Yet all of them practised the way of the buddhas and ancestors,
	and that is what was called a flourishing monastery.

	Have you not heard? One man came to the way at the sound of a
	pebble striking bamboo. Another cleared his mind at the sight of
	peach blossom. Is bamboo sharp or dull? Is a flower deep or
	shallow, wise or foolish? Flowers open year after year, and not
	everyone who sees them wakes up. Bamboo rings out again and
	again, and not everyone who hears it finds the way. It is only
	by the merit of long practice kept up, and by the conditions
	that come of hard work at the way, that a man wakes and his
	mind becomes clear.

	It is not that the bamboo's sound is especially sharp. It is not
	that its ring is especially deep. Call it wonderful if you like;
	it does not sound of itself. And the flower's colour is not
	especially beautiful either; it does not open by itself. The one
	waits for the pebble to strike it before it makes any sound. The
	other must have the spring wind before it opens. The conditions
	for studying the way are just like that. The way is complete in
	every person, but coming to it is still a matter of conditions.

That winter he made Ejo head monk, and gave him the whisk, which meant
that Ejo would give the next discourse in the Founder's place. The
Founder introduced him from the high seat. Do not sneer that the head
monk is not fit for it, he said. Take up Dongshan's three pounds of
flax, the famous answer to the question "what is the Buddha," and show
it to the assembly. Then he came down from the seat, the drum was
struck again, and Ejo took the whisk. That was the first such discourse
at Koshoji. Ejo was thirty-nine.

On the first day of the fifth month of the same year the Founder
handed on the great matter of transmission to the monk Gi'in. In the
course of that year, too, the monk who later founded the temple at
Yura stayed a while at Koshoji, changed his robe, and received the
bodhisattva precepts; the old history of Japanese Buddhism records it
as well. And on the fifth day of the eighth month the collected sayings
of his master Rujing reached him from China for the first time. The
next day he gave a discourse on them in the hall.

In 1237, the third year of Katei, on the day the summer retreat
closed, he compiled the Rite for Leaving Home and Conferring the
Precepts, the ceremony by which a person becomes a monk.

In 1242, the third year of Ninji, on the twelfth day of the fourth
month, he gave a dharma talk before Lord Konoe, one of the great
nobles of the court. Afterwards a question was put to him: had this
school ever come to Japan before?

He answered: since Buddhism in its outward forms first came to our
country, barely four hundred years and more have passed, and the
spreading of the school of the Buddha's own mind falls exactly now. In
China, the outward forms of Buddhism came first, in the Yongping years
of the Han, and the mind of it came afterwards. So it is with us.

On the seventeenth day of the twelfth month of that year he preached at
the private house of his patron Hatano Yoshishige, the governor of
Izumo, beside the temple of Rokuharamitsuji in the capital. The
evidence for it is the note at the end of one of his own essays, Total
Working, which stands twentieth in the Treasury of the True Dharma
Eye: given, it says, in the year of the tiger, Ninji, on the
seventeenth day of the twelfth month, to the assembly at the quarters
of the former governor of Izumo beside Rokuharamitsuji.

His time at the temple in Uji lasted eleven years, from 1233 to 1243.

Fukakusa was close to the capital, and the carriages and horses of the
court nobles and the great families came and went without a break. He
taught more than a hundred households as occasion gave him the chance,
and the disciples who took the bodhisattva precepts from him were more
than two thousand.

Saving people by whatever means come to hand is the old way of the
buddhas and ancestors. But what I want, he said, is quiet, and no
business. So he was always on the lookout for somewhere convenient
among mountains and woods, springs and rocks. Patrons who had a
connection with him offered him twelve sites, mountain and garden land,
as quiet places to settle in. But some were in distant provinces and
some were in the home provinces, and none of them suited him.

Then Hatano Yoshishige said to him: within the district of Yoshida in
Echizen there is an old temple, deep in the mountains and quiet, and it
lies inside my own estate. If you would go down there and teach, it
would be the good fortune of the whole province and of my house.

The Founder answered: my late master Rujing, the old buddha, was a man
of Yuezhou in China. Yuezhou is written Etsu in Japanese, and Echizen
means the near side of Etsu. So even to hear the name of the place is
dear to me. That is what I want, he said, and he agreed to go.
