Chapter Ten: Into Echizen

On the sixteenth day of the seventh month of 1243, the first year of
Kangen, he set out from Fukakusa and travelled north over the
mountains into Echizen. At first he stayed at Yoshimine, a small
temple in the hills, and began teaching there on the first day of the
extra seventh month that was added to the calendar that year. He
lodged at Yoshimine until the sixteenth day of the eleventh month, and
then moved to Zenjigamine, near the great Tendai temple of Heisenji,
where he stayed for the rest of the year. In those few months he
finished twelve essays for the Treasury of the True Dharma Eye: on the
nineteenth day of the eleventh month, Seeing the Buddha; on the
twenty-seventh, Thorough Practice; on the seventeenth day of the
twelfth month, The Eye, and Everyday Life; on the twenty-fifth, The
Dragon's Song. Until about the second month of the next year he seems
to have gone back and forth between Zenjigamine and Yoshimine.

On the fourth day of the second month of 1244 one of his essays records
that he spoke to the assembly "in the damp mountains of Echizen."
Whether that means on the mountain where Eiheiji now stands is not
clear.

On the twenty-fifth day of the same month he went on retreat at the
shrine of Tenjin, the god who had once been the scholar and statesman
Sugawara no Michizane. There he found a poem that Michizane had
written at eleven years old, on looking at plum blossom by moonlight,
and he was moved to answer it, line for line, in the same rhymes.
Michizane's poem runs:

	The moon shines like snow after clear weather;
	the plum blossom is like stars shining out.
	How lovely: the golden mirror turns,
	and above the garden the jade chambers are fragrant.

The Founder answered:

	The green pine: what has it to fear from winter snow?
	On the old tree the plum blossom flies like stars.

Meanwhile, wanting to found a temple, he searched about the estate for a
convenient place among the mountains and woods. East of Ichinoyama and
west of Sansho he found ground that suited a temple, and was glad of
it. On the seventeenth day of the extra seventh month of 1243 Hatano
Yoshishige had levelled the hillside and cleared the ground with his
own hands, and the Founder had moved from the thatched hut at Yoshimine
to this place. Hatano and a lay monk of the guard named Kakunen, whose
land lay in what is now the south-east district, undertook to build the
temple between them.

In 1244, the second year of Kangen, the building of the Great Buddha
Temple, Daibutsuji, was going on, and the Founder's own lodging was
perhaps not yet settled.
