Lewis Carroll was the pen name of Charles Lutwidge Dodgson (1832–1898), a mathematics lecturer at Christ Church, Oxford. Six years after *Alice's Adventures in Wonderland* he sent the same heroine through a mirror. Macmillan published the sequel in December 1871, with 1872 on the title page, and John Tenniel again drew the pictures: fifty of them, engraved on wood by the Dalziel brothers. The real Alice, Alice Liddell, was nineteen by then. The poem at the front, "Child of the pure unclouded brow", is addressed to her, and the poem at the end is an acrostic: read down, the first letters of its lines spell her full name.

Wonderland was built on playing cards; this book is built on chess. Alice climbs through the looking-glass over the drawing-room fireplace, finds the chessmen alive in the hearth, and learns that the country outside is laid out in squares like a board. She enters as a White pawn, and to become a queen she has to cross to the eighth square. The diagram and list of moves before the first chapter set out the game she plays. Each move is a scene, and the brooks she jumps are the lines between squares. Carroll admitted in a preface to later printings that the Red and White moves do not always alternate as the rules require.

Inside that frame are most of the book's best-known pieces: "Jabberwocky", whose first verse Carroll had written in a family magazine in 1855; Tweedledum and Tweedledee; "The Walrus and the Carpenter"; Humpty Dumpty explaining that a word means just what he chooses it to mean; the White Knight and his inventions. As in the first book, much of the comedy is logic taken literally. The White Queen lives backwards, remembering things before they happen, and her rule for jam is "jam to-morrow and jam yesterday—but never jam to-day." One episode is missing on purpose. Carroll cut an episode about a wasp in a wig after Tenniel told him that a wasp in a wig was beyond the powers of art. The proofs turned up at auction in 1974. That episode is not part of the 1871 book and is not printed here.

This is a restored edition: Carroll's words are unchanged. The text is the Project Gutenberg transcription, checked word by word against a scan of the first edition, Gettysburg College's copy. Where the transcription had changed or lost words, the printed ones go back, a few dozen in all, and so does a sentence of Humpty Dumpty's. Gutenberg has none of the pictures. All fifty of Tenniel's are cut here from that scan, made at 500 dots to the inch, and set where the book sets them. So are Carroll's chess diagram, the first verse of "Jabberwocky" as the book prints it, in mirror type, and the gilt designs from the red cloth covers. The edition adds a description of every picture, modern typesetting, a proper ebook, and this introduction.
