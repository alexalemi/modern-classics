KAMO NO CHŌMEI, 方丈記 (HŌJŌKI, 1212) -- STANDING BRIEF

WHAT THIS WORK IS
A scholarly modernization of a classic of Japanese literature. Kamo no
Chōmei (c. 1155-1216), a poet and musician from a family of Shinto shrine
officials in Kyoto, missed the family post, became a Buddhist monk, and at
about sixty built a hut ten feet square (方丈) on Mount Hino outside the
capital. There he wrote this short essay: the river that never holds the
same water; the disasters he lived through in the capital (the great fire
of 1177, the whirlwind of 1180, the removal of the capital to Fukuhara the
same year, the famine of 1181-82, the earthquake of 1185); his own life;
his hut and its seasons; and a closing self-examination. Source: Japanese
Wikisource (國文大觀, 1903). Old English versions exist (Sōseki 1891,
Dickins 1905); do NOT use or imitate them. Translate from the Japanese.

THE STANDARD: READABILITY IS THE DELIVERABLE
Every sentence must be one a bright fourteen-year-old could read aloud and
follow, with every clause of the source present and nothing summarised.
The prose is famous for its balanced pairs and its music: keep the
parallelism and the images (bubbles on the water, dew and the morning
glory, the hermit crab and its shell, the osprey on the shore), in plain,
beautiful modern English. Do not produce a crib.
- Where he leans on something his readers knew, fold it into the sentence
  (never a footnote): 淨名居士 is Vimalakīrti, the wise layman of the sutra
  who lived in a ten-foot room; 周梨槃特 is Cūḍapanthaka, the Buddha's
  dullest disciple; 三途 the three dark paths after death.
- DATES: give the era year and the Western year together the first time
  ("in the third year of Angen, 1177"). HOURS: plain clock times ("around
  eight in the evening" for 戌の時). Distances and measures in plain
  English equivalents (a jō is about ten feet).
- Buddhist terms in plain words unless the book turns on them: 無常 is
  "impermanence" (keep it); 念佛 "calling on the Buddha's name" the first
  time, then "the nembutsu" is fine.
- Waka (poems) quoted in the text: render as verse, each line on its own
  line starting with a TAB. The closing poem after the colophon stays.
- Place names as they are (Kyoto may be "the capital"; Fukuhara, Hino,
  Toyama, Kamo River, Uji, Mount Otowa).

FORMAT (exact)
Line 1: An Account of My Hut
Blank line. Then the translation in paragraphs (the source has almost no
breaks: make natural paragraphs at each change of subject), separated by
one blank line. No other headings. The colophon (dated the last day of the
third month, 1212, signed with his monk's name Ren'in) is its own short
paragraph at the end, then the poem.
Write hojoki/modern_chapters/000.txt.

RETURN: a paragraph list (one line each), and every date, era, name and
allusion you converted, so the editor can check them.
