This is the middle book of the poem Dante Alighieri wrote in exile between about 1308 and his death in 1321. He had been condemned in Florence in 1302 and could not go back on pain of burning. The poem is in three parts, all of them in this collection: the Inferno goes down through Hell, the Purgatorio climbs a mountain, and the Paradiso goes out through the spheres. Each can be read on its own, and this one is the part that readers who know only the Inferno are most surprised by.

Hell is a place where nothing can change. Purgatory is the opposite. Everyone on this mountain is going to be saved, knows it, and is being altered on the way up, so the suffering here — stones, sewn eyelids, smoke, fire, hunger — is entered willingly and has an end. Nobody argues his sentence. Seven terraces take the seven deadly sins in order, and Dante and Virgil climb them one at a time, stopping at nightfall because on this mountain you are not allowed to climb in the dark.

That clock is the thing to watch. This is the only one of the three parts with weather and a time of day. There is a sunrise on the shore, three nights on the steps, three dreams before dawn, and a sun whose position Dante checks constantly. It is also the warmest of the three. The dead here embrace, tease, apologise, remember meals, and ask to be prayed for. A musician sings a love song Dante himself wrote and the whole crowd stands still to listen until an old man shouts at them to get moving. A Florentine sits behind a rock making fun of the climb.

It is a poem about poets and about whether being good at anything is worth much. Dante keeps putting writers in front of the reader — one on the emptiness of fame, one converted by reading Virgil without Virgil ever knowing, one who names the new style Dante writes in. That thread pays off at the top of the mountain, where Virgil, who has brought him this far, has to stop.

The translation is from the Italian and keeps the shape: thirty-three cantos, 4,755 lines, three-line stanzas in the same number and order. The rhyme is not attempted, because terza rima cannot cross into English without padding. The Latin is left as Latin, since the souls are singing it and it is meant to be heard as singing. One exception is logged: eight lines near the summit are in Provençal, and those are given in English, because there was no room to gloss them and a hole in the poem is worse than a lost surface.
