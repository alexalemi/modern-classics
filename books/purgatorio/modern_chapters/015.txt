Canto Sixteen

	The dark of Hell, and of a night stripped
	of every planet, under a poor sky
	blackened with cloud as much as it can be,

	never made so thick a veil over my face
	as that smoke which covered us there,
	nor one so rough to the feeling,

	for the eye could not bear to stay open;
	so my knowing and faithful escort
	came close and offered me his shoulder.

	As a blind man walks behind his guide
	so as not to get lost and not to knock
	against something that hurts him, or perhaps kills him,

	so I went through that bitter and filthy air,
	listening to my guide, who kept saying
	only: "See that you are not cut off from me."

	I heard voices, and each one seemed
	to pray for peace and for mercy
	to the Lamb of God who takes away sins.

	They all began "Agnus Dei", Lamb of God;
	there was one word in all of them and one measure,
	so that all harmony seemed to be among them.

	"Are those spirits I hear, master?"
	I said. And he to me: "You understand rightly,
	and they are undoing the knot of anger."

	"Now who are you, splitting our smoke,
	and speaking of us just as though
	you still divided time by calendars?"

	So it was said by one voice;
	at which my master said: "Answer,
	and ask whether one goes up this way."

	And I: "Oh creature who make yourself clean
	to go back beautiful to him who made you,
	you will hear a marvel if you come along with me."

	"I will follow you as far as I am allowed,"
	he answered; "and if the smoke does not let us see,
	hearing will keep us joined instead."

	Then I began: "I am going up still wrapped
	in the body that death unwinds,
	and I came here through the anguish of Hell.

	And if God has shut me so inside his grace
	that he wills me to see his court
	in a way quite outside modern practice,

	do not hide from me who you were before your death,
	but tell me, and tell me if I am going right for the pass;
	and your words shall be our escort."

	"I was a Lombard, and I was called Marco, Marco Lombardo.
	I knew the world, and I loved that worth
	at which everybody now has unstrung his bow.

	For going up, you are walking straight."
	So he answered, and added: "I beg you
	to pray for me when you are up there."

	And I to him: "I bind myself by my faith
	to do what you ask me; but I am bursting
	inside with a doubt, if I do not open it out.

	It was single before, and now your saying
	has made it double, confirming for me
	here what I couple it with elsewhere.

	The world is indeed as barren of every virtue
	as you say to me,
	and pregnant with wickedness and covered by it;

	but I beg you to point out the cause to me,
	so that I may see it and may show it to others;
	for one man puts the cause in the stars and one down here."

	He first let out a deep sigh, which grief squeezed
	into an "ah!"; and then began: "Brother,
	the world is blind, and you certainly come from it.

	You who are alive refer every cause
	up to the heavens only, just as though
	they moved everything with them of necessity.

	If it were so, free will would be destroyed
	in you, and there would be no justice
	in joy for good and mourning for evil.

	The heavens set your motions going —
	I do not say all of them; but suppose I did say it,
	a light is given you for good and for wickedness,

	and free will, which, if it holds out through the labour
	of the first battles with the heavens,
	afterwards wins everything, if it is well nourished.

	You lie free under a greater power
	and a better nature, and that creates
	the mind in you, which the heavens have no charge of.

	So, if the present world goes astray,
	the cause is in you, and is to be looked for in you;
	and I will be a true spy on it for you now.

	Out of the hands of him who loves it
	before it exists, like a little girl
	who plays at crying and laughing,

	comes the simple soul, which knows nothing
	except that, being moved by a joyful maker,
	it turns gladly to whatever amuses it.

	First it tastes the flavour of some small good;
	there it is deceived and runs after it,
	unless a guide or a bridle bends its love.

	So it was necessary to set law as a bridle;
	it was necessary to have a king who could make out
	at least the tower of the true city.

	The laws exist, but who puts a hand to them?
	Nobody, because the shepherd who goes in front, the pope,
	chews the cud, like a clean beast in the old law, but has not the cloven hoof: he can meditate, but cannot tell one thing from another;

	so that the people, who see their guide
	striking only at the good they are greedy for,
	feed on that and ask for nothing more.

	You can see well that bad leadership
	is the cause that has made the world guilty,
	and not any nature corrupted in you.

	Rome, which made the world good,
	used to have two suns, the emperor and the pope, which made both roads
	plain, the world's road and God's.

	One has put out the other, and the sword
	is joined to the shepherd's crook in one hand, and the two together
	must by sheer force go badly;

	because, joined, neither one fears the other.
	If you do not believe me, look at the ear of grain,
	for every plant is known by its seed.

	In the country the Adige and the Po water,
	worth and courtesy used to be found
	before the emperor Frederick had his quarrel with the Church;

	now anyone can pass through it safely
	who would have kept away for shame
	of talking with good men or coming near them.

	It is true there are three old men still there
	in whom the old age reproves the new,
	and it seems long to them till God puts them in a better life:

	Currado da Palazzo, and the good Gherardo,
	and Guido da Castel, who is better called,
	in the French way, the plain Lombard.

	Say from now on that the Church of Rome,
	by confusing two governments in itself,
	falls in the mud, and dirties itself and its load."

	"Oh my Marco," I said, "you argue well;
	and now I see why the priests, the sons of Levi,
	were left out when the land was shared, so as not to mix the two powers.

	But which Gherardo is this whom you name
	as a sample left over from the vanished people,
	as a rebuke to this savage age?"

	"Either your speech deceives me or it is testing me,"
	he answered me; "for, speaking Tuscan to me,
	you seem to have heard nothing of the good Gherardo.

	I know no other surname for him,
	unless I took one from his daughter Gaia.
	God be with you: I come no further with you.

	See the dawn shining through the smoke
	and going white already, and I must leave
	before I am seen by the angel, who is there."

	So he turned back, and would not hear me any more.
