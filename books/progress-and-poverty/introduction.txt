Henry George was born in Philadelphia in 1839, left school at fourteen, went to sea, and ended up in California setting type for a living. He was a printer and then a newspaper editor in San Francisco, with no university training in economics or anything else, and he had been poor enough at points to know what he was writing about. He wrote this book between August 1877 and March 1879. When no publisher would take it he had an author's edition printed himself; a New York house brought it out the following year, and it then sold in the hundreds of thousands of copies and made him one of the best-known men in America.

The question is stated on the first page and it is a real one. The nineteenth century had multiplied the power to produce things — steam, railways, machinery, the telegraph — and every one of those advances had been expected to abolish want. Instead the deepest poverty had appeared exactly where the new wealth was thickest, in the big industrial cities, and the wages of ordinary labour stayed at subsistence. George wants to know why, and he does not accept that it is anybody's fault or anybody's laziness. He thinks it is structural.

His answer is that the gains go to land. Every improvement makes a given site more valuable, and because land is privately owned, the rise is collected by whoever holds the title rather than by the people whose work and numbers produced it. Labour and capital are squeezed between rising rent and the margin of cultivation, and speculation in land ahead of settlement makes it worse and is what brings on depressions. The remedy he proposes is a single tax. Abolish every other tax — on wages, on buildings, on goods, on trade — and take the rental value of land instead, leaving owners their titles and taking the unearned income. That is the whole policy, and he thinks it is enough on its own.

The book is complete here: ten Books and forty-six chapters, the technical economics and the argument about justice alike. Book One takes apart the wage-fund theory then standard. Book Two is a sustained attack on Malthus, whose Essay is in this collection, as is Adam Smith's Wealth of Nations; George works in the line that runs from Smith and Ricardo and says so in his preface.

The reasoning is careful and close, and the last chapters are not. George was a reformer, and the writing rises accordingly: the account of a civilization that has decayed before, the pages on what a city would look like without the fear of want, and a final chapter about individual life that he wrote for himself. Tolstoy became an open disciple. George ran for mayor of New York in 1886, came second, and died during a second campaign for the office in 1897.
