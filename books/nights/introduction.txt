The two most famous stories in the Arabian Nights are not in the Arabian Nights. No Arabic manuscript older than the eighteenth century contains Aladdin or Ali Baba. Antoine Galland, who made the first European translation between 1704 and 1717, took both of them down in Paris in 1709 from a young Maronite Christian from Aleppo named Hanna Diyab, printed them in French, and they were written back into Arabic afterwards by people who assumed they must have come from there. Both are in this volume, and a short editor's note says so where they begin.

The rest of the collection is genuinely old and genuinely unfixed. Behind the frame is a lost Persian book of a thousand stories; on top of that, tales from India, Persia, Iraq and Egypt were told, written down, translated and recopied over something like six hundred years. No two surviving manuscripts hold the same set. There has never been a correct table of contents, which is why every printed edition is somebody's selection.

The frame is what holds it together. A king finds his wife unfaithful, kills her, and concludes that all women are the same. He takes a new wife every night and has her executed at dawn, until the vizier's daughter Shahrazad volunteers to be next. She brings her sister, she begins a story, and when the sun comes up the story is not finished. That is the whole mechanism and it is on the page: she breaks off at dawn, the night is numbered, and she picks the thread up the following evening. There are a hundred and sixty-one of those breaks in this book.

This volume is the frame and eighteen of the tales, covering those nights. They are the Fisherman and the Jinni, the Porter and the Three Ladies of Baghdad, the entire Hunchback cycle, all seven of Sindbad's voyages and an eighth from a different manuscript, the City of Brass, Aladdin and Ali Baba. The registers are not the same from one to the next — the Hunchback is farce played straight, the City of Brass is an hour of tomb inscriptions, Aladdin is a pantomime — and they have not been evened out.

The text underneath is Richard Burton's, published between 1885 and 1888, the fullest and least readable English version there is. Burton wrote it in an antique diction of his own invention, full of whenas and hight and anent, which was artificial the day it was printed. None of it corresponds to anything in the Arabic. What has been taken off here is a costume the translator put on. What is under it has been left alone, including the passages a modern reader will not like: Sindbad in the burial cavern, and everything he does there to get out.
