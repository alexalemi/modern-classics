The First Kalandar's Tale

Know, my lady, that the reason my beard was shaved off and my eye was torn out was this.

My father was a king, and he had a brother who was king over another city; and it happened that my cousin — my father's brother's son — and I were both born on one and the same day.

The years and the days rolled on, and as we grew up I used to visit my uncle now and then and spend some months with him. My cousin and I were sworn friends, for he always treated me with tremendous kindness: he killed the fattest sheep for me and strained the best of his wines, and we enjoyed long hours of talking and drinking.

One day, when the wine had got the better of us, my uncle's son said to me, "Cousin, I have a great service to ask of you, and I want you not to stop me in anything I mean to do."

"With joy and goodwill," I answered.

Then he made me swear the most binding oaths, and left me. But after a little while he came back leading a lady, veiled and richly dressed, with jewellery on her worth a great deal of money.

Presently he turned to me — the woman still standing behind him — and said, "Take this lady with you and go ahead of me to such and such a burial ground" (and he described it, so that I knew the place), "and go into such and such a tomb chamber, and wait there for me to come."

The oaths I had sworn to him kept me quiet and would not let me argue with him. So I led the woman to the cemetery, and she and I sat down inside the tomb chamber; and we had hardly sat down before in came my uncle's son with a bowl of water, a bag of mortar, and an adze rather like a hoe.

He went straight to the tomb in the middle of the chamber and broke it open with the adze and set the stones to one side; and then he began digging into the earth of the tomb until he came to a large iron plate the size of a wicket door. Raising it, there appeared underneath a vaulted, winding staircase.

Then he turned to the lady and said, "Come now, and make your final choice."

She went straight down the stairs and disappeared. And then he said to me:

"Cousin, by way of completing your kindness — when I have gone down into this place, put the trapdoor back where it was and heap the earth back over it as it was before. And then, of your great goodness, mix this unslaked lime that is in the bag with this water that is in the bowl, and after building the stones back up, plaster the outside, so that nobody looking at it can say: this is a new opening in an old tomb. I have worked at this place for a whole year, and nobody knows about it except Allah. And this is the service I need from you."

And presently he added, "May Allah never take you from your friends or leave them desolate by your absence, cousin — my dear cousin!"

And he went down the stairs and disappeared for ever.

When he was out of sight I put the iron plate back and did everything he had told me until the tomb was as it had been before; and I worked almost without knowing what I was doing, for my head was hot with wine.

Going back to my uncle's palace, I was told he had gone out hunting and hawking; so I slept that night without seeing him. And when the morning dawned I remembered the scenes of the evening before and what had passed between me and my cousin, and I was sorry I had obeyed him, when being sorry was no use at all. Still, I half thought it had been a dream.

So I began asking after my uncle's son; but there was nobody who could answer me about him. And I went out to the graveyard and the tomb chambers and looked for the tomb he was under, and could not find it. And I did not stop wandering from tomb chamber to tomb chamber and from grave to grave, all without success, until night came on.

So I went back to the city, and I could neither eat nor drink; my thoughts were entirely taken up with my cousin, because I did not know what had become of him. And I grieved beyond all grieving, and passed another sorrowful night, lying awake until morning.

Then I went a second time to the cemetery, turning over what my uncle's son had done, and bitterly regretting having listened to him; and I went round all the tombs and could not find the tomb I was looking for. I mourned over the past, and stayed in my mourning seven days, looking for the place and always missing the path.

Then my torment of scruples grew on me until I very nearly went mad; and I could find no way to shake off my grief except to travel, and go back to my father.

So I set off and journeyed homewards. But as I was coming into my father's capital, a crowd of rioters sprang on me and pinioned my arms.

I wondered at it with every kind of wonder, seeing that I was the Sultan's son and these men were my father's subjects, and some of them my own slaves. A great fear fell on me and I said to myself, "I wish to heaven I knew what has happened to my father."

I asked the men who had bound me why they were doing it, but they gave me no answer. However, after a while one of them said to me — and he had been a hired servant of our household — "Fortune has been false to your father. His troops betrayed him, and the vizier who killed him now reigns in his place; and we were lying in wait to seize you at that man's bidding."

I was very nearly out of my mind, and felt ready to faint at the news of my father's death; and they carried me off and set me in front of the usurper.

Now there was an old grudge between him and me, and the cause of it was this. I was fond of shooting with the pellet bow; and it happened one day, as I was standing on the terrace roof of the palace, that a bird settled on the top of the vizier's house at a moment when he happened to be there. I shot at the bird and missed my mark — and hit the vizier in the eye and knocked it out, as fate and fortune decreed. As the poet says:

	We tread the path that Fate has led us down;
	the path Fate wrote is the path we must tread.
	And a man doomed to die in one country
	will be put to death nowhere else.

And another says the same thing:

	Let Fortune have her wanton way;
	take heart, and obey everything she says.
	Do not rejoice or mourn at anything,
	for all things pass and nothing stays.

Now when I knocked the vizier's eye out he could not say a single word, because my father was king of the city; but he hated me from then on, and the grudge between the two of us was a bitter one.

So when I was set in front of him with my hands bound and my arms pinioned, he gave orders at once for me to be beheaded.

"For what crime will you put me to death?" I asked.

And he answered, "What crime is greater than this?" — pointing all the while at the place where his eye had been.

"I did that by accident," I said, "and not out of deliberate malice."

"If you did it by accident," said he, "I shall do the same to you on purpose."

Then he cried, "Bring him forward." And they brought me up to him, and he thrust his finger into my left eye and gouged it out; and that is how I became one-eyed, as you see me.

Then he ordered me bound hand and foot and put into a chest, and said to the swordsman, "Take charge of this fellow, and go off with him to the waste ground outside the city; then draw your scimitar and kill him, and leave him for the beasts and the birds to feed on."

So the headsman went out with me; and when he was in the middle of the desert he took me out of the chest — my hands pinioned and my feet in fetters — and was about to bandage my eyes before striking off my head.

But I wept so bitterly that I made him weep with me; and looking at him, I began to recite:

	"I thought you a coat of mail that would stand up
	to the enemy's arrows — and you turned out to be the enemy's sword.
	I hoped for your help in every chance that came,
	though my left hand should fail to help my right;
	but you stand aside and listen to the mocker's gibes
	while the whole gang of mockers rains their shafts on me.
	Well — if you will not guard me from my enemies,
	then stand clear, and help neither them nor me."

And I also quoted:

	"I thought my brothers were mail of the strongest steel —
	and so they were, at fending off my own arrows from my enemies.
	I thought their arrows the surest of aim —
	and so they were, when they aimed at my heart."

When the headsman heard my lines — and he had been swordsman to my father, and he owed me a debt of gratitude — he cried, "My lord, what can I do? I am only a slave under orders."

And presently he added, "Fly for your life, and never come back to this country, or they will kill you and kill me along with you. As the poet said:

	Take your life and fly when trouble threatens;
	let the ruined house tell the tale of its owner's fate.
	You will look for a new country and find one instead of the old,
	but you must not wait about to find a new life.
	It is strange that men should sit down in a place of shame
	when Allah's world is so wide and so great.
	And in serious matters do not trust anybody else:
	life itself must act for a life under threat.
	The lion with the maned neck would never go out hunting
	if he counted on help or took thought of other creatures."

Hardly able to believe I had escaped, I kissed his hand, and thought the loss of my eye a light matter considering that I had got away without being killed.

I reached my uncle's capital; and going in to him, I told him what had happened to my father and to myself. At which he wept bitterly and said, "Truly you are adding grief to my grief and woe to my woe; for your cousin has been missing these many days, and I do not know what has happened to him, and nobody can give me news of him." And he wept until he fainted.

I grieved with him and offered him what comfort I could; and he wanted to put certain medicines on my eye, but he saw that it had become like a walnut with an empty shell. Then he said, "My son, better to lose an eye and keep your life."

After that I could not keep silent any longer about his son, who was his only son and dearly loved; so I told him everything that had happened. He was overjoyed to hear news of the boy and said, "Come now, and show me the tomb."

But I answered, "By Allah, uncle, I do not know where it is; I have hunted for it carefully a great many times and could never find the place."

However, my uncle and I went to the graveyard and looked right and left, until at last I recognised the tomb, and we were both overjoyed. We went into the chamber and loosened the earth round the grave; then, raising the trapdoor, we went down some fifty steps until we came to the foot of the staircase — and there we were stopped by a blinding smoke.

At which my uncle said the saying whose sayer shall never come to shame: "There is no Majesty and there is no Might save in Allah, the Glorious, the Great!"

And we went on, and suddenly came out into a large room whose floor was strewn with flour and grain and provisions and all sorts of necessaries; and in the middle of it stood a canopy sheltering a couch.

My uncle went up to the couch, and looking at it, found his son and the lady who had gone down into the tomb with him, lying in each other's arms — but the two of them had turned black as charred wood, as though they had been thrown into a pit of fire.

When my uncle saw this sight he spat in his son's face and said, "You have what you deserve, you pig! This is your judgement in the passing world, and the judgement in the world to come is still waiting for you — harsher, and longer-lasting":—

And Shahrazad saw the dawn breaking, and fell silent.

When it was the Twelfth Night,

she went on: It has reached me, O fortunate King, that the kalandar carried on with his story in front of the lady and the Caliph and Ja'afar:

My uncle struck his son with his slipper as he lay there, a black heap of coal. I marvelled at his hardness of heart, and grieving for my cousin and for the lady, said, "By Allah, uncle, calm your anger. Can you not see that all my thoughts are taken up with this misfortune, and how sorry I am for what has happened to your son, and how horrible it is that nothing is left of him but a black heap of charcoal? Is that not enough, without your striking him with your slipper as well?"

He answered, "Nephew, this boy was madly in love with his own sister from his boyhood. Time and time again I forbade him to go near her, saying to myself: they are only little ones. But when they grew up, sin happened between them; and although I could hardly believe it, I shut him up and scolded him and threatened him with the severest threats.

"And the eunuchs and the servants said to him: beware of such a foul thing, which nobody before you has ever done and nobody after you will ever do; and take care you are not dishonoured and disgraced among the kings of the day, to the end of time.

"And I added: a report like this will be carried abroad by the caravans, so take care not to give them anything to talk about, or I shall certainly curse you and put you to death.

"After that I lodged them apart and shut her up. But the accursed girl loved him with a passionate love, for Satan had got the mastery of her as well as of him and made their foul sin look fine to them both.

"Now when my son saw that I had separated them, he secretly built this underground place and furnished it and carried provisions down to it, exactly as you see. And when I had gone out hunting he came here with his sister and hid from me. Then Allah's righteous judgement fell on the pair of them and burnt them up with fire out of Heaven; and truly the last judgement will deal them harsher pains, and longer-lasting."

Then he wept, and I wept with him; and he looked at me and said, "You are my son in his place."

And I thought for a while about the world and the chances of it: how the vizier had killed my father and taken his place and put out my eye, and how my cousin had come to his death by the strangest chance of all. And I wept again, and my uncle wept with me.

Then we climbed the steps and let down the iron plate and heaped the earth up over it; and after putting the tomb back the way it had been, we returned to the palace.

But we had hardly sat down before we heard the drumming of kettledrums and the blaring of trumpets and the clash of cymbals, and the rattle of fighting men's lances, and the shouting of attackers, and the clanking of bits and the neighing of horses; while the whole world was canopied in thick dust and clouds of sand thrown up by the horses' hooves.

We were astonished at the sight and the sound, not knowing what the matter could be; so we asked, and were told that the vizier who had usurped my father's kingdom had marched his men against us — that after raising his army and taking a host of desert Arabs into his service, he had come down on us with forces like the sands of the sea, whose number nobody could count and against whom nobody could stand.

They attacked the city without warning; and the citizens, powerless to resist them, surrendered the place. My uncle was killed, and I made for the outskirts, saying to myself, "If you fall into this villain's hands he will certainly kill you."

And so all my troubles began again; and I turned over everything that had happened to my father and my uncle, and I did not know what to do. For if the townspeople or my father's troops had recognised me, they would have done their best to win favour by destroying me; and I could think of no way of escape except by shaving off my beard and my eyebrows.

So I shaved them off, and changing my fine clothes for a kalandar's rags, I set out from my uncle's capital and made for this city, hoping that somebody might help me into the presence of the Commander of the Faithful, the Caliph, who is the Viceregent of Allah upon earth. And so I have come here, to tell him my tale and lay my case before him.

I arrived this very night, and was standing in doubt about which way to go, when suddenly I saw this second kalandar; so I greeted him, saying, "I am a stranger!" and he answered, "I am a stranger too!"

And as we were talking, up came our companion here, this third kalandar, and greeted us, saying, "I am a stranger!" And we answered, "We are strangers too!"

Then the three of us walked on together until the dark overtook us, and Destiny drove us to your house.

So that is the reason my beard and my moustaches and my eyebrows were shaved off, and that is how I lost my left eye.

They marvelled a great deal at this tale, and the Caliph said to Ja'afar, "By Allah, I have neither seen nor heard the like of what has happened to this kalandar."

Then the lady of the house said, "Rub your head and be on your way."

But he replied, "I will not go until I have heard the history of the other two."
