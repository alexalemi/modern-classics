The Christian Broker's Story
(Part 2 of 2)

	"Had we known you were coming, your road would have been strewn
	with the blood of our hearts and the balls of our eyes;
	we would have thrown our cheeks down as a carpet to greet you,
	so that your step should land softly on our eyelids."

And she went on complaining of what had happened to her and I of what had happened to me; and love of her took such firm hold of my heart that all my wealth seemed nothing at all beside her.

Then we fell to playing and groping and kissing until nightfall, when the maids set food in front of us and a full wine service, and we sat drinking until midnight, when we lay down and I lay with her. Never in my life did I see a night like that night.

When morning came I got up and took my leave of her, throwing the handkerchief with the dinars in it under the bed; and as I went out she wept and said, "My lord, when shall I look on that lovely face again?"

"I will be with you at sunset," I answered.

And going out, I found the donkey boy who had brought me the day before waiting at the door. So I mounted and rode to the Khan al-Masrur, where I got down and gave the man half a dinar, saying, "Come back at sunset"; and he said, "I will."

Then I had my breakfast and went out to collect the price of my goods; after which I came back, and taking a roast lamb and some sweets, called a porter and put the provisions in his crate and sent them to the lady, paying the man his hire.

I went back to my business until sunset, when the donkey driver came for me; and I took fifty dinars in a handkerchief and rode to her house, where I found the marble floor swept, the brasses burnished, the branched lights burning, the wax candles already lit, the meat served up and the wine strained.

When my lady saw me she threw her arms round my neck and cried, "You have left me desolate by being away."

Then she set the tables in front of me and we ate our fill, and the slave girls carried the trays off and served the wine. We did not stop drinking until half the night was gone; and being well warmed with drink, we went into the bedchamber and lay there until morning.

Then I got up and left her, leaving the fifty dinars with her as before; and finding the donkey boy at the door, rode to the khan and slept a while.

After that I went out to get the evening meal ready, and took a brace of geese with gravy on two platters of dressed and peppered rice, and got ready taro roots fried and soaked in honey, and wax candles and fruit and preserves and nuts and almonds and sweet-scented flowers; and I sent the whole lot to her.

As soon as it was night I tied up fifty dinars in a handkerchief again, and mounting the donkey as usual, rode to the mansion, where we ate and drank and lay together till morning; when I threw her the handkerchief and the dinars and rode back to the khan.

I did not stop going on in this way until, after one sweet night, I woke up on a fine morning and found myself beggared, without a dinar or a dirham to my name.

So I said to myself, "All this is Satan's work," and began to recite these lines:

	"Poverty dims a man's brightness, whatever his wealth has been,
	just as the sun about to set shines with a yellowing light.
	Absent, he falls out of memory and is forgotten by his friends;
	present, he does not share their joys, for nobody delights in him.
	He walks the market avoided by everybody, glad enough to hide his head,
	and in lonely places he sheds tears and moans over his bitter state.
	By Allah, among his own kin a man, however good he is,
	is nothing but a stranger once want and poverty have waylaid him."

I went out from the khan and walked down the street called Between the Palaces until I came to the Zuwaylah Gate, where I found the crowd thick and the gateway blocked with people.

And by the decree of Destiny I saw there a trooper, against whom I pressed accidentally, so that my hand came against his breast pocket and I felt a purse inside it. I looked, and seeing a green silk string hanging out of the pocket, knew it for a purse.

The crush grew greater every minute; and just then a camel loaded with firewood happened to jostle the trooper on the other side, and he turned round to fend it off in case it tore his clothes. And Satan tempted me, so I pulled the string and drew out a little bag of blue silk with something in it that chinked like coin.

But the soldier, feeling his pocket suddenly lighter, put his hand to it and found it empty; at which he turned on me and, snatching his mace off his saddle bow, struck me on the head with it.

I fell to the ground, while the people gathered round us and seized the trooper's mare by the bridle and said to him, "Do you strike this young man a blow like that for a mere push?"

But the trooper shouted at them, "This fellow is a cursed thief!"

At which I came to myself and stood up; and the people looked at me and said, "No, he is a good-looking young man; he would not steal anything." And some of them took my part and others were against me, and question and answer grew loud and warm. The people pulled at me and would have rescued me out of his clutches — but as fate decreed, the Governor, the Chief of Police and the watch came in at the Zuwaylah Gate at that very moment; and seeing the people gathered round me and the soldier, the Governor asked, "What is the matter?"

"By Allah, Emir," answered the trooper, "this is a thief! I had a purse of blue silk in my pocket with twenty good gold pieces in it, and he took it while I was in the crush."

Said the Governor, "Was anybody beside you at the time?"

And the soldier said, "No."

At which the Governor called out to the Chief of Police, who seized me; and in this way the curtain of the Lord's protection was drawn back from me.

Then he said, "Strip him." And when they stripped me they found the purse in my clothes. The Governor took it and opened it and counted it; and finding twenty dinars in it, as the soldier had said, he grew extremely angry and ordered his guard to bring me before him.

Then he said to me, "Now, young man, speak the truth: did you steal this purse?"

At this I hung my head towards the ground and said to myself, "If I deny stealing it, I shall get myself into terrible trouble." So I raised my head and said, "Yes, I took it."

When the Governor heard this he wondered, and summoned witnesses, who came forward and attested my confession. All this happened at the Zuwaylah Gate.

Then the Governor ordered the torch-bearer to cut off my right hand, and he did so; after which he would have struck off my left foot as well — but the soldier's heart softened and he took pity on me and interceded for me with the Governor, so that I should not be killed.

At which the Governor left me and went away, and the people stayed round me and gave me a cup of wine to drink. As for the trooper, he pressed the purse on me and said, "You are a good-looking young man, and it does not suit you to be a thief."

So I recited these verses:

	"I swear by Allah's name, good sir, that I was no thief,
	nor, best of men, was I ever bred a bandit.
	But the chances and changes of Fortune overthrew me all at once,
	and care and trouble and poverty led me off my road.
	I did not shoot it, truly; it was Allah who shot the arrow
	that rolled the kingly crown from my head into the dust."

The soldier turned away after giving me the purse; and I too went my way, having wrapped my hand in a piece of rag and thrust it into my breast.

My whole appearance had changed, and my colour had gone yellow from the shame and the pain that had come on me. Even so, I went on to my mistress's house, where in extreme agitation of spirit I threw myself down on the bed.

She saw me in that state and asked, "What is the matter, and why do I see you so changed in your looks?"

And I answered, "My head is hurting me, and I am far from well."

At which she was upset and concerned on my account, and said, "Do not burn my heart, my lord; sit up and lift your head and tell me what has happened to you today, for your face tells me a story."

"Leave this talk," I replied.

But she wept and said, "It seems to me that you have grown tired of me, for I see you are not as you usually are."

But I was silent; and she went on talking to me although I gave her no answer, until night came on.

Then she set food in front of me; but I refused it, afraid she might see me eating with my left hand, and said to her, "I have no appetite to eat at present."

Said she, "Tell me what has happened to you today, and why you are so sorrowful and broken in spirit and in heart."

Said I, "Wait a while; I will tell you everything at my leisure."

Then she brought me wine, saying, "Down with it, this will drive off your grief. You really must drink, and tell me your news."

I asked, "Must I tell you?"

And she answered, "Yes."

Then I said, "If it must be so, then give it to me with your own hand."

She filled and drank, and filled again and gave me the cup; and I took it from her with my left hand, and wiped the tears from my eyelids and began repeating:

	"When Allah wills that something should befall a man
	who has his full share of ears and eyes and wits,
	He deafens his ears and He blinds his eyes
	and draws his wits out as we draw out a hair —
	until, having worked His purpose, He gives him back
	his wits, so that he may go more warily warned."

When I had finished my verses I wept; and she cried out with a very loud cry, "What is the cause of your tears? You are burning my heart! What makes you take the cup with your left hand?"

Said I, "The truth is I have a boil on my right hand."

And she said, "Put it out and I will open it for you."

"It is not time to open it yet," I replied, "so do not worry me with your words, for I will not take it out of the bandage at this hour."

Then I drank the cup off; and she did not stop plying me with drink until drunkenness overcame me and I fell asleep where I was sitting — at which she looked at my right hand and saw a wrist without a fist.

So she searched me closely, and found on me the purse of gold and my severed hand wrapped up in the piece of rag. At which such sorrow came over her as never came over anybody; and she did not stop lamenting on my account until the morning.

When I woke I found she had made me a dish of broth of four boiled chickens, which she brought me together with a cup of wine. I ate and drank; and laying down the purse, would have gone out — but she said to me, "Where are you going?"

And I answered, "Where my business calls me."

And she said, "You shall not go. Sit down."

So I sat down; and she went on, "Has your love for me so overpowered you that you have wasted all your wealth and lost your hand on my account? I call you to witness against me, and let Allah be my witness too, that I will never part with you, but will die under your feet; and you shall soon see that what I say is true."

Then she sent for the judge and the witnesses and said to them, "Write out my marriage contract with this young man, and bear witness that I have received the marriage settlement."

When they had drawn up the document she said, "Bear witness that all my money that is in this chest, and everything I have in slaves and maids and other property, is given as a free gift to this young man."

So they took a record of the statement, putting me in possession by right of marriage; and then they withdrew, after being paid their fees.

At which she took me by the hand and, leading me to a small room, opened a large chest and said, "Look at what is in here."

And I looked — and it was full of handkerchiefs.

Said she, "This is the money I had from you; and every handkerchief you gave me, with fifty dinars in it, I wrapped up and threw into this chest. So now take what is your own, for it comes back to you; and today you have become a man of high estate. Fortune and Fate afflicted you so that you lost your right hand for my sake, and I can never repay you; no, if I gave my life it would still be little, and I should remain in your debt."

Then she added, "Take charge of your property."

So I moved the contents of her chest into my chest, and added my wealth to her wealth, which I had given her; and my heart was eased and my sorrow ceased.

I stood up and kissed her and thanked her; and she said, "You have given your hand for love of me — and how am I able to give you anything equal to it? By Allah, if I offered my life for your love it would truly be a small thing and would not do justice to your claim on me."

Then she made over to me by deed everything she owned in clothes and ornaments of gold and pearls, and goods and farms and property; and she did not lie down to sleep that night, being so sorely grieved for my grief, until I had told her the whole of what had happened to me.

I spent the night with her. But before we had lived together a month she fell seriously ill, and her illness grew worse by reason of her grief over the loss of my hand; and she lasted only fifty days before she was numbered among the people of the world to come and the heirs of immortality.

So I laid her out and buried her body in the earth, and had a full reading of the Koran made for the health of her soul, and gave a great deal of money in alms for her; after which I turned away from the grave and went back to the house.

There I found she had left a great deal of property in ready money and slaves, houses, lands and estates; and among her storehouses was a granary of sesame seed, part of which I sold to you. And I had neither the time nor the inclination to settle accounts with you until I had sold the rest of the stock in store; nor indeed have I finished collecting the price even now.

So I want you not to baulk me in what I am about to say to you: twice I have eaten your food, and I wish to make you a present of the money for the sesame that you are holding.

Such is the cause of the cutting off of my right hand, and of my eating with my left.

"Truly," said I, "you have shown me the greatest kindness and generosity."

Then he asked me, "Why should you not travel with me to my own country, which I am about to go back to with Cairo and Alexandria goods? Tell me — will you come with me?"

And I answered, "I will."

So I agreed to go with him at the head of the month; and I sold everything I had and bought other merchandise. Then we set out and travelled, the young man and I, to this country of yours, where he sold his venture and bought another stock of country goods and went on with his journey to Egypt.

But it was my lot to stay here, so that these things happened to me in my exile which happened last night.

And is this tale, O King of the age, not more wonderful and more marvellous than the story of the hunchback?

"Not so," said the King. "I cannot accept it. There is no help for it: every one of you is to be hanged":—

And Shahrazad saw the dawn breaking, and fell silent.
