The Story of King Shahryar and His Brother
(Part 1 of 2)

And now to begin. The doings and the sayings of the people who came before us have become lessons and examples for the people of today. A man can look at what befell other men and take warning from it; he can read through the records of the ancient peoples and everything that happened to them, and be steadied and governed by what he reads. Praise, then, to Him who made the histories of the past a warning to the present.

Among such stories are the tales called A Thousand Nights and a Night, with all their famous legends and their wonders.

It is told — though Allah alone knows what is hidden, and He alone rules, and to Him belong all honour and all giving and all grace and all mercy — that in the old days, and in the old time before them, there was a king among the kings of the house of Sasan, in the islands of India and China, a lord of armies and guards and servants and dependants. He left two sons, one in the prime of his manhood and the other still a boy; and both were fine horsemen and brave men, though the elder was the better rider of the two.

The elder came to the throne, and he ruled the land and governed his people so justly that he was loved by everyone in his capital and everyone in his kingdom. His name was King Shahryar, and he made his younger brother, whose name was Shah Zaman, king of Samarkand, away in the outer lands.

The two of them stayed in their separate kingdoms, and the law was carried out in both, and each governed his own people with fairness and a straight hand, and lived in great ease and contentment. Things went on that way for twenty years.

But at the end of the twentieth year the elder king began to long for a sight of his younger brother, and felt he had to look at him once more. So he asked his vizier what he thought of paying a visit, and the vizier, who did not think the journey a good idea, suggested instead that a letter be written and a present sent, in his own charge, inviting the younger brother to come to the elder.

The king took the advice. He had handsome gifts got ready at once — horses with saddles of gold crusted with gems, Mamluks, which is to say soldiers who are also slaves, beautiful serving girls, splendid and costly cloth — and he wrote his brother a letter full of his love for him and how badly he wanted to see him. It ended:

"So we hope, out of the affection and the kindness of our beloved brother, that he will trouble himself to turn his face towards us. We have sent our vizier to make all the arrangements for the march. Our one and only wish is to see you before we die; and if you delay, or disappoint us, we shall not survive the blow. And peace be upon you."

Then King Shahryar sealed the letter and gave it to the vizier along with the gifts, and told him to tuck up his skirts and put out his strength and lose no time going or coming back.

"Hearing and obedience!" said the vizier, and he set about getting ready without a pause, and packed his loads and had everything he needed prepared without delay. It took him three days, and at dawn on the fourth he took his leave of the king and marched off — over desert and hill road, over stony waste and pleasant meadow, never stopping by night or by day. Wherever he came into a country whose ruler was subject to his own king, and was met with magnificent presents of gold and silver and every fine and rare thing, he would stay three days, which is the term a guest is owed, and when he left on the fourth he would be escorted in honour for a full day's march.

When the vizier drew near Shah Zaman's court at Samarkand, he sent ahead one of his senior officers to announce that he had come. The officer presented himself, kissed the ground before the king, and delivered his message; and the king sent out a party of his nobles and lords to meet his brother's vizier a full day's journey from the city. They met him respectfully, wished him every good thing, and formed an escort and a procession around him.

When he entered the city he went straight to the palace and presented himself to the king. He kissed the ground, prayed for the king's health and happiness and for victory over all his enemies, and told him that his brother was longing to see him and begged for the pleasure of a visit. Then he handed over the letter.

Shah Zaman took it and read it. It had hints and turns in it that needed thinking about; but once he had taken in what it meant, he said, "I hear and I obey the commands of my beloved brother!" And to the vizier he added, "But we will not march until you have had three days' hospitality."

He gave the vizier good quarters in the palace, pitched tents for the troops, and had them supplied with everything they might want in the way of food and drink and the rest. On the fourth day he made ready for the road, put together magnificent presents worthy of his elder brother's rank, and appointed his own chief vizier to govern the country while he was away. Then he had his tents and his camels and his mules brought out, and the whole camp — bales and loads, servants and guards — pitched within sight of the city, ready to set out for his brother's capital the next morning.

But when the night was half over, he remembered something he had meant to bring and had left behind in the palace. So he went back quietly and let himself into his own rooms — and found the queen his wife asleep on his own bed, with both arms around a black cook, a loathsome man, greasy and filthy from the kitchen.

The world went dark in front of him. He said, "If this is going on while I am still within sight of my own city, what will this damned woman be doing during the months I am away at my brother's court?"

He drew his scimitar and cut the two of them into four pieces with a single stroke, and left them lying on the bed, and went back to the camp without letting anyone know what had happened. Then he gave the order to move, and set off at once.

But he could not stop thinking about his wife's treachery. He kept saying to himself, "How could she do this to me? How could she work her own death?" — until grief took hold of him so hard that his colour turned yellow and his body went weak, and he looked like a man sickening for something that kills. So the vizier shortened the day's marches and lingered long at the watering places, and did what he could to comfort him.

When Shah Zaman came near his brother's capital he sent runners and messengers ahead with the good news of his arrival, and Shahryar came out to meet him with his viziers and his emirs and all the lords and great men of his kingdom. He greeted him, and was overjoyed to see him, and had the city decorated in his honour.

But when the brothers came face to face, the elder could not miss the change in the younger's colour, and asked what was wrong.

"It is only the wear of the road," Shah Zaman said, "and my health needs looking after. The change of water and air has told on me. But praise be to Allah, who has brought me back to a brother so dear and so rare!" That was how he covered it and kept his secret. And he added, "O King of the age, it is nothing but toil and travel that have turned my face yellow with bile and sunk my eyes back in my head."

So the two of them entered the capital in state, and the elder brother lodged the younger in a palace that looked out over the pleasure garden. After a while, seeing that his condition had not changed, Shahryar put it down to being away from his own country and his own kingdom, and left him to go his own way and asked him nothing — until one day he said again, "Brother, I see you are getting weaker in body and yellower in the face."

"Brother," Shah Zaman answered, "I have a wound inside me." But still he would not tell him what he had seen his wife do.

So Shahryar sent for doctors and surgeons and had them treat his brother by the rules of their art, and they worked on him for a whole month; but their syrups and their medicines did nothing at all, because he went on dwelling on what his wife had done, and instead of easing, his misery got worse. The doctors' treatment failed entirely.

One day the elder brother said to him, "I am going out hunting and coursing, to take some pleasure and pass the time. Perhaps it will lighten your heart."

But Shah Zaman refused. "Brother," he said, "my soul wants nothing of the kind. Let me have the favour of staying quietly here. I am wholly taken up with my illness."

So Shah Zaman spent the night in the palace, and next morning, when his brother had ridden out, he left his room and sat down at one of the latticed windows overlooking the pleasure grounds. He sat there thinking the saddest thoughts he had about his wife's betrayal, and burning sighs came up out of his tortured chest.

And while he was sitting like that — a small private door of the palace, one that was carefully kept shut, swung open, and out of it came twenty slave girls, and in the middle of them his brother's wife. She was extraordinarily beautiful, a model of grace and symmetry and perfect loveliness, and she walked with the step of a gazelle going down to water.

Shah Zaman drew back from the window, but kept the whole party in sight, watching from a place where he could not be seen himself.

They walked directly under his lattice and went a little way into the garden, until they came to a fountain jetting up in the middle of a great basin of water. There they stripped off their clothes — and of the twenty, ten turned out to be women, the king's concubines, and the other ten were young men, slaves, who had been dressed as women all along.

Then they paired off, each with each. But the queen was left by herself, and after a moment she called out loudly, "Here to me, Saeed, my lord!"

And down out of one of the trees, in a single dropping leap, came a huge Black man, slack-mouthed, the whites of his eyes showing as they rolled — a hideous sight, as Shah Zaman saw him from the window. He walked straight up to her and threw his arms round her neck, and she held him just as tightly; and he kissed her, and wound his legs round hers the way a loop of thread winds round a button, and threw her down and had her. The other slaves did the same with the girls, until they had all satisfied themselves; and they went on kissing and clinging and coupling and carousing until the day began to fade. Then the Mamluks got up off the women, and the Black slave got up off the queen; the young men put their disguises on again; and all of them went back into the palace and shut the private door behind them, exactly as before — all except the Black man, who went back up his tree.

When Shah Zaman had seen his sister-in-law behave like this, he said to himself, "By Allah, my trouble is lighter than his! My brother is a greater king among kings than I am, and this shameful business is going on inside his own palace, and his own wife is in love with the filthiest slave in his household. But it only shows that they all do it, and that there is not a woman alive who does not cheat her husband. The curse of Allah on every one of them, and on the fools who lean on them for support, or put the reins of their conduct into their hands."

So he put away his misery and his low spirits, his regret and his brooding, and eased his own sorrow by repeating those words over and over. He added, "It is my settled opinion that no man in this world is safe from the malice of women."

When supper time came they brought in the trays, and he ate ravenously, because he had gone a long time without touching meat and had not been able to face any dish, however fine. Then he gave grateful thanks to Almighty Allah, praising Him and blessing Him, and he slept a thoroughly restful night — it had been a long while since he had tasted the sweetness of sleep. The next day he ate a good breakfast and began to get his health and his strength back, and before long he was in excellent condition again.

His brother came back from the hunt ten days later, and Shah Zaman rode out to meet him, and they greeted one another. When King Shahryar looked at King Shah Zaman he saw that the colour of health had come back into him, that his face had gone ruddy, and that he was eating with an appetite after living on so little. He wondered at it a good deal, and said, "Brother, I was so worried that you would not come out hunting and chasing with me and take your pleasure in my country!"

Shah Zaman thanked him and made his excuses. Then the two of them took horse and rode into the city, and when they were sitting comfortably in the palace the food trays were set in front of them and they ate their fill. When the dishes had been taken away and they had washed their hands, King Shahryar turned to his brother.

"My mind is full of astonishment at the state of you," he said. "I wanted to take you hunting with me, but I saw you changed in colour, pale and worn to look at, and in real distress of mind besides. And now — alhamdulillah, praise be to Allah — I see your natural colour has come back into your face and you are in the best of health. I believed your sickness came from being cut off from your family and your friends, and from being away from your capital and your country, and so I held back from troubling you with any more questions. But now I beg you: explain to me what caused your illness and the change in your colour, and explain to me the reason for your recovery and the ruddy health I am used to seeing in you. Speak out, and hide nothing."

Shah Zaman bowed his head towards the ground for a while, then lifted it and said, "I will tell you what caused my illness and the loss of my colour. But excuse me from telling you the cause of its coming back and the reason for my recovery. I beg you not to press me for an answer."

Shahryar was very much surprised at this. "Let me hear first what made you so pale and brought you so low," he said.

"Know then, brother," Shah Zaman answered, "that when you sent your vizier with the invitation to come to you, I made ready and marched out of my city. But then I remembered a string of jewels I had left behind in the palace, which I meant as a present for you. I went back for it alone, and found my wife on my own bed in the arms of a hideous black cook. So I killed the pair of them and came on to you — and my mind kept working over the business, and I lost my bloom and grew weak. But excuse me if I still refuse to tell you the reason my colour came back."

Shahryar shook his head, marvelling at it beyond measure, and with the fire of anger flaring up in his heart he cried, "The malice of woman is a mighty thing indeed!" Then he took refuge from them with Allah, and said, "In plain truth, brother, you have escaped a great deal of evil by putting your wife to death, and your rage and your grief were entirely excusable — a thing like that has never yet happened to a crowned king. By Allah, if it had happened to me, I would not have been satisfied with less than killing a thousand women, and that way lies madness. But praise be to Allah, who has softened your trouble for you. And now you really must tell me what it was that suddenly gave you back your colour and your health, and explain to me why you are hiding it."

"O King of the age, again I beg you to excuse me."

"No. You must."

"Brother, I am afraid the telling of it will cause you more anger and more sorrow than it caused me."

"All the more reason," said Shahryar, "to tell me the whole story. And I charge you by Allah to keep nothing back."

So Shah Zaman told him everything he had seen, from beginning to end, and finished: "When I saw your calamity, brother, and the treachery of your wife, and thought about it — that you are older than I am and greater than I am in sovereignty — my own sorrow was made small by the comparison, and my mind came back into balance and good temper. And so, throwing off my misery and my low spirits, I could eat and drink and sleep, and got my health and strength back quickly. That is the truth, and the whole of it."

When King Shahryar heard this he flew into a rage so great that it nearly choked him; but he mastered himself and said, "Brother, I would not call you a liar in this matter, but I cannot believe it until I see it with my own eyes."

"If you want to look at your own calamity," said Shah Zaman, "get up at once and make ready for hunting and coursing again, and then hide yourself here with me. You will see it, and your own eyes will prove it."

"True," said the king.

So he had it proclaimed that he was going travelling, and the troops and the tents went out of the city and camped within sight of it, and Shahryar rode out with them and took his place in the middle of his army, ordering the slaves to let no one in to him. When night came he called for his vizier and said, "Sit in my place, and let no one know I am gone for the next three days."

Then the two brothers disguised themselves and came back to the palace by night, in complete secrecy, and passed the dark hours there. At dawn they settled themselves at the lattice overlooking the pleasure grounds, and before long the queen and her women came out as before, passed under the windows, and made for the fountain. There they stripped, ten of them men and ten of them women, and the king's wife called out, "Where are you, Saeed?"

The Black man dropped straight down out of the tree, and rushing into her arms without any pause at all, called out, "I am Sa'ad al-Din Saood!" The queen laughed out loud, and they all fell to satisfying their lusts, and stayed at it a couple of hours. Then the white slaves got up off the serving women, and the Black slave got up off the queen; and they all went down into the basin, and after performing the full washing they put their clothes on and went away as they had done before.

When King Shahryar saw this shame done by his wife and his concubines, he went out of his mind. "Only in complete solitude," he cried, "can a man be safe from what goes on in this vile world! By Allah, life is nothing but one great wrong."

Then he said, "Do not cross me, brother, in what I am about to propose."

"I will not," said the other.

"Then let us get up exactly as we are and go away from here at once. We have no business with kingship. Let us wander over Allah's earth and worship the Almighty until we find somebody the same calamity has happened to; and if we find nobody, then death will be more welcome to us than life."

So the two brothers went out through a second private door of the palace, and never stopped travelling by night or by day until they came to a tree standing in the middle of a meadow, beside a spring of sweet water on the shore of the salt sea. They both drank, and sat down to rest.

An hour of the day had gone by when they heard a roaring and an uproar out in the middle of the sea, as if the heavens were falling in on the earth; and the sea broke into waves in front of them, and out of it there rose a black pillar, which grew and grew until it towered up to the sky and began to come towards that meadow.

They were terrified, and climbed to the top of the tree, which was a tall one, and looked out from there to see what was happening. And it was a jinni — one of the jinn, the spirits that people nowadays call genies — enormously tall, huge in the chest and the body, broad-browed and black as pitch, carrying a crystal chest on his head. He waded to shore through the deep water, came to the tree the two kings were in, and sat himself down under it.

Then he set the chest upright and drew out of it a casket with seven steel padlocks, which he unlocked with seven steel keys that he took from beside his thigh. And out of the casket came a young woman, white-skinned and lovely-faced, slender and finely made, and as bright as the moon of the fourteenth night, which is the full moon, or as the sun pouring down its light.
