The Doorkeeper's Tale

Know, Commander of the Faithful, that I had a father who, when his time was up, died and left me a great store of wealth. I stayed single a short while and then married one of the richest men of his day. I lived with him a year, when he died too; and my share of his property came to eighty thousand gold dinars, according to the holy law of inheritance.

So I became extremely rich, and my reputation spread far and wide, for I had ten changes of clothing made for me, each one worth a thousand dinars.

One day, as I was sitting at home, in came an old woman with a lantern jaw and sunken cheeks, and eyes puckered up, and eyebrows scant and scabby, and a head bare and bald; teeth broken by time and mauled, back bending and neck-nape nodding, face blotched, nose running, hair speckled black and white like a snake, and in complexion a perfect fright — just as the poet says of such a woman:

	Ill-omened hag! May her sins never be shriven
	nor mercy visit her on her dying bed.
	Her wiles could lead a thousand of the strongest mules,
	bolt as they might, on a thread of spider silk.

And as another says:

	A hag to whom the unlawful is the most lawful thing there is,
	and witchcraft has grown into wisdom in her sight.
	A mischief-making brat, a demon girl,
	a whorish woman and a pimping crone.

When the old woman came in she greeted me and kissed the ground before me and said:

"I have an orphan daughter at home, and tonight is her wedding and her displaying. We are poor folk and strangers in this city, and we know nobody who lives here, and we are broken-hearted. So earn yourself a reward and a recompense in Heaven by being present at her displaying; and when the ladies of this city hear that you are going to put in an appearance, they will come too. And so you will comfort her affliction, for she is badly bruised in spirit and has nobody to look to but Allah the Most High."

Then she wept and kissed my feet, reciting these lines:

	"Your presence brings a grace on us
	that we acknowledge before your winsome face;
	and if you were absent there is not one
	who could stand in your stead or take your place."

So pity and compassion took hold of me, and I said, "To hear is to consent; and please Allah I will do more than that for her, and she shall not be shown to her bridegroom except in my own clothes and ornaments and jewellery."

At this the old woman rejoiced and bowed her head to my feet and kissed them, saying, "Allah repay you with good and comfort your heart as you have comforted mine! But, my lady, do not trouble yourself to do me this service now; be ready by supper time, and I will come and fetch you."

And saying that, she kissed my hand and went her way.

I set about stringing my pearls and putting on my brocades and doing my face, little reckoning what Fortune had in her womb for me — when suddenly the old woman stood in front of me, simpering and smiling until she showed every tooth stump, and said, "My mistress, the city ladies have arrived; and when I told them you had promised to be present, they were delighted, and they are waiting for you now and looking eagerly for your coming and for the honour of meeting you."

So I threw on my cloak, and making the old crone walk in front of me and my maids behind me, I went on until we came to a street well watered and swept neat, where the winnowing breeze blew cool and sweet.

Here we were stopped by a gate arched over with a dome of marble, firmly seated on the solidest foundation, and leading to a palace whose walls rose tall and proud from the ground and whose pinnacle was crowned by the clouds. And over the doorway these lines were written:

	I am the house where Mirth shall always smile,
	the home of Joy for as long as I last.
	And in my courtyard a fountain jets and flows
	that neither tears nor troubles shall defile.
	Its margin is decked with the royal anemone,
	with myrtle, and narcissus flower, and camomile.

Arriving at the gate, which had a black curtain hanging in front of it, the old woman knocked, and it was opened to us; and we went in and found a hallway spread with carpets and hung all round with lamps, all of them lit, and wax candles in candelabra decorated with pendants of precious gems and noble metals.

We passed on through this passage until we came into a great room whose like for grandeur and beauty is not to be found in this world. It was hung and carpeted with silks, and lit with branched sconces and tapers ranged in a double row — an avenue running up to the upper and noble end of the room, where stood a couch of juniper wood encrusted with pearls and gems and topped by a canopy with mosquito curtains of satin looped up with pearls.

And we had hardly taken all this in when there came out from under the canopy a young lady — and I looked, Commander of the Faithful, on a face and a figure more perfect than the moon at its fullest, with a countenance brighter than the dawn and gleaming with saffron-coloured light, exactly as the poet sang when he said:

	You pace the palace, a marvel to see,
	a bride fit for a Chosroes or a Caesar.
	The rose plays wanton on your rosy cheek,
	O cheek as bright as dragon's blood!
	Slim-waisted, languorous, sleepy-eyed,
	with charms that promise every delight of love.
	And the head-dress that dresses your crowned brow
	is a night of grief laid over a glad morning's light.

The lovely young girl came down from the dais and said to me, "Welcome, and well come, and good cheer to my sister, the dearly beloved, the illustrious — and a thousand greetings!" And then she recited these lines:

	"If the house could only know who is coming it would rejoice
	and would kiss the very dust your foot has stood on;
	and with the tongue of circumstance the walls would say,
	Welcome and hail to one graced with generous gifts!"

Then she sat down and said to me, "Sister, I have a brother who has caught sight of you at various wedding feasts and festivals. He is a young man handsomer than I am, and he has fallen desperately in love with you, because generous Destiny has gathered up all beauty and perfection in you; and he has given money to this old woman so that she might visit you, and she has contrived in this way to bring the two of us together.

"He has heard that you are one of the notables of your family, and he is nothing less in his; and being anxious to join his lot to yours, he has practised this device to bring me into your company. For he is eager to marry you according to the ordinance of Allah and His Apostle; and in what is lawful and right there is no shame."

When I heard this, and saw myself fairly trapped in the house, I said, "To hear is to consent."

She was delighted and clapped her hands; at which a door opened and out came a young man in the flower of his youth, exquisitely dressed, a model of beauty and loveliness and symmetry and perfect grace, with gentle winning manners, and eyebrows like a bent bow with the arrow on the string, and eyes that bewitched every heart with a sorcery that is lawful in the sight of the Lord — as some rhymer says, describing his like:

	His face shines like the face of the young moon
	and Fortune stamps him with pearls for signs.

And Allah favour the man who said:

	Blessed be his beauty; blessed the Lord's decree
	who cast and shaped a thing of such brightness.
	He joins all the gifts of beauty into one;
	all humanity is lost in loving him;
	for Beauty herself has written on his forehead,
	"I testify that there is no Good but he."

When I looked at him my heart inclined to him and I loved him; and he sat down at my side and talked with me a while, when the young lady clapped her hands again — and a side door opened, and out came the judge with his four assessors as witnesses; and they greeted us and sat down and drew up and wrote out the marriage contract between me and the young man, and withdrew.

Then he turned to me and said, "May our night be blessed"; and presently added, "My lady, I have a condition to lay on you."

Said I, "My lord, what is that?"

At which he got up and fetched a copy of the Holy Book and handed it to me, saying, "Swear on this that you will never look at any man but me, and never incline your body or your heart to another."

I swore to it readily enough, and he was overjoyed and put his arms round my neck, while love for him took possession of my whole heart.

Then they set the table in front of us, and we ate and drank until we were satisfied; but I was dying for the night to come. And when night did come he led me to the bridal chamber and slept with me on the bed, and went on kissing and holding me till the morning — such a night as I had never seen even in my dreams.

I lived with him a life of happiness and delight for a full month, at the end of which I asked his leave to go on foot to the market and buy myself certain particular stuffs; and he gave me permission.

So I put on my cloak, and taking with me the old woman and a slave girl, went to the silk merchants' hall, where I sat down in the shop front of a young merchant whom the old woman recommended, saying to me:

"This young man's father died when he was a boy and left him a great store of wealth. He has a very fine stock of goods by him, and you will find what you are looking for with him, for nobody in the market has better stuffs than he has."

Then she said to him, "Show this lady the most costly stuffs you have"; and he replied, "Hearing and obedience!"

Then she whispered to me, "Say a civil word to him." But I replied, "I am pledged to speak to no man except my husband." And as she started sounding his praises I said sharply to her, "We want none of your sweet speeches; what we want is to buy whatever we need from him and go home."

So he brought me everything I was looking for and I offered him his money; but he refused to take it, saying, "Let it be a gift offered to my guest today."

Then I said to the old woman, "If he will not take the money, give him back his stuff."

"By Allah," he cried, "I will take nothing from you. I do not sell it for gold or for silver; I give the whole lot as a gift, for a single kiss — a kiss more precious to me than everything in this shop."

The old woman asked, "And what will the kiss profit you?" And turning to me, she whispered, "Daughter, you hear what this young fellow says. What harm will it do you if he gets a kiss from you and you get what you were looking for at that price?"

I replied, "I take refuge with Allah from any such thing! Do you not know that I am bound by an oath?"

But she answered, "Now hush! Just let him kiss you, and neither speak to him nor lean over him; so you will keep your oath and your silver, and no harm at all will come to you."

And she did not stop persuading me and pressing me and making light of the matter, until evil got into my mind; and I put my head in the sack and, declaring I would never consent, consented.

So I veiled my eyes and held up the edge of my cloak between me and the people going past, and he put his mouth to my cheek under the veil. But while he was kissing me he bit me — so hard a bite that it tore the flesh off my cheek, and the blood ran fast, and I fainted.

The old woman caught me in her arms; and when I came round I found the shop shut up and her grieving over me and saying, "Thank Allah for averting what might have been worse."

Then she said to me, "Come, take heart, and let us go home before the matter becomes public and you are dishonoured. And when you are safe inside the house, pretend to be ill and lie down and cover yourself up; and I will bring you powders and plasters to cure this bite with, and your wound will be healed in three days at the most."

So after a while I got up, in extreme distress and with terror full upon me; but I went on little by little until I reached the house, when I pleaded illness and lay down.

When it was night my husband came in to me and said, "What has happened to you, my darling, on this excursion of yours?"

And I replied, "I am not well; my head is aching badly."

Then he lit a candle and came close to me and looked hard at me and asked, "What is that wound I see on your cheek — and in the tenderest part of it too?"

And I answered, "When I went out today with your permission to buy stuffs, a camel loaded with firewood jostled me, and one of the pieces tore my veil and wounded my cheek as you see; for the streets of this city are truly narrow."

"Tomorrow," he cried, "I will go and complain to the Governor, and he shall hang every firewood seller in Baghdad."

"In Allah's name," I said, "do not burden your soul with such a sin against any man. The fact is that I was riding on a donkey and it stumbled and threw me to the ground, and my cheek landed on a stick or a piece of glass and got this wound."

"Then," said he, "tomorrow I will go up to Ja'afar the Barmecide and tell him the story, and he shall kill every donkey boy in Baghdad."

"Would you destroy all these men because of my wound," I said, "when what happened to me was by the decree of Allah and His destiny?"

But he answered, "There is no help for it." And springing to his feet, he plied me with words and pressed me until I was confused and frightened; and I stuttered and stammered and my speech grew thick, and I said, "This is a mere accident, by the decree of Allah."

Then, Commander of the Faithful, he guessed my case, and said, "You have been false to your oath."

He cried out at once with a loud cry, at which a door opened and in came seven Black slaves, whom he ordered to drag me from my bed and throw me down in the middle of the room. Then he ordered one of them to pinion my elbows and squat on my head; and a second to sit on my knees and hold my feet; and drawing his sword, he gave it to a third and said:

"Strike her, Sa'ad, and cut her in two, and let each of you take half and throw it into the Tigris for the fish to eat. For that is the retribution due to those who violate their vows and are unfaithful to their love."

And he redoubled in fury, and recited these lines:

	"If there were somebody who shared her love with me,
	I would strangle Love, though my life were killed by Love,
	saying, O Soul, Death would be the nobler choice,
	for Love is a bad thing when shared between two partners."

Then he repeated to the slave, "Strike her, Sa'ad!"

And when the slave who was sitting on me was sure of the order, he bent down to me and said, "My mistress, repeat the profession of faith, and think whether there is anything you would like done; for truly this is the last hour of your life."

"Good slave," I said, "wait just a little, and get off my head so that I can charge you with my last instructions."

Then I raised my head and saw the state I was in — how I had fallen from a high position into the lowest disgrace, and into death after such a life as I had had; and how I had brought my punishment on myself by my own sin. And at that the tears streamed from my eyes and I wept beyond all weeping.

But he looked at me with eyes full of anger and began repeating:

	"Tell the woman who turns away from our love to work it a sore injury,
	and takes herself a fine new love and tosses the old one over:
	we have had enough of you before you shall cry enough of us.
	What passed between us is sufficient, and perhaps something more."

When I heard this, Commander of the Faithful, I wept and looked at him and began repeating these lines:

	"You doom my love to separation and stay unmoved yourself;
	you make my sore lids sleepless and sleep while I complain.
	You make a firm friendship reign between my eyes and insomnia,
	and yet my heart cannot forget you, nor can I hold back my tears.
	You made me swear with many an oath to keep my faith for ever;
	but when you reigned as the lord of my breast you worked me a traitor's ruin.
	I loved you like a silly child who does not know what Love is;
	so spare the learner, do not let her be killed by the master.
	By the name of Allah I beg you, when I am dead and gone,
	write on my tomb: this woman died of Love, whose senses Love had taken.
	Then perhaps somebody will pass that way who has felt the fire of Love,
	and treading on a lover's heart will melt with pity and with grief."

When I had finished my verses the tears came again; but the poetry and the weeping only added fury to his fury, and he recited:

	"It was not satiety that made me leave the darling of my soul,
	but that she sinned a mortal sin that caught me in its grip:
	she tried to let another man share the love between the two of us,
	but my true Faith in Unity refuses any partnership."

When he had stopped reciting I wept again, and begged his pardon and humbled myself in front of him and spoke to him softly, saying to myself, "I will work on him with words; and perhaps he will hold back from killing me, even if he takes everything I have."

So I complained of my sufferings and began to repeat these lines:

	"Now, by your life — if you were just, you would not have taken my life;
	but who can break the law of separation that parts two lovers?
	You load me with the heavy weight of longing love, when I
	can hardly bear the weight of my own shift for weakness and for pain.
	I do not marvel to see my life and soul laid in ruins;
	I marvel much to see my body bear such pangs of separation."

When I had finished my verse I wept again; and he looked at me and reviled me in abusive language, repeating these lines:

	"You were all taken up with the love of another man, not me;
	it was yours to show me the face of separation, mine only to see it.
	I will leave you, because you were the first of us to take your leave,
	and bear that parting blow as patiently as you bore it.
	Just as you went looking for another love, so I will look for another,
	and make the crime of murdering love your own atrocity."

When he had finished his verses he cried out to the slave again, "Cut her in half and free us from her, for we have no profit of her."

So the slave came close to me, Commander of the Faithful; and I stopped bandying verses and made certain of death, and despairing of life, committed my affairs to Almighty Allah — when the old woman rushed in and threw herself at my husband's feet and kissed them and wept and said:

"My son, by the rights of my having nursed you and by my long service to you, I beg you to pardon this young lady, for truly she has done nothing to deserve such a doom. You are a very young man, and I am afraid her death may be laid at your door; for it is said: whoever kills shall be killed. As for this wanton — since that is what you think her — drive her out of your doors, out of your love and out of your heart."

And she did not stop weeping and pressing him until he relented and said, "I pardon her. But I must set my mark on her, and it shall show on her all her life."

Then he ordered the slaves to drag me along the ground and lay me out at full length, after stripping me of all my clothes; and when the slaves had sat on me so that I could not move, he fetched a rod of quince wood and came down with it on my body, and went on beating me across the back and the sides until I lost consciousness from the pain, and I despaired of my life.

Then he ordered the slaves to take me away as soon as it was dark, along with the old woman to show them the way, and to throw me down on the floor of the house I had lived in before my marriage. They did their master's bidding, and threw me down in my old home, and went their way.

I did not come out of my faint until dawn appeared, when I set about dressing my wounds with ointments and other medicines; and I doctored myself — but my sides and my ribs still show the marks of the rod, as you have seen.

I lay weak and confined to my bed four months before I was able to get up, and my health came back to me. At the end of that time I went to the house where all this had happened, and found it a ruin; the whole street had been pulled down from end to end and heaps of rubbish stood where the building had been; and I could not learn how it had come about.

Then I went to this sister of mine on my father's side, and found her with these two black bitches. I greeted her and told her what had happened to me and the whole of my story, and she said, "Sister, who is safe from the spite of Time, and who is secure? Thanks be to Allah, who has brought you off safely." And she began to say:

	"Such is the world, so keep a patient heart
	when riches leave you and when friends depart."

Then she told me her own story, and what had happened to her with her two sisters, and how it had all ended. So we lived together, and the subject of marriage was never on our tongues in all these years.

After a while we were joined by our other sister, the caterer, who goes out every morning and buys everything we need for the day and the night; and we went on in that condition until last night.

In the morning our sister went out as usual to do her marketing, and then there happened to us what happened, from bringing the porter into the house and admitting these three kalandars.

We treated them kindly and honourably, and a quarter of the night had not gone by before three grave and respectable merchants from Mosul joined us and told us their adventures. We sat talking with them, but on one condition, which they broke; at which we treated them as suited their breach of promise, and made them repeat the account they had given of themselves. They did as we told them, and we forgave their offence; so they left us. And this morning we were unexpectedly summoned into your presence.

And such is our story.

The Caliph marvelled at her words and ordered the tale to be written down and put on record and laid up in his record rooms:—

And Shahrazad saw the dawn breaking, and fell silent.

When it was the Nineteenth Night,

she went on: It has reached me, O fortunate King, that the Caliph commanded this story, and those of the sister and the kalandars, to be recorded in the archives and set in the royal record rooms.

Then he asked the eldest lady, the mistress of the house, "Do you know where the ifritah is who put your sisters under a spell?"

And she answered, "Commander of the Faithful, she gave me a ringlet of her hair, saying: whenever you want to see me, burn a couple of these hairs and I will be with you at once, even if I were beyond Mount Kaf."

Said the Caliph, "Bring me the hair."

So she brought it, and he threw the whole lock on the fire. And as soon as the smell of the burning hair spread, the palace shook and trembled, and everybody there heard a rumbling and rolling of thunder and a noise as of wings — and the jinniyah who had been a serpent stood in the Caliph's presence.

Now she was a Muslim, so she greeted him and said, "Peace be with you, Vicar of Allah"; to which he replied, "And with you also be peace, and the mercy of Allah and His blessing."

Then she went on, "Know that this girl sowed the seed of kindness for me, and I cannot repay her enough, in that she delivered me from death and destroyed my enemy. Now I had seen how her sisters dealt with her, and I felt bound to avenge her on them. At first I meant to kill them, but I was afraid it would grieve her, so I transformed them into bitches. But if you want them released, Commander of the Faithful, I will release them to please you and her, for I am one of the Muslims."

Said the Caliph, "Release them; and afterwards we will look into the affair of the beaten lady and consider her case carefully; and if the truth of her story is proved, I will exact retaliation from the man who wronged her."

Said the ifritah, "Commander of the Faithful, I will release them at once; and I will show you the man who did that deed to this lady and wronged her and took her property — and he is the nearest of all men to you."

So saying, she took a cup of water and muttered a spell over it and spoke words there was no understanding; and then she sprinkled some of the water over the faces of the two bitches, saying, "Return to your former human shape!" — at which they were restored to their natural forms and fell to praising their Creator.

Then said the ifritah, "Commander of the Faithful, the truth is that the man who scourged this lady with rods is your son Al-Amin, brother of Al-Ma'mun. For he had heard of her beauty and loveliness, and he played a lover's trick on her and married her according to the law, and committed the crime — such as it is — of scourging her.

"And yet he is not to be blamed for beating her, for he laid a condition on her and swore her by a solemn oath not to do a certain thing; and she was false to her vow. He meant to put her to death, but he feared Almighty Allah and contented himself with scourging her, as you have seen, and with sending her back to her own place. Such is the story of the second lady, and the Lord knows all."

When the Caliph heard these words from the ifritah, and knew who had beaten the girl, he marvelled with a mighty marvelling and said:

"Praise be to Allah, the Most High, the Almighty, who has shown me such exceeding mercy, letting me deliver these two girls from sorcery and torture, and granting me the knowledge of the secret of this lady's history! And now, by Allah, we will do a deed that shall be recorded of us after we are gone."

Then he summoned his son Al-Amin and questioned him about the story of the second lady, the doorkeeper; and he told it in the face of truth.

At which the Caliph had the judges and their witnesses called in, and the three kalandars, and the first lady with her full sisters who had been under the spell. And he married the three sisters to the three kalandars, whom he knew to be princes and the sons of kings, and appointed them chamberlains about his own person, assigning them stipends and allowances and everything they needed, and lodging them in his palace at Baghdad.

He returned the beaten lady to his son Al-Amin, renewing the marriage contract between them and giving her great wealth, and ordered the house rebuilt finer than it had been before.

As for himself, he took the caterer as his wife and lay with her that night; and next day he set apart an apartment for her in his own palace, with maids to serve her and a fixed daily allowance.

And the people marvelled at their Caliph's generosity and his natural kindness and his princely wisdom; nor did he forget to send all these histories to be recorded in his annals.

When Shahrazad stopped speaking, Dunyazad exclaimed, "Sister, by Allah, this is truly a most pleasant tale and a delightful one; the like of it was never heard. But please tell me another story now, to while away what is left of the waking hours of this night of ours."

She replied, "With love and gladness, if the King gives me leave."

And he said, "Tell your tale, and tell it quickly."

So she began, in these words,
