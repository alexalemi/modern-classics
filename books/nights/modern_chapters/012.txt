After this the young lady turned the frying pan over and went out the way she had come in, and the kitchen wall closed behind her.

When the cook came round from her fainting fit she saw the four fish burnt black as charcoal, and cried out, "His staff broke at the first blow!" — and fell down in a faint again.

While she was lying there the vizier came for the fish; and seeing her senseless on the floor, not knowing Sunday from Thursday, he shoved her with his foot and said, "Bring the fish for the Sultan!"

At that she came round, and wept, and told him her situation and everything that had happened to her. The vizier marvelled greatly and exclaimed, "This is nothing short of an extraordinary business!" And he sent for the fisherman and said to him, "You, fisherman, must fetch us four more fish like the ones you brought before."

So the man went off to the tarn and cast his net; and when he landed it, there were four fish in it exactly like the first. He carried them straight to the vizier, who took them in to the cook and said, "Up with you, and fry these in front of me, so that I can see this business for myself."

The girl got up and cleaned the fish and set them in the frying pan over the fire. But they had not been there long before the wall split apart and the young lady appeared, dressed as before and holding the wand, which she again thrust into the frying pan, saying, "Fish! Fish! Are you keeping to your old covenant?"

And the fish lifted their heads and answered, "Yes! Yes!" and recited the couplet:

	Come back and so will I! Keep faith and so will I!
	But if you choose to abandon us, I will pay you back till we are quits!

When it was the Seventh Night,

she went on: It has reached me, O fortunate King, that when the fish had spoken, and the young lady had upset the frying pan with her rod and gone out the way she came in, and the wall had closed up behind her, the vizier cried, "This is not a thing that can be kept from the King."

So he went and told him what had happened; at which the King said, "There is no help for it. I must see this with my own eyes."

Then he sent for the fisherman and ordered him to bring four more fish like the first, and to take three men with him as witnesses. The fisherman brought the fish at once; and the King, after ordering that he be given four hundred gold pieces, turned to the vizier and said, "Up, and fry me these fish here in front of me."

The minister answered, "To hear is to obey," and had the frying pan brought, and threw the cleaned fish into it and set it over the fire. And then the wall split apart, and out burst a Black slave, huge as a boulder, or like something left over from the ancient giants of the tribe of Ad, with a branch of a green tree in his hand; and he cried in a loud and terrible voice, "Fish! Fish! Are you all keeping to your old covenant?"

At which the fish lifted their heads out of the frying pan and said, "Yes! Yes! We are true to our vow." And they recited the couplet again:

	Come back and so will I! Keep faith and so will I!
	But if you choose to abandon us, I will pay you back till we are quits!

Then the huge man came up to the frying pan and turned it over with the branch, and went out the way he had come in.

When he had vanished from their sight the King inspected the fish; and finding them all burnt black as charcoal, he was utterly bewildered, and said to the vizier, "This is truly a matter that cannot be kept quiet; and as for these fish, there is certainly some marvellous adventure attached to them."

So he had the fisherman brought, and asked him, "Confound you, man — where did these fish come from?"

"From a tarn between four heights," he answered, "lying behind that mountain there, which is in sight of your city."

"How many days' march?" asked the King.

"Our lord the Sultan," he said, "it is half an hour's walk."

The King was astonished, and immediately ordered his men to march and his horsemen to mount, and led off with the fisherman going in front as guide — privately cursing the ifrit as he went.

They travelled on until they had climbed the mountain and come down into a great desert which none of them had ever seen in all their lives; and the Sultan and all his company marvelled at this open country set among four mountains, and at the tarn, and at its fish of four colours, red and white, yellow and blue.

The King stood rooted to the spot in wonder, and asked his troops and everyone else there, "Has any one of you ever seen this piece of water before now?"

And they all answered, "O King of the age, we never set eyes on it in all our days."

They questioned the oldest inhabitants they could find as well, men well on in years; and every one of them replied, "We have never seen a little lake like this in this place."

At which the King said, "By Allah, I will not go back to my capital or sit on the throne of my forefathers until I have learned the truth about this tarn and the fish in it."

Then he ordered his men to dismount and camp all round the mountain, which they did; and he summoned his vizier, a minister of great experience, shrewd, sharp-witted and well versed in affairs, and said to him:

"There is something in my mind that I mean to do, and I will tell you what it is. My heart tells me to go out alone tonight and get to the root of the mystery of this tarn and its fish. You take your seat at my tent door, and say to the emirs and viziers, the governors and the chamberlains — in short, to everybody who asks you — that the Sultan is unwell and has ordered me to admit nobody. And take care that no one learns what I am doing."

And the vizier could not oppose him.

Then the King changed his clothes and his ornaments and slung his sword over his shoulder, and took a path that led up one of the mountains, and marched the rest of the night until the day dawned; and he did not stop travelling until the heat was too much for him. After his long walk he rested a while, and then took up the march again and went on through the second night until dawn — when suddenly a black point appeared in the distance.

He was delighted at it and said to himself, "Perhaps somebody here will tell me the mystery of the tarn and its fish."

Coming closer to the dark object, he found it was a palace built of black stone plated with iron; and one leaf of the gate stood wide open while the other was shut.

The King's spirits rose as he stood before the gate and rapped a light rap. But hearing no answer, he knocked a second and a third time; and there was still no sign of anyone. Then he knocked as loud as he could, and still no answer. So he said, "It must be empty."

At that he gathered up his resolution and walked boldly in through the main gate into the great hall, and there called out, "Hello, people of the palace! I am a stranger and a traveller. Have you anything here in the way of food?"

He repeated his call a second and a third time, and still no reply came. So, steadying his heart and making up his mind, he went on through the entrance hall into the very middle of the palace, and found not a soul in it.

And yet it was furnished with silks starred with gold, and hangings were let down over the doorways. In the middle was a spacious court, off which stood four open rooms, each with its raised dais, room facing room; a canopy shaded the court, and in the centre was a jetting fountain with four figures of lions in red gold, spouting out water as clear as pearls and as transparent as gems. Round about the palace birds had been let loose, and over the whole of it was stretched a net of golden wire to stop them flying away. In short, there was everything there except human beings.

The King marvelled greatly at it, and yet felt sad at heart, because he could see nobody to give him any account of that waste land and its tarn, of the fish, the mountains, or the palace itself.

Presently, as he sat between the doors deep in thought, he heard a voice of lamentation, as if from a heart worn out with grief, and it was chanting these verses:

	I hid what I suffered from him, and even so it came to light,
	and sleep fled from my eyelids every night and turned into sleeplessness.
	O world! O Fate! hold back your hand and stop your hurting and harming;
	look and see my luckless spirit, the colour of it and the terror of it.
	Will you never show pity to a high-born young man who lost himself
	on the road of Love, and fell from wealth and fame to the lowest and basest state?
	I used to be jealous of the west wind as it breathed over your body —
	but when Destiny comes down it blinds a man's sight.
	What can the unlucky archer do who faces his enemy
	and bends his bow to loose the arrow, and finds his bowstring gone?
	And when cares and troubles bear so heavily on a young man of generous heart,
	how is he to escape his lot, and where is his place of refuge from Fate?

Now when the Sultan heard the mournful voice he sprang to his feet, and following the sound, found a curtain let down over the door of a room. He raised it and saw behind it a young man sitting on a couch about a foot and a half above the floor — a young man beautiful to look at, well made, and finely spoken. His forehead was flower-white and his cheek was rosy bright, and there was a mole on the width of his cheek like a speck of ambergris. As the poet writes:

	A slim-waisted youth, from whose hair and forehead
	the world takes both its blackness and its light.
	In the whole round of Creation there is no fairer show,
	no rarer sight your eye has ever met:
	a nut-brown mole sits enthroned on a cheek
	of the rosiest red, beneath an eye of jet.

The King was glad, and greeted him; but the young man stayed sitting where he was, in his caftan of silk edged with Egyptian gold and his crown studded with gems of every kind; and his face was sad with the marks of sorrow. He returned the royal greeting most courteously and added, "My lord, your dignity demands that I rise to you; and my only excuse is to beg your pardon."

"You are excused, young man," said the King. "Look on me as your guest, come here on a particular errand. I want you to tell me the secrets of this tarn and its fish, and of this palace, and of your being alone in it, and the cause of your groaning and grieving."

When the young man heard this he wept bitterly, until his breast was soaked with tears, and began reciting:

	Say to the man who sleeps carelessly while the arrow of Fortune flies:
	how many does this shifting world lay low, and how many raise up?
	Though your own eye is sealed in sleep, the eyes of the Almighty do not sleep.
	And who has ever found Time fair, or Fate constant in its ways?

Then he heaved a long-drawn sigh and recited:

	Trust your case to Him, the Lord who made mankind;
	leave off worry and care and cultivate a contented mind.
	Do not ask about the past, or how or why it came about:
	all human things were designed by Fate and Destiny.

The King marvelled, and asked him, "What makes you weep, young man?"

And he answered, "How should I not weep, when this is my situation?"

Then he put out his hand and lifted the skirt of his robe — and the lower half of him was stone, all the way down to his feet, while from the navel up to the hair of his head he was a man.

The King, seeing the state he was in, grieved sorely, and out of compassion cried, "Alas and alack! Truly, young man, you are heaping sorrow on top of my sorrow. I meant to ask you only about the mystery of the fish, and now I am concerned to learn your story as well as theirs. But there is no Majesty and there is no Might save in Allah, the Glorious, the Great! Lose no time, young man — tell me your whole tale at once."

"Lend me your ears," he said, "and your sight, and your understanding."

"They are all at your service," said the King.

At which the young man began. "My case, and the case of these fish, is a wonder and a marvel; and if it were engraved with needles on the inner corners of the eye, it would be a warning to anyone willing to take warning."

"How is that?" asked the King.
