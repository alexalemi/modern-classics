The Third Kalandar's Tale
(Part 2 of 3)

When it was the Sixteenth Night,

she said: It has reached me, O fortunate King, that Ajib went on with his tale to the lady:

When I was certain I had killed him, I got up and went up the stairs and replaced the trapdoor and covered it with earth as before. Then I looked out to sea and saw the ship cleaving the water and making for the island; at which I was afraid, and said, "The moment they come and see the young man put to death, they will know it was I who killed him, and they will kill me without any delay."

So I climbed up into a tall tree and hid myself among the leaves. And I had hardly done it before the ship anchored and the slaves landed with the old man, the young man's father, and made straight for the place; and when they cleared the earth away they were surprised to find it loose.

Then they raised the trapdoor and went down, and found the young man lying at full length, dressed in fine new clothes, with his face glowing after the bath and the knife deep in his heart.

At the sight of it they shrieked and wept and beat their faces, cursing the murderer out loud; while a fainting fit came over the old man, so that the slaves thought him dead, unable to survive his son.

At last they wrapped the murdered young man in his clothes and carried him up and laid him on the ground and covered him with a shroud of silk. And while they were making for the ship the old man came round; and looking at his son stretched out there, he fell to the ground and threw dust over his head and struck his face and plucked out his beard; and his weeping redoubled as he thought of his murdered son, and he fainted away once more.

After a while a slave went and fetched a strip of silk, and they laid the old man on it and sat down at his head.

All this took place, and I was up in the tree above them watching everything that happened; and my heart turned grey before my head did, at the hard lot that was mine and the distress and anguish I had been through. And I fell to reciting:

	"How many a joy has fled by Allah's will
	with a flight that escapes the sight of the wisest head!
	How many a sadness begins the day
	and grows quite cheerful before the day is out!
	How many a good thing treads on the heels of an ill one,
	making the mourner's heart thrill with joy!"

But the old man, my lady, did not come out of his faint until near sunset; when he came to himself and looked at his dead son and remembered what had happened, and how the thing he had dreaded had come to pass. And he beat his face and his head and recited these lines:

	"My heart is racked by parting from my friends
	and two streams run for ever out of my eyelids.
	My hopes went out with them — alas, and well away!
	What way is left me now to speak or act?
	Would I had never looked on the sight of them.
	What way, good sirs, when the paths grow narrower and narrower?
	What charm shall calm my pangs when the longings of love
	burn like this and glow in my very vitals?
	Would I had trodden the road of Death along with them!
	Then this parting blow would never have fallen on us.
	Allah — I pray the Merciful to show me mercy
	and mix our lives and never part them again.
	How blessed we were, living together under one roof,
	joined in our joys and reckoning nothing of grief;
	until Fortune shot us with the arrow of separation.
	Ah, who shall bear such a parting-pang with patience?
	And the dart of Death struck down in the middle of the tribe
	the pearl of the age, whom the Morning saw shining brightest.
	I cried out, while his very case found a tongue and spoke:
	Would to Heaven, my son, that Death had let his doom run late!
	Which is the readiest road for me to meet with you,
	my son, for whom I would give away my own soul?
	If I call him the sun — no; the sun goes down.
	If I call him the moon — the moons wane. Ah, no.
	O sad mischance of you, O doom of the days;
	your place no other love will ever know.
	Your father, out of his mind, looks at you and despairs
	of overthrowing Fate by any wit or wisdom.
	Some evil eye has cast its spell this day —
	and may it fall foul on him as it has fallen foul on us."

Then he gave one single sob, and his soul fled his body.

The slaves shrieked aloud, "Alas, our lord!" and showered dust on their heads and redoubled their weeping and wailing. Presently they carried their dead master to the ship side by side with his dead son; and having moved all the goods out of the dwelling into the vessel, they set sail and disappeared from my sight.

I came down from the tree and, raising the trapdoor, went down into the underground dwelling, where everything reminded me of the young man; and I looked at the poor remains of him and began repeating these verses:

	"I see their tracks, and pine with pain and pang,
	and weep and yearn over hearths they have left.
	And I pray to Him who doomed them to depart
	that some day He may grant the gift of a safe return."

Then, my lady, I went up again through the trapdoor; and every day I used to wander round about the island, and every night I came back to the underground hall.

I lived like this for a month; until at last, looking at the western side of the island, I noticed that every day the tides ebbed and left shallow water which the flow did not make up for; and by the end of the month the sea had shown dry land in that direction.

At this I was overjoyed, making certain of my safety; so I got up and forded what little water was left and reached the mainland — where I fell in with great heaps of loose sand in which even a camel's hoof would sink to the knee.

However, I put heart into my soul, and wading through the sand, saw a fire shining a long way off and burning with a blazing light. So I made for it, hoping perhaps to find help, and broke out into these verses:

	"Perhaps my Fortune may turn her bridle round
	and Time bring me good, jealous though he is called;
	may forward my hopes and further everything I need,
	and pay for the ills that are past with present good."

And when I got near the fire — it was a palace, with gates of copper burnished red, which, when the rising sun shone on them, gleamed and glittered a long way off and had looked to me like a fire.

I was glad at the sight, and sat down opposite the gate; but I was hardly settled in my seat before I was met by ten young men in sumptuous clothes, and every one of them blind in the left eye, which looked as though it had been plucked out. They had with them an old man, an old, old man; and I marvelled a great deal at the look of them, and at their all being blind in the same eye.

When they saw me they greeted me with the salutation of peace and asked me about my circumstances and my history; at which I told them everything that had happened to me and what a full measure of misfortune had been mine.

Marvelling at my tale, they took me into the mansion, where I saw ten couches ranged round the hall, each with its blue bedding and a coverlet of blue cloth; and in the middle stood a smaller couch furnished like the rest with blue and nothing else.

As we came in, each of the young men took his seat on his own couch, and the old man seated himself on the smaller one in the middle and said to me, "Young man, sit down on the floor, and do not ask about our situation or about the loss of our eyes."

Presently he got up and set some meat in a large dish and drink in a big bowl in front of each young man, and treated me the same way; and after that they sat questioning me about my adventures and what had happened to me, and I went on telling them my tale until the night was far gone.

Then the young men said, "Master, will you not set our usual thing before us? The time has come."

He replied, "With love and gladness," and got up and went into a closet and disappeared; but presently he came back carrying ten trays on his head, each covered with a strip of blue cloth. He set a tray in front of each young man, and lighting ten wax candles, stuck one on each tray, and drew off the covers — and underneath there was nothing but ashes and powdered charcoal and kettle soot.

Then all the young men tucked their sleeves up to the elbow and fell to weeping and wailing; and they blackened their faces and smeared their clothes and beat their brows and struck their breasts, exclaiming over and over, "We were sitting at our ease, and nothing but our own meddling brought us unease!"

They did not stop doing this until the dawn drew near, when the old man got up and heated water for them; and they washed their faces and put on other, clean clothes.

Now when I saw this, my lady, my senses left me for sheer wonderment and my wits went wild, and my heart and my head were so full of it that I forgot what had happened to me. And I could not keep quiet — I felt I simply had to speak out and ask them about these strange things; so I said to them:

"How is it you do this, after we have been so open-hearted and cheerful together? Thanks be to Allah, you are all sound and sane, and yet actions like these suit nobody but madmen, or people possessed by an evil spirit. I beg you, by everything dearest to you — why do you refuse to tell me your history, and the reason for losing your eyes and for blackening your faces with ashes and soot?"

At this they turned to me and said, "Young man, do not listen to the promptings of your youth, and ask us no questions."

Then they slept, and I with them; and when they woke the old man brought us something to eat; and after we had eaten and the plates and goblets had been taken away, they sat talking until nightfall, when the old man got up and lit the wax candles and the lamps and set food and drink in front of us.

After we had eaten and drunk we sat talking and drinking in good company until midnight, when they said to the old man, "Bring us our usual thing, for the hour of sleep is at hand."

So he got up and brought them the trays of soot and ashes; and they did as they had done the night before, no more and no less.

I stayed with them like this for a month, during which they blackened their faces with ashes every night and washed and changed their clothes when the morning was young; and I only marvelled the more, and my scruples and my curiosity grew to such a pitch that I had to give up even eating and drinking.

At last I lost command of myself, for my heart was aflame with a fire that could not be put out and a flame that could not be hidden, and I said, "Young men, will you not relieve my trouble and tell me the reason for blackening your faces like this, and the meaning of your words — we were sitting at our ease, and nothing but our own meddling brought us unease?"

They said, "It would be better to keep these things secret."

Still I was so bewildered by what they did that I stopped eating and drinking altogether; and at last, losing patience entirely, I said to them, "There is no help for it. You must tell me the reason for all this."

They replied, "We kept our secret only for your own good. Gratifying you will bring evil down on you, and you will become one-eyed, exactly as we are."

I repeated, "There is no help for it; and if you will not tell me, then let me leave you and go back to my own people and be spared the sight of these things. For the proverb says:

	Better that you stay and I take my leave:
	for what the eye does not see, the heart will never grieve."

At this they said to me, "Remember, young man, that if harm comes to you we will not take you in again or let you stay among us." And bringing a ram, they slaughtered it and skinned it.

Last of all they gave me a knife, saying: "Take this skin and stretch yourself out on it, and we will sew it up around you. Presently there will come to you a certain bird called the roc, which will catch you up in its claws and tower high in the air and then set you down on a mountain. When you feel that it is no longer flying, rip the hide open with this blade and come out of it; the bird will be startled and will fly away and leave you free.

"After this, travel for half a day, and the march will bring you to a palace, wonderful to look at, towering high in the air and built of khalanj wood and aloes-wood and sandalwood, plated with red gold and studded with every kind of emerald and costly gem fit for a seal ring. Go into it and you will get what you want — for we have all gone into that palace; and that is the reason for our losing our eyes and for blackening our faces. If we were to tell you our stories now it would take far too long, for every one of us lost his left eye by an adventure of his own."

I was delighted at their words; and they did with me as they had said, and the bird, the roc, carried me off and set me down on the mountain. Then I came out of the skin and walked on until I reached the palace.

The door stood open as I came up, and I found myself in a spacious and handsome hall, exceedingly wide — as wide as a racecourse; and around it were a hundred rooms with doors of sandalwood and aloes-wood plated with red gold and fitted with silver rings for knockers.

At the upper end of the hall I saw forty girls, sumptuously dressed and ornamented, and every one of them as bright as the moon; nobody could ever tire of gazing at them, and they were all so lovely that the strictest hermit alive, on seeing them, would have become their slave and obeyed their will.

When they saw me the whole company came up to me and said, "Welcome, and well come, and good cheer to you, our lord! This whole month we have been expecting you. Praised be Allah, who has sent us someone worthy of us, as we are worthy of him."

Then they made me sit down on a high divan and said, "Today you are our lord and master, and we are your servants and your handmaids; so order us as you like."

And I marvelled at their situation.

Presently one of them got up and set food in front of me, and I ate and they ate with me; while others heated water and washed my hands and feet and changed my clothes, and others made sherbets ready and gave us to drink; and they all gathered round me, full of joy and gladness at my coming.

Then they sat down and talked with me until nightfall, when five of them got up and laid the trays and spread them with flowers and fragrant herbs and fruit, fresh and dried, and sweets in profusion. At last they brought out a fine wine service with rich old wine; and we sat down to drink, and some sang songs and others played the lute and the psaltery and the recorder and other instruments, and the bowl went merrily round.

At this such gladness took hold of me that I forgot every sorrow in the world, and said, "This is what life is; how sad that it passes!"

I enjoyed their company until the time came for rest, and our heads were all warm with wine, when they said, "Our lord, choose from among us the one who is to share your bed tonight, and who is not to lie with you again until forty days have gone by."

So I chose a girl with a lovely face and a perfect figure, her eyes edged with kohl by nature's own hand; hair long and jet black, teeth slightly parted and eyebrows meeting.
