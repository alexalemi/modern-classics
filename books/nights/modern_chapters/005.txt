The Second Old Man's Story

Know, Lord of the Kings of the Jinn, that these two dogs are my brothers, and I am the third of us.

When our father died he left us a capital of three thousand gold pieces. I opened a shop with my share and traded in it, and my two brothers did the same, each setting up a shop of his own.

But I had not been in business long before the eldest sold his stock for a thousand dinars, and bought an outfit and goods, and went off to foreign parts. He was away a whole year with the caravan.

Then one day, as I was sitting in my shop, a beggar came and stood in front of me asking for charity. "May Allah open another door to you," I said.

At which he answered, weeping, "Am I so changed that you do not know me?"

I looked at him closely — and it was my brother. So I got up and made him welcome, and sat him down in the shop and asked him what had happened.

"Do not ask," he said. "My money is all gone, and my situation is beyond describing."

So I took him to the baths and dressed him in a suit of my own clothes and gave him a room in my house. What is more, when I went over my stock accounts and the profits of my business, I found that hard work had earned me a thousand dinars, and that my capital, the head of my wealth, came to two thousand. So I split the whole lot with him, and said, "Suppose that you never went abroad at all, but stayed at home. And do not let your bad luck weigh on you."

He took his share very gladly and opened a shop of his own; and things went quietly along for a few days and nights.

But then my second brother — that other dog there — set his heart on travelling too, and sold off what goods and stock he had; and though we tried to stop him, he would not be stopped. He got himself an outfit for the journey and set off with a party of travellers.

After a whole year away he came back to me, exactly as my elder brother had.

"Brother," I said, "did I not try to talk you out of travelling?"

He burst into tears and cried, "Brother, this is what destiny has decreed. Here I am, a plain beggar, without a penny and without a shirt to my back."

So I took him to the baths as well, jinni, and dressed him in new clothes of my own, and took him along to my shop and gave him food and drink. And I said to him, "Brother, it is my habit to cast up my shop accounts at the head of every year, and whatever surplus I find is to be shared between you and me."

So I struck a balance, ifrit, and found two thousand dinars of profit; and I gave praise to the Creator, be He extolled and exalted, and made over half of it to my brother and kept the other half myself. Then he set about opening a shop, and so we went on for a long while.

After a time my brothers began pressing me to travel with them. I refused. "What did the two of you gain by travelling," I said, "that I should expect to gain by it?" And since I would not listen to them, we each went back to our own shop and bought and sold as before.

They kept on urging me to travel for a whole twelve months, and I kept refusing, until six full years had gone by. Then I agreed, and said, "Brothers, here I am, ready to travel with you. Now let me see what money you have between you."

I found they did not have a farthing. They had squandered everything on rich food and drink and pleasures of the flesh. But I did not say a word of reproach to them. Far from it: I went over my shop accounts once more, and sold what goods and stock were mine; and finding myself the owner of six thousand gold pieces, I gladly divided the sum in half.

"These three thousand gold pieces are for you and me to trade with," I said to my brothers. And I added, "Let us bury the other half in the ground, so that it will be there if any harm comes to us — and in that case each of us takes a thousand and opens a shop again."

"Your reckoning is right," they both said. So I gave each of them his thousand gold pieces and kept the same for myself, a thousand dinars.

Then we got suitable goods together and hired a ship, and loaded our merchandise, and sailed on day after day for a full month, until we came to a city where we sold our venture; and for every gold piece we took ten.

And as we were turning back to our ship we found on the seashore a girl in worn and ragged clothes, and she kissed my hand and said, "Master, is there kindness in you, and charity? I can make you a fitting return for both."

"There is," I answered. "There is goodwill in me and a wish to do right, even if you make me no return at all."

Then she said, "Take me as your wife, master, and carry me to your city, for I have given myself to you. Do me this kindness, and I am one of those who deserve kindness and charity: I will make you a fitting return for it. And do not be ashamed of the state you find me in."

When I heard her say this my heart went out to her, as Allah willed it — be He extolled and exalted. So I took her and clothed her and had a good place made ready for her on the ship, and treated her with honour.

So we sailed on; and my heart became attached to her past all attaching, and I was not away from her by night or by day, and I paid more attention to her than to my brothers. So they grew cold towards me, and became jealous of my wealth and the quantity of goods I had, and they looked greedily at everything I owned. They took counsel together to murder me and seize my money, saying, "Let us kill our brother, and all his money will be ours." And Satan made the deed look fine to them. So when they found me alone — and I asleep beside my wife — they picked the two of us up and threw us into the sea.

My wife woke with a start out of her sleep, and instantly became an ifritah, and lifted me up and carried me to an island; and she vanished for a short time, and came back in the morning and said:

"Here I am, your faithful servant, who has made you a fitting return. I carried you up out of the water and saved you from death by the command of the Almighty. You should know that I am a jinniyah; and the moment I saw you my heart loved you, by the will of the Lord — for I am a believer in Allah and in His Apostle, on whom be blessing and peace. So I came to you in the state you saw me in, and you married me; and now, you see, I have saved you from drowning. But I am angry with your brothers, and I certainly must kill them."

When I had heard her story I was astonished, and I thanked her for everything she had done, and said, "But as for killing my brothers — that must not happen."

Then I told her the whole tale of what had passed between us, from the beginning of our lives to the end of it; and when she had heard it she said, "Tonight I will fly over them as a bird, and sink their ship, and kill them."

"In Allah's name," I said, "do not. The proverb says: you who do good to the man who does evil — leave the evildoer to his own evil deeds. And besides, they are still my brothers."

But she came back at me: "By Allah, there is no help for it. I shall kill them."

I humbled myself in front of her, begging their pardon; at which she lifted me up and flew off with me, and at last set me down on the terrace roof of my own house.

I opened the doors and dug up what I had hidden in the ground; and after greeting the neighbours I opened my shop and bought goods.

Now when night came I went home, and there I saw these two hounds tied up. When they caught sight of me they got up and whined and fawned on me — and before I could understand what had happened, my wife said, "These two dogs are your brothers."

"And who has done this to them?" I said.

"I sent a message to my sister," she answered, "and she treated them like this. And these two will not be released from the shape they are in until ten years have gone by."

And now I have arrived at this place on my way to my wife's sister, so that she can deliver them from this condition, after they have endured it for a full ten years. And as I was going along I saw this young man, who told me what had happened to him, and I decided not to go on from here until I had seen what might happen between you and him.

That is my tale.

Then the jinni said, "This is certainly a strange story, and so I give you the third part of his blood and his crime."

At which the third old man, the master of the she-mule, said to the jinni, "I can tell you a tale more wonderful than either of these, if you will grant me the rest of his blood and the rest of his offence."

And the jinni answered, "So be it."
