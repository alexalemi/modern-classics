The Story of King Shahryar and His Brother
(Part 2 of 2)

	She rose like the morning breaking through the night,
	and gilded the whole grove with her graciousness;
	the sun itself takes light from her radiance
	when she puts back her veil and shames the moon.
	Every creature bows down between her hands
	when she unveils and shows what she is.
	And she floods whole cities with a torrent of tears
	when the lightning of her glance goes over them.

The jinni sat her down under the tree at his side, and looked at her and said, "Dearest love of this heart of mine! Lady of the noblest family — I carried you off on your wedding night so that nobody could get in ahead of me and take your maidenhead before I did, and nobody has ever loved you or had you but me. Sweetheart, I should like to sleep a little."

Then he laid his head on the lady's thighs, and stretched out his legs, which reached all the way down to the sea, and slept, and snored, and rumbled like rolling thunder.

After a while she lifted her head towards the top of the tree and saw the two kings perched near the summit. So she carefully slid the ifrit's head off her lap — she was tired of holding it up, and an ifrit is a jinni of the fiercer kind — and set it down on the ground, and stood up under the tree, and signalled to them: Come down, you two. Don't be afraid of this ifrit.

They were in a terrible fright, finding they had been seen, and signalled back, "In Allah's name, and by your own modesty, madam — excuse us from coming down."

"In Allah's name," she signalled, "come down this instant. If you don't, I will wake my husband here, this ifrit, and he will kill you by the worst death there is." And she went on making signs at them.

So, out of fear, they came down to her. She stood up in front of them and said, "Take me, and be quick about it. Otherwise I will wake this ifrit and set him on you, and he will kill you where you stand."

"Madam," they said, "we beg you by Allah, let us off this. We are runaways from exactly this sort of thing, and we are in absolute terror of this husband of yours. How can we possibly do what you want?"

"No more talk," she said. "It has to be." And she swore to them by Him who raised the skies up with no prop and no pillar that if they did not do what she wanted, she would have them killed and thrown into the sea.

So Shahryar, in his fear, said to his brother, "Do what she tells you."

"I will not," said Shah Zaman, "until you do it first."

And they stood there arguing about which of them was going to have her.

"What is this?" she said. "Why are you arguing and hanging back? If you two do not come forward like men and do what men do, I will wake the ifrit on you."

At that, in their terror of the jinni, they both did what she told them to do. And when they had got up off her she said, "Well done."

Then she took a purse out of her pocket and drew out a knotted string, with five hundred and seventy signet rings threaded on it. "Do you know what these are?" she asked.

"We do not," they said.

"These," she said, "are the seals of five hundred and seventy men, and every one of them has had me on the horns of this foul, stupid, filthy ifrit. So give me your two seal rings as well, the pair of you."

When they had pulled the rings off their hands and given them to her, she said, "The truth is that this ifrit carried me off on my wedding night, and put me in a casket, and set the casket inside a chest, and fastened seven strong steel padlocks on the chest, and laid me down on the floor of the raving sea, where the waves crash and clash over me. He guarded me so that I should stay chaste and honest — as he puts it — and so that nobody but him should ever have me. And I have lain with as many of my own kind as I pleased. This miserable jinni does not understand that destiny cannot be turned aside or held back by anything, and that whatever a woman wants she gets, however unwilling a man may be. As one of the poets says:

	Do not rely on women;
	do not trust their hearts.
	Their joys and their sorrows
	both hang below the belt.
	They will swear you a love
	that guile never leaves.
	Look at what happened to Yusuf —
	beware of their tricks, beware of the sting.
	Was it not through their arts
	that Iblis got Adam thrown out?

And another says:

	Ease off your blame, man — it only drives the passion higher.
	My fault is not so heavy as the fault you have found in it.
	If I have become a true lover, nothing has come to me
	but what has come to a great many others before now.
	For the man is a wonder and well worth our praise
	who has kept himself safe and sound out of the wiles of women."

Hearing all this, the two of them marvelled beyond marvelling. And she went back to the ifrit and took his head up on her thigh as before, and said to them quietly, "Now go your ways, and get yourselves out of the reach of his malice."

So they went off, saying to one another, "Allah! Allah!" and, "There is no Majesty and there is no Might save in Allah, the Glorious, the Great — and with Him we take refuge from the malice and the cunning of women, which has no equal on earth. Think about it, brother: look at what this astonishing woman has done to an ifrit, who is a great deal more powerful than we are. Something worse has happened to him than happened to us, and that ought to be consolation enough. So let us go back to our countries and our capitals — and let us settle it that we will never marry a woman again. And soon enough we will show them what we intend to do."

Then they rode back to King Shahryar's camp, which they reached on the morning of the third day; and having called together the viziers and the emirs, the chamberlains and the senior officials, the king gave a robe of honour to his deputy and gave orders to return to the city at once.

There he sat down on his throne and sent for his chief minister — the father of the two girls who, Allah willing, will be spoken of shortly — and said, "I order you to take my wife and put her to death. She has broken her word and her faith."

So the vizier carried her off to the place of execution and killed her.

Then King Shahryar took a sword in his hand and went to the women's quarters and killed all the concubines and all their Mamluks. And he swore a binding oath that from now on, whatever wife he married, he would take her maidenhead at night and kill her the next morning, to make certain of his honour. "Because," he said, "there never was and there is not now one chaste woman on the face of the earth."

Then Shah Zaman asked leave to go home, and he was fitted out and escorted, and travelled until he reached his own country.

Meanwhile Shahryar ordered his vizier to bring him that night's bride, so that he could go in to her. The vizier produced a most beautiful girl, the daughter of one of the emirs; the king went in to her in the evening, and when morning came he told his minister to strike her head off. And the vizier did it, for fear of the king.

He went on like that for three years. Every night he married a girl, and every morning he killed her — until the people raised an outcry against him and cursed him and prayed to Allah to destroy him and his rule utterly. The women wailed and the mothers wept and parents fled the city with their daughters, until there was not a girl left in the whole place old enough to be taken to bed.

Then the king ordered his chief vizier — the same man who was charged with the executions — to bring him a virgin as usual. The minister went out and searched and could not find one; and he came home in sorrow and dread, afraid for his own life.

Now this vizier had two daughters, whose names were Shahrazad and Dunyazad. Shahrazad is the name English has usually spelled Scheherazade, and she was the elder. She had read the books and the annals and the legends of earlier kings, and the stories and examples and lessons of men and things long gone by; indeed it was said that she had collected a thousand volumes of histories about ancient peoples and dead rulers. She had read the poets and knew them by heart. She had studied philosophy and the sciences and the arts and every accomplishment; and she was pleasant and polite, wise and witty, well read and well brought up.

That day she said to her father, "Why do I see you so changed, and so loaded down with worry and care? About such things one of the poets said:

	Tell anyone who is grieving:
	grief will never last.
	Just as joy has no tomorrow,
	so sorrow will go past."

When the vizier heard this from his daughter he told her, from beginning to end, everything that had passed between himself and the king.

Then she said, "By Allah, father — how long is this slaughter of women going to go on? Shall I tell you what is in my mind, to save both sides from destruction?"

"Say on, daughter," he said.

"I want you to give me in marriage to this King Shahryar," she said. "Either I live, or I am the ransom for the virgin daughters of the Muslims and the reason they are delivered out of his hands and out of yours."

"In Allah's name!" he cried, in a rage that needed no feeding. "You little fool — do not put your life in that kind of danger! How dare you speak to me in words so far from wisdom and so close to stupidity? Understand this: a person with no experience of the world falls into trouble easily, and whoever does not consider how a thing ends does not keep the world for a friend. As the common people say — I was sitting at my ease, and nothing but my own meddling brought me unease."

"You must," she broke in, "let me do this good deed. And let him kill me if he likes. I shall only die as a ransom for others."

"Daughter," he asked, "how does that do you any good, once you have thrown your life away?"

"Father," she answered, "it has to be, whatever comes of it."

The vizier flew into a fury again and scolded her and reproached her, and finished with, "In plain truth, I am afraid the same thing will happen to you that happened to the Bull and the Ass with the Farmer."

"And what happened to them, father?" she asked.
