The Sixth Voyage of Sindbad the Seaman

Know, my brothers and friends and companions all, that after I came home from my fifth voyage I lived some time in great comfort and satisfaction, in mirth and merriment, in joy and enjoyment; and I forgot everything I had suffered, seeing what a great profit I had made by it.

Then one day, as I sat making merry with my friends, there came in to me a company of merchants with the look of travel about them, who talked to me of voyages and adventures and of the greatness of money and gain. And at that I remembered the days of my return from abroad, and my delight at seeing my native land again and being back among my family and my friends; and my soul began to yearn for travel and trade.

So, driven by Fate and Fortune, I made up my mind to undertake another voyage; and, buying fine and costly merchandise suitable for foreign trade, I made it up into bales and carried it from Baghdad to Basra. There I found a great ship ready for sea, full of merchants and men of standing who had valuable goods with them; so I put my bales aboard, and we left Basra in safety and good spirits under the protection of the King, the Preserver.

And Shahrazad saw the dawn breaking, and fell silent.

When it was the Five Hundred and Sixtieth Night,

she said: It has reached me, O fortunate King, that Sindbad the Seaman went on:

And after loading my bales and leaving Basra in safety and good spirits, we continued our voyage from place to place and from city to city, buying and selling and making a profit and amusing ourselves with the sight of countries where strange people live. And Fortune and the voyage both smiled on us — until one day, as we went along, the captain suddenly gave a great cry and flung his turban down on the deck. Then he beat his own face like a woman and tore out his beard, and fell down in the waist of the ship half fainting with grief and rage, calling out, "Oh, alas for the ruin of my house and the orphaning of my poor children!"

So all the merchants and the sailors gathered round him and asked, "Master, what is the matter?" — for the daylight had turned to night before their eyes.

And he answered, "Know, good people, that we have wandered off our course and left the sea whose ways we know, and come into a sea whose ways I do not know at all; and unless Allah grants us some means of escape, we are all dead men. So pray to the Most High that He deliver us out of this strait. Perhaps there is one righteous man among you whose prayers the Lord will accept."

Then he got up and climbed the mast to see whether there was any way out of the strait, and he would have loosed the sails; but the wind blew twice as hard against the ship and spun her round three times and drove her backwards, so that her rudder broke and she fell away towards a high mountain.

At this the captain came down from the mast, saying, "There is no Majesty and there is no Might save in Allah, the Glorious, the Great! No man can prevent what fate has laid down beforehand. By Allah, we have fallen upon a place of certain destruction; there is no way of escape for us, and not one of us can be saved."

Then we all began to weep over ourselves and to bid one another farewell, for our days were at an end and we had given up all hope of life. And presently the ship struck the mountain and broke up, and everything and everybody aboard her was plunged into the sea. Some of the merchants were drowned and others managed to reach the shore and save themselves on the mountain, myself among them.

When we got ashore we found a great island — or rather a peninsula — whose base was strewn with the wreckage of ships and with goods and gear cast up by the sea out of broken vessels whose passengers had drowned; and the quantity of it defeated counting and reckoning.

So I climbed the cliffs into the interior of the island and walked on inland, until I came to a stream of sweet water that welled up at the nearest foot of the mountains and disappeared into the earth under the range of hills on the opposite side. But all the other passengers went over the mountains into the inner parts of the island, and, scattering here and there, were bewildered by what they saw and became like madmen at the sight of the wealth and the treasures strewn along the shores.

As for me, I looked into the bed of the stream I have mentioned, and saw in it a great abundance of rubies, and great royal pearls, and every kind of jewel and precious stone, lying like gravel in the beds of the rivulets that ran through the fields; and the sands sparkled and glittered with gems and precious ores. Moreover we found on the island a great quantity of the finest aloes-wood, both Chinese and Comorin.

And there is a spring of raw ambergris there too, which flows out like wax or gum over the banks of the stream in the great heat of the sun, and runs down to the seashore, where the monsters of the deep come up and swallow it and go back into the sea. But it burns in their bellies, so they bring it up again, and it hardens on the surface of the water, and by this its colour and its quantity are changed; and at last the waves throw it ashore, and the travellers and merchants who know it collect it and sell it. But as for the raw ambergris which is not swallowed, it flows over the channel and hardens on the banks; and when the sun shines on it, it melts and scents the whole valley with a fragrance like musk, and then, when the sun leaves it, it hardens again. But nobody can get to the place where the raw ambergris is, because of the mountains that shut in the island on every side, which no man's foot can climb.

We went on exploring the island in this way, marvelling at the wonderful works of Allah and at the riches we found there, but badly troubled about our own case and dismayed at our prospects. Now we had picked up a little food on the beach from the wreck, and we hoarded it carefully, eating only once every day or two out of fear that it would fail us and we should die miserably of hunger or of terror. Moreover we were weak with the cramps that come of seasickness and poor food, and my companions died one after another, until only a small company of us was left.

Each man who died we washed and wrapped in some of the clothes and linen the tide had cast ashore; and after a little the rest of my fellows perished too, one by one, until I had buried the last of the party and was left alone on the island with very little food remaining — I, who had been used to having so much.

And I wept over myself, saying, "I wish to Heaven I had died before my companions, and that they had washed me and buried me. It would have been better than this, that I should die and nobody wash me and shroud me and bury me. But there is no Majesty and there is no Might save in Allah, the Glorious, the Great!"

And Shahrazad saw the dawn breaking, and fell silent.

When it was the Five Hundred and Sixty-first Night,

she said: It has reached me, O fortunate King, that Sindbad the Seaman went on, in these words:

Now, after I had buried the last of my party and was left alone on the island, I got up and dug myself a deep grave on the seashore, saying to myself, "When I grow weak and know that death is coming to me, I will get down into the grave and die there, and the wind will drift the sand over me and cover me, and I shall be buried."

Then I fell to reproaching myself for my lack of sense, in leaving my own country and taking to travel again after everything I had suffered on my first five voyages — not one of which I had made without meeting more horrible dangers and worse hardships than the one before it — and now with no hope at all of getting out of my present trouble. And I repented my folly and lamented over myself, all the more because I had no need of money, seeing that I had enough and more than enough, and could not have spent what I had, no, nor half of it, in a whole lifetime.

However, after a while Allah put a thought into my head, and I said to myself, "By God, this stream must have an end as well as a beginning, and so it must come out somewhere; and very likely its course leads to some inhabited place. So my best plan is to make myself a little boat, just big enough to sit in, and carry it down and launch it on the river and get in and drift with the stream. If I escape, I escape, by God's leave; and if I die, better to die in the river than here."

Then, sighing for myself, I set to work gathering a number of pieces of Chinese and Comorin aloes-wood, and bound them together with ropes from the wreckage; then I picked out from the broken-up ships straight planks of an even size and fixed them firmly on top of the aloes-wood, making myself a boat-raft a little narrower than the channel of the stream; and I tied it as tightly and firmly as if it had been nailed.

Then I loaded it with the goods, the precious ores and the jewels, and the pearls that lay about like gravel, and the best of the ambergris, raw and pure, together with what I had collected on the island and what was left to me of food and wild herbs. Last of all I lashed a piece of wood on either side to serve me for oars, and launched it and got aboard, doing as the poet says:

	Run for your life when the danger is on you;
	let the house stand and tell how its builder died.
	Land after land you may look for and find,
	but no second life is waiting on the far side.
	Do not wear out your soul with your thoughts in the night;
	every sorrow comes to an end, soon or late.
	The man born in one country will die in that country,
	and nowhere else, and there he will meet his fate.
	Never trust the great business of your life to another:
	the soul has only the soul for a confederate.

My boat-raft drifted along with the stream while I sat pondering how the business would end; and it went on drifting until it came to the place where the water disappeared under the mountain. I rowed my craft into the opening, where it was intensely dark, and the current carried the raft with it down the underground channel. The narrow stream bore me on through a tunnel so tight that the raft touched both sides at once and my head rubbed against the roof, and there was no possibility of turning back.

Then I blamed myself for having risked my life like this, and said, "If this passage gets any narrower the raft will hardly get through, and I cannot turn round; so I shall certainly die a miserable death in this place." And I threw myself down flat on my face on the raft, because the channel was so low, while the stream carried me along, and I could not tell night from day for the thickness of the gloom all around me and my terror and dread for myself.

And so I went on down the channel, which now widened and now grew tighter, until, worn out with a darkness you could feel, I fell asleep as I lay face-down on the raft; and I slept, not knowing whether the time was long or short.

When I woke at last, I found myself in the light of Heaven; and opening my eyes I saw that I was on a broad river, with the raft moored to an island among a number of Indians and Abyssinians. As soon as they saw that I was awake they came up to me and spoke to me in their own language; but I did not understand what they said, and I thought this was a dream and a vision that had come to me out of my anxiety and misery. But I was delighted to have escaped from the river.

When they saw that I did not understand them and gave them no answer, one of them came forward and said to me in Arabic, "Peace be with you, brother! Who are you, and where have you come from? How did you get into this river, and what sort of land lies behind those mountains? For we never knew anybody make his way from there to us."

I said, "And peace be upon you, and the mercy of Allah and His blessing! Who are you, and what country is this?"

"Brother," he answered, "we are farmers and tillers of the soil, who came out to water our fields and our plantations; and, finding you asleep on this raft, we took hold of it and made it fast beside us, to wait until you should wake in your own good time. So tell us how you came here."

I answered, "For Allah's sake, sir, before I speak give me something to eat, for I am starving; and afterwards ask me what you like."

So he hurried to fetch me food, and I ate my fill until I was refreshed and my fear was quieted by a full stomach and my life came back to me. Then I gave thanks to the Most High for mercies great and small, glad to be out of the river and glad to be among these people; and I told them all my adventures from first to last, and especially my troubles in the narrow channel.

And Shahrazad saw the dawn breaking, and fell silent.

When it was the Five Hundred and Sixty-second Night,

she said: It has reached me, O fortunate King, that Sindbad the Seaman went on:

When I had landed and found myself among the Indians and Abyssinians and had taken some rest, they consulted among themselves and said to one another, "There is nothing for it but to take him with us and present him to our King, so that he may hear his adventures."

So they took me, together with the raft and its cargo of money and merchandise, jewels, minerals and golden gear, and brought me to their King, who was the King of Sarandib, telling him what had happened; and he greeted me and made me welcome. Then he questioned me about my situation and my adventures through the man who had spoken Arabic, and I repeated my story to him from beginning to end, at which he marvelled greatly and congratulated me on my escape.

After that I went and fetched from the raft a great store of precious ores and jewels and ambergris and aloes-wood and presented them to the King, who accepted them and treated me with the utmost honour, giving me a lodging in his own palace. So I kept company with the chief men of the islanders, and they showed me the greatest respect; and I did not leave the royal palace.

Now the island of Sarandib lies under the equator, its night and its day both measuring twelve hours. It is eighty leagues long by thirty broad, and its breadth is bounded by a lofty mountain and a deep valley. The mountain can be seen from three days' distance, and it contains many kinds of rubies and other minerals, and spice trees of every sort. Its surface is covered with emery, the stuff gems are cut and shaped with; diamonds are in its rivers and pearls are in its valleys. I climbed that mountain and refreshed myself with a view of its marvels, which are beyond describing, and afterwards came back to the King.

After that, all the travellers and merchants who came to the place questioned me about the affairs of my native land and about the Caliph Harun al-Rashid and his rule; and I told them about him and about the things he was famous for, and they praised him because of it; while I in my turn questioned them about the manners and the customs of their own countries and got the knowledge I wanted.

One day the King himself asked me about the fashions and the form of government of my country, and I told him how the Caliph ruled in the city of Baghdad and how just his rule was. The King marvelled at my account of his arrangements and said, "By Allah, the Caliph's ordinances are wise indeed and his ways are praiseworthy, and you have made me love him by what you tell me; so I have a mind to make him a present and send it by you."

I said, "To hear is to obey, my lord; I will carry your gift to him and tell him that you are his sincere admirer and his true friend."

Then I stayed with the King in great honour and regard and consideration for a long while, until one day, as I sat in his palace, I heard news of a company of merchants who were fitting out a ship for Basra; and I said to myself, "I cannot do better than sail with these men."

So I got up without pause or delay and kissed the King's hand and told him how I longed to set out with the merchants, since I was pining for my own people and my own country.

He said, "You are your own master; and yet, if you will stay with us, it shall be on our head and eyes, for you gladden us with your company."

"By Allah, my lord," I answered, "you have overwhelmed me with your favours and your kindnesses; but I am hungry for a sight of my friends and my family and my native country."

When he heard this, he sent for the merchants in question and commended me to their care, paying my freight and my passage money. Then he bestowed great riches on me out of his treasuries and charged me with a magnificent present for the Caliph Harun al-Rashid. Moreover he gave me a sealed letter, saying, "Carry this with your own hand to the Commander of the Faithful, and give him many greetings from us!"

"To hear is to obey," I replied.

The letter was written on the skin of the khawi, which is finer than lambskin parchment and yellow in colour, with ink of ultramarine; and the contents were as follows:

	Peace be with you from the King of Al-Hind, before whom stand a thousand elephants, and upon whose palace battlements are a thousand jewels. And after that (praise be to the Lord and blessings on His Prophet): we send you a trifling gift, which we hope you will be pleased to accept. You are a brother to us and a sincere friend, and great is the love we bear you in our heart; so favour us with a reply. The gift is not worthy of your dignity; but we beg you, our brother, to be gracious and accept it. And peace be with you.

And the present was: a cup of ruby a span high, the inside of it set all over with precious pearls; and a bed covered with the skin of the serpent that swallows the elephant, a skin whose spots are each of them the size of a gold dinar, and whoever sits on it never falls sick; and a hundred thousand miskals of Indian aloes-wood; and a slave girl like a shining moon.

Then I took leave of him and of all my friends and acquaintances on the island, and embarked with the merchants I have mentioned. We sailed with a fair wind, committing ourselves to the care of Allah (be He extolled and exalted!), and by His leave arrived at Basra, where I spent a few days and nights getting myself equipped and packing up my bales.

Then I went on to Baghdad, the House of Peace, where I asked for an audience with the Caliph and laid the King's presents before him. He asked me where they came from, and I said, "By Allah, Commander of the Faithful, I do not know the name of the city, nor the way there!"

Then he asked, "Sindbad, is this true, what the King writes?"

And I answered, after kissing the ground, "My lord, I saw in his kingdom a great deal more than he has written in his letter. On state occasions a throne is set for him on the back of a huge elephant eleven cubits high; and on this he sits, with his great lords and his officers and his guests standing in two ranks, on his right hand and on his left. At his head is a man holding a golden javelin, and behind him another with a great mace of gold whose head is an emerald a span long and as thick as a man's thumb. And when he takes horse, a thousand horsemen mount with him, dressed in gold brocade and silk; and as the King goes by, a man walks in front of him crying, 'This is the King of great dignity, of high authority!' And he goes on repeating his praises in words I do not remember, saying at the end of the speech, 'This is the King who owns the crown whose like neither Solomon nor the Mihrjan ever possessed.' Then he falls silent, and one behind him proclaims, saying, 'He will die! Again I say, he will die!' And the other adds, 'Extolled be the perfection of the Living One who does not die!' Moreover, by reason of his justice and his good ordering and his intelligence, there is no judge in his city at all, and every one of his subjects can tell truth from falsehood for himself."

The Caliph said, "How great this King is! His letter has shown me that; and as for the might of his dominion, you have told us what you saw with your own eyes. By Allah, he has been endowed with wisdom as well as with wide rule."

Then I related to the Commander of the Faithful everything that had happened to me on my last voyage, at which he wondered exceedingly, and gave orders to his historians to write my story down and store it up in his treasuries for the instruction of everybody who might see it. After that he conferred very great favours on me, and I went back to my own quarter and into my own house, where I warehoused all my goods and possessions. Presently my friends came to me, and I distributed presents among my family and gave alms and largesse; after which I gave myself up to joy and enjoyment, mirth and merrymaking, and forgot everything I had suffered.

Such, then, my brothers, is the history of what happened to me on my sixth voyage; and tomorrow, God willing, I will tell you the story of my seventh and last voyage, which is more wonderful and marvellous still than any of the first six.

(Says the teller of the tale:) Then he ordered the table to be laid, and the company had supper with him; after which he gave the porter a hundred dinars, as usual, and they all went their ways, marvelling beyond measure at what they had heard.

And Shahrazad saw the dawn breaking, and fell silent.
