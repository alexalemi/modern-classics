The Tale of the Husband and the Parrot

There was a certain man, a merchant into the bargain, who had married a beautiful wife — a woman of perfect beauty and grace, of fine proportions and great loveliness — and he was insanely jealous of her, and she managed very successfully to keep him from travelling anywhere.

At last something came up that forced him to leave her. So he went to the bird market and bought himself, for a hundred gold pieces, a parrot, which he set up in the house to act as a chaperone; he expected her to tell him, when he came back, everything that had gone on the whole time he was away. For the bird was knowing and clever, and never forgot anything she had seen or heard.

Now his beautiful wife had fallen in love with a young Turk who used to visit her, and she feasted him by day and slept with him by night.

When the man had made his journey and got what he wanted from it he came home, and at once had the parrot brought to him and questioned her about his wife's behaviour while he had been abroad.

"Your wife has a man friend," she said, "who spent every night with her while you were away."

At that the husband went to his wife in a violent rage and gave her a beating thorough enough to satisfy anybody.

The woman, suspecting that one of the slave girls had been telling tales to the master, called them all together and questioned them on their oaths; and every one of them swore she had kept the secret — but that the parrot had not. "And we heard her with our own ears," they said.

So the woman told one of the girls to set a hand-mill under the cage and grind with it; and a second to sprinkle water down through the roof of the cage; and a third to run about, right and left, flashing a mirror of bright steel — and to keep it up all night long.

Next morning the husband came home, having been entertained by one of his friends, and told them to bring the parrot to him, and asked what had happened while he was out.

"Forgive me, master," said the bird, "but I could neither hear nor see anything, on account of the tremendous darkness, and the thunder and the lightning, which went on all night."

As it happened to be summer, the master was amazed. "But we are in the middle of July," he cried. "This is not the season for rain and storms."

"By Allah, it is so," the bird answered. "I saw with these eyes what my tongue has told you."

At this the man, not understanding the situation and not smelling the plot, flew into a tremendous rage; and reckoning that his wife had been wrongly accused, he put out his hand and pulled the parrot out of her cage and dashed her on the ground so hard that he killed her on the spot.

Some days afterwards one of the slave girls confessed the whole truth to him; and even then he would not believe it, until he saw the young Turk, his wife's lover, coming out of her room. Then he drew his blade and killed him with a blow to the back of the neck; and he did the same to the adulteress. And so the two of them went, loaded with mortal sin, straight to the Everlasting Fire.

Then the merchant knew that the parrot had told him the truth about everything she had seen, and he grieved bitterly over losing her — when grieving was no use to him at all.

The minister, hearing King Yunan out, replied, "O Monarch, high in dignity — and what harm have I done this man, or what evil have I ever had from him, that I should be plotting his death? I would not be doing it except to serve you, and you will see soon enough that I am right. And if you take my advice you will be saved, and if you do not you will be destroyed — exactly like the vizier who dealt treacherously with the young prince."
