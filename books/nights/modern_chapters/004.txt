The First Old Man's Story

Know, jinni, that this gazelle is my father's brother's daughter, my own flesh and blood. I married her when she was a young girl, and I lived with her for the best part of thirty years, and she never gave me a child. So I took a concubine, and she gave me the gift of a son, a boy as handsome as the full moon, with beautiful shining eyes and eyebrows that met in a single line, and every limb of him perfectly made.

Little by little he grew, and grew tall; and when he was a lad of fifteen I had to travel to certain cities, and I set off with a great stock of goods.

But my cousin — this gazelle — had learned magic and sorcery and the black arts from her childhood. So she turned that son of mine into a calf, and turned my slave girl, his mother, into a heifer, and handed the pair of them over to the herdsman.

When I came home after a long absence and asked after my son and his mother, she said, "Your slave girl is dead, and your son has run off, and I do not know where he has gone."

So I stayed a whole year with a grieving heart and streaming eyes, until the time came round for the Great Feast of Allah. Then I sent to my herdsman and told him to pick me out a fat heifer; and he brought me one — and it was the girl, my slave, whom this gazelle had bewitched.

I tucked up my sleeves and my skirts and took a knife and started to cut her throat. But she bellowed aloud and wept bitter tears. It astonished me, and pity took hold of me, and I stayed my hand and said to the herdsman, "Bring me a different one."

Then my cousin cried out, "Kill her! I have none fatter and none finer."

So I went forward to sacrifice her a second time, and a second time she bellowed aloud; and out of pity I held back again, and told the herdsman to kill her and skin her himself. He killed her and skinned her, and found neither fat nor flesh on her, nothing but hide and bone. And I was sorry, when being sorry was no use to me at all.

I gave her over to the herdsman and said, "Fetch me a fat calf." And he brought my son, under the spell.

When the calf saw me he broke his tether and ran to me and fawned on me and cried, and shed tears; so that I took pity on him and said to the herdsman, "Bring me a heifer, and let this calf go."

At that my cousin — this gazelle — shouted at me, "You must kill this calf. This is a holy day and a blessed one, and nothing is sacrificed on it that is not perfect and pure; and we have no calf fatter or finer than this one."

"Look at the state of the heifer I slaughtered at your bidding," I said, "and how we turned away from her in disappointment, and how she was no good to us at all. I regret killing her bitterly. So this time I will not do what you tell me and sacrifice this calf."

"By Allah the Most Great, the Compassionate, the Merciful," she said, "there is no help for it. You must kill him on this holy day; and if you do not kill him, then you are no husband to me and I am no wife to you."

Now when I heard those hard words — not understanding what she was after — I went up to the calf with the knife in my hand:—

And Shahrazad saw the dawn breaking, and fell silent.

Then her sister said to her, "How lovely your tale is, and how pleasing, and how sweet, and how good to listen to!"

And Shahrazad answered, "And what is this compared to what I could tell you tomorrow night, if I am alive and the King spares me?"

Then the King said to himself, "By Allah, I will not kill her until I have heard the rest of her tale."

So they slept the rest of that night in each other's arms until the day was fully up. Then the King went out to his audience hall, and the vizier went up with his daughter's shroud under his arm. The King gave out his orders, and promoted this man and dismissed that one, until the end of the day; and he said not one word to the vizier about what had happened. The minister wondered at it beyond all wondering. And when the court broke up, King Shahryar went into his palace.

When it was the Second Night,

Dunyazad said to her sister Shahrazad, "Sister, finish that story of the Merchant and the Jinni for us."

And she answered, "With joy and goodwill, if the King permits me."

Then the King said, "Tell your tale."

And Shahrazad began: It has reached me, O fortunate King, that when the merchant meant to sacrifice the calf, and saw it weeping, his heart softened, and he said to the herdsman, "Keep the calf with the rest of my cattle."

All this the old man told the jinni, who marvelled a good deal at such strange words. Then the owner of the gazelle went on:

Lord of the Kings of the Jinn, all this happened, and my uncle's daughter, this gazelle, stood watching and saw it, and said, "Butcher this calf for me. It is certainly a fat one." But I told the herdsman to take it away, and he took it and turned for home.

Next day, as I was sitting in my own house, in came the herdsman, and he stood in front of me and said, "Master, I am going to tell you something that will gladden your heart, and it should earn me the reward that goes with good news."

"So it shall," I answered.

"Merchant," he said, "I have a daughter, and she learned magic in her childhood from an old woman who lived with us. Yesterday, when you gave me the calf, I took it into the house to her, and she looked at it and covered her face; and then she wept, and then she laughed, and then wept again, and at last she said: Father, has my honour become so cheap to you that you bring strange men in to me?

"I asked her: Where are these strange men, and why were you laughing and crying? And she answered: The truth is that this calf you have with you is the son of our master the merchant. He is under a spell put on him by his stepmother, who bewitched both him and his mother. That is why I laughed. And the reason I wept is his mother — because his father killed her without knowing what he was doing.

"I marvelled at this beyond all marvelling, and I could hardly wait for daylight before coming to tell you."

When I heard my herdsman's words, jinni, I went out with him — drunk without any wine, out of the sheer joy and gladness that had come over me — and we went to his house. There his daughter welcomed me and kissed my hand, and at once the calf came and fawned on me as before.

"Is this true, what you say about this calf?" I asked the herdsman's daughter.

"It is, my lord," she said. "He is your son, the very core of your heart."

I was overjoyed, and said to her, "Girl, if you will free him, everything I own that is in your father's hands, cattle and property both, shall be yours."

She smiled and answered, "My lord, I have no greed for the goods, and I will not take them except on two conditions. The first is that you marry me to your son. The second is that I may put a spell on the woman who put a spell on him, and shut her up — because otherwise I cannot be safe from her malice and her mischief."

When I heard the herdsman's daughter say this, jinni, I answered, "Besides what you are asking for, all the cattle and the household goods in your father's charge are yours; and as for my uncle's daughter, her blood is lawful to you."

When I had said it, she took a cup and filled it with water, and recited a spell over it, and sprinkled it on the calf, saying, "If Almighty Allah created you a calf, then stay in this shape and do not change; but if you are under a spell, then come back to the shape you had before, by the command of Allah Most High!"

And the calf shuddered — and became a man.

Then I fell on his neck and said, "In Allah's name, tell me everything my uncle's daughter did to you and to your mother." And when he had told me what had passed between them, I said, "My son, Allah favoured you with somebody to give you back to yourself, and what was yours has come back to you."

Then, jinni, I married the herdsman's daughter to him; and she turned my wife into this gazelle, saying, "This shape is a pretty one, and not loathsome at all."

After that she lived with us night and day and day and night, until the Almighty took her to Himself. When she died, my son set out for the cities of India — for the very city, in fact, that belongs to this man here, the one who has had done to him what has been done. And I took this gazelle, my cousin, and wandered with her from town to town looking for news of my son, until Destiny drove me to this place, where I found the merchant sitting here in tears.

That is my tale.

"That story is strange indeed," said the jinni, "and so I grant you the third part of his blood."

Then the second old man, the one who owned the two greyhounds, came up and said, "Jinni, if I tell you what happened to me because of my brothers, these two hounds, and you find it a tale more wonderful and more marvellous than the one you have just heard, will you grant me a third of this man's blood as well?"

"You have my word on it," the jinni replied, "if your adventures are more marvellous and more wonderful."
