Epictetus was born enslaved in Phrygia, in what is now Turkey, around the middle of the first century. He was owned in Rome by Epaphroditus, a freedman of Nero's, was lame in one leg, and was allowed or sent to study philosophy while still a slave. He was freed, taught in Rome, and left when Domitian expelled the philosophers from the city; he spent the rest of his life running a school at Nicopolis in western Greece, and died around 135. He wrote nothing at all. Everything under his name was taken down by a pupil, Arrian, who later wrote the standard history of Alexander the Great.

This little book is what Arrian made by cutting the lectures down: fifty-one short sections, no arguments, no system, just the drills. Enchiridion means something you keep to hand. The full lectures are in this collection as the Discourses, and almost every line here can be found in them with the reasoning still attached — so the handbook is the thing to carry and the Discourses are where to go when you want to know why.

One warning before starting. Stoicism is not stoicism. The word in ordinary English means clamping down on what you feel and saying nothing, which is close to the opposite of the doctrine. The Stoic claim is that distress comes from a judgment you have made about an event rather than from the event, and that judgments can be examined and changed. The aim is to stop wanting what was never yours to have, not to grit your teeth while still wanting it. Section one states the division the whole book runs on: some things are up to us, and some things are not.

The drills are homely and blunt and several of them are hard to read. If you are fond of a cup, say that it is a cup you are fond of, so that you can bear it breaking; when you kiss your child, say that you are kissing a mortal. That is not cruelty and it is not resignation, it is rehearsal, and Epictetus puts it first because he thinks the small cases are how anybody learns the large ones. Other sections state ancient assumptions about women and about conduct without arguing for them; they are given here as he gave them.

The last section collects the four passages he wanted kept ready: Cleanthes' prayer to Zeus and Destiny, two lines of Euripides, and Socrates twice, once in the Crito and once at his trial. The same prayer turns up five times in the Discourses, and it is set here in the same words, so that a reader who owns both books meets the same lines each time.
