Lecture Six: The Correlation of the Physical Forces

(Part 2 of 2)

Now, besides this production of light, we have all the other effects and powers of burning zinc. I have a few wires here which are not combustible, and I am going to take one of them, a small platinum wire, and hang it between these two rods that are connected to the battery. When contact is made at the battery, see what heat we get (Figure 48). Is that not beautiful? It is a complete bridge of power.

There is metallic connection all the way round in this arrangement; and where I have put in the platinum, which offers some resistance to the passage of the force, you see what an amount of heat is evolved. This is the heat the zinc would give if it were burnt in oxygen; but as it is being burnt in the voltaic battery, it is giving its heat out at this spot. I will now shorten the wire, to show you that the shorter the obstructing wire is, the more intense the heat becomes — until at last our platinum melts and falls, breaking the circuit.

[Figure 48: A short platinum wire hung as a bridge between the two rods from the battery. It glows white hot the moment contact is made, and the shorter it is made the fiercer the heat, until at last it fuses and drops.]

[Figure 49: A piece of silver laid on the lower charcoal pole, with the upper pole brought down onto it. It burns with a brilliance nothing in ordinary combustion can match.]

Here is another instance. I will take a piece of the metal silver, put it on charcoal connected to one end of the battery, and lower the other charcoal pole onto it. See how brilliantly it burns (Figure 49). Here is a piece of iron on the charcoal — see what a combustion goes on. We might go on this way, burning almost anything we put between the poles.

Now, I want to show you that this power is still chemical affinity: that whether we call the power evolved at this point heat, or electricity, or any other name referring to its source or to the way it travels, we shall still find it to be chemical action.

Here is a coloured liquid which can show us the effects of chemical action by its change of colour. I will pour some of it into this glass, and you will find that these wires have a very strong action on it. I am not going to show you any effects of burning or heat; I will take these two platinum plates, fasten one to one pole and one to the other, and put them into this solution. In a very short time you will see the blue colour entirely destroyed. See — it is colourless now. I have merely brought the ends of the wires into the solution of indigo, and the power of electricity has come along those wires and made itself evident by its chemical action.

There is another curious thing to notice, now that we are dealing with the chemistry of electricity: the chemical power which destroys the colour is due to the action on one side only. I will pour some more of this sulpho-indigotic acid into a flat dish, and then make a porous dyke of sand dividing the fluid into two parts (Figure 50). Now we shall be able to see whether there is any difference between the two ends of the battery, and which of them has this peculiar action.

You see that it is the one on my right hand which has the power of destroying the blue, for the portion on that side is thoroughly bleached, while nothing has apparently happened on the other side. I say apparently, for you must not imagine that because you cannot see any action, none has taken place.

Editor's note: Sulpho-indigotic acid. A mixture of one part indigo and fifteen parts concentrated oil of vitriol. It is bleached on the side at which hydrogen gas is evolved, because the liberated hydrogen withdraws oxygen from the indigo, forming a colourless deoxidised indigo. In making the experiment, only enough of the sulpho-indigotic acid must be added to give the water a decided blue colour.

[Figure 50: A flat dish of blue sulpho-indigotic acid divided across the middle by a porous dyke of sand, with a pole of the battery dipping into each half. Only the right-hand half is bleached.]

Here is another instance of chemical action. I take these platinum plates again and put them into this solution of copper, out of which we earlier precipitated some of the metal when the platinum and zinc were put in together. You see that these two platinum plates have no chemical action of any kind; they might stay in the solution as long as I liked without any power of their own to bring down the copper. But the moment I bring the two poles of the battery into contact with them, the chemical action which was turned into electricity and carried along the wires becomes chemical action again at the two platinum poles; and now we shall have the power appearing on the left-hand side and throwing the copper down in the metallic state on the platinum plate.

In this way I might give you many instances of the extraordinary manner in which this chemical action, or electricity, can be carried about. That strange nugget of gold, of which there is a model in the other room — which has an interest of its own in the natural history of gold, which came from Ballarat, and which was worth eight or nine thousand pounds when it was melted down last November — was brought together in the bowels of the earth, perhaps ages and ages ago, by some such power as this.

And there is another beautiful result depending on chemical affinity in that fine lead tree, the lead growing and growing by virtue of this power. The lead and the zinc are combined together there in a little voltaic arrangement, in a manner far more important than the powerful one you see here; because in nature these minute actions are going on for ever, and are of great and wonderful importance in the precipitation of metals, the formation of mineral veins and so forth. These actions are not for a limited time, like my battery here: they act for ever in small degrees, accumulating more and more of their results.

Editor's note: Lead tree. To make a lead tree, pass a bundle of brass wires through the cork of a bottle and fasten a plate of zinc round them just where they come out of the cork, so that the zinc is in contact with every one of the wires. Make the wires diverge so as to form a sort of cone; then, having filled the bottle quite full of a solution of sugar of lead, insert the wires and cork and seal it down so as to exclude the air perfectly. In a short time the metallic lead will begin to crystallise round the diverging wires and form a beautiful object.

I have now given you all the illustrations time will let me show you of chemical affinity producing electricity, and electricity becoming chemical affinity again. Let that be enough for the present, and let us go a little deeper into the subject of this chemical force, or this electricity — which shall I name first, when each produces the other in such a variety of ways?

These forces are also wonderful in their power of producing another of the forces we have been considering, namely magnetism. And you know that it is only of late years, long since I was born, that the discovery of the relations between electricity and chemical affinity in producing magnetism became known. Scientific men had suspected this relationship for a long time and had long had great hopes of success; for in the pursuit of science we start first with hopes and expectations. Then we realise them and establish them, never to be lost again; and on them we found new expectations of further discoveries, and so go on pursuing, realising, establishing, and founding new hopes over and over again.

[Figure 51: A copper wire from the battery laid over a magnetic needle. Before the circuit is completed the needle is undisturbed; the instant contact is made it swings round, and falls back the moment contact is broken.]

[Figure 52: A bar of iron slipped inside a spiral of copper wire. So long as no current passes it attracts nothing; with the current flowing it becomes a powerful magnet and picks up pieces of iron at once.]

Now observe this. Here is a piece of wire which I am going to make into a bridge of force — that is, a communicator between the two ends of the battery. It is only copper wire, and so is not magnetic in itself. We will examine this wire with our magnetic needle (Figure 51); and though it is connected to one end of the battery, you see that before the circuit is completed it has no power over the magnet. But watch when I make contact — watch the needle, see how it is swung round; and notice how indifferent it becomes again when I break contact. So you see this wire plainly affects the magnetic needle under these circumstances.

Let me show you that rather more strongly. Here is a quantity of wire wound into a spiral, and this will affect the magnetic needle in a very curious way, because its shape makes it act very much like a real magnet. The copper spiral has no power over that magnetic needle at present; but if I make the electric current circulate through it, by bringing the two ends of the battery into contact with the two ends of the wire, what will happen? Why, one end of the needle is most powerfully drawn to it; and if I offer the other end of the needle, it is repelled. So you see I have produced exactly the same phenomena as I had with the bar magnet: one end attracting and the other repelling.

Is it not curious, then, to see that we can build a magnet out of copper? Furthermore, if I take an iron bar and put it inside the coil, then so long as no electric current is circulating it has no attraction, as you will see if I bring some iron filings or nails near it. But now, if I make contact with the battery, they are attracted at once. It becomes a powerful magnet immediately — so much so that I should not wonder if these magnetic needles in different parts of the table pointed towards it.

And I will show you by another experiment what attraction it has. This piece of iron, and that piece, and many others, are now strongly attracted (Figure 52); but as soon as I break contact the power is all gone and they fall. What better or stronger proof could there be of the relation between the powers of magnetism and electricity?

Again, here is a little piece of iron which is not yet magnetised. At present it will not pick up any one of these nails. But I will take a piece of wire and coil it round the iron — the wire being covered with cotton everywhere, it does not touch the iron — so that the current must go round in this spiral coil. I am, in fact, preparing an electromagnet. (We are obliged to use such terms to express our meaning, because it is a magnet made by electricity: by the force of electricity we produce a magnet of far greater power than a permanent steel one.)

It is finished now, and I will repeat the experiment you saw the other day of building up a bridge of iron nails. Contact is now made and the current is going through; it is a powerful magnet. Here are the iron nails we had the other day; and now that I have brought the magnet near them, they cling so hard that I can scarcely move them with my hand (Figure 53). But when the contact is broken, see how they fall. What could show you better than an experiment like this the magnetic attraction we have endowed these pieces of iron with?

Here again is a fine illustration of this strong power of magnetism. It is a magnet of the same sort as the one you have just seen. I am going to pass the current of electricity through the wires round this iron, to show you what powerful effects we get. Here are the poles of the magnet; and let us place this long bar of iron on one of them. You see, as soon as contact is made, how it rises into position (Figure 54). And if I take a piece like this cylinder and put it on — woe betide me if I get my finger between. I can roll it over; but if I try to pull it off, I might lift the whole magnet: I have no power to overcome the magnetic force so plainly at work here.

I might give you an endless number of illustrations of this high magnetic power. There is that long bar of iron held out; and I have no doubt that if I examined the other end of it, I should find it was a magnet too. See what power it must have, to hold up not only these nails but all those lumps of iron hanging from the end.

What, then, could surpass these evidences of the change of chemical force into electricity, and of electricity into magnetism? I might show you many other experiments in which I could get electricity and chemical action, heat and light, from a magnet. But what more need I show you, to prove the universal correlation of the physical forces of matter, and their mutual conversion one into another?

[Figure 53: The electromagnet with a bridge of iron nails clinging to it, so hard that they can scarcely be moved by hand — and falling in a heap the moment the contact is broken.]

[Figure 54: The great electromagnet, with a long bar of iron rising into position on one of its poles and lumps of iron hanging from the far end of the bar.]

And now let us give place, as young people, to the respect we owe our elders; and let me for a moment address those of our seniors who have honoured me with their presence during these lectures. I want to claim this moment to offer them our thanks, and to offer my thanks to you all, for the way you have borne the inconvenience I subjected you to at the beginning.

I hope that the insight you have gained here into some of the laws by which the universe is governed may be the occasion of some among you turning your attention to these subjects. For what study is there more fitted to the mind of man than that of the physical sciences? And what is there more capable of giving him an insight into the working of those laws — a knowledge of which lends interest to the most trifling phenomenon in nature, and makes the observing student find

    "——tongues in trees, books in the running brooks,
    Sermons in stones, and good in everything?"
