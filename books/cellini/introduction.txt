Benvenuto Cellini was born in Florence in 1500 and trained as a goldsmith. He worked for two popes, for Francis I of France, and finally for Duke Cosimo de' Medici in Florence. He made the gold saltcellar that is now in Vienna and the bronze Perseus that still stands in the Loggia dei Lanzi, a few steps from where he grew up. In 1558, at fifty-eight, he began dictating his life to a boy of thirteen in his workshop while he went on with his own work, and kept at it for some years. It stops without an ending, in the middle of an anecdote about a trip to Pisa.

What it covers is two books and 241 short chapters. He is in Rome for the sack of 1527, firing the guns from the walls of the Castel Sant'Angelo, and claims the shot that killed the commander of the besieging army. He is later locked up in that same castle by the Pope, and gets out over the wall on a rope of knotted bedsheets, breaking his leg in the fall. In between there are lawsuits, duels, plagues, a poisoning, a fever, several killings, and a long run of employers who all, by his account, treat him badly.

He is not a reliable witness about himself, and the book is better for it. Everything that goes wrong is somebody else's doing; every rival is a fraud; God takes a close personal interest in his affairs and sends him visions in prison to prove it. He is also funny without any sign of knowing it. The whole thing is dictated in the rhythm of a man talking rather than writing — sentences that start again halfway through, and chase one more detail before they finish — and this retelling keeps that rather than tidying it into good prose.

Two things in it are ugly and are not softened. He describes killing men, and he describes how he treated the women in his household, in the same untroubled voice he uses for a well-made cup, without any sense that either wants defending. This edition renders those passages exactly as he tells them and adds no comment.

The reason to read it is Book Two. The casting of the Perseus takes a few pages and is the best account of making anything in the language: the furnace lit, the workshop roof catching fire, Cellini in bed with a fever and certain he is dying, the metal curdling in the crucible, and every pewter plate and bowl in the house thrown into the melt to save the pour. This is translated from Cellini's own Italian rather than from a Victorian English version, and nothing is abridged.
