CELLINI — RUNNING NOTES (the shared consistency ledger)

Caps spent, no re-gloss. Every entry below is locked for the whole
volume; where a decision could reasonably have gone the other way, the
reason is written beside it (the burke rule for allowance tables applied
to editorial choices).


VOICE

Fast, spoken, headlong. Cellini dictated this while working, to a boy
of thirteen, and it reads like a man talking: sentences that start
again, subordinate clauses that never close, "it is enough that",
"know that", "now hear". KEEP THAT. The temptation with a self-glorying
narrator is to tidy him into good prose, and a tidy Cellini is not
Cellini but a story about a Renaissance craftsman.

Two halves, both of them his: DIALOGUE (quarrels, boasts, courtroom
scenes, the Duke's small talk) and TECHNICAL PROCESS (the alloy, the
vents in the mould, the two outlets for the bronze). Neither is
padding, and the process passages are not to be summarised — the
casting of the Perseus is the most famous account of making anything
in Western literature and it is famous because it is exact.

He is funny without knowing it. Play it straight; the comedy is in the
gap between what he reports and what he thinks he is reporting.


LOCKED VOCABULARY

  virtù        = skill, ability, talent, or courage as the sentence
                 requires — NEVER the modern English "virtue", which
                 makes a moral claim Cellini is not making. "uomini
                 virtuosi" are men of talent, "la virtuosa Scuola" is
                 the skilled School of Florence.
  Scuola       = School (capital S), the Florentine body of artists as
                 a corporate thing, not a building.
  uomo da bene = honest man. His highest word for a person, used of
                 the Castellan, of Guido Guidi, of himself.
  ribaldo      = rascal; ribaldone/ribaldaccio = great rascal.
  poltrone     = coward.
  virtuoso as a noun = man of talent, never "virtuoso" in the modern
                 musical sense.
  scudi, ducats, giulios, carlins, crazie, soldi, farthings: coinage
                 KEPT, as in grimm/ — it is doing real work as a
                 quantity and Cellini's quarrels are almost all about
                 a number.
  braccio/braccia, palmo, once: kept as braccia, palm, ounce.
  Signor / Messer / Monsignor: kept. "Your most illustrious
                 Excellency" for "Vostra Eccellenzia illustrissima" in
                 full, every time — the length of the address is part
                 of the register of a court, and shortening it makes
                 Cellini sound modern and casual with dukes, which he
                 is not.
  Le Maçon, d'Étampes, de Marmagne, de Villeroy, Fontainebleau, the
                 Petit Nesle: French names in their French forms.


CONVENTIONS

Chapter labels: every chapter keeps its own line, "Chapter N", exactly
as prep.py writes it in chapters/. check.py compares the label
sequence exactly and in order; a lost label welds two chapters into
one and NOTHING else can see it (the prose reads perfectly, the ratio
does not move, and every later citation in that file points at the
wrong chapter).

Headings must be UNIQUE across the book — "Book One, Chapters 93 to
97" — because assemble.py makes a section's anchor by slugifying its
heading and BOTH Books have a chapter 97 (the democracy2 trap).

Verse: the Proem sonnet, the prison dialogue in Book One 119, the
sonnet to the Castellan in 123, the Capitolo (63 tercets and a closing
line), and the four lines of doggerel on Bernardone. All set as
tab-indented verse, stanza for stanza and line for line against the
Italian, and rendered UNRHYMED in clear modern English — the boethius
reasoning: forcing rhyme costs more than the rhyme is worth, and
Cellini's verse is not what anybody comes to this book for.

No all-caps lines anywhere (assemble.py reads one as a section
heading). No markup: book titles go in plain text.


TRANSLATION DECISIONS

THE ITALIAN CLOCK is kept as Cellini gives it — "two hours of the
night", "twenty-two hours", "four hours into the night" — because the
hours are how he measures a day and converting them silently would put
a modern clock in a book that does not have one. The one exception is
Book One chapter 1, where the figure could not survive into English
without becoming nonsense; logged in check.py's NUM_DROPPED.

SYMONDS IS A CRIB, NOT A SOURCE. reference/ holds John Addington
Symonds' 1888 English for consultation, and it is thoroughly Victorian;
the augustine thou-sweep in check.py exists to catch the translator
drifting into it. It fired eight times over the book and every one was
mine, not Cellini's — always the same word, "whereupon", which is
Symonds' habit and not an English sentence anybody says.

THE 128-VS-127 CHAPTER DISCREPANCY: the Italian numbers Book One to
128 and Symonds to 127, because Symonds runs the Italian's 127 and 128
together. prep.py maps the crib accordingly. This is a transmission
artefact of the English, not a variant in Cellini, and is reconciled
silently (the grimm Dümmling precedent).

SOURCE SLIPS KEPT AS HIS. Cellini gives the price Antonio Landi asked
for the great diamond as seventeen thousand scudi in Book Two chapter
60 and as sixteen thousand in chapter 64, telling the same story
twice. Both stand as written: he is a man remembering, and the second
figure is not a slip a reader could only read as ours. Likewise the
date of the move into the roomy prison, given as 1538 where the
narrative is in 1539.

ONE TRANSCRIPTION SLIP CORRECTED: "6o libbre" of tin in Book Two
chapter 77 is a letter o for a zero. Sixty pounds; logged in check.py.

REDACTED NAMES stay redacted. The Italian prints "messer…" for the
lover of Ammannati's wife; the translation prints "Messer ——" and does
not guess.


SENSITIVE CONTENT — THE VERNE RULE, WITH ONE THING TO GET RIGHT

Everything stands as Cellini dictated it, with no note anywhere in the
volume: the killings (Pompeo in the street, the goldsmith's brother,
the innkeeper at the Siena gate), the mutilation of the man who
brought the lawsuit, the sack of Rome told as marksmanship, the
duelling, the bragging.

CATERINA IS THE PASSAGE TO GET RIGHT, and it is the nights/Sindbad
problem exactly. He beats her, drags her about the room by the hair,
has his revenge on her husband by sleeping with her while she models,
and reports all of it cheerfully as a good joke, in the same voice he
uses for a well-made cup. THE EDITION IS NOT CHEERFUL, and the way to
be not cheerful is to be EXACT: nothing softened, nothing cut, and no
sympathetic phrase slipped in on her behalf that he did not write. A
modern reader will notice what Cellini does not notice, and doing that
noticing for them destroys it. The same holds for the girl he calls
Scorzone, whom he got with child at fifteen and handed back to an aunt
with a dowry, and for the two words he gives the mother he beat out of
the house.

THE SODOMY TRIAL (Book Two 29-30) and BANDINELLO'S INSULT (35) are
translated in full and unhedged, including Cellini's answer about Jove
and Ganymede, which is the boldest thing anybody says in the book. The
charge, the law's penalty of burning, and his own demand that Caterina
and her mother be burned for confessing it all stand. No note.

The accusation over the boy Cencio in Book Two 61 is kept entire,
including the boy's denial and the mother's reply.
