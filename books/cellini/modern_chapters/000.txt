Proem

	I am writing this troubled Life of mine
	to give thanks to the God of nature
	who gave me my soul and has looked after it since;
	I have done high and various things, and I am alive.

	That cruel Destiny of mine has failed to rob me
	of life, of gold, of glory, of more skill than my share,
	of grace, of courage, of looks, of the kind of figure
	that passes many men, and catches the ones who pass me.

	Only one thing grieves me badly, now that I know it:
	how much of that dear time I lost in vanity.
	The wind carries our fragile thoughts away.

	Since regretting is no use, I shall be content,
	climbing back the way I came down — Benvenuto,
	Welcome, in the flower of this worthy Tuscan ground.

I had begun to write this Life of mine with my own hand, as you can see from certain pages stuck together; but seeing that I was losing too much time over it, and that it looked to me like enormous vanity, there came into my hands a son of Michele di Goro from the Pieve a Groppine, a boy of about thirteen and rather sickly. I set him to writing, and while I worked I dictated my Life to him; and because I got some pleasure out of it, I worked much harder and got a great deal more done. So I left him that job, which I hope to keep up as far forward as my memory will carry me.
