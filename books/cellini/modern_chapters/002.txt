Book One, Chapters 9 to 14

Chapter 9

About this time the Cardinal de' Medici, who was afterwards Pope Clement, had us called back to Florence at my father's request. A certain pupil of my father's, moved by his own spite, told the Cardinal that he ought to send me to Bologna to learn to play properly under a master who was there, called Antonio, a genuinely able man in that profession of playing. The Cardinal told my father that if he sent me there he would write me letters of recommendation and support. My father, who was dying of eagerness for it, sent me; and I, being keen to see the world, went willingly.

When I got to Bologna I set to work with a man called Master Ercole del Piffero, and began to earn; and meanwhile I went every day for my playing lesson, and in a few weeks I made a great deal of progress in this cursed playing. But I made far more progress in the goldsmith's art, because, having had no help at all from the Cardinal, I went to live in the house of a Bolognese miniature painter called Scipione Cavalletti, in the street of Our Lady of the Baraccan; and there I applied myself to drawing, and to working for a man called Graziadio, a Jew, with whom I earned very well.

At the end of six months I came back to Florence, where that Pierino the piper, who had once been my father's pupil, took it very badly; and I, to please my father, went to see him at his house and played the cornet and the flute with a brother of his called Girolamo, several years younger than Piero, and a very decent, good young man, exactly the opposite of his brother. One day among others my father came to this Piero's house to hear us play; and taking the greatest pleasure in my playing, he said: "All the same I shall make a marvellous player of him, against the wish of the man who tried to stop me." To this Piero answered, and he spoke the truth: "Your Benvenuto will get much more profit and honour if he sticks to the goldsmith's art than out of this piping." My father was so angry at those words, seeing that I was of Piero's opinion too, that he said to him in a great rage: "I knew perfectly well that it was you who blocked this end I so much wanted, and it was you who had me moved out of my place at the Palace, paying me back with the great ingratitude people use as a reward for great favours. I got it given to you, and you had it taken away from me; I taught you to play, with all the arts you know, and you stop my son doing what I want. But keep these prophetic words in mind: it will not be years, I do not say, or months, but a few weeks, before you sink through the floor for this dishonest ingratitude of yours."

To these words Pierino answered and said: "Master Giovanni, most men, when they grow old, go mad along with the old age, as you have done; and I do not wonder at it, because you have given away every scrap of your property with the greatest generosity, without considering that your children were going to need it. Whereas I mean to do the exact opposite: to leave my children so much that they will be able to help yours." To this my father answered: "No bad tree ever made good fruit, and the other way round; and I tell you more: you are a bad man, and your children will be mad and poor, and will come begging from my able and wealthy children." So he left the house, the two of them grumbling wild words at each other.

I, who had taken my good father's side, came out of that house with him and told him I wanted to take revenge for the insults that scoundrel had done him — "on condition that you let me stick to the art of drawing." My father said: "Oh, my dear son, I too have been a good draughtsman; and as a refreshment after such marvellous labours, and for the love of me, who am your father and got you and brought you up and gave you the beginning of so many honourable skills, will you not promise me that, when they are resting, you will take up that flute now and then, and that most wanton cornet, and play it for your own pleasure and delight?" I said yes, and very willingly, for the love of him. Then my good father said that skills of that sort would be the greatest revenge I could take for the injuries received from his enemies.

Not a full month had gone by after these words when that Pierino, who was having a vault put in at a house of his in the Via dello Studio, was one day in his ground-floor room, over the vault they were building, with a good many companions; and it came up in conversation, and he began talking about his master, who had been my father, and repeating the words my father had said to him about sinking through the floor. He had hardly got them out when the room he was in — because the vault had been badly thrown, or else by the true power of God, who does not pay on Saturdays — sank; and the stones of the vault and the bricks came down with him and broke both his legs. The men who were with him, being left on the rim of the vault, came to no harm, but were left stunned and astonished, especially at what he had been saying so scornfully a moment before.

When my father heard of it, he armed himself and went to see him, and in the presence of his father, whose name was Niccolaio da Volterra, trumpeter to the Signory, he said: "Oh, Piero, my dear pupil, I am very sorry for your trouble; but if you remember rightly, it is not long since I warned you of it; and what I told you will happen between your children and mine will happen just the same."

A short while afterwards the ungrateful Piero died of that injury. He left his shameless wife with a son, who some years later came to me in Rome to beg. I gave to him, partly because it is my nature to give alms; and afterwards I remembered with tears the happy state Pierino had been in when my father said those words to him — that Pierino's children would one day go begging from his own able children. And enough has been said about that; and let nobody ever make fun of the forecasts of a decent man when he has been unjustly wronged, because it is not he who is speaking, but the voice of God itself.

Chapter 10

I kept on at the goldsmith's art, and with it I helped my good father. His other son, my brother, called Cecchino — as I said above, he had had him started on Latin letters, because he wanted to make me, the elder, a great player and musician, and him, the younger, a great scholar of law. But nobody can force what nature inclines us to, which made me apply myself to the art of drawing, and my brother, who was of a fine build and full of grace, entirely to arms. And being still very young, he had left a first choice of the school of that most marvellous man Signor Giovannino de' Medici; and coming home, when I was not there, being rather badly supplied with clothes, he found his sisters and mine, who, hidden from my father, gave him a beautiful new cloak and doublet of mine. Beyond the help I was giving my father and my good, honest sisters, I had made myself those respectable clothes out of my own surplus labour. Finding myself cheated and my clothes taken, and not finding my brother, whom I wanted to take them back from, I asked my father why he let so great a wrong be done to me, seeing how willingly I wore myself out to help him. To this he answered that I was his good son, and that he had got back what he had thought lost; and that it was necessary — indeed a commandment of God himself — that whoever had good things should give to whoever had none; and that for love of him I should put up with this injury, and God would increase every good thing to me.

I, being a young man with no experience, answered my poor afflicted father back; and taking a wretched remnant of my clothes and my money, I set off towards one of the city gates. Not knowing which gate would put me on the road to Rome, I found myself at Lucca, and from Lucca at Pisa. When I got to Pisa — I was about sixteen — I stopped near the middle bridge, at the place they call the Fish Stone, at a goldsmith's shop, watching attentively what the master there was doing; and the master asked me who I was and what my trade was. I told him I did a little of the same art he was doing. This decent man told me to come into his shop, and immediately put work in front of me, and said these words: "Your good face makes me believe you are honest and good." So he gave me gold and silver and jewels; and when the first day was over he took me home in the evening to his house, where he lived respectably with a beautiful wife and children.

Remembering the grief my good father might be feeling about me, I wrote to him that I was in the house of a very good and decent man, called Master Ulivieri della Chiostra, and that I was working with him on a great many fine, large pieces; and that he should be easy in his mind, because I was applying myself to learning, and that I hoped with those skills soon to bring him back profit and honour. My good father answered the letter at once, saying this: "My son, the love I bear you is so great that if it were not for the great honour of the family, which I keep above everything, I should have set out at once to come for you; because it truly seems to me that I am without the light of my eyes when I do not see you every day, as I used to. I shall attend to bringing my house through to an honourable name, and you attend to learning your skills; and I only want you to remember these four plain words. Keep them, and never forget them:

	In whatever house you mean to stay,
	live honestly, and do not steal.

Chapter 11

This letter came into the hands of my master Ulivieri, and he read it without my knowing; then he told me he had read it, and said these words: "Well, my Benvenuto, your good face did not deceive me, as a letter from your father which has come into my hands assures me; he must be a very good and decent man. So consider yourself to be in your own house and with your own father."

While I was in Pisa I went to see the Campo Santo, and there I found many beautiful antiquities — marble sarcophagi, that is — and in many other places in Pisa I saw a great many other ancient things, and I worked away at them steadily on all the days I had left over from my work in the shop. And because my master used to come very affectionately to see me in the little room he had given me, and saw that I spent all my hours on things worth doing, he came to love me as if he had been my father. I made great progress in the year I stayed there, and worked in gold and in silver on important and beautiful pieces, which gave me the greatest heart to go further forward.

Meanwhile my father wrote to me very pitifully that I ought to come back to him, and in every letter reminded me that I must not lose that playing which he had taught me with so much labour. At that, the wish to go back to him went out of me at once, I hated this cursed playing so much; and it truly seemed to me that I was in paradise for the whole year I was in Pisa, where I never played once.

At the end of the year my master Ulivieri had occasion to come to Florence to sell certain gold and silver sweepings he had; and because a touch of fever had jumped on me in that filthy air, I came back to Florence with it and with my master. My father made a great fuss of that master of mine, begging him affectionately, without my knowing, to be so kind as not to take me back to Pisa. I stayed ill about two months, and my father very lovingly had me doctored and got me well, telling me continually that a thousand years seemed to have gone by while he waited for me to be well enough to hear me play a little. And while he was talking to me about this playing, holding my fingers on the pulse — because he had some knowledge of medicine and of Latin letters — he felt in that pulse, the moment he began to speak about playing, such a great disturbance that many times he went away from me dismayed and in tears. So that, seeing this great unhappiness of his, I told one of my sisters to bring me a flute; and although I still had the fever continually, since the instrument takes very little effort, playing gave me no disturbance. I played with such a fine command of hand and tongue that when my father came in unexpectedly he blessed me a thousand times, telling me that in the time I had been away from him it seemed to him I had made a great advance; and he begged me to go on, and not to lose so fine a skill.

Chapter 12

When I was well, I went back to my Marcone, the decent man, the goldsmith, who gave me my living; and with what I earned I helped my father and my household.

At this time there came to Florence a sculptor called Piero Torrigiani, who was coming from England, where he had been for many years; and because he was a great friend of that master of mine, he came to him every day. Seeing my drawings and my work, he said: "I have come to Florence to carry off as many young men as I can; because, having a great work to do for my King, I want some of my Florentines to help me. And since your way of working and your drawings are more a sculptor's than a goldsmith's, and I have great works in bronze to do, I shall make you able and rich at the same time." This man was of the most beautiful build, extremely bold, with more the air of a great soldier than of a sculptor, especially in his astonishing gestures and his ringing voice, and a way of knitting his eyebrows fit to frighten any man off anything; and every day he was talking about his exploits with those beasts of Englishmen.

In this connection he happened to fall into talking about Michelangelo Buonarroti, the occasion of it being a drawing of mine copied from a cartoon by the most divine Michelangelo. That cartoon was the first beautiful work in which Michelangelo showed his marvellous powers, and he made it in competition with another man who was making one — with Leonardo da Vinci; and they were to serve for the Council Hall in the Palace of the Signory. They represented the taking of Pisa by the Florentines; and the wonderful Leonardo da Vinci had chosen to show a battle of horsemen with the capture of some standards, done as divinely as anyone could imagine. Michelangelo Buonarroti, in his, showed a quantity of foot soldiers who, it being summer, had gone to bathe in the Arno; and at that instant he shows the alarm being given, and those naked foot soldiers running to arms, with so many fine gestures that no work was ever seen, either of the ancients or of any of the moderns, that reached so high a mark; and, as I have said, the great Leonardo's was most beautiful and wonderful. These two cartoons stood, one in the Medici palace and one in the Pope's hall. As long as they stood there they were the school of the world. Even though the divine Michelangelo made the great chapel of Pope Julius afterwards, he never reached half so high a mark; his powers never afterwards came up to the force of those first studies.

Chapter 13

Now let us come back to Piero Torrigiani, who, with that drawing of mine in his hand, said this: "This Michelangelo Buonarroti and I used to go as boys to learn in the Carmine church, in the chapel of Masaccio; and because Buonarroti had a habit of making fun of everybody who was drawing, one day among others, when he was annoying me, I got much angrier than usual, and clenched my hand and gave him such a punch on the nose that I felt the bone and the gristle of his nose crumble under my fist like a wafer; and marked by me like that he will stay as long as he lives." These words made such a hatred grow in me — because I was constantly seeing the works of the divine Michelangelo — that far from wanting to go off to England with him, I could not bear the sight of him.

I kept on steadily in Florence, learning under Michelangelo's beautiful manner, and I have never come away from it. At this time I formed a very close acquaintance and friendship with a well-bred young man of my own age, who was also with a goldsmith. His name was Francesco, son of Filippo, son of Fra Filippo, that most excellent painter. Going about together bred such love in us that we were never apart, night or day; and since his house was full of the beautiful studies his able father had made — several books of drawings by his own hand, copied from the beautiful antiquities of Rome — seeing them, I fell in love with them. We kept company for about two years.

In this time I made a piece of silver in low relief, about as big as a small child's hand. This piece served as a clasp for a man's belt, which they wore that size in those days. Carved on it was a knot of foliage done in the antique manner, with a number of little boys and other very beautiful masks. I made this piece in the shop of a man called Francesco Salimbene. When it had been seen by the goldsmiths' guild, I was given the credit of being the best young man in that art.

Now a certain Giovanbattista, called Il Tasso, a wood carver, a young man exactly my own age, began to tell me that if I wanted to go to Rome he would willingly come with me — this conversation of ours happened just after dinner — and because I had been angry with my father again over the same business of the playing, I said to Tasso: "You are a man for words and not for deeds." Tasso said: "I have quarrelled with my mother too, and if I had money enough to get me to Rome I would not go back to shut up that little bit of a shop I keep." To this I added that if that was what was stopping him, I had money by me that was enough to carry us both to Rome. Talking together like this as we walked, we found ourselves without noticing it at the Porta San Piero Gattolini. I said to him: "My dear Tasso, it is God's doing that we have come to this gate, when neither you nor I noticed it. Now that I am here, it seems to me I have done half the journey." And so, agreeing together as we went on our way, we said: "Oh, what will our old people say tonight?" Saying which, we made a pact together not to mention them again until we had got to Rome. So we tied our aprons behind us and went almost in silence as far as Siena.

When we got to Siena, Tasso said he had hurt his feet and did not want to come any further, and asked me to lend him money to get back with. I told him: "I should have nothing left to go on with; so you ought to have thought about it before you moved out of Florence. And if it is your feet that stop you coming, we will find a return horse for Rome, and then you will have no excuse for not coming." So I took the horse; and seeing that he did not answer me, I set off on the road towards Rome. When he saw I was set on it, he did not stop grumbling, but came limping along behind, a good way back and slowly, doing the best he could. When I got to the gate, feeling sorry for my little companion, I waited for him and took him up behind me, saying: "What on earth would our friends say about us, if, having set out for Rome, we had not had the courage to get past Siena?" Then the good Tasso said that I was right; and being a cheerful person, he began to laugh and sing; and so, singing and laughing all the way, we came to Rome. I was just nineteen, and so was the century.

As soon as we got to Rome I put myself into a shop with a master called Firenzuola. His name was Giovanni, and he came from Firenzuola in Lombardy, and he was an extremely able man at making vessels and large pieces. When I had shown him something of the model of that belt clasp I had made in Florence with Salimbene, he liked it marvellously, and said these words, turning to a workman he kept, a Florentine called Giannotto Giannotti, who had been with him several years: "This is one of those Florentines who know, and you are one of those who do not." Then I, recognising Giannotto, wanted to speak to him, because before he went to Rome we had often gone drawing together, and we had been on very familiar terms. He took what his master had said to him so badly that he said he did not know me and had no idea who I might be. At which, angry at such words, I said to him: "Oh, Giannotto, once my familiar friend — we have been together in this place and that, drawing, and eating and drinking and sleeping at your house in the country. I do not care whether you bear witness about me to this decent man, your master, because I hope my hands are such that, without your help, they will say what I am."

Chapter 14

When I had finished these words, Firenzuola, who was an extremely bold and spirited person, turned to Giannotto and said to him: "Oh, you low scoundrel, are you not ashamed to use words and manners like that to a man who has been so close a companion to you?" And with the same spirit he turned to me and said: "Come into the shop, and do as you have said, and let your hands say what you are." And he gave me a most beautiful piece of silver to make for a cardinal. It was a small casket copied from the porphyry one that stands in front of the door of the Rotonda. Besides what I copied, I enriched it out of my own head with so many beautiful little masks that my master went about boasting and showing it round the trade, that so well made a piece had come out of his shop. It was about half an arm's length in size, and it was arranged so that it served as a saltcellar to stand on the table.

This was the first money I earned in Rome; and part of that money I sent to help my good father, and the other part I kept for my own living; and with it I went about studying the ancient things, until my money ran out and I had to go back to the shop to work. That Battista del Tasso, my companion, did not stay long in Rome, but went back to Florence.

Having taken on new work, I got the wish, when I had finished it, to change masters, being egged on by a certain Milanese called Master Pagolo Arsago. My first master, Firenzuola, had a great quarrel with this Arsago, saying some insulting words to him in my presence; at which I took up the words in defence of my new master. I said that I had been born free, and free I meant to live; and that he had nothing to complain of in him, still less in me, since he was left owing me a few scudi by our agreement; and that as a free workman I meant to go where I pleased, knowing that I was doing nobody any wrong. My new master also used a good many words, saying that he had not called me, and that I would do him a favour by going back to Firenzuola. To this I added that, not seeing that I was doing him any wrong in any way, and having finished the work I had begun, I meant to belong to myself and not to anyone else; and that whoever wanted me should ask me for myself.

At this Firenzuola said: "I do not want to ask you for yourself any more, and do not you come in front of me again for anything." I reminded him of my money; he jeered at me; to which I said that just as well as I could handle the tools for the pieces he had seen, I could handle a sword no worse to recover what I had worked for. At these words an old man happened to stop, whose name was Master Antonio da San Marino. He was the first and most excellent goldsmith in Rome, and he had been Firenzuola's master. Having heard my case, which I put in a way that could be perfectly well understood, he took my part at once and told Firenzuola to pay me. The disputes were great, because this Firenzuola was a marvellous handler of weapons, a good deal better at that than at the goldsmith's art; but right won its place, and I helped it with the same courage, so that I was paid. And in the course of time Firenzuola and I became friends, and I stood godfather to a son of his, at his own request.
