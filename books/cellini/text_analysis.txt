TEXT ANALYSIS — Cellini's Life
Read this before translating. The ledger is running_notes.txt.


WHAT THIS EDITION IS
---------------------------------------------------------------------
From the Italian (it.wikisource, cached in _italian.json), with
Symonds's 1888 English as a per-chapter crib under reference/ — the
ovid/de-officiis pattern. USE SYMONDS TO CHECK A SENTENCE, NEVER TO
BORROW A PHRASE. He is a fine scholar and his prose is the problem:
Cellini wrote the way he talked, and Symonds turns him into a Victorian
man of letters.

Book One 128 chapters, Book Two 113, plus the Proem sonnet and the
Capitolo. Symonds has 127 in Book One because he runs the Italian's 127
and 128 together; prep.py locates that seam and gives both chapters the
same crib text.


VOICE, AND IT IS THE ENTIRE JOB
---------------------------------------------------------------------
This is the first modern autobiography and it is written by a man who
is bragging. He is a goldsmith, a sculptor, a soldier, a duellist and a
convicted murderer, and he tells you about all of it in the same tone:
that he was right, that his enemies were fools and villains, and that
God is particularly attentive to his affairs.

  - FAST, SPOKEN, HEADLONG. His sentences run on for half a page,
    chaining clauses with "and" and "so" and "because" until they lose
    their own grammar. UNSTACK THEM ONLY AS FAR AS CLARITY REQUIRES.
    Cutting them into short tidy English sentences is the commonest way
    to destroy this book: the breathlessness IS the character.
  - HE IS FUNNY, AND HE DOES NOT KNOW IT. The comedy is almost entirely
    at his own expense and he never once concedes it. Play it straight,
    exactly as with Bunyan's Talkative and Sancho: the reader gets the
    joke, and Cellini must not.
  - HE SWEARS, THREATENS AND BOASTS IN PLAIN LANGUAGE. Keep it plain.
    Symonds softens the obscenity and the violence and both must come
    back — but never inflated either: he is coarse, not pornographic.
  - DIALOGUE IS HALF THE BOOK and it is where he is best. Everyone gets
    a voice: the Pope wheedles, the French king booms, Cosimo sulks,
    the workmen answer back. Keep them distinct.
  - THE TECHNICAL PASSAGES ARE THE OTHER HALF, and they are the reason
    the book survives as a document: how a die is cut, how enamel is
    fired, how a wax model is invested and a bronze poured. RENDER THE
    PROCESS SO IT CAN BE FOLLOWED. Do not summarise a process, ever;
    that is this book's silent summarisation.


CONSISTENT VOCABULARY
---------------------------------------------------------------------
  Names in their Italian forms — Benvenuto, Cosimo, Bindo Altoviti,
  Pier Luigi Farnese — except rulers with settled English names:
  Pope Clement, Pope Paul, Francis the First, the Emperor, Duke Cosimo.
  scudi, crowns, giulios, ducats   kept, unconverted; the sums are the
                                   point of half his quarrels.
  virtù                            "skill" when it is craft, "courage"
                                   when it is a fight, "quality" when he
                                   is boasting about his family. It is
                                   NOT "virtue" in the modern sense and
                                   that false friend runs through the
                                   whole book.
  onore / vergogna                 honour / disgrace, in the hard
                                   sixteenth-century sense: what other
                                   people can be made to say about you.
  bottega                          workshop.
  maestro                          master, of a craft, not a teacher.
  messer / madonna                 kept before names.
  la mia Medusa, il mio Perseo     his works are named plainly: the
                                   Perseus, the Medusa, the saltcellar,
                                   the Nymph of Fontainebleau.


THE VERNE RULE, AND THIS BOOK LEANS ON IT HARDER THAN MOST
---------------------------------------------------------------------
Cellini describes killing several men and is unrepentant about all of
them; he describes beating his apprentices and a model; he tells the
story of his own conviction for sodomy as a piece of court malice; his
account of the sack of Rome has him personally shooting the Constable
of Bourbon and later the Prince of Orange; and he reports a necromancy
in the Colosseum as something that happened. HE IS ALSO, DEMONSTRABLY,
NOT ALWAYS TELLING THE TRUTH.

All of it stands as his, in full, with no note anywhere in the volume.
The reader is meant to notice that the narrator is unreliable and is
meant to notice it themselves — doing that noticing for them, either by
softening the crimes or by flagging the boasts, destroys the only
experience this book offers. The same rule as nights/'s Sindbad and
augustine/'s Book Six.

WHERE THAT DOES NOT REACH: the violence against women in Book Two —
the beating of Caterina, and what he says about her — is translated
plainly and completely, without softening and without heat. Do not add
a sympathetic phrase on her behalf that Cellini does not write, and do
not let his jauntiness slide into the narration as approval. He is
cheerful about it; the edition is not, and the way to be not cheerful
is to be exact.
