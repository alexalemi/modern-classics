Jean-Henri Fabre (1823–1915) is remembered as the entomologist of Sérignan, the village in Provence where in 1879 he bought a patch of stony ground he called the Harmas and spent the rest of his life watching the insects on it. What he saw there went into the ten volumes of his Souvenirs entomologiques, published between 1879 and 1907, close accounts of wasps, beetles, spiders and crickets that made him famous in old age. For most of his working life, though, he was a poorly paid schoolteacher, and he supported his family by writing schoolbooks for the Paris publisher Charles Delagrave, among them a series built around one character, Uncle Paul.

Le Livre d'histoires, published in 1869, was a reader of that kind, "for all schools," as its title page said. Uncle Paul, a bachelor farmer, has his brother's three children to stay: Claire, who is nearly twelve, Jules, two years younger and full of questions, and Emile, the youngest, who would rather be at his box of toys. Over eighty short chapters he tells them true stories, which he argues are better than fairy tales: about ants, metals, cloth and paper, silk, spiders, thunder, volcanoes, the shape of the earth, poisonous plants, mushrooms, shells, the sea, and finally the hive. The old servants Jacques and Mother Ambroisine join in, and so, for a few chapters, do neighbors from the village.

This English version was made by Florence Constable Bicknell and published by the Century Company of New York in 1917 from the nineteenth French edition. Little is recorded about her beyond her work for Century, for whom she also translated Fabre's The Wonder Book of Chemistry (1922). Her preface says what she changed: Fabre wrote the book as a dialogue, and she turned it into narrative, "with as little violence as possible to the author's text." Some of the pictures are American: a copperhead where Uncle Paul describes the viper, a Yellowstone geyser beside his account of Iceland's, a Virginian Railway locomotive.

The book is from the 1860s, and some of its science has moved on. Uncle Paul teaches that the earth is a ball of molten rock under a thin crust, and that earthquakes are that crust shifting on the liquid beneath; the planet's interior is now known to be mostly solid, and earthquakes are explained by the movement of plates. He says only one edible mushroom can be cultivated, and his bees mourn a dead queen with a funeral dirge.

This is a restored edition. Bicknell's text is printed as it appeared in 1917, checked against three scans of that printing, with nothing modernized or cut; the edition adds descriptions of the 69 pictures. The book is in the public domain in the United States. Fabre's French original is in the public domain everywhere; outside the United States, the translation's status depends on the date of Bicknell's death, which we have not been able to establish.
