Alexis de Tocqueville arrived in the United States in May 1831 with his friend Gustave de Beaumont. He was twenty-five, a junior magistrate at Versailles from an old Norman noble family, and his official business was the American prison system. The two men did write that report. They also spent about nine months travelling — New England, the frontier in Michigan, Lower Canada, down the Ohio and the Mississippi to New Orleans, back through the South to Washington — asking almost everybody they met how the country governed itself.

His subject was not really America. Tocqueville's family had been ruined by the French Revolution; his parents were imprisoned during the Terror and his great-grandfather went to the guillotine. He believed that the levelling of ranks which had produced all that was still going on, that it could not be stopped, and that Europe was going to get it whether it wanted it or not. America was the one place where it had already happened and where a Frenchman could go and look at the result. That is what the opening chapter says, and it is why the book is as much a warning to France as a description of the United States.

It came out in two halves. The volume of 1835 is about machinery: townships and juries, the federal constitution, the courts, the newspapers, political parties, and what he calls the tyranny of the majority — which is not a legal problem but a social one, the pressure of everyone's opinion that makes dissent pointless rather than illegal. It ends with a chapter on the futures of white Americans, Native Americans and enslaved Black Americans that is the longest in the book and is here entire. The volume of 1840 is more abstract, and asks what equality does to the inside of people: to their philosophy, religion, poetry, ambition, marriages, manners and wages. Its last part contains the famous prediction of a mild, orderly despotism that leaves people free to do everything except govern themselves.

Two words will mislead a modern reader. Democracy, for Tocqueville, means equality of social conditions rather than a system of voting. Manners means the whole habitual moral texture of a people, not etiquette.

Both volumes are here complete. This edition was made from Henry Reeve's English translation, made by a contemporary who corresponded with Tocqueville about it, and it puts him into formal Victorian prose he did not write. A second edition of the same work, made directly from Tocqueville's French, is in this collection as Democracy in America (From the French).
