Fredy Perlman (1934–1985) was born in Brno, in Czechoslovakia, and spent his childhood in Cochabamba, Bolivia, where his family had fled before the war; they reached the United States in 1945. He studied at UCLA and Columbia, took a doctorate in Belgrade, and taught at Western Michigan University in Kalamazoo, and in May and June of 1968 he was in Paris among the striking students and workers. That September he started a small radical press, Black & Red, which soon moved with him to Detroit, where he spent the rest of his life printing, writing and typesetting, much of it for the anarchist paper the Fifth Estate. He died in July 1985, at fifty, after surgery to replace two heart valves.

This book came out of a novel. Perlman had spent six years studying the European conquest of North America for a novel about Detroit, The Strait, and when he read Frederick Turner's Beyond Geography (1980), which argues that the Western spirit was at war with wilderness long before Columbus, he broke off the novel to write this instead. The first two chapters ran in the Fifth Estate in the winter of 1982–83, and the whole was printed by Black & Red that year, typeset by Perlman himself.

Its argument borrows its monster from Thomas Hobbes, whose Leviathan (1651) pictured the state as an artificial giant made of the people inside it. Perlman agrees with the picture and reverses the verdict. His Leviathan is a corpse that its captives keep moving, and his history follows it from the first Sumerian city-state to the American frontier, through Egypt, Israel, Persia, Greece, Rome, Islam, Christendom and the conquest of the Americas. Each civilization is a machine for turning free people into "zeks", the Soviet word for prisoners in a labor camp; each is resisted, by people who walk away from it, and many of the resisters become machines in turn. "His-story" is his name for the record kept by the machine.

Read it as a polemic in the form of a myth, which is how Perlman wrote it. He gives no notes and says plainly that he refuses to argue from "Positive Evidence"; some episodes are fables he invents and labels as such. The rest of the history rests on secondary books, some already old in 1983, and much of its detail is wrong or long out of date, from the dates of kings to theories, such as the idea that Phoenicians reached America, which no historian now holds. The judgments are his and are meant to provoke, including his reading of Moses as the founder of a totalitarian state.

This is a restored edition: Perlman's text as printed by Black & Red, checked word by word against page images of the book, with his twenty-seven plates borrowed from William Blake. The book was published without a copyright notice and never registered, and it is in the public domain in the United States.
