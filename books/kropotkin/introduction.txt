Peter Kropotkin (1842–1921) was born a prince in Moscow, into one of the oldest families of the Russian nobility. As a young officer he chose service in Siberia, and between 1862 and 1867 he travelled thousands of miles through eastern Siberia and northern Manchuria as a geographer. Those journeys are where this book begins. He later gave up his title and his career, joined the revolutionary movement, and was imprisoned in the Peter and Paul Fortress in St Petersburg, from which he escaped in 1876. After a second imprisonment in France he settled in England in 1886, where he lived for thirty years as a writer and one of the best-known anarchists in Europe.

Mutual Aid is his answer to a famous essay. In 1888 Thomas Henry Huxley, Darwin's most effective public defender, published "The Struggle for Existence" in the Nineteenth Century, describing nature as a gladiators' show and early human life as a war of each against all. Kropotkin replied in the same review, in a series of articles from 1890 to 1896, and collected them as this book in 1902. His claim is not that competition does not exist, but that cooperation within a species is at least as important a factor in its survival, and that it runs unbroken from ants and birds through village communities and medieval guilds to the trade unions and friendly societies of his own day.

Two things will help a modern reader. First, Kropotkin wrote with the anthropology of the 1890s, and he uses its vocabulary: "savages" and "barbarians" are stages in a scheme of human development that was standard then and has since been abandoned, and much of his evidence about particular peoples has been revised. Second, the book is an argument made by assembling examples, and the footnotes are where he shows his sources; many are in French, German and Russian.

The idea outlived the evidence. Biologists now study the evolution of cooperation as a central problem, and Stephen Jay Gould's 1988 essay "Kropotkin Was No Crackpot" argued that his observations in Siberia had pointed him at something real. The best chapters for a first reading are the first, on animals, and the last two, on mutual aid among working people in Kropotkin's own time.

This is a restored edition of the Revised and Cheaper Edition (London, Heinemann, 1904), the text as Kropotkin revised it in 1904, compared word by word against scans of two printed copies. It includes the Appendix of twelve further notes, which the Project Gutenberg and Wikisource texts leave out. The footnotes follow the paragraphs that cite them. The Appendix headings keep Kropotkin's references to the pages of the 1904 book ("To p. 10"), which point to the chapter passage each note expands.
