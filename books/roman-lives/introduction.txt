Plutarch was a Greek from Chaeronea, a small town in Boeotia, born around AD 46 and a priest at Delphi for much of his later life. He wrote these biographies around AD 100, under the Roman empire, about men who had died a century and a half earlier in the republic the empire replaced. He wrote them in pairs, a Greek matched with a Roman, with a short comparison at the end of each pair.

He says what he is doing at the opening of his life of Alexander, and it is worth having in advance. He is writing lives, not histories. The most celebrated exploits do not always show what a man was; often a small thing, a phrase or a joke, reveals more of a character than battles where thousands die. So he will spend a page on a remark at a dinner and a sentence on a campaign, and a reader who wants the military narrative is reading the wrong book. He is also working from other people's books, some of them now lost, and he passes on omens, portents and dreams as part of the record without always saying what he makes of them.

This volume takes five Roman lives and puts them in the order of the story they tell together: Caesar, Cato the Younger, Cicero, Brutus, Antony. That is the fall of the Republic from five angles. The same events keep coming round — the Catiline conspiracy, the crossing of the Rubicon in 49 BC, the murder in the senate house on the Ides of March in 44, the battle at Philippi, the last campaign that ended at Actium — and each time they are seen by a different man, who wanted something different and knew something different. The repetition is the reason to read the five together. Their Greek partners in the Parallel Lives are Alexander, Phocion, Demosthenes, Dion and Demetrius, and neither those lives nor Plutarch's comparisons are included here.

All five men die violently. Caesar was stabbed in the senate house. Cato cut his own throat at Utica in 46 BC rather than accept a pardon from Caesar. Cicero was proscribed and hunted down in 43. Brutus died after Philippi in 42, Antony at Alexandria in 30. Plutarch is better on the deaths than on anything else, and he is very good on the small things that lead up to them: Caesar joking with the pirates who had captured him about coming back to crucify them, Cicero unable to resist a line that would make an enemy, Antony's cook keeping eight boars on the roast at staggered times because nobody could say when the general would want dinner.

This edition modernizes John Dryden's English translation as revised by Arthur Hugh Clough in 1859, complete for each of the five lives.
