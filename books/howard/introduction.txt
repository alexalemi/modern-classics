Ebenezer Howard (1850–1928) was a London shorthand writer who took down speeches and evidence for a living. He had no training as an architect or planner. As a young man he spent several years in the United States, farming in Nebraska and working in Chicago, and came home convinced that the crowding of English cities could be cured. In 1898 he published his cure as To-morrow: A Peaceful Path to Real Reform. In 1902 he revised it and gave it the title it has kept, Garden Cities of To-morrow. The first garden city, Letchworth in Hertfordshire, was begun the following year.

The argument starts from a diagram, the Three Magnets. The town draws people with work, wages and society but repels them with rents, smoke and slums. The country offers fresh air and beauty but no work and no company. Howard proposes a third magnet, the town-country, which combines the advantages of both. He then works it out in detail and in figures: a town of 32,000 people on 1,000 acres, laid out in rings around a central park, set in an agricultural belt of 5,000 acres that it never builds over. The whole estate is bought as farmland at farmland prices and held in trust. As the town grows, the value of the land rises, and the rents the townspeople pay, which Howard calls "rate-rents", pay for the roads, schools and services and slowly pay off the purchase. When the town is full it does not spread; it founds another town beyond its belt, and a cluster of such towns, joined by railways, becomes what he calls a Social City.

A modern reader needs to know a few things. The money is in pounds, shillings and pence, written £ s. d., with twelve pence to the shilling and twenty shillings to the pound. The long tables of estimated revenue and costs are meant to show the scheme would pay; they are the part of the book that persuaded practical men. Howard also takes up the arguments of his day about temperance, municipal trading and socialism, and places his plan between them. In the final chapter he turns to London and asks what would happen to the city if people could leave it.

This is a restored edition, and Howard's words are unchanged. The text is the Project Gutenberg transcription of the 1902 edition, compared word by word with a scanned copy of that printing. It keeps Howard's portrait, the epigraph from Lowell, the footnotes and the Postscript. The five diagrams come from the transcription's high-resolution copies, and each now has a description that also transcribes the diagram's lettering. That description serves as its alt text. The index, and the Garden City Association's list of officers bound in after it, are omitted. The edition also adds modern typesetting, an ebook, and this introduction.
