John Stuart Mill was raised as an experiment. His father started him on Greek at three and set him to teaching his younger siblings before he was ten, and at twenty he had a breakdown he later traced straight to the training. He published this book in 1859, the year after his wife died. He had married Harriet Taylor in 1851, after a friendship of twenty years; she died at Avignon in 1858, and the dedication he wrote for this volume calls her the inspirer and in part the author of all that is best in his writings, and says that the parts he most wanted her to revise will now never be revised. That dedication is printed here.

Almost nothing in the book sounds old, which is the difficulty. Three words are doing technical work in ordinary clothes. Utility is not usefulness; it is Bentham's standard, the greatest happiness, and Mill says outright that he claims no advantage from any notion of abstract right and rests everything on utility in the largest sense. Self-regarding is his own coinage and the hinge of the book: conduct whose consequences fall on the person doing it. And when he writes that mankind gain more by letting each other live as seems good to themselves, suffer means allow. A few others have moved the same way — peculiar means specific, vulgarly means commonly, obtain means hold good.

The principle is one sentence. The only purpose for which power can rightfully be exercised over any member of a civilized community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others. The five chapters are the cost of it. Chapter Two is the case for free speech, and it is not that the censor might be wrong: it is that an opinion nobody is allowed to argue with dies in the people who hold it. Chapter Three is about individuality and is the best writing in the book. Chapters Four and Five work the principle through drink, gambling, marriage, education and compulsory schooling, and Mill does not make it come out tidy.

He also limits the principle himself, in Chapter One: it applies only to human beings in the maturity of their faculties, and not, he says, to backward states of society, where despotism is a legitimate mode of government. That is the most argued-over passage in the book's modern reception, and it is here as he wrote it, with no note attached. So are the attack on Christian morality, the pages on the Mormons, and the last chapter's view that a man who cannot support children should not be permitted to have them.

What he was writing against was not a king. It was opinion — the pressure of a majority that reaches further into the details of a life than any law. He is angrier than his reputation suggests, and the prosecutions for opinion in England in 1857, the Maine liquor law and the Sabbath legislation are where the temper shows. His own footnotes are kept in place. Mary Wollstonecraft's Vindication of 1792 and Mill's own The Subjection of Women, written in 1861 and published in 1869, are both in this collection.
