Chapter Two: On the Liberty of Thought and Discussion

(Part 2 of 3)

But in fact the saying that truth always triumphs over persecution is one of those pleasant falsehoods that men repeat after one another until they pass into commonplaces, and that all experience refutes. History teems with instances of truth put down by persecution. If it is not suppressed for ever, it may be thrown back for centuries. To speak only of religious opinions: the Reformation broke out at least twenty times before Luther, and was put down. Arnold of Brescia was put down. Fra Dolcino was put down. Savonarola was put down. The Albigensians were put down. The Waldensians were put down. The Lollards were put down. The Hussites were put down. Even after Luther's time, wherever persecution was persisted in it succeeded. In Spain, Italy, Flanders and the Austrian empire, Protestantism was rooted out, and most likely would have been in England too if Queen Mary had lived, or if Queen Elizabeth had died. Persecution has always succeeded, except where the heretics were too strong a party to be effectively persecuted. No reasonable person can doubt that Christianity might have been wiped out in the Roman Empire. It spread and became dominant because the persecutions were only occasional, lasted a short time, and were separated by long intervals in which it could spread almost undisturbed. It is idle sentimentality to suppose that truth, merely because it is truth, has some inherent power denied to error of prevailing against the dungeon and the stake. Men are not more zealous for truth than they often are for error, and a sufficient application of legal or even social penalties will generally succeed in stopping the spread of either. The real advantage truth has is this: when an opinion is true, it may be put out once, twice, or many times, but in the course of ages there will generally be people to rediscover it, until one of its reappearances falls at a time when favourable circumstances let it escape persecution long enough to gain the strength to survive every later attempt to suppress it.

It will be said that we do not now put to death the introducers of new opinions; that we are not like our fathers who killed the prophets — we even build tombs for them. It is true we no longer put heretics to death, and the amount of punishment modern feeling would probably tolerate, even against the most objectionable opinions, is not enough to stamp them out. But let us not flatter ourselves that we are yet clear of the stain even of legal persecution. Penalties for opinion, or at least for expressing it, still exist in law, and their enforcement is not, even in these times, so unheard-of as to make it at all incredible that they may some day be revived in full force. In 1857, at the summer assizes for the county of Cornwall, an unfortunate man said to be of unexceptionable conduct in every relation of life was sentenced to twenty-one months' imprisonment for saying, and writing on a gate, some offensive words about Christianity. Within a month of the same date, at the Old Bailey, two people were rejected as jurymen on two separate occasions, and one of them was grossly insulted by the judge and by one of the counsel, because they honestly declared that they had no theological belief; and a third, a foreigner, was for the same reason denied justice against a thief. This refusal of redress was made under the legal doctrine that nobody may give evidence in a court of justice who does not profess belief in a God — any god will do — and in a future state. That amounts to declaring such people outlaws, shut out from the protection of the courts: they may not only be robbed or assaulted with impunity if nobody is present but themselves or people of similar opinions, but anyone else may be robbed or assaulted with impunity if proving the fact depends on their evidence. The assumption behind this is that the oath of a person who does not believe in a future state is worthless — a proposition that shows great ignorance of history in those who accept it, since it is historically true that a large proportion of unbelievers in every age have been people of distinguished integrity and honour; and it would be maintained by nobody who had the least idea how many of the people in highest repute with the world, for their virtues as much as for their accomplishments, are well known, at least to their friends, to be unbelievers. The rule is suicidal besides, and cuts away its own foundation. On the pretence that atheists must be liars, it admits the testimony of every atheist willing to lie and rejects only those who face public disgrace by confessing a detested creed rather than swear to a falsehood. A rule so self-convicted of absurdity as far as its professed purpose goes can only be kept in force as a badge of hatred, a relic of persecution — a persecution with the peculiarity that the qualification for undergoing it is being clearly proved not to deserve it. The rule, and the theory behind it, are hardly less insulting to believers than to unbelievers. For if a man who does not believe in a future state necessarily lies, it follows that those who do believe are only kept from lying, if they are kept from it, by the fear of hell. We will not do the authors and supporters of the rule the injury of supposing that the conception they have formed of Christian virtue is drawn from their own experience.

These, it is true, are only rags and remnants of persecution, and may be thought not so much a sign of the wish to persecute as an example of that very frequent weakness of English minds which makes them take a preposterous pleasure in asserting a bad principle when they are no longer bad enough to want to put it into practice. But unhappily there is nothing in the state of the public mind to guarantee that the suspension of worse forms of legal persecution, which has now lasted about a generation, will continue. In this age the quiet surface of routine is as often disturbed by attempts to revive old evils as to introduce new benefits. What is boasted of at present as the revival of religion is always, in narrow and uncultivated minds, at least as much a revival of bigotry; and where there is that strong permanent leaven of intolerance in a people's feelings which lives at all times in the middle classes of this country, it takes very little to provoke them into actively persecuting people they have never stopped thinking proper objects of persecution. For it is this — the opinions men hold and the feelings they cherish about those who disown the beliefs they think important — that makes this country not a place of mental freedom. For a long time now the chief mischief of the legal penalties has been that they strengthen the social stigma. It is the stigma that is really effective, and so effective that professing opinions under society's ban is much less common in England than professing opinions that risk judicial punishment is in many other countries. For everyone except those whose money makes them independent of other people's goodwill, opinion on this subject is as effective as law: men might as well be imprisoned as shut out from the means of earning their bread. Those whose bread is already secure, and who want no favours from men in power, or from bodies of men, or from the public, have nothing to fear from openly avowing any opinion but being ill thought of and ill spoken of — and it ought not to take a very heroic character to bear that. There is no room for any appeal *ad misericordiam*, any plea for pity, on behalf of such people. But though we no longer inflict as much harm on those who think differently from us as we used to, it may be that we do ourselves as much harm as ever by the way we treat them. Socrates was put to death, but Socratic philosophy rose like the sun in the sky and spread its light over the whole intellectual firmament. Christians were thrown to the lions, but the Christian church grew into a stately spreading tree, overtopping the older and less vigorous growths and stifling them in its shade. Our merely social intolerance kills nobody and roots out no opinions, but it induces men to disguise them, or to give up any active effort to spread them. With us, heretical opinions do not perceptibly gain ground, or even lose it, from decade to decade or generation to generation; they never blaze out far and wide, but go on smouldering in the narrow circles of thinking and studious people among whom they originate, without ever lighting up the general affairs of mankind with either a true light or a false one.

And so a state of things is kept up that is very satisfactory to some minds, because without the unpleasant business of fining or imprisoning anybody it keeps every prevailing opinion outwardly undisturbed, while not absolutely forbidding the use of reason by dissenters afflicted with the disease of thought. A convenient plan for having peace in the intellectual world and keeping everything in it going on very much as it does already. But the price paid for this sort of intellectual pacification is the sacrifice of the entire moral courage of the human mind. A state of things in which a large proportion of the most active and inquiring intellects find it advisable to keep the real principles and grounds of their convictions to themselves, and to fit as much of their own conclusions as they can, in what they say publicly, to premises they have inwardly abandoned — such a state cannot produce the open, fearless characters and the logical, consistent intellects that once adorned the thinking world. The sort of men to be looked for under it are either mere conformers to commonplace or timeservers for truth, whose arguments on every great subject are meant for their hearers and are not the arguments that convinced themselves. Those who avoid this alternative do it by narrowing their thoughts and their interests to matters that can be discussed without venturing into the region of principles — that is, to small practical questions, which would come right of themselves if only the minds of mankind were strengthened and enlarged, and which will never be effectively put right until they are; while the very thing that would strengthen and enlarge men's minds, free and daring speculation on the highest subjects, is given up.

I want to draw attention to the fact that a man who has been thought fit to fill a high office in the government of this country, under a liberal Ministry, maintains the doctrine that all who do not believe in the divinity of Christ are beyond the pale of toleration. Who, after this imbecile display, can indulge the illusion that religious persecution has passed away, never to return?

Those who see no evil in this reticence on the part of heretics should consider, first, that because of it there is never any fair and thorough discussion of heretical opinions, and that those of them which could not survive such a discussion, though they may be kept from spreading, do not disappear. But it is not the minds of the heretics that suffer most from the ban placed on every inquiry that does not end in orthodox conclusions. The greatest harm is done to those who are not heretics, and whose whole mental development is cramped and their reason cowed by the fear of heresy. Who can compute what the world loses in the multitude of promising intellects joined to timid characters, who dare not follow out any bold, vigorous, independent train of thought for fear it might land them in something that could be considered irreligious or immoral? Among them we may occasionally see a man of deep conscientiousness and subtle, refined understanding who spends his life quibbling with an intellect he cannot silence, and exhausts the resources of ingenuity in trying to reconcile the promptings of his conscience and his reason with orthodoxy — which perhaps in the end he never manages to do. Nobody can be a great thinker who does not recognise that as a thinker his first duty is to follow his intellect wherever it leads. Truth gains more even by the errors of someone who, with proper study and preparation, thinks for himself, than by the true opinions of those who hold them only because they do not allow themselves to think.

Not that freedom of thought is required solely, or even chiefly, to produce great thinkers. On the contrary, it is as much and even more indispensable to enable average human beings to reach the mental stature they are capable of. There have been, and may be again, great individual thinkers in a general atmosphere of mental slavery. But there never has been, and never will be, an intellectually active people in such an atmosphere. Where a people has made a temporary approach to such a character, it has been because the dread of unorthodox speculation was for a time suspended. Where there is a tacit agreement that principles are not to be disputed, where discussion of the greatest questions that can occupy humanity is considered closed, we cannot hope to find that generally high level of mental activity which has made some periods of history so remarkable. Never, when controversy avoided the subjects large and important enough to kindle enthusiasm, was the mind of a people stirred to its foundations and the impulse given that raised even people of the most ordinary intellect to something of the dignity of thinking beings. We have had one example of this in the condition of Europe in the years just after the Reformation; another, confined to the Continent and to a more cultivated class, in the speculative movement of the second half of the eighteenth century; and a third, of still shorter duration, in the intellectual ferment of Germany in the age of Goethe and Fichte. These periods differed widely in the particular opinions they developed, but they were alike in this: in all three the yoke of authority was broken. In each an old mental despotism had been thrown off and no new one had yet taken its place. The impulse given at these three periods has made Europe what it is now. Every single improvement that has taken place in the human mind or in institutions can be traced distinctly to one or another of them. Appearances have for some time suggested that all three impulses are nearly spent, and we can expect no fresh start until we assert our mental freedom again.

Let us now pass to the second division of the argument. Dropping the supposition that any received opinion may be false, let us assume they are all true, and examine the worth of the way they are likely to be held when their truth is not freely and openly canvassed. However unwillingly a person with a strong opinion may admit the possibility that his opinion is false, he ought to be moved by this consideration: that however true it may be, if it is not fully, frequently and fearlessly discussed, it will be held as a dead dogma, not a living truth.

There is a class of people — happily not quite so numerous as they used to be — who think it enough if a person assents without doubting to what they think true, even though he has no knowledge at all of the grounds of the opinion and could not defend it tenably against the most superficial objection. Such people, once they can get their creed taught on authority, naturally think that no good and some harm comes of allowing it to be questioned. Where their influence prevails, they make it nearly impossible for the received opinion to be rejected wisely and thoughtfully, though it may still be rejected rashly and ignorantly; for shutting out discussion entirely is seldom possible, and once it gets in, beliefs not grounded in conviction tend to give way before the slightest semblance of an argument. Setting that possibility aside, though — supposing that the true opinion stays in the mind, but stays as a prejudice, a belief independent of argument and impervious to it — this is not the way truth ought to be held by a rational being. This is not knowing the truth. Truth held this way is only one superstition the more, clinging by accident to the words that state a truth.

If the intellect and judgment of mankind ought to be cultivated — a thing Protestants at least do not deny — on what can these faculties be more suitably exercised than on the matters that concern a person so much that he is thought bound to have opinions about them? If cultivating the understanding consists in one thing more than another, it is surely in learning the grounds of one's own opinions. Whatever people believe on subjects where it is of the first importance to believe rightly, they ought to be able to defend against at least the common objections. But somebody may say: "Then let them be *taught* the grounds of their opinions. It does not follow that opinions must be merely parroted because they are never heard contradicted. People who learn geometry do not simply commit the theorems to memory; they understand and learn the proofs as well, and it would be absurd to say that they remain ignorant of the grounds of geometrical truths because they never hear anyone deny them and try to disprove them." Undoubtedly; and such teaching is sufficient on a subject like mathematics, where there is nothing at all to be said on the wrong side of the question. The peculiarity of the evidence for mathematical truths is that all the argument is on one side. There are no objections, and no answers to objections. But on every subject where a difference of opinion is possible, the truth depends on a balance struck between two sets of conflicting reasons. Even in natural philosophy there is always some other possible explanation of the same facts — a geocentric theory instead of a heliocentric one, phlogiston instead of oxygen — and it has to be shown why that other theory cannot be the true one; and until this is shown, and until we know how it is shown, we do not understand the grounds of our opinion. But when we turn to subjects infinitely more complicated — to morals, religion, politics, social relations and the business of life — three quarters of the arguments for every disputed opinion consist in dispelling the appearances that favour some different opinion. The greatest orator of antiquity but one has left it on record that he always studied his opponent's case with as much intensity as his own, if not more. What Cicero practised as the means of success in court needs to be imitated by everyone who studies any subject in order to arrive at the truth. He who knows only his own side of the case knows little of that. His reasons may be good, and nobody may have been able to refute them. But if he is equally unable to refute the reasons on the other side, if he does not so much as know what they are, he has no ground for preferring either opinion. The rational position for him would be suspension of judgment; and unless he is content with that, he is either led by authority or adopts, as most of the world does, the side he feels most inclined to. Nor is it enough that he should hear his opponents' arguments from his own teachers, presented as the teachers state them and accompanied by what they offer as refutations. That is not the way to do the arguments justice, or to bring them into real contact with his own mind. He must be able to hear them from people who actually believe them, who defend them in earnest and do their very utmost for them. He must know them in their most plausible and persuasive form; he must feel the whole force of the difficulty which the true view of the subject has to meet and dispose of, or he will never really take possession of the portion of truth that meets and removes that difficulty. Ninety-nine out of a hundred of the people we call educated are in this condition, even those who can argue fluently for their opinions. Their conclusion may be true, but for anything they know it might be false. They have never put themselves in the mental position of those who think differently, and considered what such people might have to say; and consequently they do not, in any proper sense of the word, know the doctrine they themselves profess. They do not know the parts of it that explain and justify the rest; the considerations that show how a fact which seems to conflict with another can be reconciled with it, or why, of two apparently strong reasons, one and not the other should be preferred. The whole part of the truth that tips the scale and settles the judgment of a fully informed mind is unknown to them; and it is never really known except to those who have attended equally and impartially to both sides and tried to see the reasons of each in the strongest light. This discipline is so essential to a real understanding of moral and human subjects that if opponents of any important truth do not exist, it is indispensable to imagine them and supply them with the strongest arguments the most skilful devil's advocate can conjure up.

To weaken the force of these considerations, an enemy of free discussion may be supposed to say that there is no need for mankind in general to know and understand everything philosophers and theologians can say for and against their opinions; that ordinary people need not be able to expose all the misstatements or fallacies of an ingenious opponent; that it is enough if there is always somebody capable of answering them, so that nothing likely to mislead the uninstructed goes unrefuted; that simple minds, having been taught the obvious grounds of the truths impressed on them, may trust to authority for the rest, and, knowing that they have neither the knowledge nor the talent to resolve every difficulty that can be raised, may rest in the assurance that all those which have been raised have been or can be answered by people specially trained for the task.

Grant this view of the subject the utmost that can be claimed for it by those most easily satisfied with the amount of understanding that ought to accompany belief in a truth; even so, the argument for free discussion is not weakened at all. For even this doctrine admits that mankind ought to have a rational assurance that every objection has been satisfactorily answered; and how are objections to be answered if what needs answering is never spoken? Or how can the answer be known to be satisfactory if the objectors have no opportunity of showing that it is not? If not the public, then at least the philosophers and theologians who are to resolve the difficulties must make themselves familiar with those difficulties in their most puzzling form; and that cannot be done unless the difficulties are freely stated and put in the most favourable light they admit of. The Catholic Church has its own way of dealing with this embarrassing problem. It makes a broad separation between those who may be permitted to accept its doctrines on conviction and those who must accept them on trust. Neither group is allowed any choice about what to accept; but the clergy, at least those who can be fully trusted, may permissibly and even meritoriously make themselves acquainted with their opponents' arguments in order to answer them, and may therefore read heretical books. The laity may not, except by special permission, which is hard to get. This discipline recognises that knowing the enemy's case is good for the teachers, but finds a way, consistent with that, of denying it to everybody else — giving the elite more mental cultivation, though not more mental freedom, than it allows the mass. By this device it succeeds in producing the kind of mental superiority its purposes require; for though cultivation without freedom never made a large and liberal mind, it can make a clever *nisi prius* advocate of a cause — a barrister who can argue whatever brief is handed to him. But in countries professing Protestantism this resource is unavailable, since Protestants hold, in theory at least, that responsibility for the choice of a religion must be borne by each person for himself and cannot be shifted onto teachers. Besides, in the present state of the world it is practically impossible to keep writings read by the instructed away from the uninstructed. If the teachers of mankind are to know everything they ought to know, everything must be free to be written and published without restraint.

If, however, the harm done by the absence of free discussion, where received opinions are true, were confined to leaving men ignorant of the grounds of those opinions, it might be thought that this is an intellectual evil and not a moral one, and does not affect the worth of the opinions considered in their influence on character. The fact, though, is that in the absence of discussion it is not only the grounds of the opinion that are forgotten, but too often the meaning of the opinion itself. The words that convey it stop suggesting ideas, or suggest only a small part of what they were originally used to communicate. Instead of a vivid conception and a living belief, all that remains is a few phrases learned by rote — or, if any part of the meaning survives, only the shell and husk of it, the finer essence gone. The great chapter of human history which this fact occupies and fills cannot be too earnestly studied and thought about.

It is illustrated in the experience of almost every ethical doctrine and religious creed. They are all full of meaning and vitality to those who originate them, and to the originators' immediate disciples. Their meaning goes on being felt at undiminished strength, and is perhaps brought out into even fuller consciousness, as long as the struggle lasts to give the doctrine or creed the ascendancy over other creeds. At last it either prevails and becomes the general opinion, or its progress stops: it holds the ground it has gained but spreads no further. Once either result becomes apparent, controversy on the subject flags and gradually dies away. The doctrine has taken its place, if not as a received opinion then as one of the admitted sects or divisions of opinion; those who hold it have generally inherited rather than adopted it; and since conversion from one of these doctrines to another is now exceptional, it occupies little place in the thoughts of those who profess them. Instead of being, as at first, constantly on the alert either to defend themselves against the world or to bring the world round to them, they have subsided into acquiescence, and neither listen if they can help it to arguments against their creed, nor trouble dissenters, if there are any, with arguments for it. From this time can usually be dated the decline in the living power of the doctrine. We often hear the teachers of every creed lamenting how hard it is to keep alive in believers' minds a lively apprehension of the truth they nominally recognise, so that it penetrates their feelings and gains real mastery over their conduct. No such difficulty is complained of while the creed is still fighting for its existence: even the weaker combatants then know and feel what they are fighting for, and how it differs from other doctrines; and in that period of every creed's existence there are quite a few people who have realised its fundamental principles in every form of thought, have weighed and considered them in all their important bearings, and have felt the full effect on character that belief in that creed ought to produce in a mind thoroughly steeped in it. But when it has become a hereditary creed, received passively and not actively — when the mind is no longer compelled, to the same degree as at first, to exercise its vital powers on the questions the belief presents to it — there is a progressive tendency to forget everything about the belief except the formulas, or to give it a dull, torpid assent, as though accepting it on trust dispensed with the need to realise it in consciousness or test it by personal experience; until it almost stops connecting itself with the inner life of the human being at all. Then we see the cases so frequent in this age of the world as to be almost the majority, in which the creed remains as it were outside the mind, encrusting and petrifying it against every other influence addressed to the higher parts of our nature, showing its power by not letting any fresh and living conviction in, but doing nothing itself for the mind or the heart except standing sentinel over them to keep them empty.
