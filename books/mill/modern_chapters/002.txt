Chapter One: Introductory

The subject of this essay is not what is called freedom of the will, so unfortunately set against the misnamed doctrine of philosophical necessity. It is civil, or social, liberty: the nature and the limits of the power that society may legitimately exercise over the individual. It is a question that is seldom stated and hardly ever discussed in general terms, but its unspoken presence shapes the practical controversies of the age, and it is likely soon to be recognised as the vital question of the future. It is so far from being new that in a certain sense it has divided mankind almost from the remotest ages; but at the stage of progress the more civilised part of the species has now reached, it appears under new conditions and needs a different and more fundamental treatment.

The struggle between liberty and authority is the most conspicuous feature of the history we know earliest, particularly the history of Greece, Rome and England. But in those times the contest was between subjects, or some class of subjects, and the government. By liberty people meant protection against the tyranny of their political rulers. The rulers were understood to stand — except in some of the popular governments of Greece — in a necessarily hostile position to the people they ruled. They were a governing One, or a governing tribe or caste, who held their authority by inheritance or conquest, who at any rate did not hold it at the pleasure of the governed, and whose supremacy men did not dare, and perhaps did not want, to contest, whatever precautions they might take against its being used oppressively. Their power was regarded as necessary, but also as extremely dangerous: a weapon they would try to use against their subjects as readily as against foreign enemies. To keep the weaker members of the community from being preyed on by countless vultures, there had to be a bird of prey stronger than the rest, commissioned to hold the others down. But since the king of the vultures would be no less set on preying on the flock than the lesser predators were, it was essential to stay permanently on guard against his beak and claws. So the aim of patriots was to set limits to the power the ruler was allowed to exercise over the community, and that limitation was what they meant by liberty.

They attempted it in two ways. First, by winning recognition of certain immunities, called political liberties or rights, which the ruler was held to be in breach of his duty if he violated, and which, if he did violate them, justified either specific resistance or general rebellion. A second and generally later device was to establish constitutional checks, by which the consent of the community, or of some body supposed to represent its interests, was made a necessary condition of some of the more important acts of the governing power. To the first kind of limitation the ruling power in most European countries was compelled to submit, more or less. It was not so with the second, and to achieve it — or, where it was already partly in place, to achieve it more completely — became everywhere the chief object of the friends of liberty. As long as mankind was content to fight one enemy with another, and to be ruled by a master on condition of being guaranteed more or less effectively against his tyranny, they carried their hopes no further than this.

In time, however, there came a point in human affairs when men stopped thinking it a law of nature that their governors should be an independent power with interests opposed to their own. It seemed much better that the various officers of the state should be their tenants or delegates, removable at their pleasure. That way alone, it seemed, could they be completely secure that the powers of government would never be used to their disadvantage. By degrees this new demand for elected and temporary rulers became the main object of the popular party's efforts wherever such a party existed, and it largely displaced the earlier effort to limit the ruler's power. As the struggle went on to make the ruling power emanate from the periodic choice of the ruled, some people began to think that too much importance had been attached to limiting the power itself. *That* (so it might seem) was a safeguard against rulers whose interests were habitually opposed to the people's. What was wanted now was that the rulers should be identified with the people, that their interest and will should be the nation's interest and will. The nation did not need protection against its own will. There was no danger of its tyrannising over itself. Let the rulers be genuinely answerable to it and promptly removable by it, and it could afford to trust them with power whose use it could dictate for itself. Their power was only the nation's own power, concentrated and put in a convenient form for use. This way of thinking, or rather of feeling, was common among the last generation of European liberals, and on the Continent it apparently still prevails. Anyone there who admits any limit at all on what a government may do — except in the case of governments they think ought not to exist — stands out as a brilliant exception among Continental political thinkers. A similar tone of feeling might by now have prevailed in this country too, if the circumstances that encouraged it for a time had continued unchanged.

But in political and philosophical theories, as in people, success exposes faults and weaknesses that failure might have kept out of sight. The idea that the people have no need to limit their power over themselves might seem self-evident while popular government was only dreamed about, or read about as something that had existed in the distant past. Nor was the idea necessarily disturbed by temporary aberrations like those of the French Revolution, the worst of which were the work of a usurping few, and which in any case belonged not to the permanent working of popular institutions but to a sudden convulsive revolt against monarchical and aristocratic despotism. In time, though, a democratic republic came to occupy a large part of the Earth's surface and made itself felt as one of the most powerful members of the community of nations; and elected, accountable government became subject to the observation and criticism that attend any great existing fact. It was now seen that phrases like "self-government" and "the power of the people over themselves" do not describe the true state of affairs. The "people" who exercise the power are not always the same people as those over whom it is exercised, and the "self-government" people talk about is not each man governing himself but each being governed by all the rest. The will of the people, moreover, means in practice the will of the most numerous or the most active *part* of the people — the majority, or those who succeed in getting themselves accepted as the majority. The people, in other words, *may* want to oppress a section of their own number, and precautions are as much needed against that as against any other abuse of power. Limiting the power of government over individuals therefore loses none of its importance when the holders of power are regularly accountable to the community — that is, to the strongest party in it. This view, which appeals equally to the intelligence of thinkers and to the inclinations of those important classes in European society whose real or supposed interests democracy threatens, has had no trouble establishing itself; and in political thought "the tyranny of the majority" is now generally counted among the evils that society has to guard against.

Like other tyrannies, the tyranny of the majority was at first feared, and is still commonly feared, chiefly as something exercised through the acts of public authorities. But thoughtful people saw that when society is itself the tyrant — society collectively, over the separate individuals who make it up — its means of tyrannising are not limited to what it can do through its political officials. Society can and does carry out its own orders; and if it issues wrong orders instead of right ones, or any orders at all about things it has no business meddling with, it practises a social tyranny more formidable than many kinds of political oppression. It is not usually backed by such extreme penalties, but it leaves fewer ways out, since it penetrates far more deeply into the details of life and enslaves the soul itself. Protection against the tyranny of the magistrate is therefore not enough. There must also be protection against the tyranny of prevailing opinion and feeling: against society's tendency to impose its own ideas and practices as rules of conduct, by means other than legal penalties, on those who dissent from them; to hamper the development, and if possible prevent the formation, of any individuality not in tune with its ways, and to compel every character to shape itself on the model of its own. There is a limit to the legitimate interference of collective opinion with individual independence, and to find that limit and defend it against encroachment is as indispensable to a good state of human affairs as protection against political despotism.

But though nobody is likely to dispute that proposition in general terms, the practical question — where to draw the line, how to make the right adjustment between individual independence and social control — is one on which almost everything remains to be done. Everything that makes life worth living to anyone depends on restraints being enforced on other people's actions. Some rules of conduct must therefore be imposed: by law in the first place, and by opinion in the many matters that are not fit subjects for law. What those rules should be is the chief question in human affairs; and yet, apart from a few of the most obvious cases, it is one of the questions on which least progress has been made. No two ages, and hardly any two countries, have settled it the same way, and one age's or country's decision astonishes another. Yet the people of any given age and country suspect no difficulty in it, as though it were a matter on which mankind had always agreed. The rules that hold good among themselves seem to them self-evident and self-justifying.

This nearly universal illusion is one instance of the magical influence of custom, which is not only, as the proverb says, a second nature, but is constantly mistaken for the first. Custom is all the more effective at preventing any misgiving about the rules of conduct people impose on one another because this is a subject on which nobody generally thinks it necessary to give reasons — either to other people or to himself. People are used to believing, and have been encouraged in the belief by some who aspire to be called philosophers, that on subjects of this kind their feelings are better than reasons and make reasons unnecessary. The practical principle that guides them to their opinions about the regulation of human conduct is the feeling in each person's mind that everybody ought to be required to act as he, and those he sympathises with, would like them to act. Nobody, of course, admits to himself that his standard of judgment is simply what he likes; but an opinion on a point of conduct that is not supported by reasons can only count as one person's preference, and if the reasons, when given, are merely an appeal to a similar preference felt by other people, it is still only many people's liking instead of one man's. To an ordinary man, though, his own preference, backed that way, is not only a perfectly satisfactory reason but generally the only one he has for any of his ideas about morality, taste or propriety that are not expressly written in his religious creed — and it is his chief guide even in interpreting that.

People's opinions about what is praiseworthy or blameworthy are accordingly affected by all the many causes that influence their wishes about other people's behaviour, and those causes are as numerous as the ones that determine their wishes on any other subject. Sometimes their reason; at other times their prejudices or superstitions; often their social feelings, and not seldom their antisocial ones — envy or jealousy, arrogance or contempt; but most commonly their hopes or fears for themselves, their legitimate or illegitimate self-interest. Wherever there is a dominant class, a large part of the country's morality comes from its class interests and its feelings of class superiority. The morality between Spartans and Helots, between planters and the people they enslaved, between princes and subjects, between nobles and commoners, between men and women, has for the most part been the creation of these class interests and feelings; and the sentiments so produced react in turn on the moral feelings of the members of the dominant class in their dealings with one another. Where, on the other hand, a formerly dominant class has lost its ascendancy, or where its ascendancy is unpopular, the prevailing moral sentiments often bear the mark of an impatient dislike of superiority.

Another great determining principle of the rules of conduct, in what people must do and what they must refrain from doing, has been mankind's servility towards the supposed likes and dislikes of their earthly masters, or of their gods. This servility, though essentially selfish, is not hypocrisy; it produces perfectly genuine feelings of abhorrence, and it made men burn magicians and heretics. Among so many baser influences the general and obvious interests of society have of course had a share, and a large one, in shaping moral sentiment — though less as a matter of reason and on their own account than as a consequence of the sympathies and antipathies that grew out of them; and sympathies and antipathies that had little or nothing to do with the interests of society have made themselves felt in establishing moralities with quite as much force.

What society likes and dislikes, or what some powerful section of it likes and dislikes, is thus the main thing that has in practice determined the rules laid down for everyone to observe on pain of law or opinion. And in general, those who have been ahead of society in thought and feeling have left this state of things unchallenged in principle, however much they may have collided with it in particular cases. They have busied themselves with asking what things society ought to like or dislike, rather than with questioning whether its likes and dislikes should be law for individuals. They preferred trying to change mankind's feelings on the particular points where they were themselves heretical, rather than making common cause with heretics in general in defence of freedom.

The one case in which the higher ground has been taken on principle and held consistently, by more than an individual here and there, is religious belief — a case instructive in many ways, and not least as a striking example of the fallibility of what is called the moral sense; for *odium theologicum*, the peculiar hatred a sincere bigot feels for those who believe differently, is one of the least ambiguous cases of moral feeling there is. Those who first broke the yoke of what called itself the Universal Church were in general as unwilling to allow differences of religious opinion as that church itself. But when the heat of the conflict was over without giving any party complete victory, and each church or sect was reduced to hoping it could keep the ground it already held, minorities who saw they had no chance of becoming majorities were obliged to plead with those they could not convert for permission to differ. It is on this battlefield, almost alone, that the rights of the individual against society have been asserted on broad grounds of principle, and society's claim to exercise authority over dissenters openly disputed. The great writers to whom the world owes what religious liberty it has have mostly asserted freedom of conscience as an absolute right, and denied outright that a human being is answerable to others for his religious belief. Yet intolerance about whatever we really care about is so natural to mankind that religious freedom has hardly anywhere been realised in practice, except where religious indifference — which dislikes having its peace disturbed by theological quarrels — has added its weight to the scale. In the minds of almost all religious people, even in the most tolerant countries, the duty of toleration is admitted with silent reservations. One person will put up with dissent about church government but not about doctrine; another can tolerate everybody short of a Catholic or a Unitarian; another, everyone who believes in revealed religion; a few extend their tolerance a little further, but stop at belief in God and a future life. Wherever the feeling of the majority is still genuine and intense, it is found to have given up very little of its claim to be obeyed.

In England, because of the peculiar circumstances of our political history, the yoke of opinion is perhaps heavier than in most other countries of Europe, but the yoke of law is lighter; and there is considerable suspicion of any direct interference by the legislature or the executive with private conduct. This comes not so much from any proper regard for the independence of the individual as from the still surviving habit of looking on the government as representing an interest opposed to the public's. The majority have not yet learned to feel the government's power as their power, or its opinions as their opinions. When they do, individual liberty will probably be as exposed to invasion from the government as it already is from public opinion. As yet, though, there is a good deal of feeling ready to be roused against any attempt by the law to control individuals in matters where they have not been used to being controlled by it — and roused with very little discrimination as to whether the matter does or does not fall within the legitimate sphere of legal control, so that the feeling, salutary as it is on the whole, is perhaps as often misplaced as well founded in the particular cases where it is applied. There is in fact no recognised principle by which the rightness or wrongness of government interference is customarily tested. People decide according to their personal preferences. Some, whenever they see good to be done or an evil to be remedied, would gladly urge the government to take the business on; others would rather put up with almost any amount of social evil than add one more department of human interests to those under government control. And men range themselves on one side or the other in a particular case according to the general direction of their feelings, or according to how much they care about the particular thing the government is being asked to do, or according to whether they believe the government would or would not do it in the way they prefer — but very rarely on account of any consistent opinion about what sorts of things a government is fit to do. And it seems to me that as a result of this absence of any rule or principle, one side is at present as often wrong as the other: government interference is about equally often improperly demanded and improperly condemned.

The object of this essay is to assert one very simple principle, entitled to govern absolutely the dealings of society with the individual by way of compulsion and control, whether the means used are physical force in the shape of legal penalties or the moral coercion of public opinion. The principle is this. The only end for which mankind are warranted, individually or collectively, in interfering with the liberty of action of any of their number is self-protection. The only purpose for which power can rightfully be exercised over any member of a civilised community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others. His own good, physical or moral, is not a sufficient warrant. He cannot rightfully be compelled to do or not do something because it will be better for him, because it will make him happier, or because in other people's opinion it would be wise or even right. These are good reasons for arguing with him, reasoning with him, persuading him, pleading with him — but not for compelling him, or for doing him any harm if he does otherwise. To justify that, the conduct he is to be deterred from must be calculated to produce harm to someone else. The only part of anyone's conduct for which he is answerable to society is the part that concerns others. In the part that merely concerns himself, his independence is, by right, absolute. Over himself, over his own body and mind, the individual is sovereign.

It is hardly necessary, perhaps, to say that this doctrine is meant to apply only to human beings in the maturity of their faculties. We are not talking about children, or about young people below the age the law may fix as adulthood. Those who are still in a state that requires them to be taken care of by others must be protected against their own actions as well as against injury from outside. For the same reason we may leave out of account those backward states of society in which the race itself may be regarded as in its infancy. The early obstacles to spontaneous progress are so great that there is seldom any choice of means for overcoming them, and a ruler full of the spirit of improvement is warranted in using any expedient that will attain an end otherwise perhaps unattainable. Despotism is a legitimate mode of government in dealing with barbarians, provided the end is their improvement and the means are justified by actually achieving it. Liberty, as a principle, has no application to any state of things before the time when mankind have become capable of being improved by free and equal discussion. Until then there is nothing for them but implicit obedience to an Akbar or a Charlemagne, if they are lucky enough to find one. But as soon as mankind have become capable of being guided to their own improvement by conviction or persuasion — a stage long since reached in all the nations we need concern ourselves with here — compulsion, whether direct or in the form of pains and penalties for non-compliance, is no longer admissible as a means to their own good, and is justifiable only for the security of others.

I should state that I forgo any advantage my argument could draw from the idea of abstract right as something independent of utility. I regard utility as the ultimate appeal on all ethical questions — utility, that is, in the largest sense, grounded on the permanent interests of a human being as a progressive creature. Those interests, I maintain, authorise subjecting individual spontaneity to external control only in respect of the actions of each that concern other people's interests. If anyone does something harmful to others, there is a prima facie case for punishing him, by law or, where legal penalties cannot safely be applied, by general disapproval. There are also many positive acts for the benefit of others that he may rightfully be compelled to perform: to give evidence in court; to bear his fair share in the common defence, or in any other joint work necessary to the society whose protection he enjoys; and to perform certain acts of individual kindness, such as saving a fellow creature's life, or stepping in to protect the defenceless against ill-treatment — things which, whenever it is obviously a man's duty to do them, he may rightfully be held answerable to society for not doing. A person may do harm to others not only by his actions but by his inaction, and in either case he is justly accountable to them for the injury. The second case, it is true, calls for a much more cautious use of compulsion than the first. Holding someone answerable for doing harm to others is the rule; holding him answerable for not preventing harm is, comparatively speaking, the exception. Yet there are plenty of cases clear enough and serious enough to justify the exception. In everything that concerns his external relations, the individual is by right answerable to those whose interests are involved, and if need be to society as their protector. There are often good reasons for not holding him to that responsibility, but they must arise from the special circumstances of the case: either because it is the kind of case in which he is on the whole likely to act better when left to his own discretion than when controlled in any way society has it in its power to control him, or because the attempt to control him would produce other evils greater than the ones it would prevent. Where reasons like these rule out enforcing responsibility, the agent's own conscience should step into the vacant judgment seat and protect the interests of others that have no external protection, judging himself all the more strictly because the case does not allow him to be made accountable to the judgment of his fellow creatures.

But there is a sphere of action in which society, as distinct from the individual, has at most an indirect interest: the whole of that part of a person's life and conduct which affects only himself, or which, if it affects others, does so only with their free, voluntary and undeceived consent and participation. When I say only himself, I mean directly and in the first instance; for whatever affects a man may affect others *through* him, and the objection that might be based on that possibility will be considered later. This, then, is the proper region of human liberty. It comprises, first, the inward domain of consciousness: liberty of conscience in the most comprehensive sense, liberty of thought and feeling, absolute freedom of opinion and sentiment on all subjects, practical or speculative, scientific, moral or theological. The liberty of expressing and publishing opinions may seem to fall under a different principle, since it belongs to the part of a person's conduct that concerns other people; but as it is almost as important as liberty of thought itself, and rests largely on the same reasons, it is inseparable from it in practice. Second, the principle requires liberty of tastes and pursuits: of framing the plan of our life to suit our own character, of doing as we like and taking the consequences, without being obstructed by our fellow creatures so long as what we do does no harm to them, even if they think our conduct foolish, perverse or wrong. Third, from this liberty of each individual follows, within the same limits, the liberty of individuals to combine: freedom to unite for any purpose that does no harm to others, the people combining being supposed to be adults, and neither forced nor deceived.

No society in which these liberties are not on the whole respected is free, whatever its form of government; and none is completely free in which they do not exist absolute and unqualified. The only freedom which deserves the name is that of pursuing our own good in our own way, so long as we do not try to deprive others of theirs, or obstruct their efforts to obtain it. Each is the proper guardian of his own health, whether bodily or mental and spiritual. Mankind gain more by letting each other live as seems good to themselves than by compelling each to live as seems good to the rest.

Though this doctrine is anything but new, and may strike some people as a truism, there is no doctrine that stands more directly opposed to the general tendency of existing opinion and practice. Society has spent quite as much effort in trying (according to its lights) to compel people to conform to its notions of personal excellence as of social excellence. The ancient commonwealths thought themselves entitled to regulate every part of private conduct by public authority, and the ancient philosophers approved, on the ground that the state had a deep interest in the whole bodily and mental discipline of every one of its citizens. That way of thinking may have been defensible in small republics surrounded by powerful enemies, in constant danger of being overthrown by foreign attack or internal upheaval, and to which even a short spell of relaxed energy and self-command might so easily be fatal that they could not afford to wait for the wholesome long-term effects of freedom. In the modern world, the greater size of political communities and above all the separation of spiritual from temporal authority — which put the direction of men's consciences in other hands than those that controlled their worldly affairs — prevented so great an interference by law in the details of private life. But the machinery of moral repression has been driven harder against departures from the reigning opinion in self-regarding matters than even in social ones; religion, the most powerful of the elements that have gone into the making of moral feeling, having almost always been governed either by the ambition of a hierarchy seeking control over every department of human conduct, or by the spirit of Puritanism. And some of the modern reformers who have set themselves most strongly against the religions of the past have been no way behind either churches or sects in asserting the right of spiritual domination — Monsieur Comte in particular, whose social system, as unfolded in his *Traité de Politique Positive*, aims at establishing (though by moral rather than legal machinery) a despotism of society over the individual surpassing anything contemplated in the political ideal of the strictest disciplinarian among the ancient philosophers.

Quite apart from the peculiar doctrines of individual thinkers, there is in the world at large a growing inclination to stretch the powers of society over the individual unduly, both by the force of opinion and even by that of legislation. And since the tendency of all the changes taking place in the world is to strengthen society and diminish the power of the individual, this encroachment is not one of the evils that tend to disappear of themselves — on the contrary, it grows steadily more formidable. Mankind's disposition, whether as rulers or as fellow citizens, to impose their own opinions and inclinations as a rule of conduct on others is so energetically supported by some of the best and some of the worst feelings human nature is subject to, that it is hardly ever kept in check by anything but lack of power; and as the power is not declining but growing, we must expect, in the present state of the world, to see the mischief increase, unless a strong barrier of moral conviction can be raised against it.

It will suit the argument if, instead of entering at once on the general thesis, we confine ourselves in the first place to a single branch of it, on which the principle stated here is already recognised by current opinion, if not fully, then up to a point. That branch is liberty of thought — from which it is impossible to separate the related liberty of speaking and writing. Although these liberties do to a considerable extent form part of the political morality of every country that professes religious toleration and free institutions, the grounds they rest on, both philosophical and practical, are perhaps not as familiar to the general mind, nor as thoroughly appreciated even by many leaders of opinion, as one might have expected. Those grounds, when properly understood, apply much more widely than to this one division of the subject, and a thorough consideration of this part of the question will prove the best introduction to the rest. Those to whom nothing I am about to say will be new may therefore excuse me, I hope, if on a subject that has now been discussed so often for three centuries I venture on one discussion more.
