José Raúl Capablanca (1888–1942) was a Cuban who learned chess as a small child by watching his father play, and who became one of the strongest players who have ever lived. He wrote Chess Fundamentals in 1921, the year he won the world championship from Emanuel Lasker in Havana. For a world champion to sit down and write a book for beginners was unusual, and the book shows why he thought it worth doing.

It starts with the ending, not the opening. The first pages teach how to mate with a king and a rook against a lone king, then with two bishops, then with a queen; then how a pawn becomes a queen; and only after that the middle game and the openings. Capablanca's argument is that the ending is where the value of each piece and the meaning of each principle can be seen plainly, without the clutter of a full board. The book then goes round the same circuit again at a higher level, and ends with fourteen of his own games, annotated move by move. He includes games he lost, and says where he went wrong.

The notation will look strange. It is the English descriptive notation that was standard for most of the twentieth century. Each file is named after the piece that starts on it (the Queen's Rook's file, the King's Knight's file, and so on), and each rank is counted from the player's own side of the board. So the same square has one name for White and another for Black, and "P - K 4" means a pawn moves to the fourth rank of the king's file, counted from whoever is moving. "Kt" is a knight, "×" is a capture, and "ch" is check. Modern books use algebraic notation, which names each square once. In a later printing Capablanca added a preface, dated 1934, dismissing the "Hypermodern" school that had become fashionable since the book appeared.

This is a restored edition, and Capablanca's words and notation are unchanged. The text is the Project Gutenberg transcription of the book in its 1934 state, with that preface, compared word by word with two scanned copies of the same printing. What this edition adds is for the reader who cannot see the diagrams. Every one of the 150 positions now has a caption that lists the pieces square by square in algebraic notation, and each was checked against the moves the text plays from it. The six page references in the text now point to sections and games instead. Move tables are set one move to a line. The edition also adds modern typesetting, an ebook, and this introduction.
