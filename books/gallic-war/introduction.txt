Julius Caesar, born around 100 BC, was consul at Rome in 59 BC and took up a command in Gaul the following year. He held it for nine years. This is his own account of what he did with it, one book per campaigning season, covering 58 to 52 BC: the migration of the Helvetii, the defeat of the German king Ariovistus, the Belgic and Aquitanian campaigns, a bridge thrown across the Rhine in ten days and taken down again, two crossings to Britain, and the revolt of Vercingetorix that ended at Alesia. An eighth book, by his officer Aulus Hirtius, was added after Caesar's death to finish the story; it is not by Caesar and is not in this edition.

He writes about himself in the third person, always as Caesar, never as I. The effect is of a plain, impersonal record of events, and that effect is the point. These were dispatches, written by a serving general and sent home to a city where his enemies were arguing that his command should be taken away from him and that he should be prosecuted the moment it ended. The book is a military report and a piece of political self-presentation at the same time. It kept his name in front of the Roman public for nine years, and the legions he raised and trained in Gaul are the army he led across the Rubicon in 49 BC.

So the reader should keep two things in view. Caesar is always the reasonable party, forced into every war he starts, and generous after winning it. And the campaigns he is describing are a conquest: towns stormed and their populations killed or sold, whole peoples reported as destroyed, hostages taken, a rebel chieftain flogged and beheaded, a tribe that had crossed the Rhine wiped out including its women and children. He reports all of it himself, flatly, as administration. Nothing here is softened. The peoples on the other side of it left no written account of their own, and most of what is known about the Gauls and the Germans of this period comes from these seven books, written by the man who was conquering them.

They are also a good read, which is why they have been a Latin schoolroom text for centuries. Caesar is exact about terrain, supply and timing, and he is very good at the moment a plan fails. Book Five, where an ambush destroys fifteen cohorts in a wooded valley, is told without excuses. Book Seven, the siege of Alesia, has him building two rings of fortifications at once, one facing the town and one facing the relief army behind him.

This edition is not translated from the Latin. It is a modernization of the English translation made by W. A. McDevitte and W. S. Bohn in 1869, complete, all seven books.
