Canto Fourteen

	Because love for the place where I was born
	moved me, I gathered the scattered leaves
	and gave them back to the one who was already hoarse.

	Then we came to the boundary where
	the second ring parts from the third, and where
	a horrible art of justice can be seen.

	To make these new things properly plain,
	I say that we arrived at a plain
	that pushes every plant off its bed.

	The sorrowful wood makes a garland round it,
	as the sad ditch does round the wood;
	we stopped our steps right at the very edge.

	The floor was a dry, deep sand,
	made in no other fashion than the one
	that was once trodden by Cato's feet.

	O vengeance of God, how much you ought
	to be feared by everyone who reads
	what was made plain to my eyes!

	I saw many flocks of naked souls,
	all of them weeping very miserably,
	and a different law seemed set on each.

	Some of the people lay flat on the ground;
	some were sitting all hunched up;
	and others were walking continually.

	Those going about were the most numerous,
	and those lying down in the torment the fewest,
	but they had their tongues loosest in their grief.

	Over the whole sand, falling slowly,
	broad flakes of fire were raining down,
	like snow in the mountains when there is no wind.

	As Alexander, in those hot parts of India,
	saw flames fall on his army
	and reach the ground still whole,

	so that he saw to it that the ground was trampled
	by his troops, because the vapour
	was better put out while it was still on its own:

	so the eternal burning came down;
	and the sand caught fire from it, like tinder
	under a flint, to double the pain.

	The dance of the wretched hands
	was never at rest, beating off from themselves
	now here, now there, the fresh burning.

	I began: "Master, you who overcome
	everything except the hard demons
	who came out against us at the entrance of the gate,

	who is that big one who seems not to care
	about the fire, and lies there contemptuous and twisted,
	so that the rain does not seem to torment him?"

	And that same one, who had noticed
	that I was asking my guide about him,
	shouted: "What I was alive, I am dead.

	Let Jove tire out his blacksmith, from whom
	in his anger he took the sharp thunderbolt
	with which I was struck on my last day;

	and let him tire out the others, shift after shift,
	in Mongibello at the black forge,
	calling 'Good Vulcan, help, help!'

	as he did at the battle of Phlegra,
	and let him shoot at me with all his strength:
	he would not have a happy revenge out of it."

	Then my guide spoke with such force
	that I had never heard him so loud:
	"O Capaneus, in that your pride

	is not put out, you are punished the more;
	no torment except your own rage
	would be a pain to match your fury."

	Then he turned to me with a better face,
	saying: "That was one of the seven kings
	who besieged Thebes; and he held, and it seems he still holds,

	God in contempt, and seems to value him little;
	but, as I told him, his own scorn
	is a very fitting decoration for his chest.

	Now come behind me, and take care not to set
	your feet, as yet, on the burning sand;
	but always keep them close in to the wood."

	In silence we came to where there spurts
	out of the wood a little stream,
	whose redness still makes my flesh crawl.

	As a rivulet comes out of the Bulicame
	that the sinful women share out among themselves,
	so this one went down across the sand.

	Its bed, and both its slopes,
	were made of stone, and the margins at the sides;
	and from that I understood the crossing was there.

	"Among everything else I have shown you
	since we came in through the gate
	whose threshold is refused to nobody,

	nothing has been made out by your eyes
	as notable as the present stream,
	which puts out all the little flames above it."

	These were my guide's words;
	at which I begged him to grant me the food
	of which he had granted me the appetite.

	"In the middle of the sea there sits a wasted country,"
	he said then, "which is called Crete,
	under whose king the world was once chaste.

	There is a mountain there that was once glad
	with water and with leaves, and was called Ida;
	now it is deserted like a thing gone stale.

	Rhea once chose it as the trusted cradle
	of her son, and to hide him better
	she had shouting made there when he cried.

	Inside the mountain a great old man stands upright,
	who keeps his shoulders turned towards Damietta
	and looks at Rome as if it were his mirror.

	His head is formed of fine gold,
	and his arms and his chest are pure silver,
	then he is copper as far as the fork;

	from there down he is all choice iron,
	except that his right foot is baked clay;
	and he stands on that one more than on the other.

	Every part except the gold is broken
	by a crack that drips tears,
	which, gathered together, bore through that cave.

	Their course tumbles down into this valley;
	they make Acheron, Styx and Phlegethon;
	then they go down by this narrow channel

	until, where there is no more going down,
	they make Cocytus; and what that pool is like
	you will see for yourself, so it is not told here."

	And I to him: "If the present stream
	runs down like that from our world,
	why does it only appear to us at this rim?"

	And he to me: "You know the place is round;
	and although you have come a long way,
	always to the left, going down towards the bottom,

	you have not yet turned through the whole circle;
	so, if something new appears to us,
	it should not bring astonishment to your face."

	And I again: "Master, where are Phlegethon
	and Lethe to be found? You say nothing of the one,
	and say the other is made out of this rain."

	"You please me in all your questions, certainly,"
	he answered, "but the boiling of the red water
	ought to have settled one of the two you ask.

	You will see Lethe, but outside this pit,
	there where the souls go to wash themselves
	when their guilt is repented and taken away."

	Then he said: "Now it is time to draw away
	from the wood; see that you come behind me:
	the margins make a road, because they are not burnt,

	and above them every falling flame is put out."
