Canto One

	Midway through the journey of our life
	I found myself in a dark wood,
	for the straight way had been lost.

	How hard it is to say what that wood was —
	wild, rough, relentless —
	the very thought of it brings the fear back!

	It is so bitter that death is hardly worse;
	but to tell of the good I found there
	I will tell the other things I saw.

	I cannot rightly say how I came to be in it,
	I was so full of sleep at the moment
	when I left the true road.

	But when I had come to the foot of a hill,
	there where the valley ended
	that had pierced my heart with fear,

	I looked up and saw its shoulders
	already wearing the rays of the planet
	that leads us straight on every road.

	Then the fear grew a little quieter
	that had lasted in the lake of my heart
	through the night I spent in such misery.

	And as a man with labouring breath,
	having escaped the deep water to the shore,
	turns back to the dangerous water and stares,

	so my mind, which was still running,
	turned back to look again at the pass
	that has never yet let anyone through alive.

	After I had rested my tired body a little
	I took the empty slope again,
	so that my firm foot was always the lower.

	And there, almost at the start of the climb,
	was a leopard, quick and very light,
	covered in a spotted hide;

	and she would not go from in front of my face —
	she blocked my way so completely
	that I turned again and again to go back.

	It was the beginning of the morning,
	and the sun was climbing with the stars
	that were with him when divine love

	first set those beautiful things in motion;
	so the beast with her bright hide
	gave me good reason to hope,

	and so did the hour and the sweet season;
	but not enough to keep me from fear
	when a lion appeared in front of me.

	He seemed to be coming straight at me,
	his head high, in a raging hunger,
	so that the air itself seemed to tremble.

	And a she-wolf, who in all her leanness
	seemed loaded with every craving,
	and has made many people live in misery —

	she pressed so much weight on me
	with the fear that came out of her look
	that I lost all hope of the height.

	And as a man who is glad to be gaining,
	when the time comes that makes him lose,
	weeps and grieves in all his thoughts,

	so the restless beast made me:
	coming against me, little by little,
	she drove me back to where the sun is silent.

	While I was falling back to that low place
	someone appeared before my eyes
	who seemed faint from a long silence.

	When I saw him in that great wilderness
	I cried out to him, "Have pity on me,
	whatever you are — a shadow, or a living man!"

	He answered: "Not a man; I was a man once.
	My parents were Lombards,
	both of them Mantuans by birth.

	I was born under Julius, though late in his time,
	and I lived in Rome under the good Augustus,
	in the days of the false and lying gods.

	I was a poet, and I sang of that just
	son of Anchises who came from Troy
	after proud Ilion was burned.

	But why are you going back to so much trouble?
	Why do you not climb the delightful mountain
	that is the beginning and the cause of all joy?"

	"Then are you that Virgil, that spring
	that pours out so broad a river of speech?"
	I answered him, my forehead full of shame.

	"O light and honour of all the other poets,
	let my long study count for me, and the great love
	that made me search your book.

	You are my master and my author.
	You alone are the one I took from
	the beautiful style that has done me honour.

	Look at the beast that turned me back;
	help me against her, famous sage —
	she makes my veins and my pulses shake."

	"You will have to go by another road,"
	he answered, when he saw me weeping,
	"if you want to get out of this wild place;

	because this beast you are crying out about
	lets nobody pass along her way,
	but blocks him so far that she kills him;

	and her nature is so vicious and so evil
	that her greedy appetite is never filled,
	and after feeding she is hungrier than before.

	Many are the animals she mates with,
	and there will be more, until the Hound
	comes, and puts her to death in pain.

	He will not feed on land or money
	but on wisdom, love and courage,
	and he will be born between felt and felt.

	He will be the salvation of that low Italy
	for which the virgin Camilla died,
	and Euryalus, and Turnus, and Nisus, of their wounds.

	He will hunt her through every town
	until he has put her back into Hell,
	out of which envy first let her loose.

	So for your own good I think and judge
	that you should follow me; I will be your guide,
	and take you out of here through an eternal place,

	where you will hear the screaming of despair
	and see the ancient spirits in their pain,
	every one of them crying for the second death;

	and you will see the ones who are content
	in the fire, because they hope to come,
	whenever it may be, among the blessed.

	And if you want to climb up to them after that,
	there will be a soul worthier than I am for it:
	I will leave you with her when I go;

	because that Emperor who reigns up there,
	since I was a rebel against His law,
	does not want anyone brought to His city by me.

	He governs everywhere and rules there;
	there is His city and His high seat:
	how happy is the man He chooses for it!"

	And I said to him: "Poet, I beg you
	by that God you never knew,
	so that I may escape this evil and worse,

	take me where you have just said,
	so that I may see Saint Peter's gate
	and the ones you make so sorrowful."

	Then he set out, and I kept close behind him.
