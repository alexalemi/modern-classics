Theophrastus came from Eresus, on the island of Lesbos, studied under Aristotle, and took over the Lyceum when Aristotle left Athens in 322 BC. He ran it for the rest of his life. Most of his work is scientific — the two surviving books on plants are the foundation of botany, and he also wrote on stones, on fire, on winds, on smells, and on why people sweat. The Characters, usually dated around 319 BC, is the one book of his that anybody reads for pleasure.

It is thirty short comic sketches of types of annoying men. Each one opens with a mock-solemn definition and then piles up specific behaviour until the man is completely visible: the flatterer, the coward, the bore, the man who is proud of being cheap, the man who has heard some news, the superstitious man whose day is governed by omens, the man who says the wrong thing at every possible moment. The engine is accumulation. Nothing is explained, nothing is moralized, and each sketch simply stops when it has done enough damage.

The details are Athenian and have been kept that way — the marketplace, the baths, the sacrifices, the lawsuits, the theatre, the dinner parties, the slaves. The people are not. The coward who discovers he has forgotten his sword, smears himself with somebody else's blood and comes back announcing that he saved a comrade is a man anyone would recognize. So is the flatterer picking lint off his patron's cloak. That gap, between an ancient world and a wholly familiar person inside it, is most of the comedy, which is why the examples were not transplanted to an office.

Everything after it in this line descends from it. La Bruyère translated the book into French in 1688, published his own imitations alongside, and made his reputation on them, and the character sketch has been standard equipment for satirists ever since.

It is the shortest and the easiest book of the three that run together here: Plato's Dialogues, Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics, and this. Read a few sketches and put it down; it does not build to anything. The short dedication at the front, in which the author says he is ninety-nine years old, was very likely written by somebody else and added later, but it has been printed with the book for a long time and it is printed here.
