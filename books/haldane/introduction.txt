J. B. S. Haldane (1892–1964) was a British biologist, one of the founders of population genetics, the mathematics of how the traits of living things change over generations. He was the son of the physiologist John Scott Haldane and grew up helping with his father's experiments on breathing, often as the subject. He fought in the First World War, taught at Cambridge and later in London, and was famous for testing things on himself, from bad air in submarines to doses of acid. He became a Marxist in the 1930s, and in 1957 he moved to India, where he took Indian citizenship and died.

He also wrote for general readers, and this is his best-known essay. It first appeared in Harper's Magazine in March 1926, and he collected it the next year in Possible Worlds and Other Essays. Its subject is a single idea, sometimes called the square-cube law, which Galileo had noticed three centuries earlier. If you make an animal ten times longer, ten times wider and ten times taller, its weight goes up a thousand times but the cross-section of its bones, and the surface of its skin, lungs and gut, only a hundred times. So size is not a detail. It decides whether a creature can fall safely, whether a wet insect can climb out of a drop of water, why large animals need lungs and hearts and small ones do not, and why a giant man sixty feet high would break his thighs at every step. In the last paragraphs Haldane turns the same reasoning on human societies, and asks what size a working democracy, or a socialist state, can be.

The science has worn well. A few details have not: the figures are rounded, the natural history is that of the 1920s, and the closing remarks on politics belong to their decade.

The version of this essay most often reproduced today differs in places from the one printed in 1927, most visibly in the famous passage about falling, where later copies say that a rat is killed and a man is broken, and the 1927 book says that a rat would probably be killed, though it can fall safely from the eleventh story of a building, and that a man is killed. This is a restored edition: Haldane's words as printed in Possible Worlds (London, Chatto & Windus, 1927), checked against page images of that book, unchanged. The essay is in the public domain in the United States; in countries where copyright lasts seventy years after the author's death, it remains in copyright until 2035.
