Chapter 2: Fortune Speaks in Her Own Defence

"Now I should like to argue with you a little in Fortune's own words. You judge whether her case is a fair one.

"'Man,' she might say, 'why do you come after me every day with your complaints? What harm have I done you? What goods of yours have I taken away? Pick any judge you like and let us argue in front of him about who rightfully owns wealth and rank. If you can show me that any one of these things really belongs to a mortal man, I will freely grant that the things you claim are yours.

"'When nature brought you out of your mother's womb, I took you up — naked and with nothing, as you were — I fed you out of my own substance, and, being partial to you, I brought you up rather too indulgently; and that is exactly what makes you rebellious against me now. I surrounded you with a royal abundance of everything in my power. Now it is my pleasure to draw my hand back. You have reason to thank me for the use of what was not yours; you have no right to complain, as though you had lost something entirely your own.

"'So why are you groaning? I have done you no violence. Wealth, honours and all such things are under my control. They are my servants; they know their mistress; they come with me and they leave when I leave. I would go so far as to say that if the things whose loss you are lamenting had been yours, you could never have lost them at all.

"'Am I alone to be forbidden to do what I like with my own? Nobody scolds the sky when it shows the brightness of day at one hour and wraps the daylight in the darkness of night at another; the year may garland the face of the earth with flowers and fruit and then disfigure it with storms and cold. The sea is allowed to invite you with a smooth calm surface today and to roughen tomorrow with waves and gales. Is man's insatiable greed to tie *me* down to a constancy that is not in my character?

"'This is my art; this is the game I never stop playing. I turn the wheel that spins. I enjoy watching the high come down and the low go up. Climb up if you want to — but only on the condition that you will not think it an outrage to come down when the rules of my game require it.

"'Did you not know what I was like? Did you not know how Croesus, king of the Lydians, who had once been a rival Cyrus feared, was afterwards pitifully handed over to the flames of the pyre and only saved by a shower of rain sent from heaven? Have you forgotten how Paullus wept honest tears over the misfortunes of King Perseus, his own prisoner? What else do the tragedies wail over, if not kingdoms brought down by Fortune's indiscriminate blows? Did you not learn as a child how there stand at the threshold of Zeus two jars, one full of blessings and the other of disasters? What if you have drawn rather generously from the good one? What if even now I have not left you altogether? What if this very changeableness of mine is a fair reason for hoping for something better?

"'But listen now, and stop letting your heart eat itself away with fretting, and do not expect to live on your own terms in a kingdom that belongs to everybody.'"

Song II: Man's Covetousness

	Suppose Plenty poured out her gifts
		with an unstinting hand,
	as many as the stars are,
		countless as the sand:
	would the human race be satisfied
	and stop its grumbling and complaint?
	No — though God in his generosity
		gave gold for the asking,
	and honours and rank and fame, we would be
		not one step nearer content;
	an all-devouring greed
	gapes with a need that only widens.
	Then what limits could ever hold back
		this wild appetite for having,
	when every fresh gift it is fed
		makes the frantic craving grow?
	No man is rich whose fear
	sees grim Want always at the door.
