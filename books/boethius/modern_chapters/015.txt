Chapter 1: The Promise to Lead Him to True Happiness

She stopped; but I stood fixed by the sweetness of the song, wondering and eager, my ears still straining to listen. And then after a moment I said: "You supreme comfort of the exhausted mind, what refreshment you have brought me, as much by the sweetness of your singing as by the weight of your argument! I really do not think I shall be unequal to Fortune's blows in future. So I no longer dread the remedies you said were a little too severe for my strength; on the contrary, I am eager to hear about them and I ask for them urgently."

Then she said: "I noticed you fastening on my words silently and intently, and I was expecting this state of mind in you — or, to put it more truthfully, I brought it about myself. What is left is the sort of thing that bites on the tongue and turns sweet once it is inside. But since you say you are keen to hear it, how you would burn to, if you could see where it is my job to lead you!"

"Where?" I said.

"To true happiness," she said, "which your mind sees even now in its dreams but cannot look at properly, while your eyes are taken up with appearances."

Then I said: "Please, show me its true shape, and do not lose a moment."

"Gladly, for your sake," she said. "But first I shall try to sketch in words, and describe, something you are more familiar with; so that when you have examined it carefully you can turn your eyes the other way and recognise the beauty of real happiness."

Song I: The Thorns of Error

	The man who wants to sow the fallow field
		and watch the corn come up
	must first get rid of the useless weeds,
		the bramble and the thorn.
	After a foul taste, honey
		is sweeter in the mouth;
	after the storm, the twinkling stars
		are a more cheerful sight.
	When the night has gone, the bright dawn comes
		in her rose-coloured chariot.
	So drive the false happiness out of your mind
		and you will see the true one.
