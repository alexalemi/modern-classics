Chapter 5: Why the Distribution Looks Like Chance

At this I said: "I see how there is a happiness and a misery founded on what the righteous and the wicked actually deserve. Even so, I keep wondering whether there is not some good and evil in fortune as ordinary people understand it. Surely no sensible man would rather be exiled, poor and disgraced, than live prosperously in his own country, powerful, wealthy and high in honour. Indeed, the work of wisdom shows more clearly in its working when the happiness of rulers is passed on somehow to the people around them — especially considering that prison, and the law, and the other pains of legal punishment are properly owed only to mischievous citizens, on whose account they were set up in the first place. And so I am extremely puzzled about why all this is completely reversed: why the good are harried with the penalties that crime deserves and the bad carry off the rewards of virtue. I am longing to hear from you what reason there could be for such an unjust state of disorder. I should certainly wonder less if I could believe that everything was the confused result of chance. But as it is, my belief that God governs the world adds one amazement to another. For seeing that He sometimes assigns good fortune to the good and harsh fortune to the bad, and then again deals harshly with the good and gives the bad their hearts' desire, how does this differ from chance, unless some reason is found for it all?"

"There is nothing surprising in it," she said, "if everything is thought random and confused when the principle of order is not known. And although you do not know the causes this great system depends on, still, since a good ruler governs the world, do not doubt for your part that everything is rightly done."

Song V: Wonder and Ignorance

	Anyone who does not know how close to the pole
		Bootes runs his course
	must wonder what heavenly law
		moves his Wagon so slowly,
	why he plunges so late beneath the sea
	and lights his beams again so quickly.
	When the full-orbed moon grows pale
		in the middle of the night,
	and suddenly the stars come out
		that were faint in her light,
	whole nations stand and stare, astounded,
	and beat the air in wild bewilderment.
	Nobody wonders why the storm-driven breakers
		pound on the shore,
	or why the frost-locked glaciers melt
		in the fierce heat of summer;
	for here the cause seems plain and clear,
	and it is only what is dark and hidden that we fear.
	Feeble-minded folly magnifies
		everything rare and strange,
	and the dull herd is overwhelmed with awe
		at any unexpected change.
	But wonder goes out of an enlightened mind
	as soon as ignorance stops blinding it.
