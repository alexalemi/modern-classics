Chapter 3: The Problem: Foreknowledge Seems to Destroy Freedom

Then I said: "But now I am perplexed all over again by a still harder problem."

"And what is that?" she said. "Though in fact I can guess what is troubling you."

"It seems," I said, "too much of a paradox and a contradiction that God should know all things and yet there should be free will. For if God foresees everything, and can in no way be deceived, then whatever providence foresees as going to happen must necessarily happen. So if from eternity He foreknows not only what men will do but also their designs and purposes, there can be no freedom of the will, since nothing can be done and no purpose of any kind can be formed except the ones a divine providence incapable of being deceived has seen beforehand. For if events could be turned aside to some end other than the one providence foresaw, then there would be no sure foreknowledge of the future but only uncertain guesswork instead — and to think that of God I consider impious.

"What is more, I do not accept the reasoning by which some people think this puzzle can be solved. They say it is not because God has foreseen an event coming that it is *therefore* sure to happen, but conversely that because something is going to happen it cannot be hidden from divine providence — and so the necessity passes over to the other side, and it is not that what is foreseen must necessarily happen, but that what is going to happen must necessarily be foreseen. But this is just as if the question in debate were which is cause and which effect: whether the foreknowledge of the future is the cause of the necessity, or the necessity of the future the cause of the foreknowledge. And we need not take any trouble to show that whichever way round the causal sequence runs, the occurrence of things foreseen is necessary, even though the foreknowledge of future events does not by itself impose the necessity of occurring on them. For example, if a man is sitting, the statement that he is sitting is necessarily true; and conversely, if the statement that he is sitting is true because he really is sitting, then he must necessarily be sitting. So either way some necessity is involved: in the second case the necessity of the fact, in the first the necessity of the statement's truth. But in both cases the man is not sitting because the opinion is true; rather the opinion is true because he was already sitting as a matter of fact. So although the cause of the opinion's truth comes from the one side, there is a necessity on both sides alike. Obviously we can reason in the same way about providence and the future. Even if future events are foreseen because they are going to happen, and do not happen because they are foreseen, there is still a necessity all the same — a necessity both that they should be foreseen by God as going to happen, and that when they are foreseen they should happen. And that is enough to destroy free will. However, it is preposterous to speak of the occurrence of events in time as the cause of eternal foreknowledge. And yet if we believe that God foresees future events because they are going to happen, what is that but to think that the occurrence of events is the cause of His supreme providence? Further, just as when I *know* that something is the case that thing *necessarily* is the case, so when I know that something will be, it will *necessarily* be. It follows, then, that things foreknown happen inevitably.

"Lastly, to think of a thing as being in any way other than it is, is not merely not knowledge; it is a false opinion, a long way from the truth of knowledge. So if something is going to be, and yet its occurrence is not certain and necessary, how can anyone foreknow that it will occur? For just as knowledge itself has no admixture of falsity, so any conception drawn from knowledge cannot be other than as it is conceived. This is exactly why knowledge is free of falsehood: because each thing must necessarily correspond exactly to the knowledge that grasps its nature. In what way, then, are we to suppose that God foreknows these uncertainties as going to happen? For if He thinks of events that may possibly not happen at all as inevitably bound to happen, He is deceived; and that is something it is impious not only to believe but even to put into words. If, on the other hand, He sees them in the future as they are, in the sense of knowing that they may equally happen or not, what sort of foreknowledge is this, that takes in nothing certain and nothing fixed? How is it any better than the absurd prophecy of Teiresias?

"'Whatever I say will either happen — or not.'

In that case, too, how would divine providence be superior to human opinion, if it holds things uncertain whose occurrence is uncertain, exactly as men do? But if no shadow of uncertainty can possibly be found at that perfectly sure Fountainhead of all things, then the occurrence of the things He has surely foreknown as coming is certain. And so there can be no freedom in human actions and designs; the divine mind, which foresees all things without any possibility of mistake, ties them down and binds them to one outcome only. But once that is admitted, what an overturning of human affairs plainly follows! Rewards and punishments are proposed for the good and the bad in vain, since no free and voluntary movement of the will has deserved either; indeed the punishment of the wicked and the reward of the righteous, which is now thought the perfection of justice, will look like the most flagrant injustice, since men are settled one way or the other not by any choice of their own but by the necessity of what is bound to be. And so neither virtue nor vice is anything at all, but deserving well and deserving badly are jumbled together with no distinction between them. And more: since the whole course of events is derived from providence, and nothing is left free to human design, it turns out that our vices too are referred back to the Author of all good — and no more abominable thought could possibly be conceived. Again, no ground is left for hope or prayer. For how can we hope for blessings, or pray for mercy, when every object of desire hangs on the links of an unalterable chain of causes? Then the one means of exchange between God and man is gone — the communion of hope and prayer — if it is true that we ever earn the priceless recompense of divine favour at the price of a proper humility; for this is the one way in which men seem able to hold communion with God, and are joined to that unapproachable light by the very act of asking, even before they get what they ask for. And then, since these things can hardly be believed to have any effect if the necessity of future events is admitted, what means will be left by which we can be brought near to Him who is the supreme Head of all, and cling to Him? So the human race must necessarily, just as you declared a while ago in your song, be parted and severed from its Source and fall to ruin."

Song III: Truth's Paradoxes

	Why does a strange discord break
		the fair harmony of the ordered scheme?
	Has God decreed that between truth and truth
		there may be such lasting warfare
	that truths, each of them plain on its own,
	we try and fail to reconcile?
	Or is the discord not in the truth,
		since truth is always consistent with itself?
	Is it that the mind of man, held close
		in its wrappings of flesh, and blinded,
	can never make out — her candle burns so faint —
	the subtle chain that binds it all together?
	Then why does man's restless mind burn
		to unlock the hidden gates of truth?
	Does he already know what he is looking for?
		Why labour to look for it, if he knows?
	And yet if he does not know,
	why does he blindly seek he knows not what?
	Who sighs for a good he does not know?
		Who can pursue an end he has never seen?
	How would he find it? And even if he found it,
		how would he greet a strange shape he never knew?
	Or is it that the inmost soul of man
	once knew each part, and knew the whole?
	Now, though she is dimmed by the vapours of the flesh,
		she has not forgotten everything she saw;
	for while the separate parts are lost,
		she still holds fast to the one whole;
	so the man who longs to find the truth
	is neither sound of sight nor blind.
	For he neither knows in full
		nor is stripped of knowledge altogether;
	but holding on to what is left him
		he gropes in the uncertain light,
	and by the part that still survives
	he strives bravely to win back the whole.
