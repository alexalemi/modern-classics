Chapter 6: Eternity, and Why Foreseeing Does Not Compel

"Since, then, as we proved a moment ago, everything that is known is known not according to its own nature but according to the nature of the faculty that grasps it, let us now consider, as far as we lawfully may, what the divine essence is like, so that we can understand what its knowledge is like too.

"God is eternal: on this judgment all rational beings agree. So let us consider what eternity is, for that word reveals both the divine nature and the divine knowledge. Now, eternity is the complete and perfect possession of endless life all at once. What that means becomes clearer if you compare it with things in time. For whatever lives in time is a present moving out of the past into the future, and nothing set in time can hold the whole space of its own life together at once. It has not yet got hold of tomorrow's state, and it has already lost yesterday's; and even in today's life you live no longer than one brief passing moment. So whatever is subject to the condition of time — even if, as Aristotle thought about the world, it never has a beginning or an end, and its life is stretched out over the whole extent of time's infinity — is still not the kind of thing that can rightly be thought eternal. For it does not include and embrace the whole space of an infinite life at once; it has no present hold on the things to come, which have not happened yet. Accordingly, what includes and possesses the whole fullness of an unending life at once, with nothing future missing from it and nothing past escaped out of it, is what is rightly called eternal. It must necessarily be always present to itself in full self-possession, and hold the infinity of moving time in an abiding present.

"So the people who imagine that on Plato's principles the created world is made co-eternal with its Creator are judging wrongly, just because they are told he believed the world had no beginning in time and is destined never to end. For it is one thing for existence to be prolonged endlessly, which is what Plato ascribed to the world, and quite another for the whole of an endless life to be embraced in the present, which is clearly a property belonging to the divine mind alone. Nor need God appear earlier than created things in mere length of time; He is prior only in the unique simplicity of His nature. For the infinite progression of things in time copies this immediate existence in the present of the changeless life; and when it cannot manage to equal it, it declines from stillness into motion and falls away from the simplicity of a perpetual present into the infinite duration of the future and the past. And since it cannot possess the whole fullness of its life at once — for the very reason that in a way it never stops being — it seems, up to a point, to rival what it cannot complete and express, by attaching itself indifferently to any present moment of time, however swift and brief; and since that bears some resemblance to the ever-abiding present, it grants to everything it is assigned to the appearance of existence. But since it cannot stay still, it hurries along the infinite path of time; and the result is that it continues by ceaseless movement the life whose completeness it could not embrace by standing still. So if we want to give things their right names, we shall follow Plato in saying that God is eternal, and the world everlasting.

"Since, then, every mode of judgment grasps its objects in accordance with its own nature, and since God remains for ever in an eternal present, His knowledge too transcends every movement of time and lives in the simplicity of its own changeless present; and embracing the whole infinite sweep of the past and the future, it contemplates everything that falls within its simple knowing as though it were happening now. And so, if you consider carefully that immediate presentation by which it distinguishes all things, you will more rightly judge it to be not foreknowledge of something future but knowledge of a moment that never passes. That is why the name chosen to describe it is not prevision but providence: because, being utterly removed in nature from things mean and trivial, its gaze embraces all things as though from some lofty height. Why, then, do you insist that things surveyed by the divine eye are caught up in necessity, when it is clear that men impose no necessity on things they see? Does the act of seeing add any necessity to the things in front of your eyes?"

"Certainly not."

"And yet, if we may compare God's present with man's without impropriety, then just as you see certain things in this temporary present of yours, so He sees all things in His eternal present. So this divine anticipation does not change the natures and properties of things, and it sees things as present before it exactly as they will happen later in time. Nor does it confuse things in its judgment, but in one mental view it distinguishes what will come necessarily and what will not. Just as you, when you see at one and the same moment a man walking on the ground and the sun rising in the sky, distinguish between the two, although a single glance takes in both, and judge the first a voluntary action and the second a necessary one — so also the divine vision in its universal range in no way confuses the characters of the things present to its gaze, though they are future as far as time goes. From which it follows that when it perceives that something will come into existence, and is nevertheless perfectly aware that this thing is bound by no necessity, its grasp of it is not opinion but knowledge based on truth.

"And if to this you say that what God sees as going to happen cannot fail to happen, and that what cannot fail to happen happens by necessity, and want to tie me down to that word 'necessity' — I will admit that you are asserting a very solid truth, but one that hardly anyone can get near who has not made the divine his special study. For my answer would be that the same future event is necessary from the standpoint of divine knowledge, and yet, considered in its own nature, is absolutely free and unfettered. So there are two kinds of necessity: one simple, as that men are necessarily mortal; the other conditional, as that if you know somebody is walking, he must necessarily be walking. For what is known certainly cannot be otherwise than it is known to be, and yet that fact does not carry the other, simple necessity with it at all. The first kind of necessity is not imposed by the thing's own nature but by the addition of a condition. No necessity compels a man who is walking voluntarily to move forward, although it is necessary for him to be moving forward at the moment when he is walking. In the same way, then, if Providence sees anything as present, that thing must necessarily be, even though it is bound by no necessity of nature. Now God views as present those coming events that happen by free will. So from the standpoint of the divine vision they are made necessary, conditionally on God's knowing them; but viewed in themselves they do not give up the absolute freedom that naturally belongs to them. So without doubt everything will happen that God foreknows as going to happen, but some of these things proceed from free will; and though they happen, they do not lose their proper nature by the fact of happening — the nature by which, before they happened, it really was possible that they might not have happened at all.

"What difference does it make, then, to deny that they are necessary, if, being conditioned by divine knowledge, they happen just as if they were under the compulsion of necessity in every respect? This difference, surely, which we saw in the examples I took earlier — the sun's rising and the man's walking. At the moment they happened, neither of them could have been otherwise than happening; and yet one of them was necessarily bound to be before it took place, while the other was not so at all. So likewise the things that are present to God undoubtedly exist, but some of them come from the necessity of things and others from the power of the agent. So we were quite right to say that these things are necessary if they are viewed from the standpoint of divine knowledge, but that if they are considered in themselves they are free of the bonds of necessity — just as everything accessible to the senses is universal when regarded from the standpoint of Thought, but particular when viewed in its own nature.

"'But,' you will say, 'if it is in my power to change my mind, I shall make providence void, since I may change something that falls within its foreknowledge.' My answer is this: you can certainly turn your purpose aside; but since the truth of providence is always at hand to see that you can, and whether you do, and where you turn yourself, you cannot escape divine foreknowledge — just as you cannot escape the sight of someone watching you now, even though of your own free will you turn to one action or another. Will you say, then: 'Shall the divine knowledge be changed at my discretion, so that when I will this or that, providence changes its knowledge to match?'"

"Surely not."

"Quite right; for the divine vision anticipates everything that is coming and transforms and reduces it to the form of its own present knowledge. It does not vary in its foreknowledge, as you suppose, alternating between this and that; in a single flash it forestalls and includes all your changes of mind without itself altering. And God has received this ever-present comprehension and survey of all things not from the outcome of future events but from the simplicity of His own nature. This also resolves the objection that offended you a little while ago — that our future doings were being spoken of as though they supplied the cause of God's knowledge. For this faculty of knowledge, embracing all things in its immediate awareness, has itself fixed the bounds of all things, and owes nothing to what comes after.

"And all this being so, the freedom of man's will stands unshaken, and the laws are not unjust, since their rewards and punishments are held out to wills that no necessity binds. God, who foreknows all things, still looks down from above, and the ever-present eternity of His vision runs alongside the future character of all our acts, dispensing rewards to the good and punishments to the bad. Our hopes and prayers too are not fixed on God in vain, and when they are rightly directed they cannot fail of their effect. So stand against vice, practise virtue, lift up your souls to right hopes, offer humble prayers to Heaven. A great necessity of righteousness is laid on you, if you will not hide it from yourselves — seeing that everything you do is done before the eyes of a Judge who sees all things."
