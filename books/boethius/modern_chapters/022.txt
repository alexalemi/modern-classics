Chapter 8: All Five Fail, and Each Brings Its Own Harm

"There is no doubt, then, that these roads do not lead to happiness, and cannot bring anyone to the goal they promise. Now I will show you very briefly what serious evils are involved in following them.

"Just consider. Is it your object to pile up money? Then you must wrench it away from whoever has it now. Do you mean to put on the splendour of high office? Then you must beg from the men who have it in their gift; you who want to outdo everybody else in honour must lower yourself into the humble posture of a petitioner. Do you long for power? Then you must face danger, because you will be at the mercy of your subjects' plots. Is glory your aim? Then you are lured on through every kind of hardship, and there is an end of your peace of mind. Do you want to lead a life of pleasure? But who does not despise a man who is the slave of the weakest and cheapest thing there is, the body?

"And again, how slight and perishable a possession people are relying on who set bodily excellences in front of themselves. Can you ever beat the elephant for bulk or the bull for strength? Can you outrun the tiger? Look at the infinity of the heavens, their solidity, the speed of their motion, and stop admiring worthless things for once. And even the heavens are not to be admired so much for that as for the reason that steers them.

"Then how quickly the brightness of beauty passes, and how soon it is gone — more fleeting than the fading bloom of spring flowers. And yet if, as Aristotle says, men could see with the eyes of Lynceus, so that their sight went through obstructions, would not the body of Alcibiades, so gloriously fair on the outside, look utterly disgusting once all its insides were open to view? So it is not your own nature that makes you look beautiful, but the weakness of the eyes that see you.

"Prize your body's excellences as extravagantly as you like — so long as you know that this thing you admire, whatever it is worth, can be dissolved away by the feeble heat of a three-day fever. From all of which we may conclude, on the whole, that these things which cannot deliver the advantages they promise, and which are never made perfect by the assembling of every good together, neither serve as side roads to happiness nor make men completely happy in themselves."

Song VIII: Human Folly

	How far astray
	does Ignorance lead these wretched mortals
	from the road of Truth!
	You do not look for gold
	on leafy stems in the green wood,
	or strip the vine for gems;
	you do not spread your nets
	on hilltops, to get the groaning table
	furnished with fish;
	and if you want to hunt
	the bounding goat, you do not sweep
	the ruffled face of the sea for it.
	Men know the sea's far depths,
	every hidden corner where the waves wash over
	the pearl as white as snow;
	they know where the Tyrian shellfish lurks,
	where fish and prickly urchins are plentiful —
	all this they know perfectly well.
	But not to know, or care,
	where the good that every heart wants lies hidden:
	this blindness they can live with.
	With their eyes bent low on the earth
	they look for the thing that lies far beyond
	the starry sky.
	What curse can I call down
	on minds so dull? Let them go on running
	after wealth and a great name;
	and when after all that trouble
	they have grasped the false good — ah, then, too late —
	let them make out the true one.
