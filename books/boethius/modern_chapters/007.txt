Chapter 1: Fortune's Nature Is Caprice

After that she was silent for a while; and when a measured pause in her talk had brought my flagging attention back, she began: "If I have properly worked out the character and the causes of your illness, you are wasting away with a regretful longing for the fortune you used to have. It is the change in that fortune, as you see it, that has done all this to your mind.

"I know that Siren's many tricks perfectly well — the deadly charm of the friendship she pretends to have for her victims while she is scheming to trap them, and how she abandons them without warning and leaves them flattened under a grief they cannot carry. Think about her nature, her character, what she is worth, and you will soon admit that in her you neither possessed nor have lost anything of any value. I do not think I need take much trouble bringing this back to you, since even while she was still with you, even while she was making a fuss of you, you used to attack her in manly terms and tell her off with maxims out of my own holy storehouse.

"But every sudden change of circumstances inevitably brings a certain upheaval of the spirit. That is how it has come about that you too have been separated for a while from your peace of mind. But it is time for you to take and drain a draught that is soft and pleasant to the taste, which as it works inward may prepare the way for stronger doses. So I call in the sweet persuasiveness of Rhetoric — who only walks the right road when she does not desert my instructions — and I tell my servant Music to join in with her singing, now in a lighter and now in a graver strain.

"So what is it, poor mortal, that has thrown you into weeping and mourning? Your eyes have seen something strange and unfamiliar, I think. You imagine Fortune has changed towards you; you are mistaken. Her ways were always these, and this was always her nature. In her very changeableness she has kept her real constancy towards you. She was exactly this when she was loading you with caresses, when she was leading you on with the enticements of a false happiness. You have found out how changeable the face of the blind goddess is. She still veils herself from everyone else; to you she has revealed her whole character.

"If you like her, take her as she is and do not complain. If you loathe her treachery, turn away from her in contempt, have nothing to do with her, because her illusions are poisonous. The very thing that is now the cause of your great grief ought to have brought you peace. You have been left by somebody nobody can be sure will not leave him. Or do you really put a value on a happiness that is certain to go? Let me ask again: is Fortune's presence precious to you, when she cannot be trusted to stay and will bring grief when she leaves? Why, if she cannot be kept as long as you please, and if her flight brings disaster down on you, what is this passing visitor but a sign of trouble coming?

"It is truly not enough to look only at what is in front of your eyes. Wisdom measures how things turn out, and this same changeableness, cutting both ways, makes Fortune's threats nothing to be frightened of and her caresses hardly worth wanting. Finally, you ought to put up with whatever happens inside Fortune's estate, once you have put your head under her yoke. But if you want to lay down a law about staying and going for the woman you have of your own accord chosen for your mistress, are you not in the wrong? Are you not embittering by impatience a lot you cannot change?

"If you handed your sails to the winds, you would travel not where you meant to go but where the winds drove you; if you trusted your seed to the fields, you would have to set the fruitful years against the barren ones. You have handed yourself over to Fortune's rule; you must put up with your mistress's whims. What — are you actually trying to stop the swing of a turning wheel? You most stupid of mortals: if it ever stopped turning, it would stop being Fortune's wheel."

Song I: Fortune's Malice

	Mad Fortune sweeps along in reckless pride,
	as unreliable as the tide in the Euripus strait;
	one moment she tramples great kings underfoot,
	the next she puts the beaten man in the winner's seat.
	She pays no attention to the wail of the miserable,
	but laughs at the griefs her own mischief causes.
	This is her game; this is how she proves her power;
	and it is a fine wonder when in a single hour
	she shows her favourite lifted high into bliss
	and then dropped headlong into the pit of misery.
