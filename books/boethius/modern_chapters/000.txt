Song I: Boethius' Complaint

	I who once wrote songs with pleasure,
		in the flourishing of my days,
	now am driven, weeping, to take up
		measures made for grief.
	Look — the Muses, hair torn loose,
		guide my pen and give my sorrow words;
	real tears, running down their faces,
		fall into my sad complaint.
	These alone were unafraid
		to keep me company in danger;
	these have followed the exiled man
		to the end of his lonely road.
	These that were the pride and pleasure
		of my youth and my high place
	are the only comfort left me
		in an old man's bitter fate.
	Old? Yes — quickly, before I knew it,
		age has come, pressed on me
	by these griefs; and Grief has told me
		to put on the clothes that suit her.
	White hairs, scattered on my head
		before their time, proclaim my troubles,
	and the skin hangs loose and shrivelled
		on a frame that sorrow shrank.
	Happy the death that does not come
		in the sweet, sweet years of peace,
	but that comes to the brokenhearted
		when they call, and brings release.
	Yet Death walks past the wretched,
		stops his ears and sleeps on deeply;
	will not hear the cry of anguish,
		will not close the eyes that weep.
	While inconstant Fortune poured
		her gifts on me and all was bright,
	one dark hour had nearly drowned me
		in the darkness that has no end.
	Now, because misfortune's shadow
		has come over that false face,
	cruel Life drags out its lingering,
		though I loathe its weary running.
	Friends, why did you tell me once
		so lightly that I was a happy man?
	Anyone who could fall this far
		was never firmly founded.
