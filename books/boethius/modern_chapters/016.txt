Chapter 2: The Five Things People Mistake for Happiness

For a little while she stayed gazing fixedly, withdrawn as it were into the stately chamber of her own mind; then she began:

"All mortal creatures, in the anxious aims that keep them busy in so many different pursuits, take many roads but are all trying to reach one goal: happiness. Now *the good* is what a man cannot want anything further once he has got it. This is the supreme good of all, containing every particular good inside itself; so that if anything at all were still missing from it, it could not be the supreme good, since something would be left outside that could still be wanted. It is clear, then, that happiness is a condition made perfect by the assembling of every good thing together.

"Everyone is trying to reach that condition, as we have said, but by different roads; for the desire for the true good is naturally planted in human minds, and it is only error that leads them off the road in pursuit of a false one. Some, thinking the highest good is to be short of nothing, spare no effort to get rich. Others, judging that the good is whatever most deserves respect, work to win the reverence of their fellow citizens by holding office. Some locate the chief good in supreme power; these either want to hold sovereignty themselves or try to attach themselves to somebody who does. Others again, who think reputation is something supremely excellent, are in a hurry to spread the glory of their name abroad, whether through the arts of war or of peace. A great many measure the attainment of good by joy and gladness of heart; these think the height of happiness is to give themselves up to pleasure.

"And there are others who swap ends and means about in what they aim at: some want riches for the sake of pleasure and power, some want power either for the money or to bring their name renown. So it is on these ends that human actions and wishes are centred, and on others like them — good birth and popularity, for instance, which seem to bring a certain reputation; a wife and children, which are wanted for the sweetness of having them. As for friendship, the most sacred kind is counted in the category of virtue and not of fortune, but the other kinds are entered into for the sake of power or enjoyment. And bodily excellences obviously belong with the list above: strength and stature clearly suggest power, beauty and speed of foot bring celebrity, health brings pleasure.

"It is plain, then, that the only object sought in all these ways is *happiness*; for whatever a man seeks in preference to everything else is in his judgement the supreme good, and we have defined the supreme good as happiness. So the condition each man wants in preference to all others is in his judgement the happy one.

"So you have in front of you something like a plan of human happiness: wealth, rank, power, glory, pleasure. Now Epicurus, looking at these considerations alone, concluded with some consistency that the highest good is pleasure, because all the other objects seem to bring the mind some delight.

"But to come back to human pursuits and aims: the human mind is trying to recover the good that properly belongs to it, in spite of the mistiness of its memory — but like a drunk man it does not know which road leads home. Do you think the people who struggle to escape want are wrong? Not at all; there is nothing that can complete happiness so well as a condition that has every good thing in abundance, needs nothing from outside, and is entirely self-sufficient. Do they go wrong who judge that what is best also best deserves reverence? Not at all. A thing that nearly the whole human race directs its efforts at getting cannot possibly be cheap and contemptible. Is power, then, not to be counted a good? Can what is plainly more effective than anything else be reckoned feeble and without strength? Or is reputation to be thought worthless? No; it cannot be ignored that the highest reputation is constantly found alongside the highest excellence. And what need is there to say that happiness is not haunted by anxiety and gloom, or exposed to trouble and vexation, when that is a condition we ask of the smallest things we expect any delight from owning?

"So these are the blessings men want to win: they want riches, rank, sovereignty, glory and pleasure, because they believe that by these means they will secure independence, reverence, power, reputation and gladness of heart. So it is *the good* that men are looking for along all these different roads; and here you can easily see the strength of nature's power, since although opinions are so various and contradictory, they all agree in holding *good* to be the end."

Song II: The Bent of Nature

		How the strength of nature sways
	the whole world in ordered ways,
	how laws that cannot be resisted
	control the least part of the whole —
	this is what I would gladly rehearse
	in sounding verse on my responsive strings.
		Look at the lion taken captive:
	he wears his gilded chain meekly,
	and though he is fed by hand
	and dreads a master's whip,
	if once the taste of blood
	sharpens his cruel lips again,
	his sleeping fierceness wakes at once;
	with one roar he breaks his chains,
	and takes his revenge first
	on the mangled body of his trainer.
		And the woodland songbird, shut up
	in his forlorn imprisonment,
	though a mistress's lavish care
	sets out a store of honeyed treats:
	still, if in his narrow cage,
	as he hops from bar to bar,
	he should catch sight of the woods far off,
	cool under their sheltering leaves,
	he will kick all these delicacies aside;
	his heart will turn to the woods.
	It is only the woods he longs for;
	he pipes the woods in all his songs.
		The sapling bends to rough force
	while the hand keeps up its pressure;
	let the hand relax that pressure
	and the supple wood springs straight back.
	The sun sinks into the western sea;
	but his chariot is swiftly carried
	back again by a secret road
	to the usual gates of morning.
		So everything is seen to long
	for its own return in its own time;
	and no fixed order can last
	except the one that in the appointed way
	joins the end back to the beginning
	and spins in a steady circle.
