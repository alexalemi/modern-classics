Chapter 11: Everything Alive Wants to Stay One, and Unity Is Good

"I quite agree," I said. "All your reasonings really do hold together admirably."

Then she said: "What price would you put on the gift, if you were to come to know absolute good?"

"An infinite one," I said, "if only I were lucky enough to come to know God too, who is the good."

"Then I will make that clear to you on the truest grounds of reason — provided our recent conclusions stand firm."

"They will."

"Have we not shown that the things most people want are not the true and perfect good, and precisely for this reason: that they differ from one another individually, and since each is missing what the others have, none of them can bestow the full and absolute good? And that they become the true good when they are gathered, so to speak, into a single form and a single working, so that what is independence is also power, reverence, renown and the delight of pleasure — and that unless they are all one and the same thing, they have no claim to be counted among the things worth wanting at all?"

"Yes; that was clearly proved, and cannot be doubted in any way."

"Now, when things are far from being good while they are different, but become good as soon as they are one, is it not true that they become good by acquiring unity?"

"It seems so," I said.

"But do you not grant that everything good is good by sharing in goodness?"

"I do."

"Then on similar grounds you must admit that unity and goodness are the same thing; for when things do not differ in what they naturally do, their essence is one and the same."

"There is no denying it."

"Now, do you know," she said, "that everything that is stays in being and holds together as long as it goes on being one, but as soon as it stops being one it perishes and falls to pieces?"

"How so?"

"Take animals, for instance. When soul and body come together and continue as one, we call the result a living creature; but when that unity is broken by the two separating, the creature dies, and is plainly no longer alive. The body too, while it remains in one form by the joining of its parts, presents a human appearance; but if the separation and scattering of the parts breaks up the body's unity, it stops being what it was. And if we widen the survey to everything else, it will undoubtedly become clear that each individual thing holds together while it is one, and perishes when it stops being one."

"Yes — when I think about it further, I see it is exactly as you say."

"Well, is there anything," she said, "which, in so far as it acts in accordance with its nature, gives up the wish to live and wants to come to death and decay?"

"Looking at living creatures, which have some power of choice, I can find none that gives up the will to live without being forced from outside, or that hurries towards destruction of its own accord. Every creature diligently pursues its own self-preservation and avoids death and destruction. As for plants and trees, and inanimate things generally, I am quite unsure what to think."

"And yet there is no room for a question about those either, since you can see how plants and trees grow in places that suit them, where, as far as their nature allows, they cannot quickly wither and die. Some spring up in the plains, others in the mountains; some grow in marshes, others cling to rocks; and others again find fertile soil in barren sand — and if you try transplanting them elsewhere, they wither away. Nature gives each of them the soil that suits it, and takes pains to stop any of them dying so long as it is possible for them to go on living. Why do they all draw their nourishment from their roots, like a mouth dipped into the earth, and spread the strong bark over the pith? Why are all the softer parts, like the pith, buried deep inside, while the outer parts have the tough texture of wood, and outside everything is the bark, standing up to bad weather like a champion who can take punishment? And then, how diligent nature is in securing propagation everywhere by multiplying seed! Who does not see that all these are contrivances not only for a species' present survival, but for its lasting continuance, generation after generation, for ever? And do not the things we believe to be inanimate seek what suits them too, on the same grounds of reason? Why do flames shoot lightly upwards, while the earth presses downwards with its weight, if it is not that these motions and positions suit their respective natures? What is more, each individual thing is preserved by what agrees with its nature, just as it is destroyed by what is hostile to it. Solid things like stones resist being broken up by the close adhesion of their parts. Fluid things like air and water give way easily to whatever divides them, but flow swiftly back and mingle again with the parts they were severed from; while fire refuses to be cut at all. And we are not talking here about the voluntary movements of an intelligent soul, but about the drift of nature. It is in that way that we digest our food without thinking about it, and draw our breath in our sleep without knowing it. Indeed, even in living creatures the love of life does not come from conscious will, but from the principles of nature. For often, under the pressure of circumstances, the will chooses the death that nature shrinks from; and conversely, in spite of natural appetite, the will restrains that work of reproduction by which alone perishable creatures are kept in being. So entirely does this love of self come from the drift of nature and not from animal impulse. Providence has given things this thoroughly compelling reason for continuing: they must want life, so long as it is naturally possible for them to go on living. So you may in no way doubt that things naturally aim at continuing to exist, and avoid destruction."

"I confess," I said, "that what I thought uncertain a moment ago I now see to be beyond doubt."

"Now, whatever seeks to hold together and continue wants to be one; for if its oneness goes, its very existence cannot continue."

"True," I said.

"All things, then, want to be one."

"I agree."

"But we have proved that one is the very same thing as good."

"We have."

"All things, then, seek the good. Indeed, you could put the fact this way: good is what all things want."

"Nothing could be more truly worked out. Either there is no single end that all things are related to, or else the end that all things everywhere hurry towards must be the highest good of all."

Then she said: "I am exceedingly glad, my pupil. Your eye is now fixed on the very centre of the target of truth. And what is more, this reveals the thing you declared yourself ignorant of a little while ago."

"What is that?" I said.

"The end and aim of the whole universe. Surely it is what all things want; and since we have concluded that the good is that, we ought to acknowledge that the end and aim of the whole universe is the good."

Song XI: Remembering

	Anyone chasing truth, who wants
		to keep his steps off the false roads,
	must search inside himself by an inner light,
		in deep meditation;
	he must hold back everything that pulls him outward
	to take possession of his soul's true treasure.
	Then all that the mists of error hid
		will shine out clearer than daylight,
	for the forgetful weight of this body of flesh
		has not quite put reason out;
	the seeds of truth are lying inside us still,
	and by learning we can win them all back.
	How else could you give the right answer
		to a question nobody taught you,
	if it were not that deep within the soul
		truth's secret sparks are alive?
	And if Plato's teaching does not go wrong,
	all learning is remembering what we forgot.
