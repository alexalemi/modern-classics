Castiglione

From Castiglione the wounded were supposed to be taken on to the hospitals of Brescia, Cremona, Bergamo and Milan, where they would at last get proper care or undergo the amputations they needed. But the Austrians, as they passed through, had carried off nearly every cart in the country with their forced requisitions, and the French army's own means of transport fell far short of the frightening mass of wounded. So the wounded had to be kept waiting two or three days in the mobile field hospitals before they could be brought in to Castiglione, where the crowding becomes indescribable. The whole town turns into one vast improvised hospital, for French and Austrians alike. As early as Friday the field hospital of general headquarters had set itself up there; crates of lint had been unpacked, along with splints and medicines, and the townspeople had given every blanket, every piece of linen, every straw pallet and mattress they could spare. The hospital of Castiglione, the church, cloister and barracks of San Luigi, the Capuchin church, the police barracks, and the churches of the Maggiore, San Giuseppe and Santa Rosalia are all filled with wounded men, packed together and lying on nothing but straw. Straw is spread in the streets as well, and in the courtyards and squares, where plank shelters have been knocked together here and canvas stretched there, to keep the sun off a little from the wounded who are arriving from every direction at once. Before long the private houses are taken over too. The better-off owners take in officers and men and hurry to give them whatever small comforts they can. Some of them run frantically through the streets looking for a doctor for their guests. Others go back and forth through the town, distraught, begging for someone to take away the corpses in their houses, which they have no idea how to get rid of. Generals de Ladmirault, Dieu and Auger, Colonels Broutta and Brincourt, and other senior officers were brought to Castiglione, where they are cared for by the skillful Dr. Bertherand, who has been amputating at San Luigi since Friday morning. Two other surgeons-major, Drs. Leuret and Haspel, two Italian doctors, and the assistant surgeons Riolacci and Lobstein have been setting splints and dressing wounds for two days, and they keep at their painful work even through the night. The artillery general Auger was carried first to the Casa Morino, where the field hospital of the headquarters of Marshal MacMahon's corps, to which he belonged, was set up, and then brought on to Castiglione. This outstanding officer had his left shoulder shattered by a six-pound cannonball, which stayed lodged for twenty-four hours deep in the muscles of the armpit. The general died on the 29th, from the effects of the operation to take the arm off at the shoulder, which was needed to remove the ball, and from the gangrene that had spread through the wound.

Author's note: Castiglione delle Stiviere, about fifteen miles southeast of Brescia, has fifty-three hundred inhabitants. It was just outside Castiglione, on the 5th of August, 1796, two days after General Augereau had taken the town, that General Bonaparte, at the head of the Army of Italy, won an important victory over the Austrian field marshal Wurmser. It was close by as well, on the Chiese, that on the 19th of April, 1706, the Duke of Vendôme won the battle of Calcinato over Marshal Reventlow, who commanded the Imperial army in Prince Eugene's absence.

On Saturday the convoys of wounded become so numerous that the authorities, the townspeople and the detachment of troops left at Castiglione are completely unable to cope with so much misery. Then begin scenes as pitiful as those of the day before, though of a quite different kind. There is water and there is food, and yet the wounded are dying of hunger and thirst. There is lint in plenty, but not enough hands to put it on the wounds. Most of the army's doctors have had to leave for Cavriana; there are not enough orderlies, and at this most critical moment there are no hands to do the work. So a volunteer service has to be organized, somehow or other. But that is very hard in the middle of such disorder, made worse by a kind of panic that seizes the people of Castiglione, with the disastrous result of enormously increasing the confusion and, through the fright it gives them, worsening the wretched state of the wounded.

The panic had a cause that was really quite trivial. On the day after the battle, as each corps of the French army took stock of itself after taking up its positions, convoys of prisoners were formed and sent to Brescia by way of Castiglione and Montechiaro. One of these detachments of prisoners, escorted by hussars, was approaching Castiglione in the afternoon on the road from Cavriana, and as soon as it was seen in the distance the townspeople foolishly took it for the Austrian army coming back in force. The rumor was absurd and impossible, but it was spread by peasants, by the hired drivers of the army's baggage, and by the little peddlers who usually follow troops on campaign, and when the people of the town saw these men rushing in among them, panting with terror, they believed the ridiculous story. At once the houses are shut. The townspeople barricade themselves indoors, burn the tricolor flags that had decorated their windows, and hide in their cellars or their attics. Some flee into the fields with their wives and children, carrying off everything they own of value. Others, a little less shaken, stay at home but take in the first Austrian wounded they can lay hands on, picking them up in the squares and suddenly heaping them with attentions and kindnesses. In the streets and on the roads, which are crowded with carts of wounded going to Brescia and with supply convoys coming from that city for the army, wagons are carried off at full speed, horses bolt in every direction amid cries of terror and anger, carts loaded with baggage are overturned, and loads of hardtack are thrown into the ditches along the highway. In the end the hired drivers, more and more terrified, unhitch their horses and gallop off at full speed down the road to Montechiaro and Brescia. All along it they spread panic and cause an unbelievable melee, crashing into the carts full of food and bread that the city government of Brescia is constantly sending to the allied army's camp, dragging along with them everything they meet, and trampling the wounded, who beg to be taken away. The wounded will not listen to reason: they tear off their bandages, stagger out of the churches, and set off down the streets with no idea how far they will be able to go.

What agonies and what suffering there were on the 25th, the 26th and the 27th! The wounds, poisoned by heat and dust and by the lack of water and of care, have grown more painful. Foul, noxious smells fill the air, in spite of the Intendance's praiseworthy efforts to keep the buildings that have become field hospitals in good order, and the shortage of assistants, orderlies and attendants is cruelly felt, for the convoys sent to Castiglione keep pouring new batches of wounded into the town every quarter of an hour. However hard a chief surgeon and two or three other people work to organize regular transport to Brescia in oxcarts, and however willingly those people of Brescia who own carriages come of their own accord to ask for patients, and are given the officers, the departures fall far short of the arrivals, so that the crowding only grows.

On the stone floors of the hospitals and churches of Castiglione, men of every nation have been laid side by side: Frenchmen and Arabs, Germans and Slavs. Stowed away for the time being in the depths of the chapels, they no longer have the strength to move, or cannot shift from the narrow space they lie in. Oaths, curses and cries that no words can convey ring under the vaults of these holy places. "Oh, sir, how I'm suffering!" some of these poor men said to me. "They're abandoning us. They're letting us die miserably, and yet we fought well!" In spite of all they have been through and all the nights they have spent without sleep, rest will not come to them. In their distress they beg for a doctor, or roll about in despair, in convulsions that will end in tetanus and death. Some soldiers have the absurd idea that the cold water poured on their wounds, which are already festering, produces worms, and out of that fear they will not let their bandages be moistened. Others, who had the privilege of being dressed at the mobile field hospitals, were never dressed again during their forced stay in Castiglione, and those cloths, bound extremely tight to withstand the jolts of the road, had been neither changed nor loosened, and were real torture to them. These men, their faces black with flies that cling to their wounds, cast wild looks in every direction, and nobody answers. In those men, the greatcoat, the shirt, the flesh and the blood have formed a horrible mass that cannot be told apart, and maggots have got into it. Several shudder at the thought of being eaten by the maggots, which they think they see coming out of their bodies, and which come from the myriads of flies that infest the air. Here is a soldier, completely disfigured, whose tongue hangs far out of his torn and broken jaw. He tosses about and tries to get up. I wet his parched lips and his hardened tongue with fresh water; I take a handful of lint, dip it in the bucket that someone carries behind me, and squeeze the water from this sponge into the shapeless opening where his mouth used to be. There is another poor man, part of whose face has been cut away by a saber blow: nose, lips and chin have been severed from the rest of his face. He cannot speak and is half blind, and he makes signs with his hand, and by this heartbreaking dumb show, along with sounds from his throat, he draws attention to himself. I give him a drink and let a few drops of clean water run over his bleeding face. A third man, his skull split wide open, is dying, his brains spilling out on the church floor. His companions in misfortune push him aside with their feet because he is in the way. I shelter him in his last moments and cover his poor head, which he still moves feebly, with a handkerchief.

Every house had become an infirmary, and every family had its hands full caring for the officers it had taken in. Even so, by Sunday morning I had managed to gather a number of local women, who help as best they can in the efforts being made to relieve the wounded. It is not a question of amputations or any other operation. What is needed is to give food, and above all drink, to people dying of hunger and thirst; then to dress their wounds, or to wash bloody bodies covered in mud and vermin; and all this has to be done amid fetid, sickening smells, through the moaning and the howls of pain, in a burning, foul atmosphere. A core of volunteers soon forms. The Lombard women run to the men who cry the loudest, who are not always the ones most to be pitied. I set myself to organize help as well as I can in the part of town that seems to have least of it, and in particular I adopt one of the churches of Castiglione, the one standing on a rise on the left as you come from Brescia, which is called, if I am not mistaken, the Chiesa Maggiore. Nearly five hundred soldiers are crowded into it, and there are at least another hundred lying on straw in front of the church, under canvas stretched to keep off the sun. The women who have gone inside move from one man to the next with jars and canteens full of clear water, to quench their thirst and to moisten their wounds. Some of these improvised nurses are lovely, graceful young girls. Their gentleness, their kindness, their beautiful eyes full of tears and compassion, and their careful attentions lift the courage and the spirits of the sick a little. Little boys from the town run back and forth between the church and the nearest fountains with buckets, canteens and watering cans. After the water comes broth and soup, which the supply service has to make in tremendous quantities. Enormous bales of lint have been set down here and there, and anyone can use it freely, but there are no bandages, no linen, no shirts. In this little town, which the Austrian army has just passed through, supplies are so meager that you cannot get even the barest necessities any more. Still, I manage to buy new shirts through these good women, who have already brought and given away all their old linen, and on Monday morning I send my coachman to Brescia for supplies. He comes back a few hours later with his cabriolet loaded with chamomile, mallow, elderflower, oranges, lemons, sugar, shirts, sponges, strips of linen, pins, cigars and tobacco. That lets us hand out a cooling lemonade that the men have been longing for, wash the wounds in mallow water, apply warm compresses and change the bandages on the dressings. Meanwhile we have won some recruits who join us: an old naval officer, and then two English tourists who wanted to see everything, came into the church, and were kept by us almost by force. Two other Englishmen, on the contrary, are eager to help from the start; they hand out cigars to the Austrians. An Italian priest, three or four travelers and sightseers, a Paris journalist who later takes charge of the relief work in a church nearby, and some officers whose detachment has been ordered to stay in Castiglione lend us a hand. But one of these officers soon falls ill from the strain, and our other volunteer nurses drop out one after another, unable to bear for long the sight of suffering they can do so little to relieve. The priest followed their example, but he comes back, thoughtfully, to hold aromatic herbs and bottles of smelling salts under our noses. A young French tourist, overwhelmed by the sight of these living wrecks, suddenly bursts into sobs. A merchant from Neuchâtel devotes himself for two days to dressing wounds and writing letters of farewell for the dying to their families. For his own sake we have to hold back his zeal, and also to calm a Belgian whose compassionate excitement had risen so high that we were afraid he would be seized with brain fever, like the second lieutenant beside us who had come from Milan to rejoin his unit and was struck down by it. Some soldiers of the detachment left as garrison in the town try to help their comrades, but they cannot stand the sight either; it strikes their imagination too sharply and breaks their spirit. A corporal of engineers, wounded at Magenta and nearly healed, who is on his way back to his battalion and whose travel orders give him a few days, comes with us and helps us bravely, though he faints twice in a row. The French intendant who has just set up at Castiglione at last gives permission to use healthy prisoners for hospital duty, and three Austrian doctors come to help a young Corsican assistant surgeon, who pesters me several times for a certificate attesting to his zeal during the time I saw him at work. A German surgeon who had stayed behind on the battlefield on purpose to dress the wounded of his own nation devotes himself to those of both armies. In gratitude the Intendance sends him back after three days to rejoin his countrymen at Mantua.

"Don't let me die!" some of these poor men cried out. They would seize my hand with extraordinary strength, and then die as soon as that false strength left them. A young corporal of about twenty, with a gentle, expressive face, named Claudius Mazuet, has taken a bullet in the left side. His condition is past all hope, and he understands it himself. After I have helped him drink he thanks me, and with tears in his eyes he adds, "Oh, sir, if you could write to my father, so he can comfort my mother!" I took down his parents' address, and a few moments later he had stopped living. An old sergeant, with several service stripes on his sleeve, said to me with deep sadness, with conviction and a cold bitterness, "If they'd seen to me sooner, I could have lived, but tonight I'll be dead!" By evening he was dead.

"I don't want to die, I don't want to die!" roared a grenadier of the Guard with savage energy. Three days before he had been full of strength and vigor, but now he was mortally wounded, and he felt clearly that his time was running out for good, and he fought and struggled against that dark certainty. I speak to him; he listens to me; and this man, soothed, quieted, comforted, at last resigns himself to dying with the simplicity and innocence of a child. Look over there, at the back of the church, in the recess of an altar on the left: a soldier of the Chasseurs d'Afrique lying on straw. He does not complain and hardly moves any more. Three bullets have hit him, one in the right side, one in the left shoulder, and the third is still lodged in his right leg. It is Sunday evening, and he says he has eaten nothing since Friday morning. He is disgusting with dried mud and clots of blood; his clothes are torn and his shirt is in rags. After I have washed his wounds, given him a little broth and wrapped him in a blanket, he lifts my hand to his lips with a look of gratitude beyond words. At the entrance of the church is a Hungarian who cries out without stopping, calling in Italian, in a heartrending voice, for a doctor. His back has been plowed up by fragments of grapeshot, as though furrowed by iron hooks, and a great expanse of red, quivering flesh lies exposed. The rest of his swollen body is black and greenish. He can find no way to rest or sit. I soak masses of lint in cool water and try to make him a bed of it, but gangrene will soon carry him off. A little farther on is a zouave, crying hot tears, who has to be comforted like a little child. Their earlier hardships, the lack of food and rest, a feverish excitement and the fear of dying with no one to help them were at that moment producing, even in fearless soldiers, a nervous sensitivity that came out in groans and sobs. One of the thoughts that filled their minds most, when their pain was not too cruel, was the memory of their mothers, and dread of the grief their mothers would feel on hearing what had happened to them. The body of a young man was found with a portrait of an elderly woman, his mother no doubt, hanging around his neck; with his left hand he still seemed to be pressing the locket to his heart.

Here, along the wall, about a hundred French privates and noncommissioned officers, each wrapped in his blanket, lie close together in two parallel rows, with room to walk between them. They have all been dressed, the soup has been handed round, and they are calm and peaceful. They follow me with their eyes, and all those heads turn to the right if I go right and to the left if I go left. "You can tell he's a Parisian," some say. "No," others answer, "he looks to me like a southerner." "You're from Bordeaux, aren't you, sir?" a third man asks me, and each of them wants me to be from his own province or his own town. The patience these ordinary soldiers of the line usually showed is remarkable and deserves attention. Taken one by one, what was each of them in this great upheaval? Very little. They suffered without complaining; they died humbly and quietly.

Author's note: I had the satisfaction, in the course of the following year, of meeting in Paris, especially in the Rue de Rivoli, soldiers who had lost limbs and been invalided out. They recognized me and stopped me to thank me for having looked after them at Castiglione. "We called you the gentleman in white," one of them told me, "because you were dressed all in white — and no wonder, it was hot enough in there!"

The wounded Austrian prisoners rarely tried to defy their conquerors. Some, though, refuse care because they distrust it; they tear off their bandages and make their wounds bleed. A Croat took the bullet that had just been extracted from him and threw it at the surgeon's forehead. Others remain silent, gloomy and impassive. In general they do not have the openness, the goodwill and the lively, sociable expressiveness that mark the men of the Latin peoples. Still, most of them are far from unmoved or ungrateful for good treatment, and sincere gratitude shows on their astonished faces. One of them, nineteen years old, has been pushed with forty or so of his countrymen into the farthest corner of the church and has had no food for three days. He has lost an eye, he is shaking with fever and can no longer speak, and he barely has the strength to take a little broth. Our care brought him round, and twenty-four hours later, when he could be sent on to Brescia, he left us with regret, almost with anguish. His remaining eye, which was a magnificent blue, showed his deep and lively gratitude, and he pressed the hands of the charitable women of Castiglione to his lips. Another prisoner, burning with fever, catches the eye. He is not yet twenty, and his hair is completely white. It turned white on the day of the battle, so his comrades and he himself declare.

How many young men of eighteen to twenty, who have come sadly all this way from the depths of Germany or from the eastern provinces of the vast Austrian Empire, some of them perhaps by force, roughly dragged off, will have to bear not only their bodily pain and the grief of captivity but also the ill will born of the hatred the Milanese feel for their race, their officers and their sovereign, and will meet with little sympathy before they reach the soil of France! Poor mothers, in Germany, in Austria, in Hungary, in Bohemia, how can one help thinking of your anguish when you learn that your wounded sons are prisoners in this enemy land! But the women of Castiglione, seeing that I make no distinction of nationality, follow my example and show the same kindness to all these men of such different origins, who are all equally foreign to them. "Tutti fratelli," they kept saying with feeling: "All brothers." Honor to these compassionate women, these young girls of Castiglione! Nothing put them off, nothing wore them out or discouraged them, and their modest devotion took no account of fatigue, or disgust, or sacrifice.

The feeling of one's own great inadequacy in such extraordinary and solemn circumstances is a suffering beyond words. It is extremely painful, in fact, never to be able to relieve the men in front of you, nor to reach the ones calling for you and begging, as long hours go by before you get where you want to go, stopped by one man, entreated by another, held back at every step by the crowd of unfortunates who press around you and surround you. And then, why go to the right, when on the left there are so many who are going to die without a friendly word, without a word of comfort, without so much as a glass of water to quench their burning thirst? The moral sense of how much a man's life matters, the wish to ease a little the torments of so many sufferers or to raise their fallen spirits, the forced, ceaseless activity you impose on yourself at such moments, all give you a new and supreme energy that creates something like a real thirst to bring help to as many as possible. You are no longer moved by the thousand scenes of this tremendous and solemn tragedy. You walk past the most hideously disfigured corpses with indifference. You look almost coldly on scenes even more horrible than those described here, which the pen absolutely refuses to describe. But sometimes the heart suddenly breaks, as if struck all at once by a bitter, overpowering sadness, at the sight of some simple incident, some single fact, some unexpected detail that goes more directly to the soul, that seizes our sympathy and shakes every one of the most sensitive fibers of our being.

Author's note: Since it was only after more than three years that I decided to gather up these painful memories, which I had never meant to publish, it will be understood that they had already faded somewhat, and also that they are abridged where the scenes of pain and desolation I witnessed are concerned. But if these pages could raise, or advance and press forward, the question of the relief to be given to wounded soldiers in wartime, or of the immediate care to be lavished on them after a battle; if they could draw the attention of people of humanity and goodwill; in a word, if concern for this most important subject, and study of it, could carry it a few steps forward and so improve a state of affairs in which new progress and improvements can never be too many, even in the best-organized armies, then I will have fully achieved my aim.

For the soldier who has gone back to the daily life of an army in the field, after the great hardships and powerful emotions that the day of a battle like Solferino and the day after put him through, the memory of home and family becomes more vivid and more stirring than ever. That state of mind is painted vividly in these touching lines from a brave French officer, writing from Volta to a brother at home in France: "You can't imagine how moved a soldier is when he sees the mail orderly appear, the man who hands out the army's letters. You see, he brings us news of France, of home, of our families and our friends. Everyone listens, watches, and holds out eager hands to him. The lucky ones, who have a letter, tear it open and devour it at once. The others, the left-out ones, walk away with heavy hearts and go off by themselves to think of the people back home. Sometimes a name is called and no one answers. We look at one another, ask each other, wait. 'Dead!' a voice murmurs. And the mail orderly puts that letter away, and it will go back unopened to the people who wrote it. They were happy when they wrote it. They said to themselves, 'How glad he'll be when he gets it!' And when they see it come back, their poor hearts will break."
