The Road to Brescia

The streets of Castiglione are quieter now. Deaths and departures have made room, and although new carts of wounded keep arriving, order is gradually established and the services begin to run regularly. The crowding was never the result of bad organization or a lack of foresight on the part of the authorities. It came from the unheard-of and unexpected number of men hit, and from the very small number, by comparison, of doctors, attendants and orderlies. The convoys from Castiglione to Brescia are more regular. They are made up either of hospital wagons or of crude carts drawn by oxen, which walk slowly, very slowly, under a blazing sun and through dust so deep that a man on foot sinks in those shifting, solid waves to above the ankle. Even when these very uncomfortable vehicles are covered with branches, the branches give only a poor shelter from the heat of a fiery sky to the wounded men, who are piled, so to speak, on top of each other. One can imagine the torture of that long journey! A friendly nod to these poor men as you pass them seems to do them good, and they return it eagerly, with a look of gratitude. In every village along the road to Brescia the village women sit at their doors, silently making lint. When a convoy comes in they climb up onto the carts, change the compresses, wash the wounds, put on fresh lint soaked in cool water, and spoon broth, wine or lemonade into the mouths of those who no longer have the strength to raise their heads or their arms. The wagons that keep bringing food, fodder, ammunition and supplies of every kind from France or Piedmont to the French camp do not go back empty: they take the sick to Brescia. In all the villages the convoys pass through, the local authorities have drinks, bread and meat made ready. At Montechiaro the town's three small hospitals are staffed by peasant women of the place, who look after the wounded there with as much intelligence as kindness. At Guidizzolo about a thousand of them have been properly settled in a huge castle, though only for the time being, and at Volta an old convent turned into a barracks takes in hundreds of Austrians. At Cavriana, Austrians who were badly crippled, and who had been left lying for forty-eight hours under the arcades of a wretched guardhouse, have been moved into the main church of that poor little town. At the field hospital of general headquarters, operations are performed with chloroform, which made the Austrian wounded lose feeling almost at once, but in the French brought on nervous spasms with great excitement.

The people of Cavriana have no food or supplies left at all; it is the soldiers of the Guard who feed them, sharing their rations and their mess tins with them. The countryside has been laid waste, and nearly every kind of produce that could be eaten has either been sold to the Austrian troops or seized by them as requisitions. The French army has plenty of field rations, thanks to the good sense and punctuality of its supply administration, but it has a hard time finding the butter, fat and vegetables usually added to the soldier's daily fare. The Austrians had requisitioned nearly all the livestock in the region, and cornmeal is the only thing the Allies can easily find in the places where they are now camped. All the same, anything the Lombard peasants can still sell to help feed the troops is bought from them at very high prices, always reckoned so as to satisfy the seller. And the French army's requisitions, of fodder, potatoes or other foodstuffs, have everywhere been paid for generously to the people of the country, who were likewise generously compensated for the unavoidable damage the fighting had done.

The wounded of the Sardinian army, who have been taken to Desenzano, Rivoltella, Lonato and Pozzolengo, are in less unfavorable conditions than those at Castiglione. The first two towns had not been occupied, a few days apart, by two different armies, so more food can be found there. The field hospitals are well kept; the people, less shaken and less frightened, give the nursing service active help; and the sick who are sent on from there to Brescia travel in good carts with a thick layer of hay, shaded from the sun by arbors of leafy, interwoven branches firmly fastened to the carts, with a strong canvas stretched over the top as well.

Worn out with fatigue and no longer able to sleep, I have my cabriolet harnessed on the afternoon of the 27th, and at about six o'clock I set out to breathe the cool evening air in the open and get a little rest, getting away for a while from the grim scenes that surround you on every side in Castiglione. It was a good day for it, since no troop movements, as I learned later, had been ordered for that Monday. So calm had followed the terrible upheavals of the days before on that battlefield, so melancholy now, which passion and excitement had entirely abandoned. But here and there you see pools of dried blood reddening the ground, and freshly turned earth, whitened and sprinkled with lime, marking the places where the victims of the 24th lie. At Solferino, whose square tower has looked down for centuries, unmoved and proud, on this country where two of the greatest powers of modern times had just clashed for the third time, many sad remnants of the battle are still being picked up; they cover even the crosses and bloodstained gravestones in the cemetery. I reached Cavriana at about nine o'clock. The array of war around the headquarters of the Emperor of the French was a unique and magnificent sight. I was looking for Marshal MacMahon, the Duke of Magenta, whom I had the honor of knowing personally. Not knowing exactly where his corps was camped at that moment, I had my cabriolet stop in a little square opposite the house where the Emperor Napoleon had been staying since Friday evening, and I came unexpectedly upon a group of generals who, sitting on plain straw chairs or wooden stools, were smoking their cigars and enjoying the cool air in front of their sovereign's makeshift palace. While I was asking which way Marshal MacMahon had been sent, these generals for their part questioned the corporal who was with me. He sat up on the box of my carriage beside the coachman, so they took him for my orderly. They wanted to know who I was, and to find out the purpose of the mission they assumed I had been given, for it could hardly occur to them that a mere tourist had taken it into his head to venture alone among the armies, and that, having come as far as Cavriana, he meant to go farther at so late an hour. The corporal, who knew no more than that, naturally gave nothing away, while answering their questions most respectfully, and their curiosity seemed to grow when they saw me set off again for Borghetto, where the Duke of Magenta was supposed to be. The Second Corps, which the marshal commanded, had been ordered on the 26th to move from Cavriana to Castellaro, about three miles away, and its divisions were camped on both sides of the road from Castellaro to Monzambano; the marshal himself, with his staff, was at Borghetto. But the night had worn on. Our directions were rather incomplete, and after an hour on the road we take a wrong turn. Following a road that leads to Volta, we end up crossing right through the corps of General Niel, who had been made a marshal three days before and was camped around that little town. The vague murmurs that rise under that beautiful starry sky, the bivouac fires fed with whole trees, the lighted tents of the officers, in a word the last sounds of a camp keeping watch and falling asleep, are pleasantly restful to an imagination that has been strained and overexcited. The shadows of evening and a solemn silence have taken the place of the many noises and emotions of the day, and the pure, soft air of a splendid Italian night is a delight to breathe.

Author's note: This corporal, who had been wounded at Magenta and was rejoining his battalion after his convalescence, had worked very hard at Castiglione to help the orderlies. I accepted his offer to come with me on this trip through the armies, since at such a moment his standing as a noncommissioned officer could serve me as a safe-conduct. That same day, the 27th, two Englishmen who had tried to venture inside the French lines were taken by soldiers for German spies and roughly handled all through the camp they had so unluckily blundered into, until, by good fortune for them, they met the marshal commanding the corps, who promptly put an end to a misadventure that our two islanders, for their part, were delighted with.

As for my Italian coachman, he was in such a fright in that half-darkness, and at the idea of being so close to the enemy, that more than once I had to take the reins from him and give them to the corporal or take them myself. The poor man had fled from Mantua eight or ten days before to escape service in the Austrian army. He had taken refuge in Brescia to try to earn a living there and had found work with a carriage master who employed him as a coachman. His great fear had grown a good deal worse after an Austrian fired a shot at us from a distance, discharging his musket as he ran off at our approach and disappeared into the undergrowth. During the Austrian army's retreat some soldiers had hidden in the cellars of houses in little villages that their inhabitants had abandoned and that were half sacked. These unlucky fugitives, alone and trembling, had first fed and watered themselves as best they could in those cellars, and then slipped away into the fields, where they wandered aimlessly at night. The man from Mantua could not be reassured, and he could no longer drive his horse in a straight line at all. He kept turning his head from left to right and right to left, searching every bush along the road with his wild eyes, afraid at every moment of seeing some Austrian lying in ambush there, ready to take aim at him. He peered in terror at every hedge and every hovel, and his fears doubled at the slightest bend in the road. His fright turned into indescribable terror when the deep silence of the night was suddenly broken by another shot, from a mounted sentry whom the darkness had kept us from seeing, and he nearly fainted at the sight of a large umbrella, wide open, pierced by three cannonballs and several bullets, which appeared before us at the edge of a field near the lane to Volta. The umbrella had probably been part of the baggage of some cantinière of the French army, and the storm of the 24th had carried it off.

We had turned back to find the right road to Borghetto. It was past eleven, we were driving our horse at a gallop as fast as we could, and our modest little carriage was flying silently along the Strada Cavallara when another alarm took us by surprise. "Who goes there? Who goes there? Who goes there, or I fire!" shouts a mounted sentry at point-blank range, without stopping for breath. "France!" answers our soldier at once, giving his rank at the same time: "Corporal, First Engineers, Seventh Company..." "Pass on, keep your distance," came the reply. At last, at a quarter to midnight, with no more encounters, we reach the first houses of Borghetto. Everything there is silent and dark, but in the main street a little light is shining on a ground floor, and in a low room some paymasters are at work. Though they are interrupted at their work and very surprised by so unexpected an apparition at such an hour, they are full of courtesy, and one of them, M. A. Outrey, a paymaster, offered me cordial hospitality even before seeing that I carried various letters of introduction from generals. His orderly brought a mattress, and I threw myself down on it fully dressed to rest for a few hours, after having an excellent bowl of broth, which seemed all the more restoring because for many days I had eaten nothing worth the name. And I slept peacefully, without being suffocated, as at Castiglione, by unhealthy fumes and pestered by the flies, which, glutted on corpses, still came to torment the living. The corporal and the coachman had settled down in the cabriolet, which stayed out in the street. But the unhappy man from Mantua, in a constant state of dread, could not shut his eyes all night, and in the morning I found him more dead than alive.

Author's note: Borghetto is a village of about two thousand inhabitants on the right bank of the Mincio, roughly opposite Valeggio. In 1848 the Sardinian troops under King Charles Albert crossed the Mincio there, in spite of the vigorous resistance of the Austrians under Field Marshal Radetzky.

On the 28th, at six in the morning, I was given the kindest and friendliest welcome by the good and chivalrous Marshal MacMahon, so rightly called the idol of his soldiers. At ten o'clock I was in that house at Cavriana which is now historic, since between the morning and the evening of the 24th it had received two great enemy monarchs. At three in the afternoon that same day I was back among the wounded of Castiglione, who told me how glad they were to see me again, and on the 30th of June I was in Brescia.

Author's note: The Duke of Magenta is much loved in the French army; his soldiers have as much affection as reverence for him. Here is an example. In 1856, in Algeria, on the road to Constantine, two former zouaves were riding inside a stagecoach while I had the front compartment. They were going to Batna as laborers, to cut trees in the forests. They were talking about the war in the East and about Marshal MacMahon in their colorful way of speaking, and a few of their sentences reached me. "That general," one of them said, "is there another like him? Now he knew how to command us! We're old soldiers, old grumblers, we were never afraid, and yet we cried. Do you remember when he talked to us out on the plain, when we were being discharged, when our time was up, when he said goodbye to us and told us, 'My children, you have served bravely under the flag. You are going back into civilian life. Never do anything low. Remember that you have a father, and that father is me!' — he said that striking his chest — 'and my purse is yours. All of you, shake my hand.' Do you remember when he threw us his purse full of gold and said, 'Share it, and whatever you do, don't quarrel!' And we all cried like little girls."

That city, so charming and so picturesque, has been turned not into a big makeshift field hospital like Castiglione but into one immense hospital. Its two cathedrals, its churches, its palaces, its convents, its schools, its barracks, in a word all its buildings are crowded with the victims of Solferino. Fifteen thousand beds were set up there practically overnight. The generous people of the city did more than had ever been done anywhere in the face of such events. In the middle of the city the old basilica called the Duomo Vecchio, or the Rotonda, with its two chapels, holds about a thousand wounded. The people flock to them, and women of every class bring them oranges, jellies, biscuits, candies and treats in abundance. The humble widow and the poorest little old woman do not think themselves excused from coming in person to offer their tribute of sympathy and their modest gift. The same scenes take place in the new cathedral, a magnificent temple of white marble with a vast dome, where hundreds of wounded are gathered, and they are repeated in the forty other buildings, churches or hospitals, which between them hold nearly twenty thousand wounded and sick.

The city government of Brescia rose at once to the extraordinary duties that such solemn circumstances laid on it, and it stayed worthily equal to them. It sat in permanent session, and surrounded itself with the knowledge and advice of the most distinguished citizens, who gave it effective support. On the proposal of the eminent Dr. Bartolomeo Gualla, it appointed a central commission to take overall charge of the hospitals. He was its president, and its members were Drs. Corbolani, Orefici, Ballini, Bonicelli, Cassa, C. Maggi and Abeni, who worked with admirable energy and spared themselves no effort, day or night. This commission put a special administrator and a chief surgeon at the head of each hospital, with a few doctors and a number of orderlies to assist them. By having a convent, a school or a church opened, it created, within a few hours and as if by magic, hospitals with hundreds of beds, a large kitchen and a laundry, stocked with linen and with everything that could be useful or necessary. These measures were taken so promptly and so wholeheartedly that within a few days people marveled at the good order and smooth running of these many hospitals. The astonishment was natural enough when one considers that the population of Brescia, forty thousand people, was suddenly almost doubled by more than thirty thousand wounded and sick. And how can I fail to recall here that the doctors, a hundred and forty of them, showed throughout their duties, as difficult as they were exhausting, a sublime energy and devotion, without any touchiness or rivalry of any kind ever disturbing for a moment their harmony in working for the common good? They were assisted by medical students and by a small number of volunteers. Auxiliary committees were organized; one special commission was appointed to receive donations and gifts of bedding, linen and supplies of every kind, and another was put in charge of the central depot or storehouse.

Author's note: From the 15th of June to the 31st of August, according to official figures, 19,665 soldiers were admitted to the hospitals of Brescia with fevers and illnesses alone, of whom more than 19,000 belonged to the French and Sardinian army. The Austrians, for their part, counted at least 20,000 sick in their hospitals in Venetia, not to mention the mass of wounded who were also being cared for there.

Author's note: The first of these commissions consisted of Messrs. Pallavicini, Glisenti, Averoldi and Sienna, the lawyers Zuccoli and Conter, and Canon Rossa; the second of Messrs. Basiletti, Caprioli, Rovetta and Da Ponte.
