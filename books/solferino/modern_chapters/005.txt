Milan

The wounded are moved from Brescia to Milan by night, because of the scorching heat of the day, and it is a sight as dramatic as it is gripping: the trains loaded with mutilated soldiers; their arrival at the stations, packed with a silent, somber crowd lit by the pale glow of resin torches; and that dense crowd, quivering with emotion and sympathy at once, seeming to hold its breath to listen to the groans and stifled moans that reach it from the grim convoys.

On the railway from Milan to Venice, the Austrians, as they fell back step by step during the month of June as far as Lake Garda, had cut the stretch of line running from Milan to Brescia and Peschiera at several points. But the line had been quickly repaired and reopened to traffic, so as to speed the transport of equipment, ammunition and food for the allied army and to allow the hospitals of Brescia to be emptied.

Author's note: This was due above all to the energy and drive of M. Charles Brot, a banker in Milan, the only member of the board of the Lombard-Venetian Railways who had stayed in the city.

At every station long, narrow sheds had been built to receive the wounded, who were laid, as they came out of the cars, on beds or on plain mattresses set out in rows one after another. Under these sheds stand tables piled with bread, broth, wine and above all water, as well as lint and bandages, which are needed constantly and without end. A host of torches, held by the young men of whatever town the train has stopped in, drive away the darkness, and the Lombard townspeople, turned into nurses for the occasion, hurry to bring their tribute of respect and gratitude to the victors of Solferino. Without fuss, in a reverent silence, they dress the wounded or carry them out of the cars with a father's care and lay them gently on the beds they have made ready. The ladies of the town offer cooling drinks and food of every kind, which they hand round in the cars to those who are well on the way to recovery and are to continue their journey or go on to Milan.

In Milan, where about a thousand wounded arrive at the Brescia station every night for several nights in a row, the martyrs of Solferino are received as those of Magenta and Marignano had been: with an eagerness and an affection whose persistence never tires.

Author's note: By the middle of June 1859, and so before Solferino, the hospitals of Milan already held some nine thousand wounded from the earlier battles. The Ospedale Maggiore, the great civic hospital (founded in the fifteenth century by Bianca Visconti, wife of Duke Sforza), held nearly three thousand of them by itself.

From the flag-hung balconies of the splendid palaces of the Milanese aristocracy, the graceful and lovely young ladies of the patrician families, made lovelier still by the exaltation of a passionate enthusiasm, had showered rose petals onto glittering epaulets and onto crosses of gold and enamel on watered-silk ribbon. They scatter rose petals no longer. Now what falls is burning tears, the sign of a painful shock and of a compassion that is about to turn into a Christian devotion, patient and full of self-denial.

Every family that has a carriage comes to fetch wounded men from the station, and the number of these carriages, sent of their own accord by the people of Milan, is perhaps more than five hundred. The richest barouches and the humblest carts head every evening to the Porta Tosa, where the station of the Venice railway is. The noble Italian ladies make it a point of honor to settle with their own hands, in carriages they have fitted out with mattresses, sheets and pillows, the guests who fall to their lot, and who have been carried from the railway cars to these splendid coaches by the Lombard gentlemen themselves, helped in this task by their servants, who are just as eager. The crowd cheers these men, privileged by their suffering, as they pass, respectfully bares its head, and escorts the slow procession of carriages with torches that light up the melancholy faces of the wounded, who try to smile. It goes with them as far as the doors of the palaces and the hospitable houses where the most attentive care is waiting for them.

Every family wants to have French wounded in its home, and tries in every kind of kind way to make up for the loss of country, family and friends. In private houses as in the hospitals, the best doctors are at work on them. The greatest ladies of Milan show them a care as courageous as it is lasting. They sit up at the bedside of the private soldier as well as of the officer, with the most unshakable constancy. Signora Uboldi de Capei, Signora Boselli, Signora Sala, born Countess Taverna, and many other noble ladies, forgetting their elegant and comfortable habits, spend whole months beside the sickbeds, where they become the guardian angels of the patients. All these kindnesses are given without show. And this care, these comforts, these attentions at every moment, have every right, along with the gratitude of the families of the men who received them, to everyone's respectful admiration. Some of these ladies were mothers whose mourning clothes showed painful and very recent losses. Let me recall, on this point, the sublime words one of them confided to Dr. Bertherand. "The war has taken my eldest son from me," the Marchioness L... told him. "He died eight months ago from the effects of a bullet he received fighting alongside your army at Sebastopol. When I learned that wounded Frenchmen were arriving in Milan, and that I could nurse them, I felt that God was sending me His first consolation..."

Author's note: Within a few days most of the people of Milan had to send the sick soldiers they had taken into their homes back to the hospitals, because the authorities wanted to avoid scattering the relief that had to be given, and to spare the doctors the extra strain, since they could not keep up with so many house calls. The overall supervision of the city's hospitals was entrusted to Dr. Cuvellier, who carried out with credit the difficult task given him by the chief surgeon of the Army of Italy. After the battle of Solferino the chief surgeon was given powerful support by Signor Faraldo, Intendant General of the province of Brescia, whose admirable energy in these grave circumstances, and whose high-minded feelings, deserve the greatest praise. When the French army left Milan in the middle of June to march on Brescia, it left behind it shelter ready for more than eight thousand wounded. The good organization of the French army from the humanitarian point of view must be mentioned, and it was due especially to His Excellency Marshal Randon, the Minister of War, to Marshal Vaillant, chief of staff of the Army of Italy, and to General de Martimprey, deputy chief of staff of the army.

Countess Verri-Borromeo, president of the great central relief committee, insisted on directing the movement of the stores of linen and lint herself, and in spite of her great age she also found time to spend several hours every day reading aloud to the wounded. Every palace holds sick men; the palace of the Borromeo family, the family of the islands in Lake Maggiore, holds three hundred. The mother superior of the Ursulines, Sister Marina Videmari, runs with exemplary charity a large hospital that is a model of order and cleanliness, and she and her sisters do all the work in it themselves.

Author's note: Countess Giustina Verri, born Borromeo, died in Milan in 1860, deeply mourned by everyone who had the good fortune to know her. The great storehouses of lint and bandages in the Contrada San Paolo, which she ran with great intelligence, were stocked by large shipments from various cities and countries, but above all from Turin, where the Marchioness Pallavicino-Trivulzio was doing work like Signora Verri's in Milan. Geneva and other Swiss towns, and towns in Savoy as well, sent very large quantities of linen and lint to Turin through Dr. Appia, who had taken the lead in this excellent work in Geneva. Fairly large sums of money were used to provide the wounded, without distinction of nationality, with all sorts of small comforts. With this aim the Countess de G... called for a committee to be formed, and her proposal, which was well received in Paris, was begun in Geneva. From that neutral country, where sympathies were naturally divided between the two warring armies, relief was sent to the official committees in Turin and Milan, to be shared out impartially among the French, the Germans and the Italians. In Turin the Marchioness Pallavicino-Trivulzio, so kind, so generous and so devoted, presided over the principal committee (the Ladies' Committee for the Collection of Bandages and Lint for the Wounded) with all the energy so great a task required. Other committees had been formed in Turin, whose people had shown themselves very well disposed toward the victims of the war.

But little by little you see small detachments of convalescent French soldiers passing by on the road to Turin, their faces bronzed by the Italian sun, some with an arm in a sling, others on crutches, all with the marks of serious wounds. Their regulation uniforms are worn and torn, but they are wearing magnificent linen, which wealthy Italians have generously given them in exchange for their bloodstained shirts. "Your blood was shed in defense of our country," these Italians told them. "We want to keep it in remembrance." These men, strong and sturdy a few weeks before, and now missing an arm or a leg, or with their heads bandaged and bleeding, bear their sufferings with resignation. But they will never again be able to follow a soldier's career or to help their families, and already, with painful bitterness, they see themselves becoming objects of pity and charity, a burden to others and to themselves.

I cannot help mentioning a man I met in Milan on my way back from Solferino, a venerable old gentleman, the Marquis Ch. de Bryas, a former deputy and former mayor of Bordeaux. He had a very large fortune, and he had come to Italy of his own accord with the sole aim of being of use to the wounded soldiers. I was lucky enough to be able to help this noble philanthropist leave for Brescia. During the first half of July the confusion and crowding at the Porta Tosa station, where I went with him, were such that it was unbelievably hard to get to the cars. In spite of his age, his position and his official character (for he had just, if I am not mistaken, been given a purely charitable mission by the French authorities), he could not manage to get a seat on the train he was supposed to take. This little incident may give some idea of the enormous crowds that blocked the station and the streets around it.

How many interesting facts will always remain unknown! One fine story is that of another Frenchman, almost deaf, who had also traveled some seven hundred and fifty miles to come and nurse his countrymen. When he reached Milan and saw the Austrian wounded more or less neglected, he devoted himself to them in particular and tried to do them every possible good, in return for the harm an Austrian officer had done him forty-five years earlier. In 1814, when the armies of the Holy Alliance had invaded France, this officer had been billeted on the Frenchman's parents. The Frenchman, then very young, was suffering from an illness of a kind that disgusted the foreign soldier, and the officer threw the poor child roughly out of the house, and no one could stop him. That act of brutality left the boy with a deafness he suffered from all his life.

In one of the hospitals of Milan, a sergeant of the zouaves of the Guard, a man with a strong, proud face, had had a leg amputated and had borne that cruel operation without a single complaint. A little later he was seized by a deep sadness, although his condition was improving and his convalescence was going well, so that this sadness, which grew from day to day, seemed impossible to explain. A Sister of Charity even caught sight of tears in those eyes, which had perhaps never wept before, and she pressed him with so many questions that he finally confessed to the good nun that he was the only support of his elderly, infirm mother. When he was well, he sent her five francs every month, saved out of his pay. Now he found it impossible to help her, and she must be in great need of money, since he had not been able to send her the small allowance she was used to. The Sister of Charity, moved with pity, gave him a five-franc piece, and its value was sent off to France at once. But when the Countess T..., who had taken an interest in this brave and worthy soldier and had been told the cause of his unusual sadness, wanted to give him a small sum for his mother and for himself, he refused to accept it. Thanking the lady, he answered, "Keep this money for others who need it more than I do. And as for my mother, I fully expect to send her allowance next month, because I'm counting on being able to work soon."

One of the great ladies of Milan, who bore a historic name, had put one of her palaces at the disposal of the wounded, with a hundred and fifty beds. Among the soldiers lodged in that magnificent palace was a grenadier of the 70th Regiment who had had an amputation and was in danger of dying. The lady, trying to comfort the wounded man, talked to him about his family. He told her he was the only son of peasants in the department of the Gers, in the southwest of France, and that his whole sorrow was leaving them in deep poverty, since he alone could have supported them. He added that it would have been a great comfort to him to embrace his mother before he died. Without telling him what she meant to do, the lady makes up her mind on the spot to leave Milan. She takes the train, goes to the Gers to find the family, whose address she has obtained, leaves two thousand francs with the old, infirm father, takes the wounded man's mother and brings the poor peasant woman back with her to Milan. And six days after the lady's conversation with the grenadier, the son was embracing his mother, weeping and blessing his benefactress.
