The Battle

The bloody victory at Magenta had opened the city of Milan to the French army and raised the enthusiasm of the Italians to fever pitch. Pavia, Lodi and Cremona had watched their liberators arrive and welcomed them with joy. The Austrians had abandoned their lines along the Adda, the Oglio and the Chiese, one river after another. Now, determined at last to take a dazzling revenge for their earlier defeats, they had massed large forces on the banks of the Mincio, and at their head, resolute, rode the young and valiant Emperor of Austria himself.

On the 17th of June King Victor Emmanuel reached Brescia. The people there had lived under oppression for ten long years, and they gave him the warmest of ovations; in the son of Charles Albert they saw a savior and a hero at once.

The next day the Emperor Napoleon made his own triumphal entry into the same city, in the middle of a whole people's rapture. They were glad of the chance to show their gratitude to the sovereign who had come to help them win back their freedom and independence.

On the 21st of June the Emperor of the French and the King of Sardinia left Brescia, which their armies had marched out of the day before. On the 22nd Lonato, Castenedolo and Montechiaro were occupied. On the evening of the 23rd the Emperor, who was in supreme command, gave precise orders for the morning of the 24th. King Victor Emmanuel's army, camped at Desenzano and forming the left wing of the allied army, was to advance on Pozzolengo. Marshal Baraguey d'Hilliers was to march on Solferino; Marshal MacMahon, Duke of Magenta, on Cavriana; General Niel was to go to Guidizzolo, and Marshal Canrobert to Medole; the Imperial Guard was to go to Castiglione. Together these forces came to one hundred and fifty thousand men and four hundred pieces of artillery.

The Emperor of Austria had nine army corps at his disposal in Lombardy, two hundred and fifty thousand men in all, for his invading army had been swollen by the garrisons of Verona and Mantua. On the advice of the Feldzeugmeister Baron Hess (a Feldzeugmeister was the Austrian rank just below field marshal), the imperial troops had in fact been retreating steadily ever since Milan and Brescia. The aim of that retreat was to concentrate, between the Adige and the Mincio, every soldier Austria then had in Italy. But the force that was about to go into the line of battle was only seven corps: one hundred and seventy thousand men, backed by about five hundred guns.

The Austrian imperial headquarters had moved from Verona to Villafranca, and then to Valeggio, and the troops were ordered to cross back over the Mincio at Peschiera, Salionze, Valeggio, Ferri, Goito and Mantua. The main body of the army took up quarters along a line from Pozzolengo to Guidizzolo. Several of the most experienced lieutenant field marshals had urged it, and the plan was to attack the Franco-Sardinian army between the Mincio and the Chiese.

The Austrian forces, under the Emperor's command, made up two armies. The first was led by the Feldzeugmeister Count Wimpffen, with the corps of Prince Edmund of Schwarzenberg, Count Schaffgotsche and Baron Veigl under him, as well as Count Zedtwitz's cavalry division. This was the left wing, and it had taken up position around Volta, Guidizzolo, Medole and Castel Goffredo. The second army was commanded by the cavalry general Count Schlick. Under him were the lieutenant field marshals Count Clam-Gallas, Count Stadion, Baron Zobel and the Chevalier Benedek, with Count Mensdorff's cavalry division. This was the right wing, and it held Cavriana, Solferino, Pozzolengo and San Martino.

So on the morning of the 24th the Austrians held every height between Pozzolengo, Solferino, Cavriana and Guidizzolo. They had set up their formidable artillery on a chain of small hills that formed the center of an immense attacking line. Their right and left wings could fall back, if they had to, under the shelter of those fortified heights, which they believed could not be taken.

The two enemy armies were marching toward each other, yet neither expected to run into the other so soon. The Austrians hoped that only part of the allied army had crossed the Chiese. They had no way of knowing what the Emperor Napoleon intended, and their information was wrong.

The Allies did not expect to meet the Emperor of Austria's army so suddenly either. On the 23rd there had been reconnaissance patrols, observations, reports from scouts and even ascents in hot-air balloons, and not one of them had shown any sign that the Austrians were turning back to attack.

And so, although both sides were waiting for a great battle soon, the meeting of the Austrians and the French and Sardinians on Friday the 24th of June came as a genuine surprise. Each had been mistaken about the other's movements.

Everyone has heard, or has read, some account of the battle of Solferino. Surely no one has forgotten that thrilling memory, all the more because the consequences of that day are still being felt in several of the states of Europe.

I was a mere tourist, a complete outsider to this great struggle, but by an unusual combination of circumstances I had the rare privilege of witnessing the moving scenes I have decided to set down here. In these pages I tell only my own personal impressions. Nobody should look here for technical details or strategic information; those have their place in other books.

On that memorable 24th of June more than three hundred thousand men stood face to face. The line of battle was about twelve miles long, and the fighting went on for more than fifteen hours.

The Austrian army had already worn itself out on a hard march all through the night of the 23rd. From the first light of the 24th it had to take the violent shock of the allied army, and then suffer in the stifling, excessive heat, and from hunger and thirst besides: apart from a double ration of brandy, these troops had almost nothing to eat all day Friday. The French army had been on the move since before dawn and had nothing but its morning coffee. So by the end of this terrible battle the exhaustion of the fighting men, and above all of the poor wounded, was extreme!

At about three in the morning the First and Second Corps, under Marshals Baraguey d'Hilliers and MacMahon, set off toward Solferino and Cavriana. But the heads of their columns had barely passed Castiglione when they found Austrian outposts in front of them, contesting the ground.

Both armies are on the alert.

On every side the bugles sound the charge and the drums roll.

The Emperor Napoleon, who has spent the night at Montechiaro, hurries toward Castiglione.

By six o'clock the firing has begun in earnest.

The Austrians advance along the open roads in perfect order. In the middle of their dense, white-coated masses fly their yellow-and-black standards, emblazoned with the imperial eagle of Germany.

Of all the army corps about to join the fight, the French Guard is a truly magnificent sight. The day is brilliant, and the splendid Italian sun makes the gleaming armor of the dragoons, the guides, the lancers and the cuirassiers flash.

From the very start of the action the Emperor Franz Joseph had left his headquarters with his whole staff and gone to Volta. With him were the archdukes of the House of Lorraine, among them the Grand Duke of Tuscany and the Duke of Modena.

The first clash takes place on ground the Allies know nothing about, and it is difficult ground. The French army must first force its way through rows of mulberry trees laced together with grapevines, which make real obstacles. The land is often cut by great dry ditches and by long walls three to five feet high, very broad at the base and narrowing toward the top. The horses have to scramble over the walls and leap the ditches.

The Austrians, posted on the rises and the hills, at once pound the French army with their artillery, raining down an unending hail of shells, bombs and cannonballs.

The thick clouds of smoke from the cannon and the grapeshot mix with the earth and dust thrown up as that enormous cloud of projectiles strikes the ground again and again. Facing the thunder of these batteries, which roar as they spew death on them, the French burst out of the plain like a second storm and charge the positions they are determined to take.

But it is in the scorching heat of midday that the fighting, which now rages on every side, grows fiercer and fiercer.

Packed columns hurl themselves at one another with the force of a raging torrent that sweeps away everything in its path. French regiments rush in open order at the Austrian masses, which are endlessly replaced, always more numerous and more menacing, and which take the attack with all their strength, like walls of iron. Whole divisions drop their packs so as to throw themselves at the enemy more freely, bayonets forward. If one battalion is driven back, another takes its place at once. Every hillock, every height, every rocky ridge is the scene of a stubborn fight. The dead lie in heaps on the hills and in the ravines.

Here it is hand-to-hand fighting, horrible and terrifying. Austrians and Allies trample one another underfoot and kill each other on top of bleeding corpses. They club each other with their rifle butts, smash each other's skulls, rip each other open with saber and bayonet. No quarter is given any more. It is butchery, a fight between wild beasts, maddened and drunk with blood. Even the wounded defend themselves to the very last; a man with no weapon left seizes his enemy by the throat and tears it with his teeth.

There it is the same kind of struggle, made more frightening still by a squadron of cavalry coming on at a gallop. The horses crush the dead and the dying under their iron-shod hooves. One poor wounded man has his jaw torn away, another his head crushed, a third, who might have been saved, has his chest stove in. The neighing of horses mixes with oaths, with cries of rage, with howls of pain and despair.

Farther off comes the artillery, flung forward at full speed behind the cavalry. It cuts its way through the dead and wounded who lie together on the ground with no distinction between them. Brains spurt out, limbs are broken and ground to pieces, bodies are crushed beyond recognition. The earth literally drinks blood, and the plain is strewn with human remains.

The French troops climb the hillocks and scale the steep hills and rocky slopes with the most fiery eagerness, under Austrian musket fire and bursting shells and grapeshot. No sooner is a hillock taken, and a few elite companies have managed to reach its top, worn out and bathed in sweat, than they fall on the Austrians like an avalanche, overturn them, drive them from yet another post, push them back and chase them to the very bottom of the ravines and ditches.

The Austrian positions are excellent, entrenched as they are in the houses and churches of Medole, Solferino and Cavriana. But nothing stops the slaughter, or interrupts it, or lessens it. Men are killed wholesale and men are killed one at a time. Every fold in the ground is taken with the bayonet; every spot is fought over foot by foot. The villages are torn away house by house and farm by farm, and each one becomes a siege. Doors, windows and courtyards are nothing but a hideous jumble of men cutting one another's throats.

The French grapeshot causes frightful disorder in the Austrian masses, reaching them at astonishing distances. It covers the hillsides with corpses and carries destruction as far as the German army's distant reserves. But if the Austrians give ground, they give it only step by step, and soon take the offensive again. Their ranks re-form over and over, only to be broken through again soon after.

Out on the plain the wind lifts waves of dust from the roads, which are deep in it, and builds them into thick clouds that darken the air and blind the fighting men.

If the struggle seems to stop for a moment here or there, it is only to start again more fiercely. Fresh Austrian reserves fill the gaps that this attack, as stubborn as it is deadly, tears in their ranks. Now on one side, now on another, drums beat and bugles sound the charge without end.

The Guard fights with the noblest courage. The voltigeurs, the chasseurs and the infantry of the line beside them compete in bravery and daring. The zouaves charge with the bayonet, leaping like wild animals and shouting furious cries. The French cavalry falls on the Austrian cavalry; uhlans and hussars run each other through and cut each other to pieces. The horses, excited by the heat of the fight, join in the fury themselves: they hurl themselves at the enemy's horses and bite them savagely while their riders hack and slash at one another.

The fury is such that in some places, when the ammunition is gone and the muskets are broken, men beat one another to death with stones and fight hand to hand. The Croats butcher everyone they meet. They finish off the wounded of the allied army, killing them with blows of their rifle butts. And the Algerian riflemen, in spite of their officers' efforts to calm their ferocity, strike down the dying in the same way, Austrian officers and soldiers alike, and throw themselves on the opposing ranks with savage roars and terrifying cries.

The strongest positions are taken, lost, retaken, lost again and won back once more. Everywhere men fall by the thousands, mutilated, disemboweled, riddled with bullets, or struck down by every kind of missile.

As for the spectator posted on the heights around Castiglione, even if he cannot follow the plan of the battle exactly, he can still see that the Austrians are trying to break through the center of the allied troops. They want to slow and stop the attacks on Solferino, whose splendid position is about to make it the key point of the whole battle. He can guess at the efforts of the Emperor of the French to link the different corps of his army together, so that they can support and reinforce one another.

The Emperor Napoleon, with an eye as quick as it is skillful, sees that the Austrian troops lack a strong, unified direction. He orders the corps of Baraguey d'Hilliers and of MacMahon, and soon afterward his Guard, under the brave Marshal Regnaud de Saint-Jean d'Angély, to attack the entrenchments of Solferino and San Cassiano at the same time, and so to break the enemy's center. That center was made up of the corps of Stadion, Clam-Gallas and Zobel, which come up only one after another to defend these vital positions.

At San Martino the valiant and fearless Field Marshal Benedek, with only part of the second Austrian army, holds out all day against the Sardinian army. The Sardinians fight heroically under the command of their King, whose presence among them electrifies them.

The right wing of the allied army, made up of the corps of General Niel and Marshal Canrobert, resists with unbreakable energy the first German army, commanded by Count Wimpffen; and the three corps of that army, Schwarzenberg's, Schaffgotsche's and Veigl's, never manage to act together.

Marshal Canrobert follows the Emperor Napoleon's orders to the letter and keeps a waiting position, for reasons that were entirely sound, and so he does not commit his available forces in the morning. In the end, though, most of his corps, the divisions of Renault and Trochu and General Partouneaux's cavalry, take a very lively part in the action.

At first Marshal Canrobert is held back by the expectation that the corps of Prince Edward of Liechtenstein will fall on him. That corps was not part of either Austrian army, but it had marched out of Mantua that very morning, and it worried the Emperor Napoleon. Liechtenstein's corps, in its turn, is completely paralyzed by fear that Prince Napoleon's corps is approaching, since Prince Napoleon's d'Autemarre division was on its way from Piacenza.

It was Generals Forey and de Ladmirault, with their valiant columns, who opened the fighting on that memorable day. After indescribable struggles they take the ridges and hills that lead up to the graceful hillock of the Cypresses. That hillock, together with the Tower and the cemetery of Solferino, is famous forever for the horrible slaughter of which these places were the glorious witnesses and the bloody stage. At last the Hill of the Cypresses is carried by storm, and on its summit Colonel d'Auvergne waves his handkerchief from the tip of his sword as a sign of victory.

But these successes cost the Allies dearly. A bullet shatters General de Ladmirault's shoulder. This heroic wounded man barely agrees to have it dressed at a field hospital set up in the chapel of a little hamlet, and in spite of how serious the wound is, he goes back to the fight on foot and keeps urging on his battalions, until a second bullet hits him in the left leg.

General Forey, calm and unshaken as ever in the middle of his difficult position, is wounded in the hip. The white cloak he wears over his uniform is pierced with bullets, and his aides-de-camp are struck down beside him. One of them, Captain de Kervenoël, twenty-five years old, has the top of his skull carried away by a shell fragment.

At the foot of the Hill of the Cypresses, as he is leading his skirmishers forward, General Dieu is thrown from his horse, mortally wounded. General Douay is wounded too, not far from his brother, Colonel Douay, who is killed. A cannonball shatters the left arm of the brigade commander, General Auger, and on this battlefield, which will cost him his life, he earns his promotion to the command of a division.

The French officers, always out in front, waving their swords in the air and leading the soldiers behind them by their example, are cut down in great numbers at the head of their battalions, where their decorations and their epaulets mark them out as targets for the Tyrolean riflemen.

So many dramas, so many incidents of every kind, so many moving turns of fortune!

In the First Regiment of Chasseurs d'Afrique, the French light cavalry raised in Algeria, Lieutenant Colonel Laurans des Ondes falls, suddenly struck dead. Beside him Second Lieutenant de Salignac-Fénelon, only twenty-two years old, breaks into an Austrian square and pays for that brilliant feat with his life.

At the farm of Casa Nova, under the enemy's terrible fire, Colonel de Maleville finds himself overwhelmed by numbers, and his battalion has run out of ammunition. He seizes the regiment's flag and rushes forward, shouting, "Whoever loves his flag, follow me!" His soldiers, though worn out with hunger and fatigue, charge after him with the bayonet. A bullet breaks his leg, but in spite of cruel pain he goes on commanding, held up on his horse by his men.

Near there, Major Hébert is killed throwing himself into the thick of the danger to keep a regimental eagle from being lost. Knocked down and trampled, he still finds the strength, before he dies, to call out to his men, "Courage, my boys!"

On the hillock of the Tower of Solferino, Lieutenant Moneglia, of the light infantry of the Guard, captures six pieces of artillery single-handed, four of them cannon with their teams of horses, commanded by an Austrian colonel who hands him his sword.

Lieutenant de Guiseul, carrying the flag of an infantry regiment of the line, is surrounded with his battalion by forces ten times their size. Shot, he rolls to the ground, clutching his precious charge to his chest. A sergeant snatches up the flag to save it from the enemy, and a cannonball carries away his head. A captain seizes the staff; he too is hit, and his blood stains the banner, which breaks and tears. Everyone who carries it, noncommissioned officers and privates, falls wounded one after another, but the living and the dead together make a last rampart of their bodies around it. In the end this glorious remnant, all torn to pieces, stays in the hands of a sergeant major of Colonel Abbatucci's regiment.

Major de La Rochefoucauld-Liancourt, a fearless officer of the Chasseurs d'Afrique, charges against Hungarian squares. But his horse is riddled with bullets, he himself falls wounded by two shots, and he is taken prisoner by the Hungarians, who have closed their square again behind him.

At Guidizzolo, Prince Charles of Windisch-Graetz, a valiant Austrian colonel, tries in vain at the head of his regiment to retake the strong position of Casa Nova. This unfortunate prince, a noble and generous hero, faces certain death, and even mortally wounded he goes on giving orders. His soldiers hold him up. They have taken him in their arms, and they stand still under a hail of bullets, making a last shelter for him with their bodies. They know they are going to die, but they will not abandon their colonel, whom they respect and love, and who soon breathes his last.

It is also while fighting with the greatest courage that the lieutenant field marshals Count Crenneville and Count Palffy are seriously wounded, and, in Baron Veigl's corps, Field Marshal Blomberg and his major general, Baltin. Baron Sturmfeder, Baron Pidoll and Colonel Mumb are killed. Lieutenants Steiger and Fischer fall bravely, not far from the young Prince of Isenburg, who is luckier than they are and will be carried from the battlefield still with a breath of life in him.

Marshal Baraguey d'Hilliers, followed by Generals Lebœuf, Bazaine, de Négrier, Douay, d'Alton and Forgeot, and Colonels Cambriels and Micheler, has broken into the village of Solferino. Count Stadion defends it with the lieutenant field marshals Paldy and Sternberg, and their brigades, Bils, Puchner, Gaal, Koller and Festetics, beat back the most violent attacks for a long time. In those attacks General Camou distinguishes himself with his chasseurs and voltigeurs, as do Colonels Brincourt and de Taxis, who are wounded, and Lieutenant Colonel Hémard, whose chest is pierced by two bullets.

General Desvaux, with his usual courage and his admirable coolness, takes the tremendous shock of the Hungarian infantry at the head of his cavalry, in a terrible struggle. Always in front of his division, in the most exposed places, he backs up with the irresistible dash of his squadrons the vigorous offensive of General Trochu against the corps of Veigl, Schwarzenberg and Schaffgotsche at Guidizzolo and at Rebecco. Generals Morris and Partouneaux also distinguish themselves there, against Mensdorff's cavalry.

General Niel, with Generals de Failly, Vinoy and de Luzy, holds out with unshakable steadiness on the plain of Medole against the three great divisions of Count Wimpffen's army. His stand allows Marshal MacMahon, with Generals de La Motterouge and Decaen and the cavalry of the Guard, to reach the hillocks of San Cassiano and Cavriana by going around the heights that are the key to those positions, and to settle on that series of parallel hills where the troops of Field Marshals Clam-Gallas and Zobel are massed. But the chivalrous Prince of Hesse, one of the heroes of the Austrian army and a man well worthy to measure himself against the famous victor of Magenta, who has thrown himself so fearlessly into the fight at San Cassiano, defends the three hillocks of Monte Fontana against assault after assault. General de Sévelinges has his rifled cannon hauled up there under the Austrian bullets. The grenadiers of the Guard harness themselves to the guns, since horses cannot climb such steep slopes. And so that the batteries, carried up the hills in this unusual way, can rain thunder down on the enemy, the grenadiers keep the gunners supplied with ammunition by calmly forming a human chain from the ammunition wagons left down on the plain.

General de La Motterouge at last stays master of Cavriana, in spite of the fierce resistance and the repeated counterattacks of the young German officers, who lead their detachments back into the fight again and again.

General Manèque's voltigeurs refill their empty cartridge boxes from the grenadiers' supply. But soon they are out of ammunition again, so they charge with the bayonet up the heights between Solferino and Cavriana, and although they are fighting against large forces, they take those positions with the help of the brave General Mellinet.

Rebecco falls to the Allies, then falls back into Austrian hands, is taken once more, recaptured once more, and finally stays in the possession of General Renault.

In the attack on Monte Fontana the Algerian riflemen are cut to pieces. Their colonels, Laure and Herment, are killed, and their officers fall in great numbers, which doubles their fury. They urge one another on to avenge their dead and hurl themselves on their enemies with the rage of the African and the fanaticism of the Muslim, killing them in a frenzy, without truce or rest, like tigers thirsting for blood.

The Croats throw themselves flat on the ground and hide in the ditches, letting their attackers come close; then they spring up all at once and kill them at point-blank range.

At San Martino an officer of the bersaglieri, the Italian sharpshooters, Captain Pallavicini, is wounded. His soldiers catch him in their arms, carry him and lay him in a chapel, where he receives first aid. But the Austrians, driven back for the moment, return to the attack and break into the church. The bersaglieri, too few to hold out, are forced to leave their commander behind. At once some Croats pick up big stones lying by the door and crush the poor captain's head with them, and his brains spatter their tunics.

In the middle of these fights, so varied, renewed endlessly and everywhere, curses burst from the mouths of men of so many different nations, many of whom are forced to become killers at twenty!

At the height of the fighting, while the earth shook under a hurricane of iron, sulfur and lead whose murderous volleys swept the ground, and while lines of fire streaked furiously through the air on every side like lightning that always killed, adding new martyrs to this human sacrifice, the Emperor Napoleon's chaplain, Father Laine, went from one field hospital to another, bringing words of comfort and sympathy to the dying.

A second lieutenant of the line has his left arm shattered by a canister ball, and blood pours from the wound. As he sits under a tree a Hungarian soldier takes aim at him, but the soldier is stopped by one of his own officers. The officer goes straight to the young wounded Frenchman, shakes his hand with compassion, and orders him carried to a less dangerous spot.

Cantinières, the women who kept each regiment's canteen and marched with it, go forward like ordinary soldiers under the enemy's very fire. They go to pick up poor mutilated soldiers who are begging for water, and they themselves are wounded while giving them a drink and trying to care for them. Close by, an officer of hussars struggles under the weight of his horse, which a shell fragment has brought down heavily on top of him; he is growing weak from the blood flowing from his own wounds. Near him a runaway horse goes past, dragging the bloody corpse of its rider in its headlong flight. Farther on, horses more humane than the men who ride them pick their way at every step so as not to trample the victims of this furious, passionate battle.

Author's note: These may have been the same cantinières who were burned alive by the Mexicans on the 9th of June, 1862. They were chained to wagons of gunpowder along with ten soldiers who were taking a convoy of food and ammunition from Veracruz to the French camp, and who were surrounded by guerrillas two or three miles from La Tejería.

An officer of the Foreign Legion is knocked down by a bullet that kills him outright. His dog, which was devoted to him, which he had brought back from Algeria and which was the friend of the whole battalion, had been marching beside him. Carried along by the rush of the troops, the dog falls in its turn a few steps farther on, also hit by a bullet, but it still finds the strength to drag itself back and die on its master's body. In another regiment a goat, adopted by a voltigeur and loved by all the soldiers, climbs unharmed up the slope to the assault on Solferino through the bullets and the grapeshot.

How many brave soldiers are not stopped by a first wound, and keep pushing forward until they are hit again and thrown to the ground, unable to go on fighting! Elsewhere, on the contrary, whole battalions, exposed to the deadliest fire, have to wait motionless for the order to advance. They are forced to stand by as calm spectators, but boiling with impatience, of a fight that is cutting them down.

The Sardinians, the army of the Kingdom of Sardinia and Piedmont, defend and attack, in engagement after engagement and assault after assault, from morning until evening, the hillocks of San Martino, of the Roccolo and of the Madonna della Scoperta, which are taken and retaken five and six times in a row. In the end they remain masters of Pozzolengo, even though they fight division by division, one after another and without much coordination. Their generals, Mollard, La Marmora, Della Rocca, Durando, Fanti, Cialdini, Cucchiari and de Sonnaz, with officers of every branch and every rank, back up the efforts of their King, before whose eyes Generals Perrier, Cerale and Arnoldi are wounded.

In the French army, after the marshals and the division commanders, how can I fail to mention the glorious share that belongs as well to the valiant brigade generals and all the brilliant colonels, and to so many courageous majors and brave captains, who did so much to bring about the final result of this great day? And certainly there was glory in fighting and defeating warriors such as Prince Alexander of Hesse, a Stadion, a Benedek or a Charles of Windisch-Graetz!

Author's note: On the subject of General Forey, let me borrow a few words about him from the fine book by Colonel Edmond Favre of the Swiss federal army, The Prussian Army and the Cologne Maneuvers of 1861: "The King had us all invited to dinner that very day at the castle of Benrath, near Düsseldorf. ... Before sitting down at table, the King took General Forey and General Paumgarten by the hand. 'Now that you are friends,' he told them, laughing, 'sit down here, side by side, and have a talk.' Now, Forey was the victor of Montebello and Paumgarten his opponent there, and they were free to ask each other about every detail of that day. Seeing the Austrian general's honest smile, you felt that the time for grudges was past; as for the French general, we all know he had no reason to hold one. That is war, and that is the soldier: these two generals, on such good terms this autumn, will perhaps be cutting each other to pieces next year, and then dining together again somewhere in two years' time!"

"It was as if the wind was pushing us," said one ordinary private of the line, putting it vividly, to give an idea of the dash and enthusiasm with which his comrades threw themselves into the fighting with him. "The smell of the powder, the noise of the guns, the drums beating and the bugles sounding — it gets you going, it fires you up!" And indeed in this struggle every man seemed to fight as if his own personal reputation were at stake, as if he had to make the victory his own particular business.

There really is a dash and a bravery all their own in the fearless noncommissioned officers of the French army. For them no obstacle exists. With their soldiers behind them they rush to the most dangerous and exposed places as if they were running to a party. That, no doubt, is part of what makes the French army superior to the armies of the other great nations of the world.
