Section Eight: Liberty and Necessity

(Part 3 of 3)

Part Two

There is no method of reasoning more common in philosophical disputes, and none more blameworthy, than trying to refute a hypothesis by claiming it has dangerous consequences for religion and morality. When an opinion leads to absurdities it is certainly false; but an opinion is not certainly false because it is dangerous. Such lines of attack ought to be given up altogether: they do nothing to discover truth and serve only to make an opponent odious. I say this generally, without claiming any advantage from it. I frankly submit to an examination of that kind, and shall venture to say that the doctrines both of necessity and of liberty, as explained above, are not merely consistent with morality but absolutely essential to it.

Necessity can be defined two ways, matching the two definitions of *cause* it forms an essential part of. It consists either in the constant conjunction of like objects, or in the understanding's inference from one object to another. Now necessity in both these senses — which are at bottom the same — has universally, if tacitly, been allowed in the schools, in the pulpit and in ordinary life to belong to the human will; nobody has ever claimed to deny that we can draw inferences about human actions, or that those inferences rest on the experienced union of like actions with like motives, inclinations and circumstances. The only points on which anyone can differ are these. He may refuse to give the name of necessity to this property of human actions — but as long as the meaning is understood, I hope the word can do no harm. Or he may maintain that something further can be discovered in the operations of matter. But that, it must be admitted, can be of no consequence to morality or religion, whatever it may be to natural philosophy. We may be mistaken in asserting that there is no idea of any other necessity in the actions of bodies; but we certainly attribute nothing to the actions of the mind that everyone does not readily allow. We change nothing in the received orthodox system regarding the will — only what it says about material objects and causes. So nothing could be more harmless, at the very least, than this doctrine.

Since all law is founded on rewards and punishments, it is assumed as a fundamental principle that these motives have a regular and uniform influence on the mind, producing good actions and preventing bad ones. We may give that influence whatever name we like; but since it is regularly joined with the action, it must count as a *cause*, and be regarded as an instance of the very necessity we are establishing here.

The only proper object of hatred or vengeance is a person or creature endowed with thought and consciousness; and when criminal actions arouse that passion, it is only through their relation to the person. Actions by their very nature are temporary and perishing, and where they do not proceed from some *cause* in the character of the person who performed them, they can bring him neither honour if they were good nor infamy if they were bad. The actions themselves may be blameworthy; they may be contrary to every rule of morality and religion. But the person is not answerable for them; and since they came from nothing in him that is durable and leave nothing of that kind behind them, he cannot possibly become the object of punishment on their account. On the principle that denies necessity, and consequently denies causes, a man is as pure and untainted after committing the most horrid crime as at the first moment of his birth, and his character is in no way involved in his actions, since they did not come from it, and the wickedness of the one can never be used to prove the depravity of the other.

People are not blamed for actions they perform in ignorance or by accident, whatever the consequences. Why not? Because the principles behind such actions are only momentary and end with them. People are blamed less for actions done hastily and without premeditation than for actions that come from deliberation. Why? Because a hasty temper, though a constant principle in the mind, works only intermittently and does not infect the whole character. Again, repentance wipes out every crime if it is accompanied by a reformation of life. How is that to be accounted for? Only by saying that actions make a person criminal merely as evidence of criminal principles in his mind — and when a change in those principles means the actions are no longer good evidence of them, they stop being criminal too. But except on the doctrine of necessity, they never were good evidence, and so never were criminal at all.

It is just as easy to prove, by the same arguments, that *liberty* — in the sense defined above, which everyone agrees on — is also essential to morality, and that where it is missing, no human action is capable of any moral quality or can be an object of approval or dislike. For since actions are objects of our moral feeling only so far as they indicate the inner character and passions, they cannot possibly give rise to praise or blame where they do not come from those principles but result entirely from external force.

I do not claim to have anticipated and removed every objection to this account of necessity and liberty. I can foresee others, drawn from matters not dealt with here. It may be said, for instance, that if voluntary actions are subject to the same laws of necessity as the operations of matter, there is a continuous chain of necessary causes, preordained and predetermined, running from the original cause of everything down to every single act of will of every human creature. No contingency anywhere in the universe; no indifference; no liberty. While we act, we are at the same time acted upon. The ultimate Author of all our acts of will is the Creator of the world, who first gave motion to this immense machine and placed all beings in the particular position from which every subsequent event must follow by an inevitable necessity. So human actions can either have no moral wickedness at all, since they come from so good a cause; or if they are wicked, they must implicate our Creator in the same guilt, since he is acknowledged to be their ultimate author. For just as a man who fires a mine is answerable for all the consequences, whether the fuse he used was long or short, so wherever a continuous chain of necessary causes is fixed, the Being — finite or infinite — who produces the first is likewise the author of all the rest, and must bear the blame and take the praise belonging to them. Our clear and unalterable ideas of morality establish this rule on unquestionable grounds when we examine the consequences of any human action; and these grounds must have still greater force applied to the intentions of a Being infinitely wise and powerful. Ignorance or powerlessness may be pleaded for a creature as limited as man; but those imperfections have no place in our Creator. He foresaw, he ordained, he intended all those human actions we so rashly pronounce criminal. So we must conclude either that they are not criminal, or that God and not man is accountable for them. But since either position is absurd and impious, it follows that the doctrine they are deduced from cannot possibly be true, being open to all the same objections. An absurd consequence, if it is necessary, proves the original doctrine absurd — in the same way that criminal actions make their original cause criminal, if the connection between them is necessary and inescapable.

This objection has two parts, which we shall examine separately. *First*, that if human actions can be traced by a necessary chain back to God, they can never be criminal, on account of the infinite perfection of the Being they come from, who can intend nothing but what is altogether good. Or *second*, that if they are criminal, we must withdraw the attribute of perfection we ascribe to God, and acknowledge him to be the ultimate author of guilt and moral wickedness in all his creatures.

The answer to the first objection seems obvious and convincing. There are many philosophers who, after an exact examination of all the phenomena of nature, conclude that the whole, considered as one system, is ordered at every period of its existence with perfect benevolence, and that the greatest possible happiness will in the end result to all created beings, with no admixture of positive or absolute evil. Every physical evil, they say, is an essential part of this benevolent system and could not possibly be removed, even by God himself considered as a wise agent, without letting in a greater evil or shutting out a greater good that will come of it. From this theory some philosophers, the ancient *Stoics* among them, drew a source of consolation under every affliction, teaching their pupils that the ills they suffered were really goods to the universe, and that to a wider view taking in the whole system of nature, every event became a cause for joy. But though this line is plausible and lofty, it was soon found weak and useless in practice. You would surely irritate rather than soothe a man in the racking pains of gout by preaching to him about the rightness of the general laws that produced the malignant humours in his body and carried them through the proper channels to the nerves, where they are now causing him such acute torment. These wide views may please the imagination of a speculative man for a moment, when he is comfortable and safe; but they cannot stay steadily in his mind even when it is undisturbed by pain, and much less can they hold their ground when attacked by such powerful antagonists. The affections take a narrower and more natural view of their object, and by an economy better suited to the frailty of human minds they attend only to the beings around us, and are moved by whatever appears good or bad to our own small world.

*Moral* evil is the same case as *physical* evil. It cannot reasonably be supposed that remote considerations found so ineffective against the one will have more power against the other. The human mind is so made by nature that on the appearance of certain characters and actions it immediately feels approval or blame, and there are no emotions more essential to its constitution. The characters that win our approval are chiefly those contributing to the peace and security of human society, as the characters that arouse blame are chiefly those tending to public harm — from which it may reasonably be supposed that moral feelings arise, directly or indirectly, from a reflection on these opposing interests. What if philosophical meditation does establish a different opinion, that everything is right with regard to the whole, and that the qualities disturbing society are on balance as beneficial, and as much in keeping with nature's original intention, as the ones that promote its welfare more directly? Are such remote and uncertain speculations able to outweigh the feelings that arise from the natural and immediate view of things? When a man is robbed of a considerable sum, does he find his distress at the loss at all diminished by these lofty reflections? Why then should his moral resentment at the crime be supposed incompatible with them? Or why should admitting a real distinction between vice and virtue not be reconcilable with every speculative system of philosophy, just as admitting a real distinction between personal beauty and ugliness is? Both distinctions are founded in the natural feelings of the human mind, and those feelings are not to be controlled or altered by any philosophical theory whatever.

The *second* objection does not admit of so easy and satisfactory an answer, nor is it possible to explain clearly how God can be the mediate cause of all human actions without being the author of sin and moral wickedness. These are mysteries that mere natural and unassisted reason is very unfit to handle; and whatever system she adopts, she will find herself tangled in inextricable difficulties, and even contradictions, at every step she takes on such subjects. To reconcile the contingency of human actions with foreknowledge, or to defend absolute decrees and yet free God from being the author of sin, has so far been found to exceed all the power of philosophy. Happy if she thereby becomes aware of her own rashness in prying into these lofty mysteries — and, leaving a scene so full of obscurity and perplexity, returns with suitable modesty to her true province, the examination of ordinary life, where she will find difficulties enough to occupy her without launching out on so boundless an ocean of doubt, uncertainty and contradiction.
