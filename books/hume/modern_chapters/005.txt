Section Five: A Sceptical Solution to These Doubts

(Part 1 of 2)

Part One

A passion for philosophy, like a passion for religion, seems open to one particular danger: that although it aims at correcting our behaviour and rooting out our vices, careless handling may let it do nothing but feed a tendency we already had, and drive the mind with more determined resolve towards the side that already *draws* too hard through the natural bias of our temperament. Certainly, while we aspire to the magnificent steadiness of the philosophic sage, and try to keep all our pleasures inside our own minds, we may in the end make our philosophy — like that of Epictetus and the other *Stoics* — into nothing but a more refined system of selfishness, and reason ourselves out of all virtue along with all social enjoyment. While we study the vanity of human life attentively, and turn all our thoughts to how empty and fleeting riches and honours are, we may all the while be flattering our natural laziness, which hates the bustle of the world and the drudgery of business and is looking for a reasonable-sounding excuse to indulge itself completely.

There is, though, one kind of philosophy that seems little exposed to this danger, because it chimes in with no disorderly passion of the mind and cannot mix itself with any natural inclination: the Academic, or Sceptical, philosophy. (The Academics are the sceptical school that grew out of Plato's Academy.) They are always talking about doubt and suspended judgement, about the danger of deciding in a hurry, about confining the enquiries of the understanding within very narrow bounds and giving up every speculation that falls outside ordinary life and practice. Nothing, then, could be more opposed than such a philosophy to the mind's lazy sprawl, its rash arrogance, its lofty pretensions and its superstitious credulity. Every passion is mortified by it except the love of truth, and that passion never is and never can be carried too far. It is surprising, then, that a philosophy which must in almost every case be harmless should be the object of so much groundless abuse. But perhaps the very thing that makes it so innocent is what chiefly exposes it to public hatred. Flattering no irregular passion, it wins few supporters; opposing so many vices and follies, it makes itself plenty of enemies, who brand it as libertine, profane and irreligious.

Nor need we fear that this philosophy, in trying to confine our enquiries to ordinary life, will ever undermine the reasoning of ordinary life and carry its doubts far enough to destroy action as well as speculation. Nature will always assert her rights and prevail in the end over any abstract reasoning whatever. Suppose we conclude, as in the last section, that in all reasoning from experience the mind takes a step that no argument supports: there is no danger that the reasoning almost all our knowledge depends on will ever be damaged by the discovery. If the mind is not moved by argument to take that step, it must be moved by some other principle of equal weight and authority — and that principle will keep its hold as long as human nature stays the same. What that principle is may well be worth the trouble of finding out.

Suppose a person with the strongest powers of reason and reflection were dropped suddenly into this world. He would certainly notice at once a continual succession of objects, one event following another; but he would not be able to make out anything more. He could not at first reach the idea of cause and effect by any reasoning, since the particular powers behind all natural operations never appear to the senses; and it is not reasonable to conclude, merely because one event precedes another on one occasion, that the first is the cause and the second the effect. Their occurring together may be arbitrary and accidental. There may be no reason to infer that one exists from the appearance of the other. In a word, without more experience such a person could never bring conjecture or reasoning to bear on any matter of fact, or be sure of anything beyond what was immediately present to his memory and senses.

Now suppose he has gained more experience, and has lived in the world long enough to have observed familiar objects or events constantly occurring together. What follows from that experience? He immediately infers the existence of one object from the appearance of the other. Yet for all his experience he has gained no idea or knowledge of the hidden power by which the one object produces the other; nor is it any process of reasoning that leads him to draw the inference. Still he finds himself determined to draw it — and even if he were convinced that his understanding had no part in the business, he would go on thinking in exactly the same way. Some other principle is making him form the conclusion.

That principle is Custom, or Habit. Whenever repeating some act produces a tendency to repeat it again, without being pushed by any reasoning or process of the understanding, we always say that the tendency is the effect of *Custom*. In using the word we do not claim to have given the ultimate reason for such a tendency. We are only pointing to a principle of human nature that everyone acknowledges and that is well known by its effects. Perhaps we can push our enquiries no further, or claim to give the cause of this cause, and must be content with it as the last principle we can name behind all our conclusions from experience. It is satisfaction enough that we can get this far, without complaining that our faculties are too narrow to take us further. And we are certainly putting forward a very intelligible proposition here, if not a true one, when we say that after the constant conjunction of two objects — heat and flame, for instance, or weight and solidity — custom alone determines us to expect the one when the other appears. This hypothesis even seems the only one that explains the difficulty of why we draw from a thousand instances an inference we cannot draw from one instance that differs from them in no respect at all. Reason is incapable of any such variation: the conclusions it draws from considering one circle are the same as it would form from surveying every circle in the universe. But no man who had seen only one body move after being struck by another could infer that every other body would move after a similar blow. So all inferences from experience are effects of custom, not of reasoning.

Custom, then, is the great guide of human life. It is that principle alone which makes our experience useful to us, and makes us expect a train of events in the future like the one that has appeared in the past.

Without the influence of custom we should be entirely ignorant of every matter of fact beyond what is immediately present to memory and the senses. We should never know how to fit means to ends, or how to use our natural powers to produce any effect. There would be an immediate end to all action, and to the greater part of speculation.

It is worth remarking here, though, that although our conclusions from experience carry us beyond memory and the senses, and assure us of facts that happened in the most distant places and the remotest ages, there must always be some fact present to the senses or memory to start from. A man who found the remains of grand buildings in a deserted country would conclude that it had once been settled by civilised inhabitants; but if nothing of the kind came before him, he could never form such an inference. We learn the events of former ages from history — but then we have to read the volumes that contain the information, and work our inferences back from one piece of testimony to another until we reach the eyewitnesses of those distant events. In a word, unless we start from some fact present to memory or the senses, our reasoning would be merely hypothetical, and however well the individual links held together, the whole chain of inferences would have nothing to hang from, nor could we ever reach knowledge of any real existence by it. If I ask why you believe some particular fact you are telling me, you must give me a reason, and the reason will be some other fact connected with it. But since you cannot go on that way for ever, you must end at last at some fact present to your memory or senses — or else admit that your belief has no foundation at all.

So what is the conclusion of the whole business? A simple one, though it must be admitted it is a good way from the usual theories of philosophy. All belief in matter of fact or real existence comes merely from some object present to the memory or senses, together with a customary conjunction between that object and some other. Or in other words: having found on many occasions that two kinds of object — flame and heat, snow and cold — have always gone together, then if flame or snow is presented to the senses again, the mind is carried by custom to expect heat or cold, and to *believe* that the quality does exist and will show itself on a closer approach. This belief is the necessary result of putting the mind in such a position. It is an operation of the soul which, when we are so placed, is as unavoidable as feeling love when we are done a kindness, or hatred when we are injured. All these operations are a kind of natural instinct, which no reasoning or process of thought can either produce or prevent.

At this point we might quite properly stop our philosophical researches. On most questions we can never take a single step further, and on all questions we must end here at last, however restlessly and curiously we enquire. But our curiosity will still be pardonable, perhaps even creditable, if it carries us on to further research, and makes us examine more closely the nature of this *belief* and of the *customary conjunction* it comes from. That way we may meet with some explanations and analogies that satisfy — at any rate those who love the abstract sciences and can enjoy speculations that keep a degree of doubt about them however carefully they are made. Readers of a different taste will find the rest of this section is not written for them, and the enquiries that follow can be understood perfectly well without it.

Part Two

Nothing is freer than human imagination. Though it cannot go beyond the original stock of ideas supplied by the inner and outer senses, it has unlimited power to mix, combine, separate and divide those ideas into every variety of fiction and vision. It can invent a train of events with every appearance of reality, assign them a particular time and place, conceive them as actually existing, and paint them out to itself with every detail belonging to a historical fact that it believes with the greatest certainty. So where does the difference between such a fiction and a belief lie? Not merely in some special idea attached to the conception that commands our assent and missing from every acknowledged fiction. For since the mind has authority over all its ideas, it could attach this particular idea to any fiction it liked, and would therefore be able to believe whatever it pleased — which is contrary to what we find every day. We can join the head of a man to the body of a horse in our imagination; but it is not in our power to believe that such an animal has ever really existed.

It follows that the difference between *fiction* and *belief* lies in some sentiment or feeling attached to the second and not the first, which does not depend on the will and cannot be summoned at pleasure. It must be aroused by nature, like every other sentiment, and must arise from the particular situation the mind is in at that moment. Whenever an object is presented to the memory or senses, the force of custom immediately carries the imagination to conceive the object usually joined with it — and that conception comes with a feeling different from the loose daydreams of fancy. That is the whole nature of belief. Since there is no matter of fact we believe so firmly that we cannot conceive the contrary, there would be no difference between the conception we assent to and the one we reject, were it not for some feeling that marks one off from the other. If I see a billiard ball rolling towards another on a smooth table, I can easily conceive it stopping on contact. There is no contradiction in the idea; but it still feels very different from the conception in which I picture the impact and the passing on of the motion from one ball to the other.
