Section Two: Where Our Ideas Come From

Everyone will readily grant that there is a considerable difference between what goes on in the mind when a man feels the pain of scorching heat or the pleasure of comfortable warmth, and what goes on when he afterwards remembers that sensation, or looks forward to it in imagination. Memory and imagination can mimic or copy what the senses deliver, but they can never quite match the force and liveliness of the original. The most we say of them, even when they are working at full strength, is that they represent their object so vividly that we could *almost* say we feel it or see it. Unless the mind is deranged by illness or madness, they can never reach a pitch of liveliness that makes the two impossible to tell apart. All the colours of poetry, however splendid, can never paint natural objects so well that the description gets taken for a real landscape. The liveliest thought is still weaker than the dullest sensation.

The same distinction runs through everything else the mind does. A man in a fit of anger is moved in a quite different way from one who is merely thinking about that emotion. If you tell me somebody is in love, I understand you easily enough and form a fair idea of his situation — but I could never mistake that idea for the real disturbance and agitation of the passion itself. When we look back on our past feelings, our thought is a faithful mirror and copies its objects truly; but the colours it works in are faint and dull beside the ones our original perceptions came dressed in. It takes no fine discrimination or metaphysical head to see the difference.

So we can divide everything the mind perceives into two classes, marked off by their different degrees of force and liveliness. The weaker and less lively ones are usually called *Thoughts* or *Ideas*. The other class has no name in our language, or in most others — I suppose because there was never any need outside philosophy to group them under a general term. So let us take a small liberty and call them *Impressions*, using the word in a sense somewhat different from the usual one. By *impression* I mean all our livelier perceptions: hearing, seeing, feeling, loving, hating, desiring, willing. Impressions are distinguished from ideas, which are the fainter perceptions we are aware of when we reflect on any of the sensations or movements just mentioned.

At first glance nothing seems less bounded than human thought. It escapes not only every human power and authority but even the limits of nature and reality. Assembling monsters, joining together shapes and appearances that do not belong together, costs the imagination no more effort than conceiving the most ordinary object. And while the body is confined to one planet, which it creeps about with pain and difficulty, thought can carry us in an instant to the remotest regions of the universe — or beyond the universe, into the boundless chaos where nature is supposed to lie in total confusion. What has never been seen or heard of can still be conceived, and nothing at all is beyond the power of thought except what involves a flat contradiction.

But though our thought seems to have this unbounded freedom, a closer look shows that it is really confined within very narrow limits, and that all this creative power of the mind comes to nothing more than a capacity for compounding, transposing, enlarging, or shrinking the materials the senses and experience have supplied. When we think of a golden mountain, we are only joining two ideas we already had, *gold* and *mountain*. We can conceive a virtuous horse, because from our own feelings we can conceive virtue, and we can attach that to the shape of an animal we know. In short, all the raw material of thinking comes from what we feel, outwardly or inwardly; the mixing and arranging of it is the only part that belongs to the mind. Or to put it in the language of philosophy: all our ideas, our fainter perceptions, are copies of our impressions, the livelier ones.

Two arguments should be enough to prove this. First: when we take apart our thoughts or ideas, however complex or lofty, we always find that they resolve into simple ideas copied from some earlier feeling. Even the ideas that look furthest from such an origin turn out, on closer inspection, to have come from it. The idea of God — an infinitely intelligent, wise and good Being — arises from reflecting on the operations of our own mind and enlarging those qualities of goodness and wisdom without limit. We can push this enquiry as far as we like, and we shall always find that every idea we examine is copied from a matching impression. Anyone who wants to deny that this holds universally has one easy way to refute it: produce the idea that in his opinion did not come from that source. It would then be up to us, if we wanted to keep our doctrine, to produce the impression that corresponds to it.

Second: if it happens, through some defect in the organ, that a man cannot have a certain kind of sensation, we always find he cannot have the corresponding ideas either. A blind man can form no notion of colours, a deaf man none of sounds. Restore the missing sense, and by opening this new entrance for his sensations you open one for the ideas as well, and he has no trouble conceiving those objects. It is the same if the object that would produce the sensation has simply never reached the organ. A Laplander or an African has no notion of the taste of wine. There are few if any cases of a comparable gap in the mind — a person who has never felt, or is entirely incapable of, some feeling or passion belonging to his species — but the same thing happens to a lesser degree. A mild-mannered man can form no idea of settled revenge or cruelty, and a selfish heart cannot easily conceive the heights of friendship and generosity. It is readily granted that other beings may have many senses we can form no conception of, because the ideas of them have never been introduced to us in the only way an idea can get into the mind: by actually feeling the sensation.

There is, however, one contrary case, which may show that it is not absolutely impossible for an idea to arise without the matching impression. It will readily be granted, I think, that the various distinct ideas of colour that come in by the eye, or of sound that come in by the ear, really are different from one another, resembling though they are. Now if that is true of different colours, it must be equally true of the different shades of a single colour, and each shade produces a distinct idea independent of the rest. Deny that, and you could run one colour by imperceptible gradations into the colour furthest from it — and if you refuse to allow that any of the steps differ, you cannot without absurdity deny that the two ends are the same. So suppose a man has had his sight for thirty years and has become perfectly familiar with colours of every kind except one particular shade of blue, which he has never happened to meet with. Lay out before him all the shades of that colour except the one, descending gradually from the deepest to the lightest. Plainly he will notice a gap where the missing shade should be, and will be aware that the distance between neighbouring colours is greater there than anywhere else. Now I ask: is it possible for him, out of his own imagination, to make up the deficiency and raise up the idea of that particular shade, although it has never been delivered to him by his senses? Few people, I think, will say he cannot — and that will serve as a proof that simple ideas are not always, in every single instance, derived from the matching impression. But the case is so peculiar that it is hardly worth noticing, and does not deserve to make us change our general rule for its sake alone.

So here is a proposition that not only looks simple and intelligible in itself, but which, if it were properly used, might make every dispute equally intelligible and banish the jargon that has for so long occupied metaphysical reasoning and brought it into disrepute. All ideas, and abstract ones especially, are naturally faint and obscure. The mind gets only a slender grip on them, they are easily confused with other ideas that resemble them, and once we have used a term often enough — even without any distinct meaning — we tend to assume there must be a definite idea attached to it. Impressions, on the other hand — all sensations, whether outward or inward — are strong and vivid. The boundaries between them are much more exactly fixed, and it is not easy to go wrong about them. So whenever we suspect that a philosophical term is being used with no meaning or idea behind it, which happens far too often, we need only ask: from what impression is that supposed idea derived? And if none can be produced, that confirms the suspicion. By bringing ideas into so clear a light, we may reasonably hope to settle any dispute that arises about what they are and whether they are real.
