Section Nine: The Reasoning of Animals

All our reasoning about matters of fact is founded on a kind of analogy, which leads us to expect from any cause the same events we have observed to follow from similar causes. Where the causes are exactly similar the analogy is perfect, and the inference drawn from it counts as certain: nobody who sees a piece of iron ever doubts that it will have weight and hold together, as in every other instance he has ever observed. But where the objects are not so exactly similar the analogy is less perfect and the inference less conclusive, though it still has some force in proportion to the degree of resemblance. Anatomical observations made on one animal are extended to all animals by this kind of reasoning; and certainly when the circulation of the blood is clearly proved in one creature, a frog or a fish, that creates a strong presumption that the same principle holds in all. These analogical observations can be carried further still, even into the science we are dealing with here: any theory by which we explain the operations of the understanding, or the origin and connection of the passions in man, gains extra authority if we find the same theory is needed to explain the same phenomena in every other animal. We shall try this out on the hypothesis by which we have been attempting to account for all reasoning from experience — and it is hoped that this fresh point of view will confirm everything observed so far.

*First*, it seems clear that animals as well as men learn a great deal from experience and infer that the same events will always follow from the same causes. By this principle they get to know the more obvious properties of external things, and gradually, from birth onwards, store up a knowledge of the nature of fire, water, earth, stones, heights, depths and so on, and of what results from them. The ignorance and inexperience of the young are plainly distinguishable here from the cunning of the old, who have learned by long observation to avoid what hurt them and to go after what gave them ease or pleasure. A horse used to the field comes to know exactly how high he can jump, and will never attempt more than he is capable of. An old greyhound will leave the more exhausting part of the chase to the younger dogs, and will place himself so as to meet the hare when she doubles back — and the guesses he makes on such an occasion are founded on nothing but his own observation and experience.

This is still clearer from the effects of training on animals, who by the proper use of rewards and punishments can be taught any course of action, even one entirely contrary to their natural instincts. Is it not experience that makes a dog fear pain when you threaten him or raise the whip? Is it not experience, too, that makes him answer to his name, and infer from such an arbitrary sound that you mean him rather than any of the others, and are calling him, when you pronounce it in a certain way and with a certain tone?

In all these cases we can see that the animal infers some fact beyond what immediately strikes his senses, and that the inference is founded entirely on past experience, the creature expecting from the object in front of him the same consequences he has always found to follow from similar objects.

*Second*, it is impossible that this inference of the animal's could be founded on any process of argument, by which he concludes that like events must follow like objects and that the course of nature will always be regular. For if there really are any arguments of that kind, they surely lie too deep for such imperfect understandings to notice, since it may take the utmost care and attention of a philosophical genius to discover them. So animals are not guided in these inferences by reasoning. Neither are children. Neither are most people in their ordinary actions and conclusions. Neither, for that matter, are philosophers, who in all the active parts of life are much the same as everybody else and are governed by the same maxims. Nature must have provided some other principle, more readily available and of more general application; an operation of such immense consequence in life as inferring effects from causes cannot be entrusted to the uncertain process of reasoning. If this were doubtful in the case of men, it seems beyond question in the case of animals — and once the conclusion is firmly established in the one, all the rules of analogy give us a strong presumption that it ought to be admitted universally, without exception or reserve. It is custom alone that leads animals to infer, from every object striking their senses, its usual companion, and carries their imagination from the appearance of the one to a conception of the other in the particular manner we call *belief*. No other explanation can be given of this operation in any of the sensitive beings we can observe, higher or lower.

But though animals learn much of their knowledge from observation, there is also much they get from nature's own hand — much that far exceeds the capacity they show on ordinary occasions, and in which they improve little or not at all with the longest practice. This we call Instinct, and we are apt to marvel at it as something quite extraordinary and inexplicable by anything human understanding can do. But our wonder will perhaps stop, or lessen, when we consider that the reasoning from experience which we share with animals, and on which the whole conduct of life depends, is itself nothing but a kind of instinct or mechanical power, working in us without our knowing it, and in its chief operations not directed by any of those relations and comparisons of ideas that are the proper objects of our intellectual faculties. The instinct is a different one, but it is still an instinct that teaches a man to avoid fire — just as much as the instinct that teaches a bird, with such exactness, the art of sitting on its eggs and the whole ordering of its nursery.
