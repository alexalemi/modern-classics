Section Ten: Miracles

(Part 2 of 2)

I may add a *fourth* reason for reducing the authority of marvels: that there is no testimony for any of them, even those never expressly exposed, which is not opposed by an infinite number of witnesses — so that not only does the miracle destroy the credit of the testimony, but the testimony destroys itself. To make this clearer, consider that in matters of religion whatever is different is contrary, and that it is impossible that the religions of ancient Rome, of Turkey, of Siam and of China should all be established on any solid foundation. So every miracle claimed to have been worked in any of these religions — and they all abound in miracles — has as its direct purpose the establishing of the particular system it belongs to, and by the same token has the same force, though more indirectly, to overthrow every other system. In destroying a rival system it also destroys the credit of the miracles that system was founded on; so that the marvels of the different religions must be regarded as contrary facts, and the evidence for them, weak or strong, as opposed to one another. On this way of reasoning, when we believe a miracle of Muhammad or his successors, our warrant is the testimony of a few barbarous Arabs — while on the other side we have the authority of Livy, Plutarch, Tacitus, and in short of every author and witness, Greek, Chinese and Roman Catholic, who has ever recorded a miracle in his own religion. Their testimony, I say, must be regarded exactly as if they had mentioned that Muhammadan miracle and had denied it in so many words, with the same certainty they have for the miracle they do relate. The argument may look over-subtle, but it is really no different from the reasoning of a judge, who takes it that the credit of two witnesses charging someone with a crime is destroyed by the testimony of two others who say he was two hundred leagues away at the very moment the crime is supposed to have been committed.

One of the best attested miracles in all profane history is the one Tacitus reports of Vespasian, who cured a blind man in Alexandria with his spittle and a lame man with the mere touch of his foot, obeying a vision of the god Serapis, who had told them to apply to the Emperor for these miraculous cures. The story can be found in that fine historian, where every detail seems to add weight to the testimony, and could be set out at length with all the force of argument and eloquence, if anyone nowadays had an interest in pressing the evidence for that exploded and idolatrous superstition. There is the gravity, solidity, age and honesty of so great an emperor, who all his life talked familiarly with his friends and courtiers and never affected the extraordinary airs of divinity that Alexander and Demetrius assumed. There is the historian, a contemporary writer, noted for candour and truthfulness, and perhaps the greatest and most penetrating genius of all antiquity — and so free from any tendency to credulity that he lies under the opposite charge, of atheism and profanity. There are the people on whose authority he relates the miracle, of established character for judgement and truthfulness, as we may well suppose, eyewitnesses of the fact, and confirming their testimony after the Flavian family had been stripped of the empire and could no longer pay anything for a lie. Utrumque, qui interfuere, nunc quoque memorant, postquam nullum mendacio pretium — those who were present still tell both stories now, when there is no longer any reward for lying. Add to this the public nature of the facts as related, and it will appear that no evidence could well be imagined stronger for so gross and palpable a falsehood.

There is also a memorable story related by Cardinal de Retz which deserves our attention. When that intriguing politician fled into Spain to escape his enemies, he passed through Saragossa, the capital of Aragon, where he was shown in the cathedral a man who had served seven years as a doorkeeper and was well known to everyone in town who had ever worshipped at that church. For all that time he had been seen to be missing a leg — but had recovered the limb by rubbing holy oil on the stump, and the cardinal assures us that he saw him with two legs. The miracle was vouched for by all the canons of the church, and the whole town was appealed to for confirmation; and the cardinal found them, by their zealous devotion, to be thorough believers in it. Here the reporter was again a contemporary of the supposed marvel, of an incredulous and libertine character as well as of great ability; the miracle was of so *singular* a kind that it could scarcely be counterfeited; and the witnesses were very numerous, and all of them in a manner spectators of what they testified to. And what adds enormously to the force of the evidence, and may double our surprise, is that the cardinal himself, who tells the story, seems to give it no credit at all, and so cannot be suspected of any part in the holy fraud. He judged rightly that in order to reject a fact of this kind it was not necessary to be able to disprove the testimony exactly, and trace its falsehood through all the knavery and credulity that produced it. He knew that this was usually quite impossible at any distance of time or place, and extremely difficult even for someone immediately present, on account of the bigotry, ignorance, cunning and roguery of a great part of mankind. So he concluded, like a sound reasoner, that such evidence carried falsehood on the very face of it, and that a miracle supported by any human testimony was more properly a subject for derision than for argument.

There was surely never a greater number of miracles ascribed to one person than those recently said to have been worked in France at the tomb of the Abbé Paris, the famous Jansenist, with whose sanctity the people were so long deluded. Curing the sick, giving hearing to the deaf and sight to the blind were everywhere talked of as the usual effects of that holy tomb. But what is more extraordinary, many of the miracles were immediately proved on the spot before judges of unquestioned integrity, attested by witnesses of credit and distinction, in a learned age, and on the most eminent stage now in the world. Nor is that all: an account of them was published and distributed everywhere; and the *Jesuits*, though a learned body backed by the civil magistrate and determined enemies of the opinions the miracles were supposed to favour, were never able to refute or expose them in detail. Where shall we find such a number of circumstances all converging to corroborate one fact? And what have we to set against such a cloud of witnesses, except the absolute impossibility, the miraculous nature, of the events they relate? And that surely, in the eyes of all reasonable people, will be regarded as refutation enough on its own.

Is the inference sound that because some human testimony has the utmost force and authority in some cases — when it reports the battle of Philippi or Pharsalia, for instance — therefore all kinds of testimony must have equal force in all cases? Suppose the Caesarean and Pompeian factions had each claimed the victory in those battles, and the historians of each side had uniformly given the advantage to their own: how could anyone at this distance decide between them? The conflict is just as strong between the miracles reported by Herodotus or Plutarch and those handed down by Mariana, Bede, or any monkish historian.

The wise lend a very sceptical faith to any report that flatters the reporter's own passion — whether it magnifies his country, his family or himself, or chimes in with his natural inclinations in some other way. And what greater temptation is there than to appear a missionary, a prophet, an ambassador from heaven? Who would not face many dangers and difficulties to attain so lofty a character? Or if, with the help of vanity and a heated imagination, a man has first converted himself and entered seriously into the delusion — who then hesitates to use pious frauds in support of so holy and meritorious a cause?

The smallest spark may kindle the greatest flame here, because the materials are always laid ready for it. The avidum genus auricularum, the gaping crowd, take in greedily and without examination whatever soothes superstition and feeds wonder.

How many stories of this kind have in every age been detected and exploded in their infancy? How many more have been celebrated for a time and then sunk into oblivion? Where such reports are flying about, then, the explanation is obvious, and we are judging in accordance with regular experience when we account for them by the known and natural principles of credulity and delusion. And shall we, rather than resort to so natural an explanation, allow a miraculous violation of the best established laws of nature?

I need not mention how hard it is to detect a falsehood in any private or even public history, in the very place where it is supposed to have happened — much more when the scene is moved to any distance at all. Even a court of law, with all the authority and accuracy and judgement it can bring to bear, often finds itself at a loss to tell truth from falsehood in the most recent events. But the matter never comes to any conclusion at all if it is left to the ordinary method of wrangling and debate and flying rumour, especially when people's passions have taken sides.

In the infancy of a new religion the wise and learned usually think the matter too trivial to deserve their attention. And when afterwards they would gladly expose the fraud and undeceive the deluded multitude, the moment has passed, and the records and witnesses that might have cleared the matter up have perished beyond recovery.

No means of detection is left except what can be drawn from the testimony of the reporters itself; and these, though always sufficient for the judicious and knowledgeable, are usually too fine to be grasped by ordinary people.

On the whole, then, it appears that no testimony for any kind of miracle has ever amounted to a probability, much less to a proof — and that even supposing it amounted to a proof, it would be opposed by another proof, drawn from the very nature of the fact it was trying to establish. It is experience alone that gives authority to human testimony, and it is the same experience that assures us of the laws of nature. So when these two kinds of experience conflict, we have nothing to do but subtract the one from the other and adopt an opinion on one side or the other with whatever assurance the remainder gives us. But on the principle explained here, this subtraction, applied to all popular religions, amounts to a total annihilation; and therefore we may lay it down as a maxim that no human testimony can have force enough to prove a miracle and make it a sound foundation for any such system of religion.

I beg that the limits I am setting here may be noticed, when I say that a miracle can never be proved so as to be the foundation of a system of religion. For I grant that there may otherwise possibly be miracles, violations of the usual course of nature, of a kind that would admit of proof from human testimony — though perhaps it will be impossible to find any such in all the records of history. Suppose, for instance, that all authors in all languages agree that from the first of January 1600 there was a total darkness over the whole earth for eight days; suppose the tradition of this extraordinary event is still strong and lively among the people; that all travellers returning from foreign countries bring us accounts of the same tradition without the least variation or contradiction. Plainly our present philosophers, instead of doubting the fact, ought to accept it as certain and go looking for the causes it might have arisen from. The decay and dissolution of nature is an event made probable by so many analogies that any phenomenon seeming to tend towards that catastrophe comes within the reach of human testimony, if that testimony is very extensive and uniform.

But suppose all the historians who deal with England agreed that on the first of January 1600 Queen Elizabeth died; that both before and after her death she was seen by her physicians and the whole court, as is usual with people of her rank; that her successor was acknowledged and proclaimed by parliament; and that after being buried a month she appeared again, took back the throne, and governed England for three years. I confess I should be astonished at the concurrence of so many odd circumstances, but I should not have the least inclination to believe so miraculous an event. I should not doubt her supposed death, or the other public circumstances that followed it. I should only maintain that it had been staged, and that it neither was nor possibly could be real. You would object in vain that it is difficult, almost impossible, to deceive the world in a matter of such consequence; that the queen was famously wise and sound in judgement; that she could gain little or nothing by so poor a trick. All this might astonish me — but I should still reply that the knavery and folly of men are such common phenomena that I would sooner believe the most extraordinary events to have arisen from them than admit so signal a violation of the laws of nature.

But if this miracle were ascribed to some new system of religion, then — men in every age having been so much imposed on by ridiculous stories of that kind — that very circumstance would be full proof of a fraud, and enough for all sensible people not merely to reject the fact but to reject it without further examination. Though the Being to whom the miracle is ascribed is in this case Almighty, that does not make it a whit more probable, since it is impossible for us to know the attributes or actions of such a Being except from the experience we have of what he produces in the usual course of nature. This still brings us back to past observation, and obliges us to compare the instances of men violating truth in their testimony with the instances of the laws of nature being violated by miracles, in order to judge which is the more likely. And since violations of truth are more common in testimony about religious miracles than in testimony about any other matter of fact, this must greatly reduce the authority of such testimony, and make us form a general resolution never to pay it any attention, under whatever plausible pretence it may be dressed up.

Lord Bacon seems to have adopted the same principles of reasoning. "We ought," he says, "to make a collection or particular history of all monsters and prodigious births or productions, and in a word of everything new, rare, and extraordinary in nature. But this must be done with the most severe scrutiny, lest we depart from truth. Above all, every relation must be considered as suspicious, which depends in any degree upon religion, as the prodigies of Livy: And no less so, everything that is to be found in the writers of natural magic or alchemy, or such authors, who seem, all of them, to have an unconquerable appetite for falsehood and fable."

I am the better pleased with the method of reasoning set out here because I think it may serve to confound those dangerous friends, or disguised enemies, of the *Christian Religion* who have undertaken to defend it on the principles of human reason. Our most holy religion is founded on *Faith*, not on reason; and it is a sure way of exposing it to put it to a trial it is by no means fitted to endure. To make this plainer, let us examine the miracles related in scripture — and so as not to lose ourselves in too wide a field, let us confine ourselves to the ones in the *Pentateuch*, which we shall examine on the principles of these supposed Christians, not as the word or testimony of God himself, but as the work of a merely human writer and historian. Here, then, we have first to consider a book presented to us by a barbarous and ignorant people, written in an age when they were still more barbarous, and in all probability long after the events it relates, corroborated by no concurring testimony, and resembling the fabulous accounts every nation gives of its own origin. On reading this book we find it full of marvels and miracles. It gives an account of a state of the world and of human nature entirely different from the present; of our fall from that state; of a human lifespan stretched to nearly a thousand years; of the destruction of the world by a flood; of the arbitrary choice of one people as the favourites of heaven, that people being the author's own countrymen; of their deliverance from slavery by the most astonishing marvels imaginable. I ask anyone to lay his hand on his heart and, after serious consideration, declare whether he thinks the falsehood of such a book, supported by such testimony, would be more extraordinary and miraculous than all the miracles it relates — which is nevertheless what is required for it to be accepted, by the measures of probability established above.

What has been said of miracles applies without any change to prophecies; and indeed all prophecies are real miracles, and only as such can be admitted as proofs of any revelation. If foretelling future events did not exceed the capacity of human nature, it would be absurd to use any prophecy as an argument for a divine mission. So on the whole we may conclude that the *Christian Religion* not only was attended with miracles at first, but cannot to this day be believed by any reasonable person without one. Mere reason is not enough to convince us of its truth; and whoever is moved by *Faith* to assent to it is conscious of a continued miracle in his own person, which overturns all the principles of his understanding and gives him a determination to believe what is most contrary to custom and experience.
