Section Four: Sceptical Doubts About How the Mind Works

(Part 1 of 2)

Part One

Everything human reason can enquire into falls naturally into two kinds: *Relations of Ideas* and *Matters of Fact*. Of the first kind are geometry, algebra and arithmetic — in short, every statement that is certain either immediately or by proof. *That the square of the hypotenuse is equal to the square of the two sides* is a proposition expressing a relation between those figures. *That three times five is equal to the half of thirty* expresses a relation between those numbers. Propositions of this kind can be discovered by thought alone, without depending on anything that exists anywhere in the universe. If there had never been a circle or a triangle in nature, the truths Euclid proved would keep their certainty and their evidence for ever.

Matters of fact — the second thing human reason enquires into — are not established in that way, and our evidence for them, however strong, is not of the same kind. The contrary of every matter of fact is still possible, because it never involves a contradiction and the mind conceives it just as easily and clearly as if it agreed perfectly with reality. (A matter of fact, in the sense used throughout this book, is a claim about how the world actually stands — one that could have been otherwise, and that no amount of reasoning alone will settle.) *That the sun will not rise tomorrow* is a perfectly intelligible proposition and involves no more contradiction than the statement *that it will rise*. So it would be useless to try to prove it false. If it were provably false, it would involve a contradiction, and the mind could never conceive it clearly.

It may be worth some curiosity, then, to ask what sort of evidence it is that assures us of any real existence or matter of fact beyond what our senses are reporting now and what our memory has recorded. This part of philosophy has been little worked on, by ancients or moderns alike, so our doubts and mistakes in pursuing so important an enquiry may be the more excusable — we are marching down difficult roads with no guide. They may even do some good, by arousing curiosity and destroying the unquestioning confidence that is the ruin of all reasoning and free enquiry. Finding defects in the received philosophy, if there are any, will not discourage anybody, I imagine, but do what such discoveries usually do: provoke an attempt at something fuller and more satisfactory than has yet been offered to the public.

All reasoning about matters of fact seems to be founded on the relation of *Cause and Effect*. That relation is the only thing that lets us get beyond the evidence of memory and the senses. Ask a man why he believes some fact he is not currently observing — that his friend is in the country, say, or in France — and he will give you a reason, and the reason will be some other fact: a letter he has had, or his knowledge of the friend's earlier plans and promises. A man finding a watch, or any other machine, on a desert island would conclude that there had once been men there. All our reasoning about fact works the same way. And it is always assumed that there is a connection between the fact in front of us and the fact we infer from it. If nothing tied them together the inference would be entirely unfounded. Hearing an articulate voice and rational conversation in the dark assures us that somebody is there — why? Because those are the effects of the human make-up and closely connected with it. Dissect any other reasoning of this kind and you will find it founded on cause and effect, the relation being either near or remote, direct or sideways-on. Heat and light are two effects of a fire standing side by side, and either may fairly be inferred from the other.

So if we want to be satisfied about the nature of the evidence that assures us of matters of fact, we must ask how we come to know about cause and effect in the first place.

I shall venture a general proposition that admits of no exception: knowledge of this relation is never, in any single case, arrived at by reasoning in advance of experience. It comes entirely from experience — from finding that particular objects are constantly conjoined with one another. Present an object to a man of the strongest natural reason and ability; if the object is entirely new to him, no amount of examining the qualities he can see and touch will reveal any of its causes or effects. Adam, even supposing his rational faculties perfect from the first moment, could not have inferred from the fluidity and transparency of water that it would drown him, or from the light and warmth of fire that it would burn him. No object ever reveals, through the qualities that appear to the senses, either the causes that produced it or the effects that will follow from it; and reason, without help from experience, can never draw any conclusion about real existence and matter of fact.

This proposition — *that causes and effects are discoverable, not by reason but by experience* — will readily be granted for objects we can remember being entirely unfamiliar with, since we must be conscious of how completely unable we then were to say what would come of them. Show two smooth pieces of marble to a man with no trace of physics in him, and he will never work out that they will stick together so firmly that great force is needed to pull them straight apart, while they yield to the slightest sideways push. Events that bear little resemblance to nature's ordinary course are also readily admitted to be known only by experience; nobody imagines that the explosion of gunpowder, or the attraction of a magnet, could ever have been worked out by reasoning in advance. In the same way, where an effect is thought to depend on intricate machinery or a hidden structure of parts, we have no trouble crediting all our knowledge of it to experience. Who is going to claim that he can give the final reason why milk or bread is proper nourishment for a man and not for a lion or a tiger?

But the same truth may not look as obvious for events that have been familiar to us since we first appeared in the world, that closely resemble the whole course of nature, and that seem to depend on the simple qualities of objects with no hidden structure behind them. We are inclined to imagine that we could have discovered such effects by reason alone, without experience. We fancy that if we were dropped into this world all at once we could have inferred straight away that one billiard ball would pass on its motion to another when it struck it, and that there was no need to wait and see before pronouncing on it with certainty. Such is the influence of custom that where it is strongest it not only covers up our natural ignorance but hides itself as well, and seems not to be operating at all — precisely because it is operating so completely.

But the following considerations should be enough to convince us that all the laws of nature, and everything bodies do without exception, are known only by experience. Suppose an object were put in front of us and we were required to say what effect would follow from it, without consulting past observation. How, I ask you, is the mind supposed to go about this? It has to invent or imagine some event and assign it to the object as its effect — and the invention is plainly quite arbitrary. The mind can never find the effect in the supposed cause, however closely it scrutinises. For the effect is entirely different from the cause and consequently can never be discovered in it. Motion in the second billiard ball is a quite distinct event from motion in the first, and there is nothing in the one to give the slightest hint of the other. A stone or a piece of metal raised into the air and let go immediately falls; but considering the matter in advance of experience, is there anything in that situation that suggests the idea of a downward motion rather than an upward one, or any other? And since the first imagining of a particular effect is arbitrary in all natural operations, as long as we do not consult experience, so we must think the same of the supposed tie or connection between cause and effect that binds them together and makes it impossible for any other effect to follow from that cause. When I see a billiard ball rolling in a straight line towards another, and even supposing motion in the second ball happens to occur to me as the result of their contact, may I not just as easily conceive a hundred different events following from that cause? May not both balls stay perfectly still? May not the first ball bounce straight back, or spring off the second in any line or direction at all? All these suppositions are consistent and conceivable. So why should we prefer one, when it is no more consistent or conceivable than the rest? No amount of reasoning in advance of experience will ever show us any ground for the preference.

In a word: every effect is a distinct event from its cause. It could not therefore have been discovered in the cause, and the first imagining of it, in advance of experience, must be entirely arbitrary. Even once it has occurred to us, joining it to the cause must seem equally arbitrary, since there are always plenty of other effects that must strike reason as just as consistent and just as natural. It is no use pretending, then, that we can settle any single event, or infer any cause or effect, without the help of observation and experience.

This shows us why no philosopher who is rational and modest has ever claimed to give the ultimate cause of any natural operation, or to display clearly the working of the power that produces any single effect in the universe. It is admitted that the most human reason can do is reduce the principles behind natural phenomena to a greater simplicity, and resolve many particular effects into a few general causes, by reasoning from analogy, experience and observation. But the causes of those general causes we shall never discover, however hard we try, and no particular explanation of them will ever satisfy us. These ultimate springs and principles are entirely closed off from human curiosity. Elasticity, gravity, cohesion, the passing on of motion by impact: these are probably the ultimate causes and principles we shall ever find in nature, and we may count ourselves lucky enough if careful enquiry and reasoning can trace particular phenomena back to them, or somewhere near them. The most perfect philosophy of the natural kind only postpones our ignorance a little; the most perfect philosophy of the moral or metaphysical kind perhaps only uncovers more of it. So the observation of human blindness and weakness is what all philosophy comes to, and it meets us at every turn however hard we try to dodge it.

Nor can geometry, brought in to help natural philosophy, ever repair this defect or lead us to knowledge of ultimate causes, for all the exactness of reasoning it is so justly famous for. Every branch of applied mathematics proceeds on the assumption that nature has established certain laws in her operations, and uses abstract reasoning either to help experience discover those laws or to work out how they apply in particular cases where some precise distance or quantity is involved. It is a law of motion, discovered by experience, that the force of a moving body is in the compound ratio of its mass and its velocity — and consequently that a small force may move the greatest obstacle or lift the greatest weight, if by some contrivance we can raise the velocity of that force enough to overmatch what it is working against. Geometry helps us apply this law by giving us the exact dimensions of all the parts and shapes that can go into a machine of any kind; but the discovery of the law itself is owed entirely to experience, and all the abstract reasoning in the world could never have brought us a step nearer it. When we reason in advance of experience, and consider an object or a cause simply as it appears to the mind, independently of all observation, it could never suggest to us the idea of some distinct object as its effect — much less show us the inseparable and unbreakable connection between them. A man would have to be very shrewd indeed to discover by reasoning that crystal is the effect of heat and ice the effect of cold, without having previously seen what heat and cold do.

Part Two

But we have still not reached any tolerable answer to the question we began with. Every solution raises a new question as hard as the last, and leads us further on. When it is asked, "What is the nature of all our reasoning about matters of fact?" the proper answer seems to be that it is founded on the relation of cause and effect. When it is asked next, "What is the foundation of all our reasoning and conclusions about that relation?" the answer can be given in one word: experience. But if we go on sifting and ask, "What is the foundation of all conclusions drawn from experience?" that raises a new question, and one that may be harder to solve and to explain. Philosophers who give themselves airs of superior wisdom and self-sufficiency have a hard time of it when they meet inquisitive people, who push them out of every corner they retreat into and are certain to drive them at last into some awkward dilemma. The best way to avoid that embarrassment is to be modest in our claims, and even to point out the difficulty ourselves before anyone else raises it. That way we can make a sort of merit out of our own ignorance.
