Section Twelve: The Academic, or Sceptical, Philosophy

(Part 2 of 2)

The absurdity of these bold pronouncements of the abstract sciences becomes, if anything, still more palpable with time than with extension. An infinite number of real parts of time, passing in succession and used up one after another, looks like so evident a contradiction that no man whose judgement has not been corrupted rather than improved by the sciences could ever, one would think, bring himself to admit it.

Yet reason must remain restless even about the scepticism these apparent contradictions drive her into. How any clear, distinct idea can contain features contradicting itself, or contradicting some other clear, distinct idea, is absolutely incomprehensible, and is perhaps as absurd as any proposition that can be formed. So nothing can be more sceptical, or fuller of doubt and hesitation, than this scepticism itself, arising as it does out of some of the paradoxical conclusions of geometry.

The sceptical objections to *moral* evidence, to reasoning about matters of fact, are either *popular* or *philosophical*. The popular ones are drawn from the natural weakness of human understanding; from the contradictory opinions held in different ages and nations; from the way our judgement varies in sickness and health, youth and age, prosperity and adversity; from the perpetual self-contradiction of each individual's opinions; and other points of that kind. There is no need to dwell on this. These objections are weak. Since in ordinary life we reason about fact and existence at every moment, and could not possibly survive without continually using this kind of argument, no popular objection drawn from it can be enough to destroy that evidence. The great overthrower of *Pyrrhonism*, of the excessive principles of scepticism, is action, and employment, and the occupations of ordinary life. These principles may flourish and triumph in the schools, where it is indeed difficult if not impossible to refute them. But as soon as they leave the shade, and are set against the more powerful principles of our nature by the presence of real objects that stir our passions, they vanish like smoke and leave the most determined sceptic in the same condition as the rest of us.

So the sceptic had better keep to his proper sphere and set out the *philosophical* objections, which come from deeper researches. Here he seems to have ample grounds for triumph. He rightly insists that all our evidence for any matter of fact lying beyond the testimony of sense or memory is drawn entirely from cause and effect; that we have no idea of that relation except of two objects which have frequently been found *conjoined*; that we have no argument to convince us that objects frequently conjoined in our experience will also be conjoined in the same way in other cases; and that nothing leads us to this inference but custom, a certain instinct of our nature, which is admittedly hard to resist, but which like other instincts may be deceptive. While the sceptic presses these points, he shows his strength — or rather, in truth, his own weakness and ours — and seems, for the moment at least, to destroy all conviction. These arguments could be set out at greater length, if any lasting good to society could ever be expected to come of them.

For here is the chief and most confounding objection to *excessive* scepticism: that no lasting good can ever come of it, so long as it retains its full force. We need only ask such a sceptic *what he means, and what he proposes by all these curious researches.* He is immediately at a loss and does not know what to answer. A Copernican or a Ptolemaist, each supporting his own system of astronomy, may hope to produce a conviction in his audience that will last. A Stoic or an Epicurean puts forward principles which may not last, but which do have an effect on conduct. But a Pyrrhonian cannot expect his philosophy to have any constant influence on the mind — or that if it did, the influence would be good for society. On the contrary, he must acknowledge, if he will acknowledge anything, that all human life would perish if his principles universally and steadily prevailed. All talk, all action would cease immediately, and people would lie in a total lethargy until the unmet necessities of nature put an end to their miserable existence. It is true that so fatal an outcome is very little to be feared. Nature is always too strong for principle. And though a Pyrrhonian may throw himself and others into a momentary amazement by his profound reasoning, the first and most trivial event in life will put all his doubts to flight and leave him exactly like the philosophers of every other sect, in every point of action and speculation — or like people who never troubled themselves with philosophy at all. When he wakes from his dream he will be the first to join in the laugh against himself, and to admit that all his objections are mere amusement, and can show nothing except the whimsical condition of mankind, who must act and reason and believe, even though they cannot by their most diligent enquiry satisfy themselves about the foundation of these operations, or remove the objections that may be raised against them.

Part Three

There is, however, a more *mitigated* scepticism, an *academic* philosophy, which may be both lasting and useful, and which may partly result from this Pyrrhonism, this *excessive* scepticism, once its indiscriminate doubts have been corrected to some extent by common sense and reflection. Most people are naturally inclined to be positive and dogmatic in their opinions; and while they see things from one side only and have no idea of any counterbalancing argument, they throw themselves headlong into whatever principles they are inclined to, with no patience for anyone who thinks otherwise. To hesitate or weigh things up perplexes their understanding, checks their passion and suspends their action. So they are impatient to escape a state they find so uncomfortable, and think they can never get far enough away from it by the violence of their assertions and the obstinacy of their belief. But if such dogmatic reasoners could be made aware of the strange infirmities of human understanding, even at its best and most careful, the reflection would naturally inspire more modesty and reserve, and would reduce their high opinion of themselves and their prejudice against their opponents. The unlearned may reflect on the disposition of the learned, who with all the advantages of study are commonly still diffident in their conclusions; and if any of the learned are inclined by temperament to haughtiness, a small dose of Pyrrhonism might reduce their pride, by showing them that the few advantages they have gained over their fellows are trifling compared with the universal perplexity inherent in human nature. In general there is a degree of doubt, caution and modesty which ought always to accompany a sound reasoner in every kind of enquiry and decision.

Another kind of *mitigated* scepticism which may be of benefit to mankind, and which may follow naturally from Pyrrhonian doubts, is the limiting of our enquiries to the subjects best suited to the narrow capacity of human understanding. Human *imagination* is naturally sublime, delighted with whatever is remote and extraordinary, and running unchecked into the most distant parts of space and time to escape the objects custom has made too familiar. Sound *Judgement* does the opposite: it avoids all distant and lofty enquiries and confines itself to ordinary life and to the subjects of daily practice and experience, leaving the more sublime topics to the ornaments of poets and orators, or to the arts of priests and politicians. Nothing can help bring us to so wholesome a resolution more than being thoroughly convinced, once, of the force of Pyrrhonian doubt, and of the impossibility of anything but the strong power of natural instinct freeing us from it. Those with a bent for philosophy will still carry on their researches, reflecting that besides the immediate pleasure of the occupation, philosophical conclusions are nothing but the reflections of ordinary life, made methodical and corrected. But they will never be tempted to go beyond ordinary life, so long as they keep in mind the imperfection of the faculties they are using, their narrow reach and their inexact operation. While we cannot give a satisfactory reason why we believe, after a thousand trials, that a stone will fall or fire burn, how can we ever satisfy ourselves about any conclusion we may form concerning the origin of worlds, and the state of nature from and to eternity?

This narrow limiting of our enquiries is in every respect so reasonable that the slightest examination of the natural powers of the human mind, compared with their objects, is enough to recommend it to us. We shall then find what the proper subjects of science and enquiry are.

It seems to me that the only objects of the abstract sciences, the only objects of demonstration, are quantity and number, and that every attempt to extend this more perfect kind of knowledge beyond those bounds is mere sophistry and illusion. Since the component parts of quantity and number are entirely similar to one another, their relations become intricate and involved, and nothing could be more curious or more useful than tracing their equality or inequality through their different appearances by a variety of means. But since all other ideas are clearly distinct from one another, the closest scrutiny can never take us further than observing that difference and pronouncing, by an obvious reflection, that one thing is not another. Or if there is any difficulty in such judgements, it comes entirely from the undetermined meaning of words, and is corrected by better definitions. That *the square of the hypotenuse is equal to the squares of the other two sides* cannot be known, however exactly the terms are defined, without a train of reasoning. But to convince us of the proposition *that where there is no property, there can be no injustice*, we need only define the terms, and explain injustice as a violation of property. That proposition is really nothing but an imperfect definition. It is the same with all the supposedly syllogistic reasonings to be found in every other branch of learning except the sciences of quantity and number — which may safely, I think, be pronounced the only proper objects of knowledge and demonstration.

All other human enquiries concern only matter of fact and existence, and these plainly cannot be demonstrated. Whatever *is* may *not be*. No denial of a fact can involve a contradiction. The non-existence of any being whatever is as clear and distinct an idea as its existence. The proposition asserting that it is not, however false, is no less conceivable than the one asserting that it is. It is otherwise with the sciences properly so called. There, every proposition that is not true is confused and unintelligible. That the cube root of 64 is equal to half of 10 is a false proposition and can never be distinctly conceived. But that Caesar, or the angel Gabriel, or any being never existed may be a false proposition, yet is perfectly conceivable and involves no contradiction.

So the existence of any being can only be proved by arguments from its cause or its effect, and those arguments are founded entirely on experience. If we reason in advance of experience, anything may appear capable of producing anything. The fall of a pebble might, for all we know, put out the sun, or a man's wish steer the planets in their orbits. It is only experience that teaches us the nature and limits of cause and effect, and lets us infer the existence of one object from another. Such is the foundation of moral reasoning, which makes up the greater part of human knowledge and is the source of all human action.

Moral reasoning concerns either particular facts or general ones. All the deliberations of life concern the first, as do all enquiries in history, chronology, geography and astronomy.

The sciences that deal with general facts are politics, natural philosophy, medicine, chemistry and so on, where the qualities, causes and effects of a whole class of objects are investigated.

Divinity, or theology, in so far as it proves the existence of a God and the immortality of the soul, is composed partly of reasoning about particular facts and partly about general ones. It has a foundation in *reason* so far as it is supported by experience. But its best and firmest foundation is *faith* and divine revelation.

Morals and criticism are not properly objects of the understanding so much as of taste and feeling. Beauty, whether moral or natural, is felt rather than perceived. Or if we do reason about it and try to fix its standard, we are attending to a new fact — the general tastes of mankind, or something of that sort, which can be an object of reasoning and enquiry.

When we run over a library, persuaded of these principles, what havoc must we make? If we take in our hand any volume — of divinity, say, or school metaphysics — let us ask: "Does it contain any abstract reasoning concerning quantity or number?" No. "Does it contain any experimental reasoning concerning matter of fact and existence?" No. Commit it then to the flames: for it can contain nothing but sophistry and illusion.
