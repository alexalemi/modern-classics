Section Seven: The Idea of Necessary Connection

(Part 1 of 2)

Part One

The great advantage the mathematical sciences have over the moral ones is that mathematical ideas, being perceptible, are always clear and definite; the smallest distinction between them is immediately noticeable, and the same terms go on expressing the same ideas without ambiguity or drift. An oval is never mistaken for a circle, nor a hyperbola for an ellipse. An isosceles triangle and a scalene one are marked off by more exact boundaries than vice and virtue, right and wrong. If a term is defined in geometry, the mind readily substitutes the definition for the term on every occasion; and even where no definition is used, the object itself can be presented to the senses and steadily grasped that way. But the finer feelings of the mind, the operations of the understanding, the various agitations of the passions — though genuinely distinct in themselves — easily slip away from us when we survey them by reflection, and it is not in our power to summon back the original object every time we need to look at it. Ambiguity gradually creeps into our reasoning this way. Objects that merely resemble each other get taken for the same, and the conclusion ends up a long way from the premises.

Still, one may safely say that if these sciences are viewed properly, their advantages and disadvantages roughly cancel out and leave them level. If the mind retains the ideas of geometry clear and definite more easily, it also has to carry on a much longer and more intricate chain of reasoning, and compare ideas much further apart, to reach that science's deeper truths. And if moral ideas are apt to slide into obscurity and confusion without extreme care, the inferences in such enquiries are always much shorter, and the intermediate steps far fewer, than in the sciences of quantity and number. In fact there is scarcely a proposition in Euclid so simple that it does not have more parts than any piece of moral reasoning that stays clear of fantasy. When we can trace the principles of the human mind through a few steps, we may be well satisfied with our progress, considering how soon nature bars all our enquiries into causes and reduces us to admitting our ignorance. The chief obstacle to progress in the moral or metaphysical sciences, then, is the obscurity of the ideas and the ambiguity of the terms. The chief difficulty in mathematics is the length of the inferences and the reach of thought needed to form any conclusion. And our progress in natural philosophy is probably held back mainly by the lack of the right experiments and phenomena, which are often found by accident and cannot always be produced when they are wanted, even by the most diligent enquiry. Since moral philosophy seems so far to have advanced less than either geometry or physics, we may conclude that if there is any difference among these sciences in this respect, the difficulties obstructing the first require greater care and greater ability to overcome.

No ideas in metaphysics are more obscure and uncertain than those of *power, force, energy* or *necessary connection*, which we are obliged to handle at every moment in every enquiry. So in this section I shall try, if possible, to fix the precise meaning of these terms, and thereby remove some part of the obscurity that people complain of so much in this kind of philosophy.

It seems a proposition not open to much dispute that all our ideas are nothing but copies of our impressions — in other words, that it is impossible for us to *think* of anything we have not previously *felt*, by the outer senses or the inner ones. I have tried to explain and prove this proposition, and have said I hope that by using it properly people may reach greater clarity and precision in philosophical reasoning than they have so far managed. Complex ideas can perhaps be well enough known by definition, which is nothing but a listing of the parts, the simple ideas, that make them up. But when we have pushed our definitions down to the simplest ideas and still find some ambiguity and obscurity, what resource is left to us? By what device can we throw light on these ideas and make them altogether precise? Produce the impressions, the original feelings, that the ideas were copied from. These impressions are all strong and vivid. They admit of no ambiguity. Not only are they in full light themselves; they can throw light on the corresponding ideas lying in the dark. And by this means we may perhaps arrive at a new microscope, a new kind of optics, by which in the moral sciences the smallest and simplest ideas can be enlarged enough to fall easily within our grasp, and be known as well as the coarsest and most obvious ideas we ever enquire into.

To get fully acquainted with the idea of power, or necessary connection, then, let us examine its impression — and to find that impression with more certainty, let us look for it in every source it could possibly have come from.

When we look around us at external objects and consider causes at work, we are never able in a single instance to make out any power or necessary connection: any quality that binds the effect to the cause and makes the one an infallible consequence of the other. All we find is that the one does, as a matter of fact, follow the other. The impact of one billiard ball is followed by motion in the second. That is the whole of what appears to the *outward* senses. The mind gets no feeling, no *inward* impression, from this succession of objects. Consequently there is nothing in any single instance of cause and effect that could suggest the idea of power or necessary connection.

From the first appearance of an object we can never guess what effect will follow from it. But if the power or energy of a cause could be made out by the mind, we could foresee the effect even without experience, and could pronounce on it with certainty at once by sheer thought and reasoning.

In fact there is no part of matter that ever reveals, through the qualities we can perceive, any power or energy, or gives us grounds to imagine that it could produce anything, or be followed by any other object we could call its effect. Solidity, extension, motion — these qualities are all complete in themselves and never point to any further event that might follow from them. The scenes of the universe are continually shifting, and one object follows another in an unbroken succession; but the power or force that drives the whole machine is entirely hidden from us, and never shows itself in any perceptible quality of a body. We know that heat is in fact a constant companion of flame; but what the connection between them is, we have not even room to guess. So the idea of power cannot possibly come from contemplating bodies in single instances of their operation, because no body ever reveals any power that could be the origin of the idea.

Since external objects as they appear to the senses give us no idea of power or necessary connection through what they do in particular instances, let us see whether the idea comes from reflecting on the operations of our own minds, and is copied from some inward impression. It may be said that we are conscious of an inward power at every moment, since we feel that by a simple command of the will we can move the organs of our body or direct the faculties of our mind. An act of will produces motion in our limbs, or raises a new idea in our imagination. We know this influence of the will by consciousness. From it we get the idea of power or energy, and we are certain that we ourselves, and all other intelligent beings, possess power. The idea, on this view, is an idea of reflection, since it arises from reflecting on the operations of our own mind, and on the command the will exercises over the organs of the body and the faculties of the soul.

Let us examine this claim, and first with regard to the will's influence over the organs of the body. This influence is a fact which, like every other natural event, can be known only by experience, and can never be foreseen from any apparent energy or power in the cause that would connect it with the effect and make the one an infallible consequence of the other. Our body moves at the command of our will; we are conscious of that every moment. But the means by which it is done, the energy by which the will performs so extraordinary an operation — of that we are so far from being immediately conscious that it must for ever escape our most diligent enquiry.

For *first*: is there any principle in all nature more mysterious than the union of soul with body, by which a supposedly spiritual substance acquires such influence over a material one that the most refined thought is able to move the grossest matter? If we were empowered to move mountains by a secret wish, or to steer the planets in their orbits, that far-reaching authority would be no more extraordinary and no further beyond our comprehension. But if we perceived any power or energy in the will by consciousness, we should have to know that power; we should have to know its connection with the effect; we should have to know the hidden union of soul and body, and the nature of both substances, by which the one is able to act on the other in so many cases.

*Second*: we cannot move all the organs of the body with the same authority, and we can give no reason for so remarkable a difference except experience. Why does the will have influence over the tongue and the fingers, but not over the heart or the liver? The question would never trouble us if we were conscious of a power in the first case and not in the second. We should then see, independently of experience, why the will's authority over the body's organs is confined within just those limits. Being in that case fully acquainted with the power by which it works, we should also know why its influence reaches exactly so far and no further.

A man suddenly struck with palsy in the leg or arm, or who has just lost those limbs, often tries at first to move them and use them as usual. He is as conscious of a power to command those limbs as a healthy man is of a power to move any limb still in its natural condition. But consciousness never deceives. Consequently in neither case are we ever conscious of any power at all. We learn the influence of our will from experience alone — and experience only teaches us that one event constantly follows another, without instructing us in the hidden connection that binds them together and makes them inseparable.

*Third*: we learn from anatomy that the immediate object of power in voluntary motion is not the limb that moves, but certain muscles and nerves and animal spirits — and perhaps something smaller and less known still — through which the motion is passed along step by step before it reaches the limb whose movement was what the will actually intended. Could there be a surer proof that the power performing this whole operation, so far from being directly and fully known by an inward feeling, is mysterious and unintelligible in the last degree? Here the mind wills a certain event; immediately another event, unknown to us and entirely different from the one intended, is produced; that event produces another, equally unknown; until at last, through a long succession, the desired event comes about. But if the original power were felt, it would have to be known; and if it were known, its effect would have to be known too, since all power is relative to its effect. And conversely, if the effect is not known, the power can be neither known nor felt. How indeed can we be conscious of a power to move our limbs, when we have no such power — only a power to move certain animal spirits, which do produce the motion of our limbs in the end, but work in a way entirely beyond our comprehension?

We may conclude from all this, I hope without rashness though with confidence, that our idea of power is not copied from any feeling or consciousness of power within ourselves when we set our bodies in motion or put our limbs to their proper use. That they move at the command of the will is a matter of common experience, like other natural events; but the power by which it is done, like the power in other natural events, is unknown and inconceivable.

Shall we say, then, that we are conscious of a power in our own minds when, by an act of will, we raise up a new idea, fix the mind on contemplating it, turn it over on all sides, and finally dismiss it for some other idea once we think we have examined it closely enough? I believe the same arguments will show that even this command of the will gives us no real idea of force.

*First*, it must be granted that when we know a power, we know the very feature in the cause that enables it to produce the effect — these are taken to be the same thing. So we should have to know both cause and effect, and the relation between them. But do we claim to be acquainted with the nature of the human soul, and the nature of an idea, and the fitness of the one to produce the other? This is a real creation, a production of something out of nothing, which implies a power so great that it might seem at first sight beyond the reach of any being short of an infinite one. At the very least it must be admitted that no such power is felt, or known, or even conceivable by the mind. All we feel is the event — the existence of an idea following a command of the will. The manner in which the operation is performed, and the power that produces it, are entirely beyond our comprehension.

*Second*, the mind's command over itself is limited, just as its command over the body is, and the limits are not known by reason or by any acquaintance with the nature of cause and effect, but only by experience and observation, as with every other natural event and with the working of external objects. Our authority over our feelings and passions is much weaker than over our ideas, and even that authority is confined within very narrow bounds. Will anyone claim to give the ultimate reason for these bounds, or to show why the power falls short in one case and not in another?

*Third*, this self-command varies greatly from one time to another. A man in health has more of it than one weakened by sickness. We are more master of our thoughts in the morning than in the evening, and fasting than after a heavy meal. Can we give any reason for these variations except experience? Where, then, is this power we claim to be conscious of? Is there not, either in a spiritual substance or a material one or both, some hidden mechanism or structure of parts on which the effect depends — and which, being entirely unknown to us, leaves the power of the will equally unknown and incomprehensible?

An act of will is surely something we know well enough. Reflect on it. Consider it from every side. Do you find anything in it like this creative power, by which it raises a new idea out of nothing, and with a kind of *Fiat* imitates the omnipotence of its Maker — if I may put it that way — who called all the various scenes of nature into existence? So far from being conscious of any such energy in the will, we need experience as sure as the experience we have to convince us that such extraordinary effects ever do follow from a simple act of will.

Most people never find any difficulty in accounting for nature's more common and familiar operations — heavy bodies falling, plants growing, animals being born, bodies nourished by food. They suppose that in all these cases they perceive the very force in the cause by which it is connected with its effect and is for ever infallible in what it does. Long habit gives them such a cast of mind that as soon as the cause appears they confidently expect its usual companion, and can hardly conceive that any other result was possible. It is only when they meet extraordinary phenomena — earthquakes, plagues, marvels of any kind — that they find themselves at a loss to assign a proper cause and explain how the effect was produced. In such difficulties people usually fall back on some invisible intelligent principle as the immediate cause of the event that has surprised them, and which they think the ordinary powers of nature cannot account for. But philosophers who carry their scrutiny a little further see at once that even in the most familiar events the energy of the cause is exactly as unintelligible as in the strangest, and that experience only ever teaches us the frequent *Conjunction* of objects, without our being able to grasp anything like a *Connection* between them.
