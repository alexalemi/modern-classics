Miguel de Cervantes was fifty-seven when the first part of Don Quixote was published in Madrid in 1605, and he had spent most of his life doing other things. He was a soldier at the sea battle of Lepanto in 1571, where a shot ruined his left hand for good. Four years later, sailing home, he was taken by corsairs and held as a slave in Algiers for five years. He tried to escape four times and failed four times, and was finally ransomed in 1580. Back in Spain he worked as a government agent buying grain and oil for the fleet, and was jailed over the accounts. He wrote the book poor and old, and he died in 1616, the year after he finished it.

The story is simple enough to explain in a sentence. An elderly gentleman of La Mancha reads so many books about knights that he decides to become one, puts on his great-grandfather's rusty armour, renames his broken-down horse Rocinante, and rides out with a farm labourer named Sancho Panza, whom he has promised the governorship of an island. The windmills come in chapter eight of a hundred and twenty-six. They are two pages of a very long book, and almost nothing else in it is what the reputation suggests.

Part One sold everywhere and was pirated everywhere, and in 1614 someone else published a sequel to it under the name Alonso Fernández de Avellaneda. Cervantes' own Part Two came out in 1615 and answered it inside the story. His characters have read Part One. They know they are in print and famous, and so does everyone they meet, so that people set traps for them based on what they have read. Later in the book they run into a man who has read the fake sequel, and Don Quixote changes his route to Barcelona purely to prove that other book a liar. Nobody had written anything like this before.

It is much funnier than its reputation and much sadder. The comedy is physical and relentless in Part One; Part Two is quieter, and the world stops laughing at the two of them by accident and starts arranging cruelties for them on purpose.

This is a translation from the Spanish, complete: both parts, all one hundred and twenty-six chapters, and every one of the interpolated tales that abridged editions cut out. Sancho's proverbs and his manglings of long words are rebuilt on English words rather than carried across, because they are jokes, and a literal rendering of a joke is a dead one. Where a proverb of his does not fit the situation, it still does not fit. That is the point of it.
