Chapter 61: Of What Happened to Don Quixote on Entering Barcelona, with Other Things That Have More of the True in Them Than of the Judicious

Three days and three nights Don Quixote spent with Roque; and if he had spent three hundred years, he would not have run out of things to watch and marvel at in his way of life. They woke up in one place and dined in another; sometimes they fled without knowing from whom, and other times they waited without knowing for whom. They slept standing up, breaking off their sleep and shifting from one place to another. It was all posting spies, listening to sentries, blowing on the matches of the harquebuses — though they carried few, because they all used flintlocks.

Roque spent the nights away from his men, in parts and places where they could not know where he was; because the many proclamations the viceroy of Barcelona had issued against his life kept him uneasy and afraid, and he did not dare to trust anybody, fearing that his own men would either kill him or hand him over to the law: a wretched and tiresome life, certainly.

Finally, by unused roads, by short cuts and hidden paths, Roque, Don Quixote and Sancho set off for Barcelona with six other squires. They reached its beach on the eve of Saint John at night; and, Roque embracing Don Quixote and Sancho — to whom he gave the ten promised crowns, for until then he had not given them him — he left them, with a thousand offers made on both sides.

Roque went back. Don Quixote stayed waiting for the day just as he was, on horseback; and it was not long before there began to appear over the balconies of the East the face of the white dawn, gladdening the grass and the flowers instead of gladdening the ear — although at that very moment the ear was gladdened too by the sound of a great many shawms and kettledrums, the noise of little bells, the "tramp, tramp, out of the way, out of the way!" of runners who seemed to be coming out of the city. The dawn gave place to the sun, which, with a face bigger than a buckler, was little by little rising up over the lowest horizon.

Don Quixote and Sancho cast their eyes in every direction: they saw the sea, which they had never seen until then; it seemed to them extremely spacious and long, a great deal more so than the Lagoons of Ruidera they had seen in La Mancha. They saw the galleys that were on the beach, which, dropping their awnings, showed themselves full of pennants and streamers that fluttered in the wind and kissed and swept the water; inside them sounded clarions, trumpets and shawms, which near and far filled the air with sweet and warlike notes.

They began to move and to make a kind of skirmish over the quiet waters, matched in almost the same way by an infinite number of knights who came out of the city on beautiful horses and in showy liveries. The soldiers of the galleys fired off endless artillery, which was answered by those on the walls and forts of the city; and the heavy artillery broke the winds with a terrible uproar, which was answered by the gangway cannon of the galleys. The sea glad, the earth joyful, the air clear — only now and then clouded by the smoke of the artillery — it all seemed to be pouring in and breeding a sudden pleasure in everybody.

Sancho could not imagine how those hulks that moved over the sea could have so many feet. At this, the men in liveries came running up, with shouting and cries and uproar, to where Don Quixote stood bewildered and astounded; and one of them, the man Roque had written to, said to Don Quixote in a loud voice:

"Welcome to our city, the mirror, the lantern, the star and the pole star of all knight-errantry, as far as it is contained anywhere. Welcome, I say, to the valorous Don Quixote of La Mancha: not the false one, not the fictitious one, not the apocryphal one that false histories have shown us these last days, but the true, the lawful and the faithful one that Cide Hamete Benengeli, the flower of historians, described to us."

Don Quixote answered not a word, and the knights did not wait for him to answer; instead, wheeling and rewheeling with the rest who followed them, they began to make a tangled caracole round Don Quixote. And he, turning to Sancho, said:

"These men have recognised us well enough: I would wager they have read our history, and even the Aragonese one that has just been printed."

The knight who had spoken to Don Quixote came back again and said to him:

"Let your worship, Señor Don Quixote, come with us; for we are all your servants and great friends of Roque Guinart."

To which Don Quixote answered:

"If courtesies breed courtesies, yours, sir knight, is the daughter or a very near relation of the great Roque's. Take me wherever you like, for I shall have no will but yours — and the more so if you want to employ it in your service."

With words no less civil than these the knight answered him; and, closing him in among them all, to the sound of the shawms and the kettledrums they made their way with him to the city. At the entrance of which, the Evil One — who arranges all evil — and boys, who are worse than the Evil One, two mischievous and daring ones of them pushed in through all the people; and, lifting the tail of the grey and the other the tail of Rocinante, they put and stuck a bunch of gorse under each.

The poor animals felt these new spurs and, clamping their tails down, increased their distress in such a way that, giving a thousand bucks, they brought their owners down on the ground. Don Quixote, put out and affronted, ran to take the plumage off his nag's tail, and Sancho off his grey's. Those who were guiding Don Quixote would have liked to punish the boys' impudence, and it was not possible, because they got away among more than a thousand others who were following them.

Don Quixote and Sancho got up again, and with the same applause and music they came to their guide's house, which was large and of good quality — in short, like a rich gentleman's; where we shall leave him for now, because that is what Cide Hamete wants.
