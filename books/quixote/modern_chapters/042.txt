Chapter 38: Which Treats of Don Quixote's Curious Discourse on Arms and Letters

Going on, Don Quixote said:

"Since we began with the student's poverty and its parts, let us see whether the soldier is any richer. And we shall see that there is nobody poorer in poverty itself, because he depends on the miserableness of his pay, which comes either late or never, or on whatever he can scrape together with his own hands, at notable danger to his life and his conscience. And sometimes his nakedness is such that a slashed jerkin serves him for finery and for a shirt; and in the middle of winter, out in the open country, he generally repairs himself against the inclemency of the sky with nothing but the breath of his mouth — which, since it comes out of an empty place, I take it as established must come out cold, against all nature.

"Well, wait for him to wait for night to come so as to restore himself from all these discomforts in the bed that is waiting for him, which, unless it is his own fault, will never sin by being too narrow; for he may measure out on the ground as many feet as he likes, and turn over in it as he pleases, without any fear that the sheets will shrink on him.

"Let there come now, on top of all this, the day and the hour of taking his degree in his calling; let there come a day of battle, when they will put the doctor's cap on his head — made of lint, to dress some bullet wound which will perhaps have gone through his temples, or will leave him crippled of an arm or a leg. And when this does not happen, but merciful heaven guards him and keeps him alive and whole, it may be that he will be left in the same poverty he was in before, and that it will be necessary for one encounter after another, one battle after another, to happen, and for him to come out of them all the winner, in order to get on at all. But these miracles are seldom seen.

"But tell me, gentlemen, if you have thought about it: how many fewer are those rewarded by war than those who have perished in it? Without any doubt you must answer that there is no comparison, and that the dead cannot be brought to an account, while the living who have been rewarded could be counted in three figures.

"All this is the other way round with scholars, because out of their gowns' skirts — I will not say out of their sleeves — they all have something to keep themselves going with. So that although the soldier's labour is greater, his reward is a great deal less.

"But to this it can be answered that it is easier to reward two thousand scholars than thirty thousand soldiers, because the first are rewarded by giving them posts, which must of necessity be given to men of their profession, and the second cannot be rewarded except out of the very property of the lord they serve; and this impossibility strengthens my argument further. But let us leave this aside, for it is a labyrinth very hard to get out of, and come back to the pre-eminence of arms over letters — a matter which is still to be settled, considering the arguments each side puts forward for itself.

"Among the ones I have mentioned, letters say that without them arms could not be kept up, because war too has its laws and is subject to them, and laws fall under what letters and scholars are. To this arms answer that laws could not be kept up without them, because it is with arms that states are defended, kingdoms preserved, cities guarded, roads made safe and seas cleared of pirates; and in the end, if it were not for arms, states, kingdoms, monarchies, cities and the roads by sea and land would be subject to the severity and the confusion that war brings with it while it lasts and has licence to use its privileges and its forces.

"And it is a settled truth that the thing that costs most is valued, and ought to be valued, most highly. To reach eminence in letters costs a man time, sleepless nights, hunger, nakedness, dizziness, indigestion and other things attached to these, some of which I have already mentioned. But for a man to come by the proper steps to be a good soldier costs him everything it costs the student, in so much greater a degree that there is no comparison, because at every step he stands to lose his life.

"And what fear of want and poverty can reach or weary the student that comes up to the one a soldier has, who, finding himself besieged in some stronghold and standing at his post or on guard in some ravelin or cavalier, hears that the enemy are mining towards the place where he is, and cannot on any account move away from there or run away from the danger that threatens him so close at hand? The only thing he can do is to give his captain notice of what is happening, so that he can remedy it with a countermine, and to stay where he is himself, fearing and expecting the moment when he shall suddenly go up to the clouds without wings and come down to the depths against his will.

"And if this seems a small danger, let us see whether it is matched or bettered by that of two galleys ramming each other prow to prow in the middle of the wide sea, which, being locked and grappled together, leave the soldier no more space than the two feet of plank on the beak-head. And in spite of this, seeing that he has in front of him as many ministers of death threatening him as there are guns trained on him from the other side, not a lance-length from his body; and seeing that at the first slip of his feet he would go to visit the deep bosom of Neptune — in spite of all this, with an intrepid heart, carried by the honour that urges him on, he sets himself up as a target for all that musketry and tries to get across so narrow a passage to the enemy vessel.

"And what is most to be marvelled at is that one man has hardly fallen where he will not be able to get up again until the end of the world before another takes his very place; and if this one too falls into the sea, which is waiting for him like an enemy, another and another follows him without giving time to time for their deaths: the greatest courage and daring that can be found in all the passages of war.

"Blessed be those happy ages that were without the appalling fury of these devilish instruments of artillery, whose inventor is at this moment, I am convinced, being given in hell the reward of his diabolical invention — with which he made it possible for a base and cowardly arm to take the life of a valiant knight; and for a stray ball, without his knowing how or from where, in the middle of the courage and spirit that fires and animates brave breasts, fired off by a man who perhaps ran away and was frightened by the flash the powder made in going off in that accursed machine, to come and cut short and end in an instant the thoughts and the life of a man who deserved to enjoy them for long ages.

"And so, considering this, I am ready to say that it grieves me in my soul to have taken up this calling of knight errant in an age as detestable as this one we now live in. Because although no danger frightens me, still it makes me uneasy to think that powder and lead may take away from me the chance of making myself famous and known, by the valour of my arm and the edge of my sword, throughout the whole of the discovered earth. But let heaven do what it is pleased to do; for I shall be all the more esteemed, if I come out with what I am after, in proportion as I have exposed myself to greater dangers than the knights errant of past ages exposed themselves to."

All this long preamble Don Quixote made while the others were having supper, forgetting to lift a mouthful to his own mouth, although Sancho Panza had told him several times to eat his supper, for there would be time afterwards to say everything he wanted.

In those who had listened to him there came a fresh pity at seeing that a man who apparently had a good understanding and reasoned well about everything he discussed should have lost it so completely when it came to that black and pitch-dark chivalry of his. The curate told him that he was quite right in everything he had said in favour of arms, and that he himself, although a scholar and a graduate, was of the same opinion.

They finished supper and the cloth was cleared; and while the innkeeper's wife, her daughter and Maritornes were getting ready Don Quixote of La Mancha's garret, where they had decided that the women should shut themselves up alone that night, Don Fernando begged the captive to tell them the course of his life, because it could not fail to be strange and enjoyable, judging by the signs he had begun to give in coming in Zoraida's company.

To which the captive answered that he would very willingly do what he was asked, and that he was only afraid the tale would not be such as to give them the pleasure he wanted to; but that for all that, so as not to fail in obeying him, he would tell it. The curate and all the rest thanked him and begged him again; and he, seeing himself begged by so many, said that there was no need for begging where commanding had so much force.

"And so, let your worships give me your attention, and you will hear a true account, which perhaps the lying ones that are generally put together with careful and studied artifice could not come up to."
