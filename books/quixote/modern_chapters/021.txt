Chapter 19: Of the Sensible Remarks Sancho Exchanged with His Master, and of the Adventure That Happened to Him with a Dead Body, and Other Famous Events

"It seems to me, sir, that all these misfortunes that have been happening to us these last few days have without any doubt been a punishment for the sin your worship committed against the order of your chivalry, in not keeping the oath you swore not to eat bread off a tablecloth or take your pleasure with the queen, and all the rest of it that goes with that and that your worship swore to keep, until you took that helmet of Malandrino's, or whatever the Moor's name is — I don't remember it properly."

"You are quite right, Sancho," said Don Quixote; "but to tell you the truth, it had gone clean out of my memory. And you may also take it as certain that the business of the blanket happened to you through your own fault in not reminding me of it in time. But I shall make amends, for there are ways of composition in the order of chivalry for everything."

"But did I swear anything, by any chance?" answered Sancho.

"It does not matter that you have not sworn," said Don Quixote. "It is enough that I understand you are not very safe from being an accessory; and one way or the other, it will do no harm to provide ourselves with a remedy."

"Well, if that's how it is," said Sancho, "mind your worship doesn't go and forget this too, the way you forgot about the oath. Perhaps the phantoms will get the urge to amuse themselves with me all over again — and with your worship too, if they see you so stubborn."

They were in this and other talk when night caught them in the middle of the road, without their having or being able to find anywhere to take shelter that night. And what was worse about it was that they were dying of hunger; for with the loss of the saddlebags they had lost the whole of their larder and provisions. And to complete this misfortune, an adventure happened to them which, without any contrivance at all, genuinely looked like one.

It came about like this. Night closed in with some darkness; but for all that they kept going, Sancho believing that since this was the high road he would in all reason find some inn on it within a league or two. Going along, then, in this fashion — the night dark, the squire hungry and the master with an appetite for his dinner — they saw coming towards them along the very road they were on a great multitude of lights, which looked like nothing so much as moving stars. Sancho was thunderstruck at the sight of them, and Don Quixote was not entirely at his ease. The one pulled at his ass's halter and the other at his hack's reins, and they stood still, watching attentively to see what this might be; and they saw that the lights kept getting nearer, and the nearer they came the bigger they looked. At which sight Sancho began to shake like a man with quicksilver in him, and Don Quixote's hair stood on end; and Don Quixote, plucking up a little courage, said:

"This, Sancho, must without any doubt be a very great and very dangerous adventure, in which it will be necessary for me to show all my valour and my strength."

"Unlucky me!" answered Sancho. "If this adventure should happen to be one of phantoms, as it is beginning to look to me, where are the ribs to be found that can put up with it?"

"However much they may be phantoms," said Don Quixote, "I shall not let them touch a thread of your clothing. If they made a joke of you the other time, it was because I could not get over the walls of the yard; but now we are out in open country, where I can wield my sword exactly as I please."

"And if they enchant you and stiffen you up as they did the other time," said Sancho, "what good will it do to be in open country or not?"

"For all that," replied Don Quixote, "I beg you, Sancho, to keep your courage up; experience will show you the courage I have."

"I will keep it up, if it please God," answered Sancho.

And the two of them drew off to one side of the road and went back to watching attentively to see what all this business of the travelling lights could be. And very shortly afterwards they made out a great many men in white robes, whose fearful appearance finished off Sancho Panza's courage altogether: he began to knock his teeth together like a man shivering with the ague, and the knocking and chattering got worse when they saw clearly what it was. For they made out as many as twenty of the white-robed men, all on horseback, with lighted torches in their hands; behind whom came a litter draped in mourning, followed by another six riders in mourning down to the feet of their mules — which they could tell were not horses by the quiet way they went. The white-robed men were murmuring among themselves in a low, pitying voice.

This strange vision, at such an hour and in such a lonely place, was quite enough to put fear into Sancho's heart, and even into his master's; and so it would have been in Don Quixote's case — for Sancho's courage had already gone to pieces. The opposite happened to his master: at that moment it presented itself vividly to his imagination that this was one of the adventures out of his books. He fancied that the litter was a bier carrying some sorely wounded or dead knight whose vengeance was reserved for him alone; and without any further reasoning he levelled his pike, settled himself firmly in the saddle, and with a fine spirit and bearing placed himself in the middle of the road along which the white-robed men had to pass. And when he saw them close by, he raised his voice and said:

"Halt, knights, or whoever you may be, and give me an account of who you are, where you come from, where you are going, and what it is you are carrying on that bier. For by all appearances either you have done, or somebody has done to you, some outrage; and it is fitting and necessary that I should know of it, either to punish you for the wrong you have done, or to avenge you for the injury that has been done to you."

"We are in a hurry," answered one of the white-robed men, "and the inn is a long way off, and we cannot stop to give as long an account as you ask for."

And spurring his mule, he went on past. Don Quixote took this answer very much amiss, and grabbing the bridle he said:

"Halt, and be better bred, and give me an account of what I have asked you; if not, you are all in battle with me."

The mule was a nervous one, and on being seized by the bridle it took such fright that it reared up and threw its master to the ground over its hindquarters. A servant who was going along on foot, seeing the white-robed man come down, began to abuse Don Quixote; who, thoroughly angry by now, levelled his pike without waiting for any more and charged one of the mourners, and brought him down badly wounded on the ground. And wheeling round on the rest of them, it was a thing worth seeing, the speed with which he attacked and scattered them; it looked for all the world as if wings had sprouted on Rocinante at that instant, so lightly and so proudly did he go.

The white-robed men were all timid people and unarmed, and so they easily left the fight in a moment and set off running across that field with their lighted torches, looking exactly like masqueraders running about on a night of merrymaking and festival. The mourners, for their part, were so tangled and wrapped up in their long skirts and gowns that they could not move; and so, in complete safety, Don Quixote cudgelled the lot of them and made them quit the place very much against their will — because they all thought this was no man but a devil out of hell, come out to take from them the dead body they were carrying in the litter. Sancho watched the whole thing, marvelling at his master's fire, and said to himself:

"There is no doubt about it, this master of mine is as brave and as strong as he says he is."

There was a torch burning on the ground next to the first man the mule had thrown, by whose light Don Quixote was able to see him; and coming up to him, he put the point of his pike in his face, telling him to surrender or he would kill him. To which the fallen man answered:

"I am surrendered enough already, since I cannot move — I have a broken leg. I beg your worship, if you are a Christian knight, not to kill me; you would be committing a great sacrilege, for I am a licentiate and I have taken minor orders."

"Then who the devil brought you here," said Don Quixote, "since you are a churchman?"

"Who, sir?" replied the fallen man. "My bad luck."

"Then a worse one is threatening you," said Don Quixote, "unless you satisfy me on everything I asked you before."

"Your worship shall be satisfied easily," answered the licentiate. "And so your worship must know that although I said just now that I was a licentiate, I am only a bachelor of arts, and my name is Alonso López. I come from the city of Baeza with eleven other priests, the ones who ran off with the torches. We are going to the city of Segovia, accompanying a dead body which is in that litter — that of a gentleman who died in Baeza, where he was left for the time being; and now, as I say, we were taking his bones to his tomb, which is in Segovia, where he was born."

"And who killed him?" asked Don Quixote.

"God, by means of a pestilential fever he caught," answered the bachelor.

"In that case," said Don Quixote, "Our Lord has taken from me the trouble I should have had in avenging his death, if anybody else had killed him. But since the one who killed him did kill him, there is nothing for it but to keep quiet and shrug my shoulders, because I should do the same if he killed me myself. And I should like your reverence to know that I am a knight of La Mancha, called Don Quixote, and that my office and calling is to travel the world putting the crooked straight and undoing injuries."

"I don't know how that can be about putting the crooked straight," said the bachelor, "since you have turned me from straight to crooked, leaving me with a broken leg that will never be seen straight again as long as it lives. And the injury you have undone in me has been to leave me injured in such a way that I shall stay injured for ever. And it has been misfortune enough to run into you, who go about looking for adventures."

"Not all things," answered Don Quixote, "happen in the same way. The damage lay, Señor Bachelor Alonso López, in your coming as you did, by night, dressed in those surplices, with lighted torches, praying, covered in mourning — so that you looked exactly like something evil out of the other world. And so I could not fail to do my duty by attacking you, and I would have attacked you even if I had known for certain that you were the actual satans of hell, which is what I judged and always took you for."

"Since my luck has willed it so," said the bachelor, "I beg your worship, sir knight errant — who have given me such a bad turn — to help me get out from under this mule, which has my leg caught between the stirrup and the saddle."

"I might have gone on talking till tomorrow!" said Don Quixote. "And how long were you going to wait before telling me your trouble?"

He at once shouted to Sancho Panza to come; but Sancho did not trouble to come, because he was busy stripping a pack-mule those good gentlemen had brought along with them, very well stocked with things to eat. Sancho made a sack out of his coat, and gathering up everything he could get and everything that would fit into the bag, he loaded his donkey; and then he answered his master's shouts and helped to get the bachelor out from the weight of the mule. And putting him up on it, they gave him his torch; and Don Quixote told him to follow the track of his companions and to beg their pardon on his behalf for the injury, which it had not been in his power to avoid doing them. And Sancho said to him too:

"If those gentlemen should happen to want to know who the valiant man was who put them in that state, your worship may tell them it was the famous Don Quixote of La Mancha, who by another name is called the Knight of the Sorrowful Countenance."

With that the bachelor went off; and Don Quixote asked Sancho what had moved him to call him the Knight of the Sorrowful Countenance just then rather than at any other time.

"I'll tell you," answered Sancho. "It's because I have been looking at you for a while by the light of that torch that poor unfortunate is carrying, and your worship really does have the worst-looking face I have ever seen in my life — the worst lately, at any rate. And it must have been caused either by the exhaustion of this fight or by the lack of the teeth and molars."

"It is not that," answered Don Quixote, "but that the wise man whose business it is to write the history of my deeds must have thought it well that I should take some sort of by-name, as all the knights of the past used to do. One called himself the Knight of the Burning Sword; another, the Knight of the Unicorn; that one, the Knight of the Maidens; this one, the Knight of the Phoenix; another, the Knight of the Griffin; another again, the Knight of Death — and by these names and emblems they were known all over the round world. And so I say that the wise man I have mentioned must have put it on your tongue and into your thoughts just now to call me the Knight of the Sorrowful Countenance, which is what I intend to call myself from today on. And so that the name may suit me better, I mean to have a very sorrowful figure painted on my shield when I get the chance."

"There's no need to spend time and money on having that figure painted," said Sancho. "What ought to be done is for your worship to uncover your own and show your face to the people who look at you; and without anything more and without any other picture or shield they'll call you the Knight of the Sorrowful Countenance. And believe me, I'm telling you the truth, because I promise your worship — and let this be said in fun — that hunger and the missing molars give you such a bad face that, as I've said already, the sorrowful painting can very well be spared."

Don Quixote laughed at Sancho's wit, but for all that he resolved to call himself by that name as soon as he could have his shield, or buckler, painted as he had imagined it.

At this the bachelor came back and said to Don Quixote:

"I was forgetting to tell you: your worship should be aware that you are excommunicated for having laid violent hands on a holy thing — juxta illud: Si quis suadente diabolo, and so on."

"I do not understand that Latin," answered Don Quixote, "but I know very well that I did not lay hands on you, only this pike. What is more, I did not think I was assaulting priests or things belonging to the Church, which I respect and adore as the Catholic and faithful Christian I am, but phantoms and monsters from the other world. And even if that were how it was, I have in my memory what happened to the Cid Ruy Díaz when he smashed the chair of that king's ambassador in front of His Holiness the Pope, for which the Pope excommunicated him — and that day the good Rodrigo de Vivar went about as a very honourable and valiant knight."

On hearing this the bachelor went off, as has been said, without answering him a word. Don Quixote wanted to look and see whether the body in the litter was bones or not, but Sancho would not have it, saying:

"Sir, your worship has finished this dangerous adventure more safely than any of the ones I have seen. These people, although they were beaten and scattered, might just possibly realise that it was one man alone who beat them, and, ashamed and embarrassed about it, get themselves together again and come looking for us and give us something to think about. The donkey is as he ought to be, the hills are close, hunger is pressing, and there is nothing to do but retire in good order; and, as they say, let the dead go to the grave and the living to the loaf."

And driving his ass in front of him, he asked his master to follow; who, thinking Sancho was right, followed him without arguing any further. And after they had gone a little way between two small hills they found themselves in a wide, hidden valley, where they dismounted. Sancho unloaded the donkey, and stretched out on the green grass, with hunger for their sauce, they breakfasted, lunched, had their tea and had their supper all at the same time, satisfying their stomachs with more than one cold pie that the dead man's reverend gentlemen — who rarely let themselves go short — had been carrying on their pack-mule of supplies.

But another misfortune befell them, which Sancho considered the worst of the lot: they had no wine to drink, nor even any water to put to their lips. And, harried by thirst, Sancho — seeing that the meadow they were in was thick with fine green grass — said what will be told in the following chapter.
