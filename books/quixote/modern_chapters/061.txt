Chapter 2: Which Treats of the Notable Quarrel Sancho Panza Had with Don Quixote's Niece and Housekeeper, with Other Amusing Matters

The history relates that the shouts Don Quixote, the curate and the barber heard came from the niece and the housekeeper, who were making them at Sancho Panza, who was struggling to get in and see Don Quixote while they blocked the door against him:

"What does this stray creature want in this house? Get off to your own, brother; for it is you and nobody else who leads my master astray and coaxes him away and takes him off along those byways."

To which Sancho answered:

"You housekeeper of Satan's, the man who is coaxed away and led astray and taken off along those byways is me, not your master. He took me off round the world, and you are cheating yourselves by half the just price: he got me out of my house with tricks, promising me an isle that I am waiting for to this day."

"May bad isles choke you, you accursed Sancho," answered the niece. "And what are isles? Are they something to eat, you greedy glutton that you are?"

"They are not to eat," replied Sancho, "but to govern and rule — and better than four cities and four magistrates of the court."

"For all that," said the housekeeper, "you shall not come in here, you sack of wickedness and bag of malice. Go and govern your own house and till your own patches of land, and give up trying for isles or islets."

The curate and the barber got great pleasure from hearing the three of them talk; but Don Quixote, afraid that Sancho would come unstitched and blurt out a heap of malicious foolishness and touch on points that would not do his own reputation any good, called him, and made the two women be quiet and let him in.

Sancho came in, and the curate and the barber took their leave of Don Quixote, of whose health they despaired, seeing how fixed he was in his deranged notions and how steeped in the simplicity of his ill-erring chivalries. And so the curate said to the barber:

"You will see, neighbour, how when we least expect it our gentleman will be off again to fly along the riverbank."

"I do not doubt it," answered the barber; "but I am not so much amazed at the knight's madness as at the squire's simplicity, who has that business of the isle so firmly believed in that I do not think all the undeceivings that can be imagined will get it out of his skull."

"God help them," said the curate; "and let us keep watch and see what all this machine of absurdities of such a knight and such a squire comes to — for it seems they were forged, the two of them, in one and the same mould, and that the master's madnesses without the servant's foolishnesses would not be worth a farthing."

"That is so," said the barber, "and I should very much like to know what the two of them will be discussing now."

"I am certain," answered the curate, "that the niece or the housekeeper will tell us afterwards, for they are not of the sort to leave off listening."

Meanwhile, Don Quixote shut himself up with Sancho in his room; and, being alone, he said to him:

"It grieves me a great deal, Sancho, that you have said and go on saying that I was the man who got you out of your own little house — knowing that I did not stay in my own houses either. We came out together, we went together and we wandered together: one and the same fortune and one and the same luck has run for the two of us. If they tossed you in a blanket once, they have pounded me a hundred times, and this is the advantage I have over you."

"That stands to reason," answered Sancho, "because, according to what your worship says, misfortunes belong more properly to knights errant than to their squires."

"You are mistaken, Sancho," said Don Quixote. "Following the maxim, quando caput dolet — and so on."

"I understand no language but my own," answered Sancho.

"I mean," said Don Quixote, "that when the head aches, all the limbs ache; and so, being your master and lord, I am your head and you are a part of me, since you are my servant. And for this reason the harm that touches me, or shall touch me, must hurt you, and yours must hurt me."

"So it ought to have been," said Sancho; "but when they were tossing me as a limb, my head was standing behind the wall watching me fly through the air without feeling any pain at all. And since the limbs are bound to feel the head's pain, the head ought to be bound to feel theirs."

"Do you mean to say now, Sancho," answered Don Quixote, "that I did not feel pain when they were tossing you? And if you do say it, do not say it or think it; for I felt more pain then in my spirit than you did in your body.

"But let us leave this aside for now, for there will be a time when we can weigh it up and set it in its proper place. And tell me, friend Sancho: what do they say about me in the village? What opinion do the common people hold of me, what the gentry and what the gentlemen? What do they say about my courage, what about my exploits and what about my courtesy? What is being said about the task I have taken on of bringing back to life and restoring to the world the now forgotten order of chivalry?

"In short, Sancho, I want you to tell me what has come to your ears about this; and you are to tell me this without adding anything to the good or taking anything from the bad. For it is the part of loyal vassals to tell their lords the truth in its own shape and figure, without flattery's enlarging it or any other empty consideration diminishing it.

"And I want you to know, Sancho, that if the naked truth reached the ears of princes, without the clothes of flattery, other centuries would run and other ages would be counted more of iron than ours — which I take to be, of all the ages now in use, the golden one. Let this warning serve you, Sancho, so that judiciously and with good intentions you may put into my ears the truth of what you know about what I have asked you."

"That I shall do very willingly, my dear sir," answered Sancho, "on condition that your worship is not to be angry at what I say, since you want me to say it naked, without dressing it in any other clothes than the ones it came to my knowledge in."

"On no account shall I be angry," answered Don Quixote. "You may well speak freely, Sancho, and without any beating about the bush."

"Well, the first thing I say," he said, "is that the common people hold your worship for an enormous madman, and me for no less of an idiot. The gentry say that, not keeping himself within the limits of the gentry, your worship has put a 'Don' on himself and rushed into being a gentleman with four vine-stocks and two acres of land and a rag behind and another in front. The gentlemen say they would rather the gentry did not set themselves up against them — especially those squirely gentry who blacken their shoes with smoke and darn the holes in their black stockings with green silk."

"That," said Don Quixote, "has nothing to do with me, since I always go well dressed and never patched. Torn, that may well be — and torn more by my armour than by time."

"As for your worship's courage, courtesy, exploits and undertaking," Sancho went on, "there are different opinions. Some say: 'mad, but amusing'; others, 'brave, but unlucky'; others, 'courteous, but impertinent' — and so they run on through so many things that they leave neither your worship nor me a whole bone."

"Look, Sancho," said Don Quixote: "wherever virtue is at a high degree, it is persecuted. Few or none of the famous men of the past escaped being slandered by malice.

"Julius Caesar, that most spirited, most prudent and most valiant captain, was set down as ambitious and somewhat unclean, both in his dress and in his habits. Alexander, whose exploits earned him the name of the Great, they say of him that he had his certain touch of the drunkard. Of Hercules, the man of the many labours, it is told that he was lascivious and soft. Of Don Galaor, Amadis of Gaul's brother, it is whispered that he was more than excessively lustful; and of his brother, that he was a weeper.

"So that, Sancho, among so many slanders on good men, mine may well pass, so long as they are no more than the ones you have said."

"There's the rub, body of my father!" replied Sancho.

"Why, is there more?" asked Don Quixote.

"The tail is still to be skinned," said Sancho. "Everything so far is cakes and ale; but if your worship wants to know all there is about the calumnies they lay on you, I shall bring you here this very moment somebody who will tell you all of them without a crumb missing.

"Because last night Bartolomé Carrasco's son arrived, who is coming from his studies at Salamanca made a bachelor of arts; and when I went to give him welcome, he told me that your worship's history is going about in books now, under the title of The Ingenious Gentleman Don Quixote of La Mancha. And he says they mention me in it under my own name of Sancho Panza, and the lady Dulcinea of El Toboso, along with other things that passed between us two when we were alone — so that I crossed myself in fright at how the historian who wrote them could have known them."

"I assure you, Sancho," said Don Quixote, "that the author of our history must be some wise enchanter; for nothing they want to write about is hidden from such men."

"And how could he help being wise and an enchanter," said Sancho, "when — according to Bachelor Sansón Carrasco, for that is the name of the man I have mentioned — the author of the history is called Cide Hamete Aubergine!"

"That name is a Moorish one," answered Don Quixote.

"That will be it," answered Sancho, "because I have generally heard it said that the Moors are fond of aubergines."

"You must be mistaking, Sancho," said Don Quixote, "the surname of this Cide, which in Arabic means lord."

"That may well be," replied Sancho; "but if your worship would like me to bring him here, I shall go for him on wings."

"You will do me a great pleasure, my friend," said Don Quixote; "for what you have told me holds me in suspense, and I shall not eat a mouthful that tastes good to me until I am informed of it all."

"Then I am off for him," answered Sancho.

And leaving his master, he went to look for the bachelor, with whom he came back a little while later; and between the three of them there passed a most amusing conversation.
