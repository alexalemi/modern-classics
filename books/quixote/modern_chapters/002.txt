Verses for the Book of Don Quixote of La Mancha

[Modern note: what follows is a set of commendatory poems supposedly written by Amadís of Gaul, Orlando, the Lady Oriana and other characters out of the very romances the book is attacking — a parody of the puffs that real books of the day carried at the front. Two of them are in what the Spanish calls versos de cabo roto, "broken-tail verse": the last syllable of every line is lopped off. It makes the rhyme and mocks affected poets at the same time, and the English does the same thing, so read the half-words aloud and let your ear finish them.]

Amadís of Gaul, to Don Quixote of La Mancha

Sonnet

	You who copied out the weeping life
	that I led, absent and disdained, up there
	on the great bare crag of the Poor Rock,
	turned from cheerfulness to penitence;

	you whose eyes gave you your only drink,
	abundant liquor, though it tasted salt,
	and who, with silver, tin and copper taken
	from you, were fed by earth on earth itself:

	live certain of this, that eternally —
	as long, at least, as in the fourth sphere
	the golden Apollo spurs his horses on —

	you will keep a clear name for courage;
	your country will stand first among them all;
	and your wise author, unique in the world, alone.

Don Belianís of Greece, to Don Quixote of La Mancha

Sonnet

	I broke, I hacked, I dented, said and did
	more than any knight errant in the world;
	I was skilful, I was brave, I was arrogant;
	I avenged a thousand wrongs, undid a hundred thousand.

	I gave Fame such deeds as make a man eternal;
	I was a courteous and an attentive lover;
	every giant was a dwarf to me,
	and I gave satisfaction in every duel.

	I had Fortune prostrate at my feet,
	and my good sense dragged bald Occasion
	about by the forelock as it pleased.

	But although my luck was always seen
	perched above the very horn of the moon,
	I envy your exploits, great Quixote!

The Lady Oriana, to Dulcinea of El Toboso

Sonnet

	Oh, that I had, lovely Dulcinea,
	for greater comfort and for greater peace,
	set Miraflores down in El Toboso,
	and traded my London for your village!

	Oh, that I could have dressed both soul and body
	in your desires and in your livery,
	and watched some unequal battle fought
	by the famous knight you made so fortunate!

	Oh, that I had got away as chastely
	from the lord Amadís as you did
	from the well-behaved gentleman Don Quixote!

	Then I should have been envied and not envious,
	and the time that was sad would have been merry,
	and I should have had the pleasures without the bill.

Gandalín, Squire to Amadís of Gaul, to Sancho Panza, Squire to Don Quixote

Sonnet

	Hail, famous man, to whom Fortune,
	when she put you into the squiring trade,
	arranged things so gently and so sensibly
	that you got through it without a single mishap.

	The spade and the sickle no longer quarrel
	with knight-errantry; plain squiring is
	the fashion now, and by it I accuse
	the proud man who tries to trample on the moon.

	I envy you your donkey and your name,
	and I envy your saddlebags just as much,
	which showed what a shrewd provider you were.

	Hail again, Sancho! So good a man
	that to you alone our Spanish Ovid
	makes his bow — and a smack on the back of the neck with it.

Orlando Furioso, to Don Quixote of La Mancha

Sonnet

	If you are not a peer, you have had none:
	you could have been a peer among a thousand peers;
	nor can there be one where you are to be found,
	unconquered conqueror, never yet conquered.

	Orlando I am, Quixote, who, lost
	for Angelica, saw far-off seas,
	offering up on Fame's own altars
	that courage which oblivion respected.

	I cannot be your equal; that honour
	is owed to your exploits and to your fame,
	although, like me, you lost your wits.

	But mine you shall be, if you tame the proud Moor
	and the fierce Scythian, who between them call us
	equals in love, and equal in bad luck.

The Knight of Phoebus, to Don Quixote of La Mancha

Sonnet

	My sword was never the equal of yours,
	Spanish Phoebus, courtier full of curiosity;
	nor was my hand, that was a lightning-bolt
	where day is born and where day dies, the equal of your glory.

	I despised empires; the monarchy
	the red East offered me I left behind,
	all for the sake of seeing the sovereign face
	of Claridiana, my beautiful dawn.

	I loved her as a rare and single miracle;
	and, banished in her disgrace, hell itself
	feared this arm of mine, which tamed its fury.

	But you, illustrious Gothic Quixote,
	are eternal in the world for Dulcinea's sake,
	and she, for yours, is famous, chaste and wise.

From Solisdán, to Don Quixote of La Mancha

Sonnet

	Albeit, Sir Quixote, that such foolishness
	hath tumbled all thy brainpan out of joint,
	yet shall no man reproach thee ever
	as one of vile and filthy deeds.

	Thy feats shall be the judges of it,
	sith thou hast gone about undoing wrongs,
	being a thousand times well cudgelled
	by rogues and knaves and low-born varlets.

	And if thy fair Dulcinea should
	commit some discourtesy against thee,
	and show no kindly face towards thy griefs,

	in such mischance let this be thy comfort:
	that Sancho Panza was a poor go-between,
	he a fool, she hard, and thou no lover at all.

Dialogue between Babieca and Rocinante

Sonnet

	B. How is it, Rocinante, you're so thin?
	R. Because one never eats, and always works.
	B. But what about the barley and the straw?
	R. My master doesn't leave me a mouthful.
	B. Go on with you, sir, you're very badly bred,
	using that ass's tongue of yours on your master.
	R. He's an ass from the cradle to the grave.
	Do you want to see it? Look at him in love.
	B. Is it stupid to love? R. It isn't very clever.
	B. You're getting metaphysical. R. It's because I don't eat.
	B. Then complain of the squire. R. That's not enough.
	How am I to complain of what ails me,
	if the master and the squire, or the steward,
	are every bit as much hacks as Rocinante?
