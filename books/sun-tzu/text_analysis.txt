THE ART OF WAR — TRANSLATION STRATEGY
Sun Tzu, c. 5th century BC, in Lionel Giles's translation of 1910.
Source: Project Gutenberg #132. 14 files, 40,966 words.


1. WHAT THIS BOOK IS, AND THE CAVEAT THAT MUST BE STATED
---------------------------------------------------------------------
THIS IS A CRIB MODERNISATION, NOT A TRANSLATION FROM THE ORIGINAL, and
that is a first for this collection. Every other book taken from a
foreign source works from the source language with an English crib
beside it: Ovid and Cicero from the Latin, Galileo from the Italian, the
three Vernes from the French, Theophrastus from the Greek. Nobody here
reads classical Chinese, so what is modernised is GILES'S ENGLISH.

That is a weaker position and the front matter says so plainly. It also
changes what the work IS: this is not "Sun Tzu retold" but "Giles's Sun
Tzu made readable", and the difference matters most exactly where Giles
is interpreting rather than translating — which he does openly, in the
commentary, all the time.

WHAT IT BUYS. Giles's is the scholarly edition, and its distinction is
the apparatus: 416 bracketed notes, of which 214 quote the classical
Chinese commentators — Ts'ao Kung (who is Cao Cao, the warlord of the
Three Kingdoms), Tu Mu, Li Ch'uan, Mei Yao-ch'en, Chang Yu, Wang Hsi, Ho
Shih, Chia Lin, Tu Yu, Meng Shih, Ch'en Hao. Most modern editions print
the verses alone, which makes the book look like a series of fortune
cookies. WITH the commentary it is something else entirely: a text with
two thousand years of professional soldiers arguing in the margins,
often flatly disagreeing with each other about what the sentence means.
That argument is the reason to publish this edition.


2. THE JUSTIFICATION
---------------------------------------------------------------------
Giles's English is not archaic in the bunyan or Burton sense. It is
Edwardian scholarly prose: long, hedged, Latinate, and interrupted every
few lines by a bracket. The obstacles are

  (a) THE APPARATUS ITSELF. The reading experience of the source is
      being stopped dead by a bracket every two sentences, many of them
      philological ("the T'u Shu reads X for Y"). The single biggest
      improvement this edition makes is simply LABELLING THE VOICES —
      "Commentary: Tu Mu says..." instead of an anonymous bracket — so
      a reader always knows whether they are hearing Sun Tzu, a Chinese
      general of the third century, or an Englishman of 1910.
  (b) VICTORIAN CIRCUMLOCUTION in the connective tissue: "It is by
      attention to this point that I can foresee who is likely to win or
      lose", "This need not be taken to mean that...".
  (c) FALSE FRIENDS, few but load-bearing: "energy" is Giles's word for
      shih, closer to momentum or force-of-position than to vigour;
      "moral law" is his rendering of tao and does NOT mean morality;
      "method and discipline" is fa, closer to organisation and
      logistics; "indirect tactics" and "direct tactics" are ch'i and
      cheng, the extraordinary and the normal.

AND THE MAXIMS ARE ALREADY PERFECT. "All warfare is based on
deception." "Attack him where he is unprepared." "Let your plans be dark
and impenetrable as night." THE HUME RULE GOVERNS THEM ABSOLUTELY: these
are why the book is read, they are already short and already modern, and
every one of them is pinned UNCHANGED in must_contain. A file in which
the aphorisms have been improved has been damaged. The work is in the
connective prose and the commentary — which is 53% of the text.


3. THE THREE VOICES, AND KEEPING THEM APART
---------------------------------------------------------------------
  SUN TZU — numbered verses. Terse, declarative, absolutely confident,
    no hedging, no argument, no examples. Short sentences. He never
    explains himself. KEEP THE NUMBERS: they are the canonical citation
    system (VII § 11), the commentators use them constantly to
    cross-refer, and they are this book's equivalent of Spinoza's
    proposition numbers.
  THE CHINESE COMMENTATORS — inside "Commentary:". Practical soldiers
    and scholars, writing centuries apart, and often disagreeing. Tu Mu
    tells stories; Ts'ao Kung is curt and military; Chang Yu is
    systematic; Mei Yao-ch'en is gnomic and short. LET THEM DISAGREE —
    Giles frequently sets two readings side by side and picks neither,
    and flattening that into one smooth gloss destroys the thing that
    makes the edition worth having.
  GILES — also inside "Commentary:", plus his sixteen own footnotes. An
    Edwardian scholar, dry, occasionally waspish, fond of a parallel
    from British military history (Wellington, Stonewall Jackson, Lord
    Roberts at the Peiwar Kotal). He signals himself with "I think", "I
    feel convinced", "in my opinion". KEEP THOSE FIRST-PERSON MARKERS:
    they are what tells the reader that a judgement is being made.


4. CONVENTIONS
---------------------------------------------------------------------
  - "Commentary: ..." begins every commentator paragraph, "Footnote: ..."
    every one of Giles's own sixteen notes. Both are mechanical, both are
    checked, and neither may be dropped or invented.
  - VERSE NUMBERS ARE PRESERVED EXACTLY, including Giles's compound
    forms ("5, 6." where one note covers two verses).
  - CHINESE NAMES KEEP GILES'S ROMANISATION. He writes Wade-Giles —
    Ts'ao Kung, Sun Tzu, Ch'i, Wu — and this edition does not convert to
    Pinyin. Converting would be a scholarly act beyond a modernisation,
    it would break every cross-reference to the commentary literature,
    and it would silently assert an identification the source does not
    make. Where a figure is famous under another name and the
    identification is not in doubt, the commentary may say so once.
  - ITALICS: Giles italicises transliterated Chinese (_cheng_, _ch'i_,
    _li_, _Tao Te Ching_) and the term under definition. Carried through
    as *emphasis*, and the count is checked per file.
  - CROSS-REFERENCES: Giles cites as "Cf. V. § 19" — book-numeral,
    section-sign, number. Rendered in words as "compare Chapter Five,
    verse 19", because the reader has a page and not a scholar's
    edition, and because "§" is not a character a modern reader parses.
    This is the spinoza ruling applied at much smaller scale: 137 shapes
    there, one shape here.


5. SENSITIVE CONTENT — THE VERNE RULE, NO NOTES
---------------------------------------------------------------------
It is a book about how to win wars, and it is unsentimental in a way
that modern military writing is not. Kept entire and unhedged:
  - the treatment of one's own soldiers as material to be managed by
    deception — "he must be able to mystify his officers and men by
    false reports and appearances", the general who throws his army into
    a desperate position so that it must fight, the burning of the boats
    and the smashing of the cooking pots;
  - Chapter Twelve's attack by fire, including burning soldiers alive,
    and Li Ch'uan's "Set fire to the camp, and kill the soldiers" when
    they run out;
  - Chapter Thirteen's doomed spies, who are given false information
    ON PURPOSE so that they will be caught and tortured into repeating
    it. Giles's own note is that the doomed spy's fate is "the most
    unpleasant of all", and it stands.
  - the executions in the Sun Tzu biography and the commentary's
    approving anecdotes about generals who behead a hesitating officer.
None of this gets a modern note. The book's coldness is the reading
experience and doing the noticing for the reader destroys it.


6. WHAT PREP HAD TO REPAIR, AND WHY IT MATTERS TO THE TRANSLATION
---------------------------------------------------------------------
Every defect found in the source was of ONE class: A PARAGRAPH IN THE
WRONG VOICE. Not a word was missing anywhere; the cross-check matches
187,420 characters in order, and every defect read perfectly.

  - SIX COMMENTARY BLOCKS HAVE NO CLOSING BRACKET, so each ran on into
    Sun Tzu's next words and handed them to a commentator. Where each
    note ends had to be READ, not guessed: chapter Four's ends on a
    couplet it is quoting, so the bracket goes after the verse; chapter
    Nine's is followed by an UNNUMBERED continuation ("Do not climb
    heights in order to fight"), so a rule keyed on verse numbers alone
    would not have caught it; chapter Thirteen's interrupts a sentence
    that resumes with "was due to I Chih".
  - ONE BLOCK HAS NO OPENING BRACKET (chapter Eight, the note on the
    fourth besetting sin), so a commentator's gloss read as Sun Tzu.
  - ONE VERSE IS WELDED to the end of a note (chapter Twelve, verse 10).
  - AND THE PREP'S OWN FIRST ATTEMPT CREATED A SEVENTH: collapsing the
    source's stray bracket runs ate the closing bracket of any note that
    ended on a footnote marker, because "[1] ]" looks like the noise. The
    casualty was "All warfare is based on deception", the single most
    quoted line in the book, which shipped as a comment on itself.
    Markers now ride through on a sentinel.

THE LESSON FOR WHOEVER TRANSLATES THIS: the label is the architecture.
If a paragraph's voice is wrong, the sentence is still true, still
grammatical and still in the right place, and nothing but reading it
will tell you. check.py compares the voice SEQUENCE per file for that
reason.
