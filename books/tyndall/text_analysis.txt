TEXT ANALYSIS — John Tyndall, SOUND (1867; third edition 1875)
Eight lectures delivered at the Royal Institution

READ FIRST: candle/text_analysis.txt and thompson/text_analysis.txt.
This is the SEVENTH Royal Institution lecture volume in the collection,
after Boys, Faraday (twice), Fleming, Ball and Thompson — and it is the
ancestor of all of them. Everything those documents say about keeping a
demonstration a demonstration applies here without change.

What is different is that Tyndall came first. He is the man who
invented the form the other five inherit: the lecture that is a
sequence of things happening on a table, narrated in the present tense
by the person doing them. When Fleming says "you see" because his
audience can, and when Thompson says "I am now dipping it in the
water", they are writing Tyndall's sentences. This book is where the
style is made.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. WHO TYNDALL IS
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

John Tyndall (1820-1893): Professor of Natural Philosophy at the Royal
Institution and Faraday's successor there, mountaineer, and the most
effective popular expositor of science in Victorian Britain. He worked
on radiant heat, on why the sky is blue, on glacier motion, and — the
seventh chapter of this book — on fog-signalling for the Trinity House,
which is original research reported as it happened.

His voice against the others in the collection:

  - FARADAY is affectionate and self-interrupting.
  - BOYS is delighted.
  - BALL is funny.
  - FLEMING is an engineer.
  - THOMPSON is a reformer.
  - TYNDALL IS A WRITER. He is the most consciously literary of the
    six, and the only one who is also a working researcher reporting
    his own unfinished work. He builds a paragraph, and he ends a
    chapter on a cadence. Do not flatten his prose into plain
    exposition: the shape of his sentences is part of what made these
    lectures famous.

He is also generous and exact about other people's work — Helmholtz,
Wertheim, Chladni, Savart, Le Conte, Melde — and scrupulous about
saying which parts are his own. Keep the attributions.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. THE ARC OF THE NINE CHAPTERS
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

  I.    The Nature of Sound — sound as motion in air; experiments in a
        vacuum and in hydrogen; reflection, refraction, diffraction;
        the velocity of sound and Laplace's correction to Newton
  II.   Pitch — taps, puffs, tuning-forks; the siren; wave-lengths;
        the limits of the ear; Helmholtz's double siren
  III.  Vibrating Strings — the laws of strings; nodes and stationary
        waves; Melde's experiments; overtones and clang-tint
  IV.   Vibrating Rods, Plates and Bells — Chladni's figures; nodal
        lines on square and circular plates; bells made visible
  V.    Resonance, Organ-Pipes and the Voice — resonant jars;
        Helmholtz's resonators; open and stopped pipes; reeds; the
        voice; vowel sounds; Kundt's dust figures
  VI.   Singing Flames and Sensitive Flames — flames that sing in
        tubes, and flames so sensitive they duck at a word from across
        the room; sensitive smoke-jets and water-jets
  VII.  Sound in the Open Air — the fog-signal work: why a horn
        audible for miles one day is silent the next, and the discovery
        of "acoustic clouds" in optically clear air
  VIII. The Interference of Sound — beats; silence made out of two
        sounds; resultant tones
  IX.   Musical Consonance and Dissonance — Pythagoras, Euler,
        Helmholtz; why some intervals please and others do not

EACH CHAPTER CLOSES WITH TYNDALL'S OWN SUMMARY, headed "Summary of
Chapter N". These are not editorial furniture: they are his own recap,
written for students, and they are among the clearest things in the
book. Translate them in full and keep them as summaries — short,
numbered-feeling, declarative.

EACH CHAPTER ALSO OPENS with a dash-separated synopsis of its contents
("Singing Flames—Influence of the Tube surrounding the Flame—..."). Keep
it as its own paragraph in the same dash-separated form, as with
thompson/'s lecture summaries.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. THE VERNE RULE
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

KEEP AS HIS CLAIMS, unhedged, no editorial correction:

  - The ether. Tyndall uses it for light and for radiant heat as an
    established medium.
  - "Acoustic clouds" — his own explanation of why fog-signals fail,
    which was contested in his lifetime (he argues with Joseph Henry
    about it in chapter seven, in print, at length). Render the
    argument as HIS argument. Do not adjudicate it and do not add that
    the modern account leans on wind shear and temperature gradients.
  - His figures: 1,090 feet a second for the velocity of sound in air
    at freezing point, 93 feet a second for a nerve impulse, Cp/Cv =
    1.42. All as printed.
  - The physiology of hearing as he has it, including his account of
    Corti's fibres as a set of resonators.

MODERNIZE SILENTLY (vocabulary, not claims):

  - vapor/color/labor -> vapour/colour/labour. THIS IS AN AMERICAN
    REPRINT of an English book; the spellings are the printer's, not
    Tyndall's. Use British spelling throughout.
  - "sonorous motion" -> keep; it is his running term and he defines it
  - "clang-tint" / "Klangfarbe" -> keep both, he gives them together;
    "timbre" is his own preferred English
  - "vein" for a jet of liquid -> "jet" or "stream" where it is not a
    term of art; keep "liquid vein" where he is naming Plateau's
    subject
  - inflexion/reflexion/connexion -> inflection/reflection/connection
  - "caoutchouc" -> rubber; "india-rubber" -> indiarubber

KEEP UNCHANGED, load-bearing terms:

  - node, ventral segment, loop; fundamental, overtone, harmonic
  - "sensitive flame", "singing flame", "smoke-jet"
  - the instrument names: siren, Chladni plate, Kundt's tube,
    Helmholtz resonator, Savart's wheel, kaleidophone, claque-bois
  - "acoustic cloud" and "acoustic opacity" — his coinages


-------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. THE PLATES
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

182 plates covering 186 numbered figures, plus the Fog-Siren
frontispiece. They ride as `[Figure N]` markers and must come back as
`[Figure N: caption]` — the same set, in the same order.

CAPTIONS ARE NEW WRITING. The original captions every plate "Fig. 62."
and nothing more. Write a descriptive one-liner for each from the
adjacent prose, naming the lettered points the text refers to (a, b, m,
n) since the reader has to find them in the picture.

Compound ids (`[Figure 134-135]`, `[Figure 143-144-145]`) are ONE image
carrying several numbered figures; the caption must cover each of them
in order and say which is which.

`[Figure front]` is the Fog-Siren frontispiece and renders with its
caption alone, with no "Figure N" label.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. THE TABLES AND THE MATHEMATICS
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

34 tables arrive as indented blocks and must come back as indented
blocks with their columns aligned. Modernize the column HEADINGS into
plain English; do not touch a figure inside.

TWO THINGS TO WATCH IN THE TABLES:

  - DITTO MARKS. prep expands a cell that is nothing but a ditto, but
    a cell like `" carbonate of soda` (ditto plus text) is left as it
    stands. Expand those in the translation — write what the row
    means. A ditto mark is meaningless to a screen reader and nearly
    meaningless on a phone.
  - Some tables were laid out with a printed brace spanning two
    columns. That brace is gone; make the heading say what it said.

Two displayed equations arrive as indented lines (Cp/Cv = V′²/V² and
Cp/Cv = 1.42). Keep them exactly.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------
6. THE FOOTNOTES
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

94 of them, arriving as paragraphs beginning "Footnote: ", already
placed after the paragraph that cites them. They are Tyndall's own —
references, courtesies, and several substantial asides — and they are
translated in his voice. Keep the form and the position.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------
7. HANDLING NOTES
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

CHAPTER SEVEN IS DIFFERENT FROM THE REST OF THE BOOK, and it is the
best thing in it. It is not a lecture: it is a research report, written
in the field, about why a fog-signal that can be heard for twelve miles
one day cannot be heard for three the next. It contains long quoted
letters from other observers, a running argument with Joseph Henry of
the Smithsonian conducted in print, and a genuine discovery — that
optically clear air can be acoustically opaque. Let it keep its
different register: cooler, more documentary, first-person, dated.

THE QUOTED LETTERS in that chapter keep their salutations and
signatures. One of them (R. G. H. Kean's account of hearing a battle)
is a Civil War recollection; render it plainly and completely.

THE CHINESE TRANSLATION STORY in the preface to the third edition —
Tyndall receives a Chinese edition of his own book from Shanghai and
cannot read a word of it — is a lovely passage and stays in full. He is
delighted, not condescending; keep it that way.

TYNDALL ON HIS OWN PROSE. He says in the first preface that he has
tried to make the illustrations fit "for educational purposes". Where
he is at his most literary — the ends of chapters, the passage on the
"soundless" sea, the closing pages of chapter seven — let the writing
be writing.

THE AUDIENCE is adults at the Royal Institution, not children: this is
the one volume of the seven where the room is not full of young people.
Keep it a little more formal than Faraday or Ball, but never dry.
