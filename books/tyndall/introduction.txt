John Tyndall published this book in 1867, out of a course of eight lectures he had given at the Royal Institution in London. It is the earliest of the Royal Institution lecture volumes in this collection, and the others are imitating it. Tyndall was professor of natural philosophy at the Institution and Faraday's successor there, and the method here — a sequence of things happening on a table, narrated in the present tense by the man doing them — is the one Boys, Fleming, Ball and Thompson all inherit. These are not the Christmas lectures for children that the later volumes record. Tyndall is talking to a general audience and assumes it can follow an argument for an hour. He was a working researcher as well as a lecturer, known for his work on radiant heat, on glaciers and on why the sky is blue, and he was the best popular expositor of science in Victorian Britain.

Nothing in the course is described rather than shown. Five assistants stand in a row with their hands on each other's backs, and the push that runs down the line is a sound wave. A glass tube is rubbed until it shivers itself to pieces. Sand on a vibrating plate collects into the figures Chladni had drawn. A gas flame ducks and roars at the hiss of the letter S, at a bunch of keys shaken across the room, at a sixpence dropped into a hand twenty yards off — and takes no notice at all if the sound is aimed half an inch above the burner.

The book runs from what sound is to what music is: the siren that counts vibrations, strings and rods and bells, organ pipes and the human voice, the interference by which one sound cancels another, and finally Helmholtz's account of why some combinations of notes please us and others grate.

The longest chapter is the newest, and it is original research reported as it happened. In 1873 and 1874 Tyndall was scientific adviser to the Trinity House, and he spent months on a steamer off the South Foreland firing guns and sounding sirens into the Channel to find out how far a fog signal carries. The results contradicted each other flatly, day by day, until he saw why. Air that is optically clear can be full of invisible acoustic clouds that stop sound dead, while a dense fog is usually the best carrier of all. Everything the century believed about fog and rain deadening sound was wrong, and ships had been lost on the strength of it.

This edition is the third, of 1875, which is the one that carries that chapter. All 187 plates are here, each with a descriptive caption written for this edition, and Le Conte's 1858 paper on the sensitive flame — the observation at a musical party that began the whole line of work — is reprinted in full.
