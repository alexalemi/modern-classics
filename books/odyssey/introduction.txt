The Odyssey was composed around 700 BC, at the end of a long tradition of poems that were sung rather than written. Nobody knows who Homer was, or whether one poet or many stand behind the name. What is clear is that the poem was made for performance, in a six-beat line, by singers who held enormous amounts of verse in their heads and assembled it in front of an audience out of ready-made phrases.

Those phrases are the first thing a modern reader notices and the first thing to stop worrying about. Dawn is rosy-fingered every single morning. The sea is wine-dark. Odysseus endures everything, Athena is grey-eyed, and whenever anybody finishes a speech the others hold their peace in silence. This is not a poverty of vocabulary. It is the machinery of a poem built to be performed, and the repetitions were a pleasure to the first audiences in the way a returning chorus is. They are kept here, and kept consistent, for the same reason.

The story starts ten years after the fall of Troy and assumes you know how that war ended. The opening four books are not about Odysseus at all but about his son, a boy of twenty who has never met his father and is being eaten out of house and home by a hundred and eight men courting his mother. Odysseus himself appears in book five, weeping on a beach. The monsters everybody remembers — the Cyclops, the witch, the whirlpool, the cattle of the Sun — are not narrated by the poet: Odysseus tells them himself, as an after-dinner story to strangers, which is worth keeping in mind given how much of the second half turns on his gift for lying.

One custom holds the whole thing together. A stranger who arrives at your door is fed, bathed, given a bed and a parting gift, and only then asked who he is. Every household in the poem is measured against that rule, and the Cyclops who eats his guests and the suitors who devour their host's property are the two ends of the same scale. The other thing worth stating plainly is that these houses run on slaves, that Homer says so constantly, and that this edition does not soften it.

The translation is from the Greek — all twenty-four books, nothing cut — and it is prose. Twelve thousand lines of hexameter has no English equivalent that does not end in padding, and this is a novel-length story that a reader wants to be able to read. The poem is far funnier, stranger and more domestic than its reputation: a man who cries easily, a dog on a dung-heap, a scar noticed in a footbath, and a wife every bit as careful as her husband.
