Book Six

So he lay there asleep, much-enduring noble Odysseus, overcome with sleep and weariness. But Athena went to the country and the city of the Phaeacians. They had lived once in spacious Hypereia, close to the Cyclopes, men overbearing in their pride, who kept plundering them and were stronger than they were. Godlike Nausithous had moved them from there and settled them in Scheria, far from men who live by their labour. He had drawn a wall round the city and built houses and made temples for the gods and divided up the ploughland. But fate had struck him down before this and he had gone to the house of Hades, and Alcinous was king now, made wise in counsel by the gods.

The goddess grey-eyed Athena went to his house, planning the homecoming of great-hearted Odysseus. She went to a richly worked room where a girl was sleeping who was like the immortal goddesses in shape and beauty, Nausicaa, the daughter of great-hearted Alcinous. Two maids slept close by, given their beauty by the Graces, one on either side of the doorposts, and the shining doors were shut. But the goddess sped to the girl's bed like a breath of air and stood over her head and spoke to her, taking the form of the daughter of Dymas, who was famous for his ships, a girl of the same age as Nausicaa and dear to her heart. Looking like her, grey-eyed Athena said:

"Nausicaa, how did your mother come to bear such a careless daughter? Your bright clothes are lying about uncared for, and your marriage is close, when you will have to be dressed in fine things yourself and give the same to the people who escort you. It is out of things like these that a good name goes up among men, and a father and an honoured mother are glad. Come, let us go and wash them at daybreak. I will come along and help you, so that you can get ready quickly; you are not going to stay unmarried much longer. You have suitors in the country already, the noblest of all the Phaeacians, where your own family comes from. Come, stir your noble father up early this morning to get mules and a wagon ready for you, to carry the sashes and the robes and the bright coverlets. And it is much more fitting for you to go like that than on foot; the washing tanks are a long way from the city."

With that the goddess grey-eyed Athena went off to Olympus, where they say the home of the gods stands firm for ever. It is not shaken by winds, nor ever wet with rain, nor does snow fall on it, but the air is spread out clear and cloudless, and a radiant whiteness plays over it. There the blessed gods take their pleasure all their days; and there the grey-eyed one went, when she had said everything to the girl.

At once fair-throned Dawn came and woke Nausicaa of the beautiful robes, and she was astonished at once by her dream and went through the house to tell her parents, her dear father and her mother; and she found them both inside. Her mother was sitting at the hearth with her women, spinning the purple-dyed yarn, and she met her father as he was going out to join the famous kings at the council, where the lordly Phaeacians had called him. She came up close to her dear father and said:

"Papa dear, will you not get a wagon ready for me, a high one with strong wheels, so that I can take the good clothes of mine that are lying here dirty down to the river to wash? Besides, it is only fitting that you should have clean clothes on when you are at the council with the princes. And you have five sons living in your halls — two are married and three are sturdy bachelors — and they always want freshly washed clothes to put on when they go to the dance. All this is on my mind."

That is what she said, because she was ashamed to speak of a joyful marriage to her father; but he understood it all, and answered: "I do not grudge you the mules, my child, or anything else. Go. The slaves will get the wagon ready for you, a high one with strong wheels and a box fitted on top."

With that he called to the slaves, and they listened. They got the light-running mule wagon ready outside the palace, and led the mules up and yoked them to it; and the girl brought the bright clothes out of her room and put them on the polished car, while her mother packed food of every kind in a chest to please the heart, and put good things in it, and poured wine into a goatskin flask. The girl climbed onto the wagon, and her mother gave her soft olive oil in a golden flask as well, so that she and her maids could use it after bathing.

Then Nausicaa took the whip and the bright reins and struck the mules to start them, and the mules went clattering off at speed, carrying the clothes and the girl. She did not go alone; her women went with her.

Now when they came to the beautiful streams of the river, where the washing tanks were that never ran dry — plenty of clear water welled up from below and flowed over, enough to clean clothes however dirty — they unyoked the mules from the wagon and drove them along the eddying river to graze on the honey-sweet grass by the water, and took the clothes out of the wagon in their arms and carried them into the dark water and trampled them in the trenches, working busily and racing each other. And when they had washed the clothes and got all the stains out, they spread them in rows on the shore of the sea, where the waves beating against the land washed the pebbles cleanest. Then they bathed and rubbed themselves richly with oil, and had their meal on the river banks, and waited for the clothes to dry in the bright sun.

And when they had enjoyed their food, she and her women threw off their headscarves and started playing at ball, and white-armed Nausicaa led the singing. As Artemis the archer ranges over the mountains, along the ridges of high Taygetus or Erymanthus, enjoying the chase of boars and swift deer, and the wood-nymphs play with her, the daughters of Zeus who holds the aegis, and Leto is glad in her heart — Artemis holds her head and her brows above them all, and is easy to pick out, though every one of them is lovely — so the unmarried girl shone among her women.

But when she was about to yoke the mules and fold the fine clothes to go home, then the goddess grey-eyed Athena thought of something else, so that Odysseus would wake and see the lovely girl, and she would lead him to the city of the Phaeacians. So the princess threw the ball to one of her maids, and missed the maid and threw it into a deep eddy; and they all cried out at it, and noble Odysseus woke and sat up, and turned it over in his mind and heart.

"What country have I come to now? What kind of people? Are they cruel and wild and unjust, or do they welcome strangers, and do their minds fear the gods? A cry rang in my ears like the cry of girls — nymphs, who haunt the high peaks of the mountains and the springs that feed the rivers and the grassy meadows. Can it be that I am somewhere near men with human speech? I will go and see for myself."

With that noble Odysseus came out from under the bushes, and broke a leafy branch off the thick wood with his strong hand, to hold in front of him and hide his nakedness with. He came out like a mountain-bred lion trusting his own strength, who goes out beaten by rain and wind with his two eyes blazing, and goes in among the cattle or the sheep or after the wild deer, and his belly drives him to try the flocks even in a close-built fold. That is how Odysseus was about to go among the girls with the lovely hair, naked as he was, because need had come on him.

He looked terrible to them, all fouled with brine, and they shrank away in fear, one here, one there, along the jutting sandspits. Only the daughter of Alcinous stood her ground, because Athena put courage in her heart and took the fear out of her limbs. She did not run, but stood and faced him. And Odysseus turned over whether he should take the lovely girl by the knees and make his plea, or whether he should keep his distance and beg her with gentle words, hoping she would show him the city and give him clothes. And as he turned it over it seemed better to keep his distance and beg her with gentle words, in case the girl's heart should be angry with him for taking hold of her knees. So he spoke at once, gently and with cunning in it.

"I beg you, my queen. Are you a goddess, or a mortal? If you are a goddess, one of those who hold the wide sky, then it is Artemis, the daughter of great Zeus, that I would compare you to most closely, in beauty and in height and in build. And if you are one of the mortals who live on the earth, then three times blessed are your father and your honoured mother, and three times blessed your brothers. Their hearts must be warmed with pleasure over and over because of you, when they see a plant as lovely as you going into the dance. But the man who is blessed in his heart above all the others is the one who wins you with his wedding gifts and takes you home. I never yet set eyes on a mortal like you, man or woman. Amazement takes hold of me looking at you.

"Once in Delos I saw such a thing, a young shoot of a palm springing up beside the altar of Apollo. I went there too, and a great many people followed me, on the journey that was to bring me so much misery. And when I saw that, I marvelled at it a long while in my heart, because no such tree had ever sprung up out of the earth. In the same way, lady, I marvel at you and am amazed, and I am very much afraid to touch your knees. But hard grief has come on me. Yesterday, on the twentieth day, I got clear of the wine-dark sea; until then the wave and the swift winds carried me from the island of Ogygia, and now fate has thrown me ashore here, so that here too, I suppose, I may suffer something. I do not think my troubles will stop yet; the gods will bring on a good many more before that.

"Have pity, my queen. It is you I have come to first, after all this hard suffering, and I know nobody else of all the people who hold this city and this land. Show me the city, and give me some rag to throw round me, if you had any wrapping for the clothes when you came here. And for yourself, may the gods grant you everything your heart desires — a husband and a home may they grant you, and one mind between you, which is a good gift. There is nothing greater or better than this, when a man and a woman keep house together with one mind: a great grief to their enemies and a joy to their friends. But they know it best themselves."

Then white-armed Nausicaa answered him: "Stranger, since you seem to be neither a bad man nor a fool — and it is Zeus himself, the Olympian, who gives good fortune to men, to the good and the bad alike, to each man as he pleases — he must have given you this lot, and you have to endure it. But now, since you have come to our city and our country, you shall not go short of clothes or of anything else that is right for a hard-pressed suppliant who comes our way. I will show you the city and tell you the name of the people. The Phaeacians hold this city and this land, and I am the daughter of great-hearted Alcinous, on whom the strength and the power of the Phaeacians depend."

She spoke, and called to her women with the lovely hair. "Stand still, my girls. Where are you running to at the sight of a man? You do not think, surely, that he is an enemy? The mortal man is not alive, and does not exist, and will never be born, who could come to the land of the Phaeacians as an enemy, because we are very dear to the immortals. We live far off in the surging sea, the furthest of men, and no other mortals have dealings with us. This is some unlucky wanderer who has come here, and we have to look after him now. All strangers and beggars come from Zeus, and a gift is welcome even when it is small. Come, my girls, give the stranger food and drink, and bathe him in the river where there is shelter from the wind."

So she spoke, and they stopped and called to each other, and set Odysseus in a sheltered place, as Nausicaa, the daughter of great-hearted Alcinous, told them, and put a cloak and a tunic down beside him for clothes, and gave him the soft olive oil in the golden flask, and told him to bathe in the streams of the river. Then noble Odysseus spoke among the girls.

"Girls, stand over there, away from me, so that I can wash the brine off my shoulders myself and rub myself with oil. It is a long time since oil came near my skin. I will not bathe in front of you. I am ashamed to be naked among girls with such lovely hair."

So he said, and they went off and told the princess. Then noble Odysseus washed the brine from his skin with water from the river, the brine that clothed his back and his broad shoulders, and wiped the scurf of the restless sea off his head. And when he had washed his whole body and rubbed himself with oil and put on the clothes the unmarried girl had given him, then Athena, daughter of Zeus, made him taller to look at and stronger, and made the hair flow down from his head in curls like the hyacinth flower. As when a man lays gold over silver, a skilled workman whom Hephaestus and Pallas Athena have taught every kind of craft, and the work he turns out is full of grace, so the goddess poured grace over his head and shoulders. Then he went off and sat down on the shore of the sea, gleaming with beauty and grace.

The girl looked at him and marvelled, and said to her women with the lovely hair: "Listen, white-armed girls, and let me say something. It is not against the will of all the gods who hold Olympus that this man has come among the godlike Phaeacians. Before, he seemed to me a rough-looking creature; now he is like the gods who hold the wide sky. I wish a man like him might be called my husband, living here, and that it might please him to stay. But come, my girls, give the stranger food and drink."

So she spoke, and they listened readily and obeyed, and set food and drink in front of Odysseus. And much-enduring noble Odysseus drank and ate ravenously, because it was a long time since he had tasted food.

Then white-armed Nausicaa thought of something else. She folded the clothes and put them in the fine wagon and yoked the strong-hoofed mules and got up on the car herself. Then she called Odysseus and said to him:

"Get up now, stranger, and come to the city, so that I can send you on to the house of my wise father, where I promise you will get to know all the best of the Phaeacians. Only do it like this — and I do not think you lack understanding. As long as we are going through the country and the ploughed fields of men, walk quickly with the women behind the mules and the wagon, and I will lead the way. But when we are about to go into the city, which has a high wall running round it — there is a fine harbour on either side of the city and the entrance is narrow, and the curved ships are drawn up along the road, because every man has a berth of his own for his ship. Their place of assembly is there too, round the fine temple of Poseidon, made of huge stones set deep in the earth. There the men busy themselves with the tackle of their black ships, with cables and sails, and shape the thin oar-blades. The Phaeacians care nothing for the bow or the quiver, but for masts and ships' oars and the trim ships they take such pleasure in crossing the grey sea in.

"It is their rough talk I want to avoid, in case somebody sneers at me afterwards — there are insolent people in this country — and some low fellow, meeting us, might say: 'Who is this following Nausicaa, this tall good-looking stranger? Where did she find him? He will be a husband for her, no doubt. Perhaps she has picked up some wanderer off his ship, from a people who live far away — there is nobody near us — or perhaps a god has come down from heaven in answer to her prayers, after all this praying, and she will have him for a husband all her days. Better so, if she has gone out and found herself a husband from somewhere else; she certainly looks down on the Phaeacians here in her own country, where she has plenty of noble suitors.' That is what they would say, and it would be thrown up at me. And I would blame another girl myself who did such a thing, and went about with men before the day of her open marriage, in spite of her dear father and mother, while they were still alive.

"So listen to what I say, stranger, and be quick about it, so that you get an escort from my father as soon as possible, and your journey home. You will find a fine grove of Athena close to the road, a grove of poplars. A spring wells up in it, and there is a meadow round about. That is my father's park and his fruitful vineyard, as far from the city as a man's voice carries when he shouts. Sit down there and wait a while, until we get to the city and reach my father's house. And when you think we have reached the house, then go into the city of the Phaeacians and ask for the house of my father, great-hearted Alcinous. It is easy to recognise, and a child could show you the way, a mere baby; the Phaeacians' houses are not built at all like the palace of the lord Alcinous.

"But when the house and the courtyard have taken you in, go quickly through the great hall until you come to my mother. She sits at the hearth in the firelight, spinning the purple yarn, a wonder to look at, leaning against a pillar, with her women sitting behind her. My father's throne is set against the same pillar, and he sits on it drinking his wine like an immortal. Go past him and put your arms round my mother's knees, so that you may see the day of your homecoming quickly and with joy, however far away you have come from. If she thinks well of you, then there is hope that you will see your friends and get back to your well-built house and your own country."

With that she struck the mules with the shining whip, and they quickly left the streams of the river behind. They trotted well and worked their ambling feet well, and she drove carefully, so that the girls and Odysseus could follow on foot, and used the lash with judgment.

Then the sun set, and they came to the famous grove sacred to Athena. Odysseus sat down there and prayed at once to the daughter of great Zeus.

"Hear me, child of Zeus who holds the aegis, unwearied one. Listen to me now, since you did not listen before, when I was being battered, when the glorious earth-shaker was battering me. Grant that I come to the Phaeacians as a man to be welcomed and pitied."

So he spoke in prayer, and Pallas Athena heard him. But she did not yet appear to him face to face, because she was wary of her father's brother; and he went on raging furiously against godlike Odysseus, until at last he reached his own land.
