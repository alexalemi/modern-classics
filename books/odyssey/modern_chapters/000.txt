Book One

Tell me, Muse, of the man of many turns, who was driven far and wide after he sacked the holy citadel of Troy. He saw the cities of many peoples and came to know their minds, and on the sea he suffered many griefs in his own heart, fighting for his life and for the homecoming of his companions. Even so he did not save them, hard as he tried: they died by their own recklessness, the fools, who ate the cattle of Helios the Sun, and the god took from them the day of their return. Of these things, goddess, daughter of Zeus, begin where you will, and tell them to us.

By now all the others who had escaped being killed outright were home, safe from the war and the sea. He alone was still kept from his wife and his own country, held by the queenly nymph Calypso, a great goddess, in her hollow caves, because she wanted him for a husband. But when the years came round and the season arrived that the gods had marked for his return to Ithaca, he was not free of his troubles even then, even among his own people. All the gods pitied him except Poseidon, who stayed furious with godlike Odysseus until at last he reached his own land.

Now Poseidon had gone off to the Ethiopians, who live at the ends of the earth in two divisions, some where the Sun goes down and some where he rises, to receive a sacrifice of bulls and rams, and he was sitting at the feast enjoying himself. The rest of the gods were gathered in the halls of Olympian Zeus, and the father of gods and men was the first to speak. He was thinking of Aegisthus, whom Orestes, Agamemnon's famous son, had killed; and with that in his mind he said to the immortals:

"It is astonishing how ready human beings are to blame the gods. Everything bad comes from us, they say — when in fact it is their own recklessness that brings them suffering beyond their share. Look at Aegisthus. He took the wife of the son of Atreus, beyond his share, and killed the man when he came home, and he knew what it would cost him: we warned him ourselves. We sent Hermes, the sharp-eyed slayer of Argus, to tell him not to kill the man and not to court the wife, because Orestes would come to avenge the son of Atreus once he was grown and wanted his own land. That is what Hermes told him, meaning it kindly, and he could not persuade the heart of Aegisthus. Now Aegisthus has paid for all of it at once."

Then the goddess grey-eyed Athena answered him: "Father of us all, son of Cronos, highest of the powers, that man is lying where he deserves to lie, and so may anyone else die who does what he did. But my heart is torn for Odysseus, who is clever and unlucky, and who has been suffering a long way from anyone who loves him, on an island in the middle of the sea, where the navel of the water is. There is a wooded island, and a goddess lives there, the daughter of Atlas, who thinks bad thoughts and knows the depths of the whole sea, and who holds up the tall pillars that keep earth and sky apart. It is his daughter who keeps that unhappy man from going home. She works on him, day after day, with soft coaxing words, so that he will forget Ithaca. And Odysseus, who would be glad just to see the smoke going up from his own country, wants to die.

"Does none of this touch you, Olympian? Did Odysseus not do everything for you beside the Argive ships in the wide land of Troy? Why are you so set against him, Zeus?"

Then Zeus who gathers the clouds answered her. "My child, what a thing to let past your teeth. How could I forget godlike Odysseus, who has more sense than any man alive, and who gave more than any man to the immortal gods who hold the wide sky? It is Poseidon, who holds the earth, who keeps up this stubborn anger, because of the Cyclops whose eye Odysseus put out — godlike Polyphemus, the strongest of all the Cyclopes. The nymph Thoosa bore him, the daughter of Phorcys who rules the restless sea, after she lay with Poseidon in the hollow caves. That is why Poseidon the earth-shaker does not kill Odysseus, but keeps him wandering, away from his own country.

"Come, though — let all of us here work out how he is to get home. Poseidon will let his anger go. He will not be able to hold out alone against the will of every immortal."

Then the goddess grey-eyed Athena answered him: "Father of us all, son of Cronos, highest of the powers, if it truly is the pleasure of the blessed gods now that clever Odysseus should get home, then let us send Hermes the messenger, the slayer of Argus, to the island of Ogygia, to tell the nymph with the lovely hair, as quickly as he can, what we have firmly decided: that Odysseus, who endures everything, is to go home. And I will go to Ithaca myself, to stir up his son and put some courage into him — to have him call the long-haired Achaeans to an assembly and speak his mind to the suitors, who go on killing his crowding sheep and his slow-moving cattle. And I will send him to Sparta and to sandy Pylos to ask after his father's homecoming, in case he hears something, and so that men may think well of him."

With that she bound on her feet the beautiful sandals, immortal and golden, that carry her over the water and over the endless land as fast as a gust of wind, and she took up her heavy spear, tipped with sharp bronze, huge and strong, the spear she breaks the ranks of fighting men with when she is angry with them, she who is the daughter of a mighty father. Then she went darting down from the peaks of Olympus and stood in Ithaca, at the outer gate of Odysseus, on the threshold of the courtyard, holding the bronze spear in her hand, in the likeness of a stranger — Mentes, the chief of the Taphians.

She found the arrogant suitors there. They were playing draughts in front of the doors, sitting on the hides of oxen they had killed themselves, while the heralds and the busy attendants worked round them, some mixing wine and water in the bowls, some wiping the tables down with sponges and setting them out, and some carving up a great deal of meat.

Telemachus saw her long before anyone else. He was sitting among the suitors, sick at heart, imagining his noble father — how he might come from somewhere and scatter the suitors through the house, and take back his honour and rule over what was his. That was what he was thinking about, sitting among them, when he saw Athena, and he went straight to the outer door, ashamed in his own mind that a stranger should be left standing at the gate. He went up to her and took her right hand and took the bronze spear from her, and spoke to her with winged words:

"Welcome, stranger. You will be treated well in this house, and when you have eaten you can tell us what you need."

He led the way and Pallas Athena followed him. When they were inside the high house he carried the spear to a tall pillar and set it in the polished spear-rack, where a good many other spears already stood, the spears of Odysseus, who endures everything. He led Athena to a chair and sat her down, spreading a linen cloth under her — a beautiful chair, finely made, with a footstool below it — and drew up an inlaid seat for himself beside her, away from the others, so that the noise of the arrogant crowd would not spoil the stranger's meal, and so that he could ask him about his father, who was gone.

A maid brought water in a lovely golden jug and poured it over a silver basin for them to wash their hands, and drew a polished table up beside them. The housekeeper, a grave woman, brought bread and set it out, and plenty of good things with it, giving freely from her stores. A carver lifted platters of every kind of meat and set them down, and put out golden cups, and a herald kept walking to and fro pouring the wine.

Then the arrogant suitors came in and sat down in rows on the chairs and the high seats. Heralds poured water over their hands, women heaped bread in baskets beside them, and boys filled the bowls to the brim, and they put out their hands to the good food in front of them. When they had eaten and drunk as much as they wanted, they turned their minds to other things, to singing and dancing, which are the crown of a feast. A herald put a beautiful lyre into the hands of Phemius, who sang for the suitors because they made him. He struck the strings and began his lovely song.

But Telemachus leaned his head close to grey-eyed Athena, so that the others would not hear, and said:

"Dear guest, will you be angry with me if I say something? These men care about nothing but this, the lyre and the singing — easily enough, when they are eating up another man's living and paying nothing for it, a man whose white bones are rotting in the rain somewhere on land, or rolling in the surf. If they saw him come back to Ithaca, every one of them would pray to be quicker on his feet rather than richer in gold and fine clothes. As it is, he has died badly, and there is no comfort for us, not even if somebody somewhere on earth says he is coming. The day of his homecoming is gone.

"But tell me this, and tell me the truth. Who are you, and where do you come from? Where is your city, and where are your parents? What sort of ship did you come on, and how did the sailors bring you to Ithaca? Who did they say they were? Because you certainly did not get here on foot. And tell me this too, so that I know: is this your first time here, or were you a guest-friend of my father's? A great many men used to come to this house — he went about among people himself."

Then the goddess grey-eyed Athena answered him: "I will tell you all of it plainly. I am Mentes, son of Anchialus, who was a wise man, and I am lord of the Taphians, who love the oar. I have put in here with my ship and my crew, as you see, sailing over the wine-dark sea to people of another language, on my way to Temese for copper; I am carrying shining iron. My ship is lying out there beyond the town, away in the fields, in the harbour of Rheithron, under wooded Neion. Our families are guest-friends, and have been from the old days. Go and ask the old soldier Laertes, if you like. They tell me he does not come into the town any more, but suffers out in the country with an old woman to look after him, who puts food and drink in front of him when his legs give out from dragging himself along the slope of his vineyard.

"I came because they told me he was back among his people — your father, I mean. But it seems the gods are keeping him from the road home. Because noble Odysseus is not dead, not yet, not anywhere on this earth. He is alive, I am sure of it, and held back on the wide sea, on an island in the middle of the water, and hard men have him, savages, and they are keeping him there against his will.

"Now I will make you a prophecy, the way the immortals put it in my mind, and the way I think it will turn out — though I am no prophet and I know nothing about the meaning of birds. He will not be away from his own country much longer. Not even if they had him in chains. He will work out a way home; he is a man who works things out.

"But tell me this, and tell me the truth. Tall as you are, are you really the son of Odysseus himself? Your head and your fine eyes are astonishingly like his. We were often together, before he sailed for Troy, where the rest of them went too, the best of the Argives, in their hollow ships. Since that day I have not seen Odysseus, and he has not seen me."

Then clear-headed Telemachus answered her: "I will tell you plainly too, stranger. My mother says I am his son. I do not know myself. Nobody has ever known for certain who fathered him. I wish I had been the son of some lucky man who grew old among his own possessions. But since you ask me, they say I am the son of the unluckiest man who ever lived."

Then the goddess grey-eyed Athena answered him: "Well, the gods have not given you a nameless family for the future, if Penelope bore a son like you. But tell me this, and tell me the truth. What is this feast, this crowd? What do you want with it? Is it a drinking party, or a wedding? It is certainly not a meal that each man brought his share to. They seem to me to be eating in your house with an insolence past bearing. Any decent man coming in here and seeing all this would be disgusted."

Then clear-headed Telemachus answered her: "Stranger, since you ask me about it — this house was once on its way to being rich and respected, as long as that man was still among his people. But the gods have willed it otherwise, and thought up something worse: they have made him vanish as they have made no other man vanish. I would not grieve so much for his death, if he had been killed among his companions in the land of the Trojans, or had died in the arms of his friends when he had wound up the whole skein of the war. Then the whole Achaean army would have built him a tomb, and he would have won great glory for his son as well, in the days to come. As it is, the storm winds have taken him away and left nothing behind. He is out of sight, out of hearing, and what he has left me is pain and weeping.

"And I do not grieve and cry only for him. The gods have brought me other troubles as bad. All the princes who hold power over the islands — Dulichium and Same and wooded Zacynthus — and the ones who lord it over rocky Ithaca, every one of them is courting my mother and wearing out my house. And she will not refuse the marriage, which she hates, and cannot bring the matter to an end, while they eat up everything I own. Before long they will finish me too."

Then Pallas Athena, angry now, said to him: "This is disgraceful. You need Odysseus back, and badly, to lay his hands on these shameless men. If only he would come now and stand at the outer gate of the house with his helmet and shield and his two spears — the man I first saw in our house, drinking and enjoying himself, on his way back from Ephyre, from the house of Ilus son of Mermerus. Odysseus had gone there in his fast ship looking for a poison to smear on his bronze arrowheads, and Ilus would not give it to him, because he was afraid of the gods who live for ever; but my father gave it to him, because he was extraordinarily fond of him. If Odysseus in that condition were to walk in among the suitors, every one of them would find a quick death and a bitter courtship.

"But all this lies on the knees of the gods, whether he comes back and takes his revenge in his own halls or whether he does not. What I want you to think about is how you are going to drive the suitors out of the house. Listen to me now, and take in what I say. Tomorrow morning call the Achaean lords to an assembly and speak your mind to all of them, with the gods as your witnesses. Tell the suitors to scatter, each to his own place. As for your mother, if her heart is set on marrying, let her go back to the house of her powerful father, and they will make the wedding there and put together the gifts, all of them, everything that ought to go with a much-loved daughter. And I will give you good advice for yourself, if you will take it.

"Fit out the best ship you have with twenty rowers, and go and look for news of your father, who has been gone so long. Somebody may tell you something, or you may hear a word from Zeus, which is what most often brings men news. Go first to Pylos and ask noble Nestor, and from there to Sparta, to fair-haired Menelaus, who was the last of the bronze-armoured Achaeans to get home. If you hear your father is alive and on his way, then you could hold out for another year, hard as it is. And if you hear he is dead and gone, then come back to your own country, heap up a mound for him, pay him the funeral rites, all of them, as is proper, and give your mother to a husband. Then, when all that is done and finished, think hard about how you are going to kill the suitors in your own hall, by a trick or in the open. You are not the right age any more to be behaving like a child.

"Or have you not heard what a name Orestes made for himself, everywhere, when he killed the man who murdered his father — sly Aegisthus, who had killed his glorious father? You too, my friend — and I can see you are tall and good-looking — be brave, so that men who are not born yet will speak well of you.

"But I must go down now to my fast ship and my crew, who will be getting impatient waiting for me. Look after yourself, and think about what I have said."

Then clear-headed Telemachus answered her: "Stranger, you have said all this out of kindness, the way a father speaks to his son, and I will not forget any of it. But stay a little, however eager you are to be off. Have a bath, and enjoy yourself, and then go back to your ship in good spirits with a gift from me, something valuable and very beautiful, to keep as an heirloom — the kind of gift friends give to friends."

Then the goddess grey-eyed Athena answered him: "Do not hold me here any longer. I want to be off. Whatever gift your heart tells you to give me, give it to me when I come back, to take home with me. Choose a really beautiful one; you will get its worth in return."

And with that grey-eyed Athena was gone, flying upward like a bird. She had put strength and courage into him, and made him think about his father even more than before. He turned the thing over in his mind and was amazed, because he understood that she had been a god; and he went straight back among the suitors, godlike himself.

The famous singer was still singing to them, and they sat in silence, listening. He was singing of the Achaeans' journey home from Troy, the bitter one that Pallas Athena laid on them. And upstairs the daughter of Icarius, wise Penelope, heard the wonderful song, and came down the high staircase from her room, not alone — two women came with her. When she reached the suitors, that lovely woman, she stood by the doorpost of the well-built hall, holding her shining veil in front of her face, with a faithful maid on either side of her. Then she began to cry, and said to the divine singer:

"Phemius, you know many other things to hold an audience with, the deeds of men and gods that singers make famous. Sing them one of those, and let them drink their wine in silence. But stop this miserable song. It tears at the heart in my breast every time, because a grief no one could forget has come to me above all women. That is the head I always remember and long for — my husband, whose fame goes wide through Hellas and the middle of Argos."

Then clear-headed Telemachus answered her: "Mother, why do you grudge the singer the right to entertain us however his heart moves him? Singers are not to blame. Zeus is to blame, I think, who gives to men who work for their living, to each one as he pleases. There is no being angry with this man for singing the bad end of the Danaans. People always praise the newest song they hear most highly. Make your own heart and mind endure the listening. Odysseus was not the only man who lost the day of his homecoming at Troy. Plenty of others died as well.

"Go back to your room and see to your own work, the loom and the distaff, and tell the women to get on with theirs. Talking will be the men's business, all of them, and mine most of all: the authority in this house is mine."

She went back upstairs astonished, taking her son's good sense to heart. She went up to her room with her women, and there she cried for Odysseus, her dear husband, until grey-eyed Athena put sweet sleep on her eyelids.

The suitors broke into uproar through the shadowy hall, and every one of them prayed to be the man who lay beside her. Then clear-headed Telemachus was the first to speak.

"Suitors of my mother, with your intolerable arrogance — let us enjoy the feast for now, and no shouting, since it is a fine thing to listen to a singer as good as this man, whose voice is like a god's. But in the morning let us go to the assembly and take our places, all of us, so that I can tell you outright to get out of these halls. Make your feasts somewhere else. Eat your own food, and move from house to house. Or if this looks better to you and more profitable, that one man's living should be destroyed and nothing paid for it, then eat it. But I will call on the gods who live for ever, in the hope that Zeus grants that the work be paid for; and then you will die inside this house, and nothing will be paid for that either."

So he spoke, and every one of them bit his lip and was astonished at Telemachus, at how boldly he had spoken.

Then Antinous, son of Eupeithes, answered him: "Telemachus, it must be the gods themselves teaching you to talk so big and to speak so boldly. I hope the son of Cronos never makes you king in sea-girt Ithaca, which is yours by birth."

Then clear-headed Telemachus answered him: "Antinous, will you be angry with me if I say something? I would be glad to take even that from the hand of Zeus. Do you think it is the worst thing that can happen to a man? It is no bad thing to be a king. Your house grows rich straight away and you are held in more honour yourself. But there are plenty of other Achaean kings in sea-girt Ithaca, young ones and old ones, and one of them may well have the place, now that noble Odysseus is dead. What I will be is master of my own house, and of the slaves that noble Odysseus won for me."

Then Eurymachus, son of Polybus, answered him: "Telemachus, this lies on the knees of the gods, which of the Achaeans is to be king in sea-girt Ithaca. Your possessions you may keep, and be master in your own house. No man will ever come and take what is yours away from you by force, against your will, while there are men living in Ithaca. But I should like to ask you about the stranger, my friend. Where does the man come from? What country does he say he belongs to? Where is his family, and the fields he was born on? Does he bring some news of your father coming home, or did he come here on business of his own? How he jumped up and was gone at once! He did not wait to be recognised — and he did not look like a common man, either."

Then clear-headed Telemachus answered him: "Eurymachus, my father's homecoming is lost and gone. I no longer believe any news, wherever it comes from, and I take no notice of the prophecies my mother gets out of some seer she has called into the hall. That stranger is a guest-friend of my father's from Taphos. He says he is Mentes, son of Anchialus, who was a wise man, and that he is lord of the Taphians, who love the oar."

That is what Telemachus said. But in his heart he knew the immortal goddess.

The suitors turned to dancing and to the pleasure of singing, and enjoyed themselves, and waited for the evening to come; and while they were enjoying themselves the dark evening came. Then they went off, each man to his own house, to sleep. But Telemachus went up to his bedroom, which had been built in the beautiful courtyard, high up, with a wide view all round it, and he went to bed there with a great deal on his mind. Faithful Eurycleia went with him, carrying blazing torches — the daughter of Ops, son of Peisenor. Laertes had bought her long ago with his own money, when she was a girl, and had paid the price of twenty oxen for her, and he honoured her in his house as much as he honoured his own faithful wife, but he never slept with her, because he was afraid of his wife's anger. She was the one who carried the blazing torches for Telemachus. Of all the women she loved him most, and she had nursed him when he was small.

He opened the doors of the well-built room and sat down on the bed and pulled off his soft tunic and put it into the wise old woman's hands, and she folded it and smoothed it and hung it on a peg beside the corded bedstead, and then went out of the room, pulling the door to by its silver handle and driving the bolt home with the strap. And there, all night, wrapped in a fleece of wool, he thought about the journey Athena had shown him.
