Book Five

Dawn rose from her bed beside lordly Tithonus, to bring light to the immortals and to mortal men. And the gods were sitting down to council, and among them Zeus who thunders on high, whose strength is greatest. Athena was telling them the many troubles of Odysseus, calling them to mind, because it disturbed her that he was staying in the nymph's house.

"Father Zeus, and you other blessed gods who live for ever — from now on let no sceptred king be deliberately kind and gentle, or take any care for what is right in his mind. Let him be harsh, always, and do injustice, seeing that nobody remembers godlike Odysseus, among the people he was lord over, and he was gentle as a father. He is on an island suffering terribly, in the halls of the nymph Calypso, who keeps him there by force; and he cannot get back to his own land, because he has no ships with oars at hand and no companions to send him on his way over the broad back of the sea. And now on top of that they mean to kill his well-loved son on his way home; for he has gone after news of his father to sacred Pylos and to lovely Lacedaemon."

Then Zeus who gathers the clouds answered her and said: "My child, what a thing to let past your teeth. Did you not plan this yourself — that Odysseus should take his revenge on those men when he came? As for Telemachus, guide him with your own skill, since you can, so that he reaches his own country entirely unhurt, and the suitors come back in their ship with nothing accomplished."

He spoke, and said to Hermes, his dear son: "Hermes, since you are our messenger at other times, go now and tell the nymph with the lovely hair what we have firmly decided: the homecoming of Odysseus, who endures everything. He is to go back with no guidance from gods or from mortal men, but on a raft bound firm, suffering as he goes, and on the twentieth day he is to come to deep-soiled Scheria, the land of the Phaeacians, who are close kin to the gods. They will honour him heartily, as if he were a god, and send him in a ship to his own dear country, after giving him stores of bronze and gold and clothing, more than Odysseus would ever have brought back from Troy if he had come home unhurt with his fair share of the plunder. That is how it is his fate to see his friends and reach his high-roofed house and his own country."

So he spoke, and the messenger, the slayer of Argus, did not fail to listen. Straight away he bound the beautiful sandals under his feet, immortal and golden, which carry him over the water and over the endless land as fast as a gust of wind. And he took up the wand with which he lulls the eyes of whoever he likes to sleep, and wakes others again out of their slumber. With this in his hand the strong slayer of Argus flew.

He stepped onto Pieria out of the upper air and swooped down on the sea, and then went skimming over the wave like a bird, the cormorant, which hunts fish over the dreadful gulfs of the restless sea and wets its thick feathers in the salt water. That was how Hermes rode over the crowding waves.

But when he reached the island, which lay far off, he came out of the violet sea onto the land, and went on until he came to a great cave, where the nymph with the lovely hair lived; and he found her inside. A great fire was burning on the hearth, and the smell of split cedar and juniper burning carried far over the island; and inside she was singing in a sweet voice as she went to and fro at the loom, weaving with a golden shuttle.

Round the cave a thick wood was growing, alder and poplar and sweet-smelling cypress, where long-winged birds nested, owls and falcons and chattering sea-crows, who do their business on the sea. And right there about the hollow cave a garden vine ran trailing, in the pride of its season, thick with clusters. And four springs in a row were flowing with bright water, close beside one another, turned one this way and one that. And soft meadows of violets and parsley were in bloom all round. Even an immortal coming there might stand and stare and take delight in it; and the messenger, the slayer of Argus, stood there and stared.

But when he had marvelled at it all in his heart, he went into the wide cave. Calypso, the beautiful goddess, knew him at once when she saw him face to face; the immortal gods are not strangers to one another, even when one of them lives far away. But he did not find great-hearted Odysseus inside, because he was sitting on the shore, as he always did, wearing out his soul with tears and groans and grief, looking out over the restless sea and weeping.

And Calypso, the beautiful goddess, questioned Hermes, after she had made him sit down on a bright shining chair.

"Why have you come here, Hermes of the golden wand, an honoured guest and a welcome one? You have not been in the habit of coming before. Say what is in your mind. My heart tells me to do it, if I can do it, and if it is a thing that can be done. But come further in and let me set something before you."

With that the goddess set a table beside him loaded with ambrosia, and mixed the red nectar. So the messenger, the slayer of Argus, drank and ate. And when he had dined and satisfied himself with food, he answered her and said:

"You question me on my coming, a goddess questioning a god, and I will tell you the truth, since you ask me. It was Zeus who told me to come here, against my will. Who would choose to run across such a stretch of salt water, past all telling? And there is no city of mortals near who make sacrifice to the gods and choice hecatombs. But there is no possible way for any other god to get round the will of Zeus who holds the aegis, or make it come to nothing.

"He says there is a man here with you who is the most wretched of all those fighters who fought round Priam's city for nine years, and in the tenth year sacked the city and set out for home. On the way they sinned against Athena, and she sent an evil wind and long waves against them. There all the rest of his fine companions died, and as for him, the wind and the wave carried him and brought him here. Zeus now tells you to send him on his way as quickly as possible. It is not his fate to die here, far from his friends. It is still his lot to see his friends and to reach his high-roofed house and his own country."

So he spoke, and Calypso, the beautiful goddess, shuddered, and spoke to him with winged words.

"You are cruel, you gods, and jealous beyond anything, since you grudge it to goddesses to sleep with men openly, if any of us takes a mortal for a lover. So when rosy-fingered Dawn took Orion for herself, you gods who live at your ease grudged it to her, until in Ortygia chaste Artemis of the golden throne attacked him with her gentle arrows and killed him. So too, when Demeter with the lovely hair gave way to her feeling and lay in love with Iasion in the thrice-ploughed fallow field, Zeus did not stay ignorant of it long, but struck him with his bright thunderbolt and killed him. And now you grudge it to me in the same way, you gods, that a mortal man should stay with me.

"I saved him when he was astride the keel and all alone, because Zeus had struck his fast ship with his bright thunderbolt and smashed it in the middle of the wine-dark sea. There all the rest of his fine companions died, and as for him, the wind and the wave carried him and brought him here. I welcomed him kindly and gave him food, and said I would make him immortal and ageless all his days. But since there is no possible way for any other god to get round the will of Zeus who holds the aegis, or make it come to nothing, let him go, if Zeus orders it and commands it, over the restless sea. But I will not be the one to see him off, because I have no ships with oars at hand and no men to send him on his way over the broad back of the sea. Still, I will advise him willingly and hide nothing, so that he can get back to his own country entirely unhurt."

Then the messenger, the slayer of Argus, answered her: "Send him off now, then, and beware of the anger of Zeus, in case he grows angry with you and takes it out on you afterwards."

With that the strong slayer of Argus went away, and the queenly nymph went to great-hearted Odysseus, now that she had heard the message of Zeus. She found him sitting on the shore, and his eyes were never dry of tears, and his sweet life was ebbing away as he mourned for his homecoming, since the nymph no longer pleased him. At night he did sleep beside her in the hollow caves, because he had to, unwilling beside a willing woman; but by day he would sit on the rocks and the sands, wearing out his soul with tears and groans and grief, and look out over the restless sea and weep.

Coming up close to him, the beautiful goddess spoke to him. "Unhappy man, do not go on grieving here, and do not let your life waste away. I am ready now to send you off. Come, cut long beams with the axe and make a broad raft, and fix cross-planks on it for a deck well up above, to carry you over the misty water. And I will put bread and water and red wine in it to please your heart and keep hunger off you. And I will put clothes on you and send a fair wind behind you, so that you get back to your own country entirely unhurt — if it is the will of the gods who hold the wide sky, who are stronger than I am both to plan and to bring about."

So she spoke, and much-enduring noble Odysseus shuddered, and spoke to her with winged words. "You are planning something else here, goddess, and not my sending off, when you tell me to cross the great gulf of the sea on a raft — a dreadful and hard crossing, which not even the trim fast-running ships get over, glad as they are of a wind from Zeus. I will not set foot on a raft against your will, unless you will bring yourself, goddess, to swear a great oath that you are not plotting some fresh mischief against me to my harm."

So he spoke, and Calypso, the beautiful goddess, smiled and stroked him with her hand and said to him: "You are a rogue, and no fool either, to have thought of saying such a thing. Let the earth be my witness in this, then, and the wide sky above, and the down-flowing water of the Styx, which is the greatest and most dreadful oath for the blessed gods, that I am not plotting any fresh mischief against you to your harm. What I have in mind, and what I mean to advise, is what I would work out for myself if such a need came on me. My mind is a fair one, and the heart in this breast of mine is not made of iron: it feels pity."

With that the beautiful goddess led the way quickly, and he followed in the goddess's footsteps. And they came to the hollow cave, the goddess and the man, and he sat down on the chair Hermes had got up from, and the nymph set out every kind of food for him to eat and drink, the kind mortal men eat. She herself sat down facing godlike Odysseus, and the women set ambrosia and nectar in front of her. So they put out their hands to the good food in front of them. And when they had had their fill of food and drink, Calypso, the beautiful goddess, spoke first.

"Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of the many tricks — is it home to your own dear country that you want to go, straight away, now? Well, even so, I wish you well. But if you knew in your heart the full measure of suffering it is your fate to fill up before you reach your own country, you would stay here and keep this house with me and be immortal — for all your longing to see your wife, whom you want day after day. I do not think I am the lesser of us in looks or in build. It is not seemly for mortal women to compete with immortals in shape or beauty."

Then Odysseus of the many tricks answered her and said: "Great goddess, do not be angry with me over this. I know perfectly well myself that wise Penelope is less to look at than you, in beauty and in build. She is mortal, and you are immortal and ageless. But even so I want, and I long day after day, to get home and see the day of my return. And if some god strikes me again on the wine-dark sea, I will endure it; I have a heart in my breast that endures affliction. I have suffered a great deal already and worked hard, on the waves and in the war. Let this be added to that."

So he spoke, and the sun went down and the dark came on. And the two of them went into the innermost recess of the hollow cave and took their pleasure in love, staying beside one another.

As soon as the early dawn appeared, rosy-fingered, Odysseus put on a cloak and a tunic, and the nymph dressed herself in a long white robe, finely woven and beautiful, and put a fine golden girdle round her waist and a veil on her head. Then she set about planning the sending-off of great-hearted Odysseus. She gave him a great axe that fitted his hands, an axe of bronze, sharpened on both sides, with a beautiful olive-wood handle fastened firmly in it; and after that she gave him a polished adze. Then she led the way to the far edge of the island where tall trees were standing, alder and poplar and fir, reaching up to the sky, long dead and well seasoned, which would float lightly for him.

And when she had shown him where the tall trees grew, Calypso, the beautiful goddess, went home, and he set to cutting timber, and the work went fast. He felled twenty trees in all and trimmed them with the axe, and then smoothed them all skilfully and made them straight to the line. Meanwhile Calypso, the beautiful goddess, brought him augers, and he bored all the pieces and fitted them to one another and hammered it together with pegs and joints. As wide as a man well skilled in carpentry marks out the curve of the hull of a broad-beamed freighter, that wide did Odysseus make his raft. He set up the deck-beams, bolting them to the close-set ribs, and worked on, and finished the raft with long gunwales. He set a mast in it and a yard-arm fitted to it, and made himself a steering-oar besides, to steer with. Then he fenced the whole thing in from stem to stern with willow withes, to keep the waves off, and piled brush on top. Meanwhile Calypso, the beautiful goddess, brought him cloth to make a sail, and he made that skilfully too, and made the braces and halyards and sheets fast in the raft, and then forced it down into the bright sea with levers.

The fourth day came and all his work was done, and on the fifth beautiful Calypso sent him off from the island, after she had bathed him and dressed him in sweet-smelling clothes. The goddess put a skin of dark wine on the raft, and another, a big one, of water, and provisions in a bag, and plenty of good things in it to please his heart, and she sent out a gentle warm wind. Noble Odysseus spread his sail to the breeze gladly, and sat and steered his raft skilfully with the steering-oar, and no sleep fell on his eyelids as he watched the Pleiades, and late-setting Bootes, and the Bear, which men also call the Wain, which always turns where it is and keeps watch on Orion, and alone has no share in the baths of Ocean. Calypso, the beautiful goddess, had told him to keep that star on his left as he sailed over the sea.

So for seventeen days he sailed over the sea, and on the eighteenth the shadowy mountains of the land of the Phaeacians appeared, at the point where it lay nearest to him; and it looked like a shield in the misty water.

But the glorious earth-shaker, coming back from the Ethiopians, saw him far off from the mountains of the Solymi, because Odysseus came into his sight sailing over the sea. He grew angrier still in his heart, and shook his head, and said to himself:

"So the gods have changed their minds about Odysseus while I was among the Ethiopians. And there he is, close to the land of the Phaeacians, where it is his fate to get clear of the great chain of misery that has come on him. But even yet, I think, I shall drive him to his fill of trouble."

With that he gathered the clouds, and took his trident in his hands and stirred up the sea, and roused the blasts of every kind of wind, and hid land and sea alike in cloud; and night rushed down out of the sky. The east wind and the south wind clashed together, and the hard-blowing west wind and the north wind, born in the bright sky, rolling a huge wave before it.

Then the knees of Odysseus went slack and his heart melted, and in his distress he said to his own great spirit: "Wretch that I am — what is going to happen to me at the end of it? I am afraid everything the goddess said was true, when she told me that on the sea, before I reached my own country, I should fill up my measure of suffering. All of it is happening now. Look how Zeus is covering the wide sky with clouds and has stirred up the sea, and the blasts of every kind of wind sweep down on me. Now my destruction is certain.

"Three times blessed those Danaans, four times blessed, who died long ago in the wide land of Troy doing what the sons of Atreus wanted. I wish I had died and met my fate that day when the crowds of Trojans were throwing bronze-tipped spears at me, fighting over the dead body of the son of Peleus. Then I would have had funeral rites, and the Achaeans would have spread my fame; but now it was appointed for me to be cut off by a miserable death."

Even as he spoke the great wave struck him from above, rushing on him with terrible force, and whirled his raft round. He fell far from the raft and let the steering-oar go out of his hand, and the mast was broken in the middle by the fierce blast of the tumbling winds that came on it, and the sail and yard-arm fell far out in the sea. The wave held him under a long time, and he could not come up at once from under the rush of the mighty water, because the clothes beautiful Calypso had given him weighed him down. At last he came up and spat the bitter brine out of his mouth, which ran in streams off his head. Even so he did not forget his raft, badly off as he was, but went after it through the waves and got hold of it and sat down in the middle of it, trying to keep clear of the doom of death; and a great wave kept carrying him this way and that along its course.

As when in autumn the north wind carries the thistle-tufts over the plain and they cling close to one another, so the winds carried the raft this way and that over the sea. Now the south wind would fling it to the north wind to drive, and now the east wind would give it up to the west wind to chase.

But the daughter of Cadmus saw him, Ino of the lovely ankles, Leucothea, who was once a mortal with human speech and now has her share of honour from the gods in the depths of the sea. She was touched with pity for Odysseus as he drifted in his misery, and she rose up out of the deep like a sea-mew on the wing, and sat on the firmly-bound raft, and said:

"Unhappy man, why has Poseidon the earth-shaker taken such a furious anger against you, that he is sowing so many troubles for you? Yet he shall not destroy you altogether, for all his rage. Do this — and I do not think you lack understanding. Strip off these clothes and leave your raft for the winds to drive, and swim with your hands and try to reach the land of the Phaeacians, where it is your fate to escape. Here, take this veil and stretch it under your breast. It is immortal; there is no fear that you will suffer anything or drown. But when you have got your hands on the land, untie it and throw it into the wine-dark sea, well away from the land, and turn your own face away."

With that the goddess gave him the veil, and plunged back into the surging deep herself like a sea-mew, and the dark wave hid her.

Then much-enduring noble Odysseus turned it over, and in his distress he said to his own great spirit: "Oh no. Let it not be that one of the immortals is weaving another snare for me, telling me to leave my raft. I will not obey yet, because with my own eyes I saw that the land is a long way off, where she said I was to escape. This is what I will do, and it seems best: as long as the timbers hold firm in their fastenings, I will stay here and endure the suffering; and when the wave has smashed the raft to pieces, I will swim, since there is nothing better to think of."

While he was turning it over in his mind and heart, Poseidon the earth-shaker raised up a great wave, dreadful and hard, arching over from above, and drove it at him. As a strong wind tosses a heap of dry straw and scatters some this way and some that, so the wave scattered the long timbers of the raft. But Odysseus got astride one plank as if he were riding a horse, and stripped off the clothes beautiful Calypso had given him. Then he stretched the veil under his breast at once and threw himself headlong into the sea with his hands spread, ready to swim.

And the lord, the earth-shaker, saw him, and shook his head, and said to himself: "So now go wandering over the deep, after all the trouble you have suffered, until you come among the people whom Zeus brought up. Even so, I do not think you will make light of your suffering."

With that he whipped up his fine-maned horses and came to Aegae, where his glorious palace is.

But Athena, daughter of Zeus, thought of something else. She blocked the paths of the other winds and told them all to stop and be lulled to rest; but she roused the swift north wind and broke the waves down in front of him, so that Odysseus, born of Zeus, might come among the Phaeacians, who love the oar, and get clear of death and the fates.

So for two nights and two days he was driven about on the swollen waves, and again and again his heart foresaw destruction. But when Dawn with the lovely hair brought the third day to its birth, then the wind dropped and there was a windless calm, and he caught sight of the shore close at hand, throwing a quick glance forward as a great wave lifted him. And as a father's life is most welcome to his children when he has been lying ill, suffering hard pains, wasting away a long while, with some cruel god attacking him, and then to their joy the gods free him from his trouble — so welcome the land and the woods looked to Odysseus, and he swam on, eager to set foot on the ground.

But when he was as far off as a man's voice carries when he shouts, and heard the boom of the sea on the reefs — for the great wave was thundering against the dry land, belching over it terribly, and everything was wrapped in the foam of the sea, since there were no harbours there where ships could ride, and no roadsteads, only jutting headlands and reefs and cliffs — then the knees of Odysseus went slack and his heart melted, and in his distress he said to his own great spirit:

"Oh no. Zeus has granted me to see land at last, beyond all my hoping, and I have managed to cut my way across this gulf, and now nowhere is there a way out of the grey sea. Outside there are sharp crags, and the wave roars foaming round them, and the rock runs up sheer, and the water is deep close in, so that there is no way at all to plant both feet firmly and get clear. If I try to land, a great wave may seize me and dash me against the jagged rock, and my struggle will have been for nothing. But if I swim on further, hoping to find shelving beaches and harbours of the sea, I am afraid the storm wind may catch me up again and carry me groaning heavily over the teeming deep — or a god may send some great monster out of the sea at me, and glorious Amphitrite breeds a great many of them. For I know the glorious earth-shaker is full of anger against me."

While he was turning it over in his mind and heart, a great wave carried him against the rugged shore. There his skin would have been stripped off and his bones broken, if the goddess grey-eyed Athena had not put a thought in his mind. He rushed on and seized the rock with both hands and clung to it, groaning, until the great wave went past. So he got clear of that wave; but in its backward rush it came at him again and struck him and flung him far out to sea. As when a cuttlefish is dragged out of its hole and a great many pebbles cling to its suckers, so bits of skin were stripped off his strong hands against the rocks; and the great wave covered him.

Then unlucky Odysseus would have died before his time, if grey-eyed Athena had not given him presence of mind. He made his way out of the surge where it belched on the shore, and swam along outside, keeping his eyes on the land all the time, hoping to find shelving beaches and harbours of the sea. But when, swimming on, he came to the mouth of a fair-flowing river, the place looked best to him: it was smooth of stones, and there was shelter from the wind besides. He recognised the river as it flowed out, and prayed to him in his heart:

"Hear me, king, whoever you are. I come to you as to one greatly longed for, trying to get out of the sea, away from the threats of Poseidon. Even in the eyes of the immortal gods that man deserves respect who comes as a wanderer, as I have come now to your stream and to your knees, after so much hard labour. Pity me, king. I declare that I am your suppliant."

So he spoke, and the god stopped his stream at once, and checked the waves, and made a calm in front of him, and brought him safely to the river mouth. He let his two knees bend and his strong hands fall, because the sea had crushed his spirit. All his flesh was swollen, and sea water ran in streams up through his mouth and his nostrils. So he lay there breathless and speechless, with hardly the strength to move; a terrible weariness had come over him.

But when he came round, and his spirit came back into his breast, he loosed the goddess's veil from him and let it fall into the river that murmured seaward. The great wave carried it back down the stream, and Ino caught it at once in her hands. Then Odysseus went back from the river and sank down in the reeds and kissed the earth, the giver of grain; and in his distress he said to his own great spirit:

"What is to become of me? What is going to happen to me at the end of it? If I keep watch here in the river bed through the miserable night, I am afraid the bitter frost and the fresh dew together will finish me, when I have breathed out my spirit from weakness; and the breeze off the river blows cold in the early morning. But if I climb the slope to the shady wood and lie down to rest in the thick brushwood, hoping the cold and the weariness will leave me and sweet sleep come over me, then I am afraid I shall be a prey and a spoil for wild animals."

As he turned it over, this seemed the better course. He went off to the wood and found it near the water in an open space, and crept under two bushes that grew from the same spot, one of thorn and one of olive. The strength of the wet winds could never blow through them, nor could the rays of the bright sun beat in, nor the rain get through, so closely they grew, twisted into one another. Odysseus crept in under these and at once gathered a wide bed with his hands, for there were fallen leaves there in plenty, enough to shelter two men or three in winter, however bitter the weather. Much-enduring noble Odysseus saw it and was glad, and lay down in the middle and heaped the fallen leaves over himself.

And as a man hides a brand under the dark embers on an outlying farm, a man who has no neighbours, and so saves a seed of fire and does not have to kindle it from somewhere else, so Odysseus covered himself with leaves. And Athena poured sleep over his eyes, to close his eyelids and free him quickly from his wearing exhaustion.
