Book Seven

So he prayed there, much-enduring noble Odysseus, while the two strong mules carried the girl to the city. And when she came to her father's splendid palace she stopped the mules at the outer gate, and her brothers came crowding round her, men like the immortals, and unyoked the mules from the wagon and carried the clothes inside; and she went to her room. There a fire was lit for her by her attendant, Eurymedusa, an old woman from Apeire. The curved ships had brought her from Apeire long ago, and the men had picked her out of the plunder as a gift of honour for Alcinous, because he was king over all the Phaeacians and the people listened to him as to a god. It was she who had brought up white-armed Nausicaa in the palace, and she who lit the fire for her and got her supper ready in her room.

Then Odysseus roused himself to go to the city, and Athena, meaning kindly, poured a thick mist round him, so that none of the great-hearted Phaeacians should meet him and speak mockingly to him and ask him who he was. But when he was about to go into the lovely city, the goddess grey-eyed Athena met him, in the likeness of a young girl carrying a jug, and stood in front of him; and noble Odysseus asked her:

"My child, could you show me the way to the house of the man they call Alcinous, who is lord among the people here? I have come as a stranger who has suffered a great deal, from far away, from a distant country, and so I know nobody among the people who hold this city and this land."

Then the goddess grey-eyed Athena answered him: "Then I will show you the palace, sir, since you ask me. It stands close to the house of my own noble father. Only go quietly, and I will lead the way. Do not look at any man, and do not question anybody. The men here have no patience with strangers, and give no kindly welcome to anyone who comes from another country. They trust in their fast ships and cross the great gulf of the sea, because the earth-shaker granted them that; and their ships are as swift as a bird on the wing, or as a thought."

So speaking, Pallas Athena led the way quickly, and he followed in the goddess's footsteps. And as he went through the city in the middle of them, the Phaeacians, who are famous for their ships, took no notice of him, because fair-haired Athena, the dread goddess, would not allow it, and poured a marvellous mist round him out of the kindness of her heart. And Odysseus marvelled at the harbours and the stately ships, and at the meeting-places where the heroes themselves gathered, and at the walls, long and high and crowned with palisades, a wonder to look at.

But when they had come to the king's splendid palace, the goddess grey-eyed Athena spoke first.

"Here, sir, is the house you told me to show you, and you will find the kings, brought up by Zeus, feasting at the banquet. Go in, and let your heart be afraid of nothing. A bold man does better in everything, even a stranger from another country. It is the queen you must go to first in the palace. Arete is her name, and she comes from the same line as king Alcinous does. Nausithous was first born to the earth-shaker Poseidon and to Periboea, the loveliest of women, the youngest daughter of great-hearted Eurymedon, who was once king over the insolent Giants. But he brought ruin on his stubborn people, and was ruined himself. Poseidon lay with Periboea and fathered a son, great-hearted Nausithous, who ruled over the Phaeacians; and Nausithous fathered Rhexenor and Alcinous. Rhexenor, when he had no son yet, was struck down in his hall by Apollo of the silver bow, a bridegroom as he was, and left only one daughter, Arete. Alcinous made her his wife and honours her as no other woman on earth is honoured of all those who keep house nowadays under their husbands. That is how heartily she is honoured, and always has been, by her children and by Alcinous himself and by the people, who look on her as on a goddess and greet her as she goes through the city. She has no lack of good understanding herself, and for the women she wishes well to she puts an end to quarrels even among their husbands. If she thinks well of you, then there is hope that you will see your friends and get back to your high-roofed house and your own country."

With that grey-eyed Athena went away over the restless sea and left lovely Scheria. She came to Marathon and broad-wayed Athens, and went into the well-built house of Erechtheus; and Odysseus went to the splendid palace of Alcinous. He stood there and his heart turned over a great deal before he reached the bronze threshold; because there was a gleam as of sun or moon over the high-roofed house of great-hearted Alcinous.

The walls were of bronze, running this way and that from the threshold to the innermost room, with a cornice of blue enamel round them. Golden doors closed the well-built house in, and silver doorposts stood in a bronze threshold, with a silver lintel above and a golden handle. On either side of the door stood gold and silver dogs, which Hephaestus had made with cunning skill to guard the palace of great-hearted Alcinous; and they were immortal and ageless all their days. Inside, seats were fixed along the wall on either hand from the threshold to the innermost room, and robes of soft cloth were thrown over them, finely woven, the work of women. On these the leaders of the Phaeacians used to sit drinking and eating, because they had unfailing supplies. And golden youths stood on well-built pedestals holding lighted torches in their hands, to give light by night to the banqueters in the hall.

He had fifty slave women in the house. Some of them grind the yellow grain on the millstone, and others weave cloth or sit twirling the yarn, like the leaves of a tall poplar tree; and the soft olive oil drips off the closely woven linen. For as the Phaeacian men are more skilled than anyone else at driving a fast ship over the sea, so the women are cunning workers at the loom, since Athena has given them, above all others, skill in fine work and an understanding mind.

Outside the courtyard, close to the door, is a great orchard of four acres, with a hedge running round it on either side. Tall thriving trees grow there, pears and pomegranates and apple trees with their bright fruit, and sweet figs and thriving olives. Their fruit never fails or gives out, winter or summer, but lasts the whole year through; and the west wind, blowing steadily, is always bringing some to life and ripening others: pear ripens on pear, apple on apple, cluster on cluster and fig on fig. And his fruitful vineyard is planted there, one part of it a warm patch on level ground being dried in the sun, while other grapes are being gathered and others trodden; and in front are unripe grapes just shedding the blossom, and others turning purple. And there, along the last row of vines, grow trim garden beds of every kind, in bloom the year round. There are two springs in it, one of which sends its water all through the garden, while the other, opposite it, runs under the threshold of the court towards the high house, and the townspeople draw their water from that one. Such were the splendid gifts of the gods in the palace of Alcinous.

Much-enduring noble Odysseus stood there and gazed. And when he had marvelled at it all in his heart, he went quickly over the threshold into the house. There he found the leaders and counsellors of the Phaeacians pouring offerings from their cups to the sharp-eyed slayer of Argus, to whom they always poured the wine last of all, when they were ready to go to bed. And much-enduring noble Odysseus went through the hall, wrapped in the thick mist Athena had poured round him, until he came to Arete and to Alcinous the king. He threw his arms round Arete's knees, and at that moment the marvellous mist melted off him. A hush fell on everyone in the room at the sight of the man, and they stared at him in astonishment. And Odysseus made his plea.

"Arete, daughter of godlike Rhexenor — after all my hard labours I have come to your husband and to your knees, and to these banqueters too, to whom may the gods grant happiness in life, and may each of them hand down to his children the wealth in his halls and the honours the people have given him. But send me on my way, so that I may come to my own country, and quickly. I have been suffering a long time, far from my friends."

With that he sat down on the hearth in the ashes by the fire, and they were all hushed and silent. At last the old lord Echeneus spoke among them, who was an elder among the Phaeacians, skilled in speech and knowing all the wisdom of the old days. He meant it kindly, and spoke to the gathering.

"Alcinous, this is not the better way and it is not seemly, that a stranger should sit on the ground on the hearth in the ashes, while the rest of us hold back waiting for your word. Come, get the stranger up and set him on a silver-studded chair, and tell the heralds to mix wine, so that we can pour an offering to Zeus who hurls the thunderbolt, since he always attends on suppliants who deserve respect. And let the housekeeper give the stranger supper out of the store in the house."

When the strong and mighty Alcinous heard this, he took Odysseus by the hand — Odysseus, the wise and cunning-minded — and raised him from the hearth and set him on a bright chair, telling his son, the good Laodamas, to get up from it; for he sat next to him and was his best loved. A maid brought water for his hands in a lovely golden jug and poured it over a silver basin for him to wash, and drew a polished table up beside him. The housekeeper, a grave woman, brought bread and set it out, and plenty of good things with it, giving freely from her stores. So much-enduring noble Odysseus drank and ate.

Then mighty Alcinous spoke to the herald. "Pontonous, mix the bowl and serve wine to everyone in the hall, so that we can pour an offering to Zeus who hurls the thunderbolt, since he always attends on suppliants who deserve respect."

He spoke, and Pontonous mixed the honey-hearted wine and served it out to everyone, pouring the first drops into the cups for an offering. And when they had poured the offering and drunk as much as they wanted, Alcinous spoke to the gathering.

"Listen to me, leaders and counsellors of the Phaeacians, and let me say what the heart in my breast tells me. Now that you have finished your feast, go home, each of you, and rest. In the morning we will call more of the elders together and entertain the stranger in our halls and make fine offerings to the gods. After that we will see about his sending, so that this stranger may come to his own country under our escort, without labour or pain, quickly and rejoicing, however far away he has come from. And he shall suffer no harm or trouble on the way, until he sets foot on his own land; after that he must suffer whatever Fate and the dread Spinners spun into his thread at his birth, when his mother bore him.

"But if he is one of the immortals come down from heaven, then this is some new thing the gods are planning. Up to now they have always appeared to us plainly, when we sacrifice them splendid hecatombs, and they feast among us sitting where we sit. And if one of us meets them travelling alone, they use no disguise, because we are close kin to them, as the Cyclopes are and the wild tribes of the Giants."

Then Odysseus of the many tricks answered him and said: "Alcinous, put that thought away. I am not like the immortals who hold the wide sky, either in height or in build, but like mortal men. Whoever you know among men who carries the heaviest load of trouble, those are the men I would compare myself to in my sorrows. Yes, and I could tell a longer tale still of all the evils I have suffered by the will of the gods.

"But let me eat now, in spite of my grief. There is nothing more shameless than a hateful belly, which insists that a man take notice of it however badly off he is and however weighed down with grief he is — as I am weighed down with grief, and yet my belly keeps ordering me to eat and drink, and makes me forget everything I have suffered, and commands me to eat my fill. But be quick at daybreak, so that you set me, unlucky as I am, on the soil of my own country, after all my troubles. And let life leave me, once I have seen my possessions again, and my slaves, and my great high-roofed house."

So he spoke, and they all praised his words and said the stranger should be sent on his way, since he had spoken properly. And when they had poured the offering and drunk as much as they wanted, they went off, each man to his own house, to rest.

Noble Odysseus was left behind in the hall, and Arete and godlike Alcinous sat beside him, while the women cleared away the dishes from the feast. Then white-armed Arete spoke first; for she had recognised his fine clothes when she saw them, the cloak and the tunic, which she had made herself with her women. And she spoke to him with winged words.

"Stranger, this is the question I will ask you myself, first. Who are you among men, and where from? Who gave you these clothes? Did you not say you came here wandering over the sea?"

Then Odysseus of the many tricks answered her and said: "It would be hard, my queen, to tell the whole tale of my troubles to the end, because the heavenly gods have given me a great many. But I will tell you this, which you ask me about. There is an island, Ogygia, lying far off in the sea. The daughter of Atlas lives there, Calypso of the lovely hair, full of tricks, a dread goddess, and nobody has anything to do with her, god or mortal. But fate brought me to her hearth alone, wretch that I am, because Zeus had struck my fast ship with his bright thunderbolt and smashed it in the middle of the wine-dark sea. There all the rest of my trusty companions died, and I got my arms round the keel of my curved ship and was carried adrift nine days, and on the tenth black night the gods brought me to the island of Ogygia, where Calypso of the lovely hair lives, a dread goddess.

"She took me into her house with a kind welcome, and gave me food, and said she would make me immortal and ageless all my days; but she could never persuade the heart in my breast. I stayed there without a break for seven years, and all that time I wetted with my tears the immortal clothes Calypso gave me. But when the eighth year came round, then she roused me and told me to go — either because of some message from Zeus, or because her own mind had changed. And she sent me off on a raft bound firm, and gave me plenty of bread and sweet wine, and dressed me in immortal clothes, and sent out a gentle warm wind.

"So for seventeen days I sailed over the sea, and on the eighteenth the shadowy mountains of your country appeared, and my heart was glad — ill-starred as I am, since I still had a great deal of misery to keep company with, which Poseidon the earth-shaker sent on me. He stirred the winds up against me and blocked my way, and roused the sea to an extraordinary pitch, and the wave would not let me stay on my raft while I groaned without stopping. The storm smashed the raft, but I swam and cut my way through that gulf of the sea, until the wind and the waves carried me and brought me to your shores. There, if I had tried to land, the waves would have flung me on the shore and dashed me against the great crags in a cheerless place; so I gave way and swam back until I came to a river, where the place looked best to me, since it was smooth of rocks and there was shelter from the wind besides. I struggled out and sank down, gasping for breath, and immortal night came on. Then I went away from the heaven-fed river and lay down to sleep in the bushes, gathering leaves round me; and a god poured endless sleep over me.

"So I slept there among the leaves, with my heart badly stricken, the whole night through and until morning and past midday, and the sun was turning towards his setting before sweet sleep let me go. Then I saw your daughter's women playing on the shore, and she was among them, lovely as the goddesses. I made my plea to her; and she showed no lack of good understanding, and behaved as you would not expect a younger person to behave on meeting you, since young people are always thoughtless. She gave me bread in plenty and bright wine, and bathed me in the river, and gave me these clothes. That is the truth, for all my sorrows."

Then Alcinous answered him and said: "Stranger, my daughter did not judge this rightly, in not bringing you to our house with her women. It was to her that you made your plea first."

Then Odysseus of the many tricks answered him and said: "Prince, do not blame your blameless daughter for that. She did tell me to follow with her women, but I would not, out of fear and shame, in case your heart should darken with anger when you saw it. We are quick to anger, we tribes of men on the earth."

And Alcinous answered him again: "Stranger, the heart in my breast is not the kind to fill with anger over nothing. Due measure is better in everything. Father Zeus, and Athena, and Apollo — I wish that a man as fine as you, and of one mind with me, would take my daughter for a wife and be called my son and stay here. I would give you a house and possessions, if you chose to remain; but no Phaeacian will keep you here against your will. Let that never be the will of father Zeus.

"As for your sending, so that you may be sure of it, I fix the time for it: tomorrow. Then you shall lie down, overcome with sleep, and they will row you over the calm sea until you come to your country and your house, or wherever you like — even far beyond Euboea, which those of our people who saw it say is the furthest of lands, when they carried fair-haired Rhadamanthus to visit Tityus, the son of Gaea. They went there and finished the journey without labour, and came back home on the same day. So you shall find out for yourself how far my ships are the best, and my young men at tossing the salt water with the oar-blade."

So he spoke, and much-enduring noble Odysseus was glad, and spoke in prayer and said: "Father Zeus, grant that Alcinous brings about everything he has said. Then his fame will never be put out over the grain-giving earth, and I shall reach my own country."

So they spoke to one another; and white-armed Arete told her women to set a bedstead under cover of the portico and to lay fine purple blankets on it and spread covers over the top, and put fleecy cloaks on those to wear. So they went out of the hall with torches in their hands, and when they had busily made up the strong-built bed they came to Odysseus and called him and said: "Get up now, stranger, and come to bed. Your bed is made."

So they spoke, and it seemed welcome to him to lie down and sleep. So he slept there, much-enduring noble Odysseus, on the corded bedstead under the echoing portico; and Alcinous lay down in the inner room of the high house, with the lady his wife beside him, who had made up the bed.
