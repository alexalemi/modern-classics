Book Seventeen

As soon as the early dawn appeared, rosy-fingered, Telemachus, the dear son of godlike Odysseus, bound the fine sandals under his feet and took his mighty spear, which fitted his grip, hurrying to the city; and he said to his swineherd:

"Father, I am going to the city, so that my mother may see me; because I do not think she will stop her miserable wailing and tearful lamenting until she sees me in person. And this is my charge to you: bring this wretched stranger to the city, so that he can beg his food there, and whoever wants to will give him a loaf and a cup of water. I cannot possibly burden myself with everybody, when I have grief at heart. And if the stranger is badly angered at that, it will be the worse for him. I like to speak the truth."

Then Odysseus of the many tricks answered him and said: "Friend, I have no wish to be left here myself. It is better for a beggar to beg his food in the town than in the fields, and whoever wants to will give it me. I am no longer of an age to stay at a farm and do everything an overseer tells me. Go on, then; this man you have told will bring me as soon as I have warmed myself at the fire and the sun has grown hot. These clothes I have on are miserably poor, and I am afraid the morning frost will finish me; and you say the city is a long way off."

So he spoke, and Telemachus went out through the farm with rapid strides, and was sowing the seeds of trouble for the suitors. And when he came to the stately house he set his spear in place, leaning it against a tall pillar, and went in himself and crossed the stone threshold.

The nurse Eurycleia was far the first to see him, as she was spreading fleeces over the richly worked chairs. She came straight towards him, bursting into tears; and the other women of Odysseus, who endures everything, gathered round them and kissed his head and his shoulders in loving welcome. Then wise Penelope came out of her room, like Artemis or golden Aphrodite, and burst into tears and flung her arms round her dear son and kissed his head and both his beautiful eyes; and she spoke to him with winged words, wailing:

"You have come, Telemachus, sweet light of my eyes. I thought I would never see you again after you had gone to Pylos in your ship — secretly, and in spite of me, to look for news of your dear father. Come, tell me what sight you had of him."

Then clear-headed Telemachus answered her: "Mother, do not stir up lamenting, and do not rouse the heart in my breast, when I have escaped utter destruction. Wash, and take clean clothes for your body, and go up to your room with your women and make a vow to all the gods that you will offer hecatombs that bring fulfilment, in the hope that Zeus may one day bring about deeds of requital. And I will go to the meeting place to invite to our house a stranger who followed me from Pylos on my way here. I sent him on ahead with my godlike companions, and told Peiraeus to take him home and give him a kindly welcome and show him honour until I came."

So he spoke, and her word stayed unwinged. Then she washed and took clean clothes for her body and made a vow to all the gods that she would offer hecatombs that bring fulfilment, in the hope that Zeus would one day bring about deeds of requital.

But Telemachus went out through the hall with his spear in his hand, and two swift hounds went with him. And Athena poured a marvellous grace over him, and all the people stared at him as he came. The proud suitors thronged round him, speaking him fair and turning over evil in the depths of their hearts. But he avoided the great crowd of them, and went and sat down where Mentor was sitting, and Antiphus, and Halitherses, who were friends of his father's house from of old; and they questioned him about everything.

Then Peiraeus, the famous spearman, came up, leading the stranger through the city to the meeting place; and Telemachus did not turn away from his guest for long, but went up to him. Peiraeus was the first to speak:

"Telemachus, send women to my house quickly, so that I can send you the gifts Menelaus gave you."

Then clear-headed Telemachus answered him: "Peiraeus, we do not know how these things will go. If the proud suitors kill me secretly in my hall and divide all my father's goods among them, I would rather you kept these things and enjoyed them yourself than one of them. But if I sow the seeds of death and doom for them, then bring it all to my house gladly, as I shall be glad."

With that he led the sorely tried stranger to the house. Now when they had come to the stately house they laid their cloaks on the chairs and high seats, and went into the polished baths and washed. And when the maids had bathed them and rubbed them with oil and put fleecy cloaks and tunics on them, they came out of the baths and sat down on the chairs. And a maid brought water for their hands in a lovely golden jug and poured it over a silver basin for them to wash, and drew a polished table up beside them. And the housekeeper, a grave woman, brought bread and set it in front of them, and plenty of meat with it, giving freely from her stores. And his mother sat opposite Telemachus by the doorpost of the hall, leaning against a chair and spinning fine threads of yarn. So they put out their hands to the good food in front of them.

But when they had eaten and drunk as much as they wanted, wise Penelope spoke first among them: "Telemachus, I shall go up to my room and lie down on my bed, which has become a bed of wailing for me, always wet with my tears, since the day Odysseus set out for Ilios with the sons of Atreus. And you took no care, before the proud suitors come into this house, to tell me plainly about your father's homecoming, if you heard anything."

And clear-headed Telemachus answered her: "Then I will tell you the whole truth, mother. We went to Pylos and to Nestor, the shepherd of the people, and he received me in his high house and gave me a kindly welcome, as a father might his own son who had newly come home after a long time from far away; that is how kindly he tended me, with his glorious sons. But of Odysseus, who endures everything, he said he had heard nothing from any man on earth, whether he was living or dead. But he sent me on with horses and a jointed car to Menelaus, son of Atreus, the famous spearman. There I saw Argive Helen, for whose sake Argives and Trojans laboured so much by the will of the gods.

"And Menelaus, good at the war-cry, asked me at once what I had come to lovely Lacedaemon in search of; and I told him the whole truth. Then he answered me and said:

"'The disgrace of it — that men with no courage in them should want to lie in the bed of a man of such a heart. It is as when a hind puts her new-born suckling fawns to sleep in the thicket lair of a strong lion, and goes off over the mountain slopes and the grassy valleys looking for pasture, and then the lion comes back to his lair and lets loose a cruel doom on the two of them. That is how Odysseus will let loose a cruel doom on these men. Father Zeus, and Athena, and Apollo — I wish that Odysseus might come among the suitors with the strength he had that time in well-built Lesbos, when he stood up and wrestled a match with Philomeleides and threw him hard, and all the Achaeans were delighted. If he came in that strength, every one of them would find a quick death and a bitter courtship. But as for what you are asking me and begging me about, I will not turn aside and talk of other things, or deceive you; of everything the unerring old man of the sea told me, I will not hide or keep back one word. He said he had seen Odysseus on an island in bitter distress, in the halls of the nymph Calypso, who keeps him there by force. And he cannot come to his own country, because he has no ships with oars at hand and no companions to send him on his way over the broad back of the sea.'

"That is what Menelaus said, son of Atreus, the famous spearman. And when I had finished all this I set out for home, and the immortals gave me a fair wind and brought me quickly to my own dear country."

So he spoke, and stirred the heart in her breast. Then godlike Theoclymenus spoke among them too, and said:

"Honoured wife of Odysseus, son of Laertes — he truly has no clear understanding of it; but listen to my words, because I will prophesy to you with certain knowledge and hide nothing. Let Zeus be my witness above all gods, and this hospitable table, and the hearth of noble Odysseus that I have come to, that Odysseus is in his own country even now, resting or moving about, learning of these evil doings, and sowing the seeds of trouble for all the suitors. So plain a bird of omen did I mark as I sat on the benched ship, and I declared it to Telemachus."

Then wise Penelope answered him: "Stranger, I wish this word of yours might be fulfilled. Then you would soon know kindness and many a gift from me, so that anyone who met you would call you a lucky man."

So they talked to one another. And meanwhile the suitors in front of the palace of Odysseus were amusing themselves, throwing the discus and the javelin on the levelled ground, in their usual insolence of heart. But when it was the hour for dinner, and the flocks came in from the fields on all sides, and the men who usually led them led them in, then Medon spoke to them, who of all the heralds was the most to their liking and was always present at their feasts:

"Young men, now that you have all gladdened your hearts with sport, come to the house so that we can get a feast ready; it is no bad thing to take one's dinner at the right hour."

So he spoke, and they got up and went, and did as he said. And when they had come to the stately house they laid their cloaks on the chairs and high seats, and men set about killing great sheep and fat goats, and fatted hogs, and a heifer from the herd, and so got the meal ready.

But Odysseus and the good swineherd were hurrying to go from the farm to the city; and the swineherd, a leader of men, spoke first and said:

"Stranger, since you are eager to go to the city today, as my master told you — though for myself I would rather you were left here to keep the farm, but I respect him and I am afraid of him, in case he scolds me afterwards, and masters' rebukes are hard — come now, let us go. The day is far gone, and you will soon find it colder towards evening."

Then Odysseus of the many tricks answered him and said: "I see, I take your point; you are telling this to a man who understands. Come, let us go, and be my guide the whole way. But give me a staff to lean on, if you have one cut anywhere, because you said the road was treacherous."

He spoke, and flung his miserable bag round his shoulders, full of holes, slung on a twisted cord, and Eumaeus gave him a staff that suited him. So the two of them set out, and the dogs and the herdsmen stayed behind to guard the farm; and the swineherd led his master to the city in the likeness of a miserable old beggar, leaning on a staff, and the clothes he wore on his body were wretched.

But when, going along the rugged path, they were near the city and had come to a well-made, fair-flowing fountain where the townspeople drew their water — Ithacus had made it, and Neritus, and Polyctor — and there was a grove of poplars round about it, the kind that grow by water, circling it on every side, and the cold water flowed down from the rock above, and on the top an altar to the nymphs was built where everyone who passed made offerings — there Melanthius, son of Dolius, met them, driving his she-goats, the best in all the herds, to make a feast for the suitors; and two herdsmen followed with him.

When he saw them he spoke and abused them in terrible and unseemly words, and stirred the heart of Odysseus:

"Now here is the vile leading the vile, truly. As always, the god brings like and like together. Where are you taking this filthy wretch, you miserable swineherd, this nuisance of a beggar to spoil our feasts? He is the kind of man to stand and rub his shoulders on a great many doorposts, begging for scraps and not for swords or cauldrons. If you would give me this fellow to keep my farm, to sweep out the pens and carry young shoots to the kids, he might get himself a sturdy thigh by drinking whey. But since he has learned nothing but evil ways, he will not want to busy himself with work, but would rather go skulking through the country and feed his insatiable belly by begging. But I tell you outright, and this word will come to pass: if he comes to the palace of godlike Odysseus, a good many footstools flung at his head by the hands of men will be broken on his ribs as he is pelted through the house."

So he spoke, and as he passed he kicked Odysseus on the hip, in his folly; but he did not drive him off the path, and Odysseus stood firm. And Odysseus turned over whether to leap on him and take his life with the staff, or to seize him round the middle and lift him up and dash his head on the ground. But he endured it and held himself back from his purpose.

And the swineherd looked the man in the face and rebuked him, and lifted up his hands and prayed aloud: "Nymphs of the fountain, daughters of Zeus, if ever Odysseus burned pieces of the thighs of lambs or kids on your altars, wrapped in rich fat, fulfil this prayer for me: grant that he, my master, may come back, and that some god may guide him. Then he would scatter all these proud airs you put on in your insolence, always roaming about the city, while evil herdsmen destroy the flock."

Then Melanthius the goatherd answered him: "Listen to the cur talk, with his mind full of mischief. One day I will take him on a black benched ship far from Ithaca, so that he brings me in a great deal of wealth. I wish Apollo of the silver bow would strike Telemachus down in the halls today, or that he might be killed by the suitors, as surely as the day of homecoming has been lost for Odysseus in a far country."

With that he left them there, walking slowly on, and strode forward himself and came very quickly to the palace of the king. He went straight in and sat down among the suitors opposite Eurymachus, because he loved him best of all. And the servants set a portion of meat beside him, and the housekeeper, a grave woman, brought bread and set it in front of him to eat.

And Odysseus and the good swineherd halted as they came near, and the sound of the hollow lyre rang round them, because Phemius was striking the chords to sing before the suitors. Then Odysseus took the swineherd by the hand and said:

"Eumaeus, this must be the beautiful house of Odysseus. It would be easy to recognise, even seen among a great many others. There is building on building, and the court is built with a wall and a coping, and the double gates are well fenced; no man could look down on it. And I can tell that a great many men are feasting inside, because the savour of meat rises from it, and the sound of the lyre goes with it, which the gods have made the companion of the feast."

And you answered him, Eumaeus the swineherd, and said: "You have recognised it easily; you are quick-witted in everything. But come, let us think how this shall be done. Either you go first into the stately palace and go in among the suitors, and I will stay behind here; or, if you like, stay here and I will go in front of you. But do not wait long, in case somebody sees you outside and pelts you or strikes you. I tell you to think about that."

Then much-enduring noble Odysseus answered him: "I see, I take your point; you are telling this to a man who understands. Go on ahead, and I will stay here; I am not unused to blows and peltings. My heart is steady; I have suffered a great deal on the waves and in war. Let this be added to what has gone before. But a ravening belly is a thing no man can hide, an accursed plague that brings a great many troubles on men. It is because of the belly that the benched ships are fitted out too, carrying harm to enemies over the restless sea."

So they talked to one another. And a dog that was lying there raised his head and pricked up his ears: Argos, the dog of Odysseus, who endures everything, whom he had bred himself long ago and had no joy of, because he went to sacred Ilios before that. In the old days the young men used to take the dog out to hunt wild goats and deer and hares; but now he lay neglected, with his master gone, in the deep dung of mules and cattle which lay in heaps in front of the doors, until the slaves of Odysseus should take it away to manure his wide lands. There the dog Argos lay, full of vermin.

And even now, when he saw Odysseus standing near, he wagged his tail and dropped both his ears; but he had no strength left to come nearer to his master. And Odysseus looked aside and wiped away a tear, hiding it easily from Eumaeus; and at once he questioned him and said:

"Eumaeus, it is strange that this dog lies here in the dung. He is fine in form, but I cannot tell clearly whether he had speed to match his beauty, or whether he was only the kind of dog men keep at their tables for show."

And you answered him, Eumaeus the swineherd, and said: "That is the dog of a man who has died far away. If he were still in body and in action what he was when Odysseus left him and went to Troy, you would be amazed straight away, seeing his speed and his strength. Nothing he started in the depths of the thick wood could get away from him, and he was keen of scent in tracking too. But now he is in a bad way, and his master has died far from his own country, and the heedless women give him no care. Slaves, when their masters lose their power, no longer want to do honest service afterwards; because Zeus, whose voice carries far, takes half a man's worth away from him when the day of slavery comes on him."

With that he went into the stately house and straight to the hall, to join the company of the lordly suitors. And as for Argos, the fate of black death took him at once, when he had seen Odysseus in the twentieth year.

Now as the swineherd came through the hall, godlike Telemachus was far the first to see him, and called him quickly to his side with a nod. And Eumaeus looked about and took a stool that lay near, where the carver used to sit when he was carving the many joints of meat for the suitors as they feasted in the hall. He took this and set it at the table of Telemachus, opposite him, and sat down there himself. And a herald took a portion of meat and set it in front of him, and bread out of the basket.

And Odysseus came into the palace after him in the likeness of a miserable old beggar, leaning on a staff, and the clothes he wore on his body were wretched. He sat down on the ashen threshold inside the doorway, leaning against a post of cypress wood which a carpenter had planed skilfully long ago and made straight to the line.

Then Telemachus called the swineherd to him, and took a whole loaf out of the beautiful basket, and all the meat his hands could hold in their grasp, and said to him: "Take this and give it to the stranger over there, and tell him to go about and beg from the suitors, every one of them. Shame is no good companion for a man in need."

So he spoke, and the swineherd went when he had heard this, and came up to Odysseus and spoke winged words to him: "Stranger, Telemachus gives you these, and tells you to go about and beg from the suitors, every one of them. Shame, he says, is no good thing in a beggar man."

Then Odysseus of the many tricks answered him and said: "King Zeus, grant that Telemachus may be blessed among men, and may have everything his heart desires."

He spoke, and took the food in both hands and set it down there in front of his feet on his miserable bag. And he ate as long as the singer sang in the halls. But when he had dined and the divine singer was leaving off, the suitors broke into uproar through the halls; and Athena came close to Odysseus, son of Laertes, and roused him to go among the suitors and gather bits of bread, and learn which of them were righteous and which lawless. But even so she did not mean to save one of them from ruin.

So he set out to beg from every man, beginning on the right, holding his hand out on every side as though he had long been a beggar. And they pitied him and gave, and marvelled at him, asking one another who he was and where he came from. Then Melanthius the goatherd spoke among them:

"Listen to me, suitors of the glorious queen, about this stranger; I have seen him before. It was certainly the swineherd who brought him here, but I do not know for certain about the man himself, where he says he was born."

So he spoke, and Antinous rebuked the swineherd, saying: "Notorious swineherd, why did you bring this man to the city? Have we not enough vagabonds without him, nuisances of beggars to spoil our feast? Is it not enough for you that they gather here and eat up your master's substance, that you invite this one too?"

And you answered him, Eumaeus the swineherd, and said: "Antinous, those are not fair words, noble as you are. Who ever goes out and invites a stranger from abroad himself, unless it is one of those who are masters of some public craft — a prophet, or a healer of ills, or a builder, or a divine singer, who gives delight with his song? Those men are invited all over the boundless earth. But nobody would invite a beggar to be a burden to himself. You are always harsh above all the suitors to the slaves of Odysseus, and to me most of all; but I do not care, as long as my lady, steady Penelope, lives in the hall, and godlike Telemachus."

Then clear-headed Telemachus said to him: "Be quiet; do not answer that man with many words. Antinous is always provoking people to anger with harsh words, and urging the others on too."

And with that he spoke winged words to Antinous: "Antinous, you certainly care for me well, like a father for his son, when you tell me to drive that stranger from the hall with a word of compulsion. May the god never bring such a thing to pass. Take something and give it to him; I do not grudge it, I tell you to give it. And in this matter take no notice of my mother, or of any of the slaves who are in the house of godlike Odysseus. But the thought in your breast is quite different; you are far more eager to eat yourself than to give to another."

Then Antinous answered him and said: "Telemachus, you big talker with no check on your nerve, what a thing to say. If all the suitors handed him as much as I shall, this house would keep him at a distance for three whole months."

So he spoke, and seized the footstool he used to rest his shining feet on as he feasted, and showed it from under the table where it lay. But all the rest gave gifts, and filled his bag with bread and bits of meat. And Odysseus was about to go back to the threshold and to have tried the Achaeans at no cost to himself; but he stopped by Antinous and said to him:

"Friend, give me something. You do not seem to me the meanest of the Achaeans, but the noblest, because you are like a king. So it is fitting that you should give a better portion of bread than the rest; and then I would make your fame known all over the boundless earth. I too once lived in a house of my own among men, a rich man in a wealthy house, and often gave gifts to a wanderer, whoever he was and with whatever need he came. And I had slaves past counting, and everything else in plenty, the things by which men live well and are reputed wealthy.

"But Zeus, son of Cronos, brought it all to nothing — that must have been his pleasure — who sent me off with roaming pirates to go to Egypt, a long voyage, so that I should meet my ruin; and I moored my curved ships in the river Aegyptus. Then I told my trusty companions to stay there by the ships and guard the ships, and I sent scouts out to go to places of outlook. But my companions gave way to their own wantonness, and, led on by their own strength, set about wasting the fine fields of the men of Egypt straight away; and they carried off the women and the little children and killed the men; and the cry went up to the city quickly. Then, hearing the shouting, the people came out at daybreak, and the whole plain was filled with foot-soldiers and chariots and the flashing of bronze. And Zeus, who hurls the thunderbolt, threw an evil panic on my companions, and none of them had the courage to stand his ground and face the enemy; trouble surrounded us on every side. So they killed a great many of us with the sharp bronze, and led others up to their city alive, to work for them because they had to. But they gave me to a friend of theirs who met them, to take to Cyprus, to Dmetor, son of Iasus, who ruled mightily over Cyprus; and from there I have come here now, in bitter distress."

Then Antinous answered him and said: "What god has brought this pest here to spoil our feast? Stand off over there in the middle, away from my table, or you may come to a bitter Egypt and a bitter Cyprus, seeing that you are a bold and shameless beggar. You come up to every man in turn, and they give recklessly; there is no restraint or scruple in giving freely from another man's goods, since each of them has plenty beside him."

Then Odysseus of the many tricks drew back and said to him: "So it seems you have no wits to match your beauty. You would not give a suppliant a grain of salt out of your own substance, you who now, sitting at another man's table, could not bring yourself to take a piece of the bread and give it me — and here it lies in plenty at your hand."

So he spoke, and Antinous grew angrier still at heart, and gave him a black look from under his brows and spoke winged words to him: "Now I do not think you will get out of the hall in good order any more, since you go so far as to speak abuse."

With that he seized the footstool and flung it, and struck Odysseus at the base of the right shoulder, where it joins the back. But he stood firm as a rock, and the missile of Antinous did not make him reel; he only shook his head in silence, turning evil over in the depths of his heart. Then he went back to the threshold and sat down and laid down his well-filled bag; and he spoke among the suitors:

"Listen to me, suitors of the glorious queen, so that I can say what the heart in my breast tells me. There is no pain of heart and no grief when a man is struck fighting for his own possessions, for his cattle or for his white sheep. But Antinous has struck me for my wretched belly's sake, an accursed plague that brings a great many troubles on men. And if there are gods and avengers for beggars, may the doom of death come on Antinous before his marriage."

Then Antinous, son of Eupeithes, answered him: "Sit still and eat, stranger, or go somewhere else; or the young men will drag you by hand or foot through the house for words like these, and strip all the skin off you."

So he spoke, but they were all filled with enormous indignation; and one of the proud young men would say: "Antinous, you did wrong to strike that wretched wanderer. Doomed man, what if he happens to be some god come down from heaven? The gods do take the likeness of strangers from far away and put on every kind of shape, and visit the cities, looking at the violence and the righteousness of men."

That is what the suitors said, but Antinous paid no attention to their words. And Telemachus nursed great grief in his heart at the blow, though he let no tear fall from his eyelids to the ground; he only shook his head in silence, turning evil over in the depths of his heart.

But when wise Penelope heard of the man being struck in the hall, she spoke among her women and said: "May the famous archer Apollo strike you the same way."

And Eurynome the housekeeper said to her: "I wish our prayers might be fulfilled. Then not one of these men would come to fair-throned Dawn."

And wise Penelope answered her: "Nurse, they are all enemies, because they contrive evil. But Antinous more than any of them is like black fate. Some wretched stranger is roaming through the house, begging alms of the men, because want drives him to it, and all the others filled his bag and gave him things, and Antinous flung a footstool and struck him at the base of the right shoulder."

So she spoke among her women, sitting in her room, while noble Odysseus was at his meal. Then she called the good swineherd to her and said:

"Go, good Eumaeus, and tell the stranger to come here, so that I can greet him and ask him whether he has heard anything of Odysseus, who endures everything, or seen him with his eyes. He looks like a man who has wandered far."

And you answered her, Eumaeus the swineherd, and said: "I wish the Achaeans would keep quiet, my queen; he says such things as would charm your very soul. I had him with me three nights, and kept him three days in my hut — because it was to me he came first when he ran away from a ship — and he had still not finished the tale of his sufferings. As a man gazes at a singer who sings songs of longing to mortals which the gods have taught him, and their desire to hear him has no end, however long he sings, so he charmed me as he sat in my house. He says he is a friend of Odysseus by descent, and that he lives in Crete, where the race of Minos is. From there he has come on this journey here, suffering as he wanders on and on. And he insists he has heard news of Odysseus, alive and near at hand, in the rich land of the Thesprotians; and he is bringing a great deal of treasure home."

Then wise Penelope answered him: "Go and call him here, so that he can tell me to my face. And as for these men, let them make sport, sitting in the doorway or here in the house, since their hearts are merry. Their own possessions lie untouched in their homes, bread and sweet wine, and their servants feed on them. But they crowd our house day after day, killing our oxen and sheep and fat goats, and feasting and drinking the flaming wine recklessly, and all this wealth is being wasted; because there is no man here as Odysseus was, to keep ruin off the house. But if Odysseus should come back to his own country, he and his son would take vengeance on these men for their violence at once."

So she spoke, and Telemachus sneezed loudly, and the whole room echoed round about extraordinarily. And Penelope laughed, and at once spoke winged words to Eumaeus:

"Go, call the stranger here in front of me. Do you not notice that my son has sneezed at everything I said? So utter death will fall on the suitors, every one of them, and not one of them will escape death and the fates. And there is another thing I will tell you, and you must take it to heart. If I find that he speaks the truth in everything, I will dress him in a cloak and a tunic, good clothes."

So she spoke, and the swineherd went when he had heard this; and he came up to Odysseus and spoke winged words to him: "Sir stranger, wise Penelope is calling for you, the mother of Telemachus, and her heart tells her to make enquiries about her husband, though she has suffered a great deal. And if she finds you speak the truth in everything, she will dress you in a cloak and a tunic, which you need most of all. And as for your food, you shall beg it through the country and feed your belly, and whoever wants to will give it you."

Then much-enduring noble Odysseus answered him: "Eumaeus, I will soon tell the whole truth to the daughter of Icarius, wise Penelope. I know all about Odysseus, and we have borne affliction in common. But I am afraid of this crowd of harsh suitors, whose wantonness and violence reach the iron sky. Just now, as I was going through the hall doing no harm, that man struck me and hurt me, and neither Telemachus nor anyone else did anything to keep the blow off. So tell Penelope to wait in the halls, eager as she is, until sunset; and then let her ask me about her husband and the day of his homecoming, giving me a seat nearer the fire, since the clothes I have on are poor — and you know this yourself, because it was to you that I made my plea first."

So he spoke, and the swineherd went when he had heard this. And as he crossed the threshold Penelope said to him: "You are not bringing him, Eumaeus. What does the wanderer mean by this? Is he afraid of somebody beyond all measure, or does he feel ashamed for no reason in the house? Shame is a bad thing in a beggar."

And you answered her, Eumaeus the swineherd, and said: "He speaks rightly, and any other man would think the same, in trying to avoid the insolence of overbearing men. He tells you to wait until sunset. And for yourself too it is far more seemly, my queen, to speak to the stranger alone and hear what he says."

Then wise Penelope answered him: "The stranger is not without wisdom; he sees how it may be. There are no mortal men, I think, who contrive such wicked folly in their wantonness as these do."

So she spoke, and the good swineherd went off into the crowd of the suitors when he had told her everything. And at once he spoke winged words to Telemachus, holding his head close to him so that the others should not hear:

"Friend, I am going out to guard the pigs and everything there, your living and mine; and you take charge of everything here. Keep yourself safe first of all, and let your mind be on guard in case some harm comes to you; a great many of the Achaeans are contriving evil, and I hope Zeus destroys them utterly before harm falls on us."

Then clear-headed Telemachus answered him: "So it shall be, father; go on your way when you have eaten. And come in the morning and bring good victims. But everything here shall be a care to me and to the immortals."

So he spoke, and the swineherd sat down again on the polished chair. And when he had satisfied his heart with meat and drink he went off to the pigs, and left the courts and the hall full of banqueters. And they were making merry with dance and song, because evening had come on by now.
