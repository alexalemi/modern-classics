Book Nine

Then Odysseus of the many tricks answered him.

Lord Alcinous, renowned above all men, this is a good thing indeed, to listen to a singer as fine as this man, whose voice is like a god's. For myself, I say there is no better fulfilment of delight than when joy holds a whole people, and banqueters in the halls listen to a singer as they sit in their order, and the tables beside them are loaded with bread and meat, and the wine-steward draws wine from the bowl and carries it round and pours it into the cups. That seems to my mind the fairest thing there is.

But your heart has turned to asking about my miserable troubles, so that I may weep and groan the more. What shall I tell you first, then, and what last? The heavenly gods have given me a great many.

First I will tell you my name, so that you know it too, and so that afterwards, when I have got clear of the pitiless day of doom, I may be your host, though my home is far off. I am Odysseus, son of Laertes, known among men for every kind of trick, and my fame reaches to heaven. I live in clear-seen Ithaca, where there is a mountain, Neriton, covered with waving forests, that can be seen from far off; and round it lie a good many islands close to one another, Dulichium and Same and wooded Zacynthus. Ithaca itself lies low, furthest out towards the dark, and the others lie apart towards the Dawn and the sun — a rugged island, but a good nurse of young men; and for myself I can see nothing sweeter than one's own country.

Calypso, the beautiful goddess, tried to keep me with her in her hollow caves, wanting me for a husband; and in the same way Circe held me back in her halls, the cunning lady of Aeaea, wanting me for a husband. But neither of them could ever persuade the heart in my breast. So true is it that nothing is sweeter than a man's own country and his parents, even if he is living in a rich house far away in a foreign land, away from his parents.

But come, let me tell you about my miserable homecoming too, which Zeus laid on me as I came from Troy.

The wind carried me from Ilios and brought me to the Cicones, to Ismarus. There I sacked the city and killed the men; and we took their wives out of the city, and a great store of treasure, and divided it among us, so that as far as it lay with me no man should go cheated of an equal share. Then I gave the order that we should get away as fast as we could; but the others in their great folly would not listen. There was a great deal of wine drunk, and they killed a great many sheep by the shore, and slow-moving cattle.

Meanwhile the Cicones went and called on other Cicones, their neighbours, more numerous than they were and braver — men who lived inland and were skilled at fighting from chariots, and on foot if they had to. They came in the morning, as thick as the leaves or the flowers come up in their season; and then an evil fate from Zeus fell on us, poor wretches, so that we should suffer a great deal. They drew up their line and fought beside the fast ships, and both sides threw bronze-tipped spears at each other. As long as it was morning and the sacred day was growing, so long we held our ground and beat them off, though they outnumbered us. But when the sun turned towards the hour for unyoking oxen, then the Cicones got the upper hand and routed the Achaeans, and six of my well-greaved companions died from each ship; the rest of us got clear of death and fate.

From there we sailed on, grieving in our hearts, glad to have escaped death, though we had lost our dear companions. And I did not let my curved ships go on until we had called three times on each of those unlucky companions of ours who died on the plain, cut down by the Cicones. But Zeus the cloud-gatherer roused the north wind against our ships with an extraordinary storm, and hid land and sea alike in cloud, and night rushed down out of the sky. The ships were driven headlong, and their sails were torn to shreds by the violence of the wind. So we lowered the sails and stowed them aboard, in fear of death, and rowed the ships hard towards the land. We lay there two nights and two days without a break, eating our hearts out with weariness and grief. But when fair-haired Dawn brought the third day to its birth, we set up the masts and hoisted the white sails and sat down, and the wind and the helmsmen steered the ships.

And now I would have come to my own country unhurt; but the wave and the current and the north wind beat me back as I was rounding Malea, and drove me off my course past Cythera.

From there I was carried for nine days by deadly winds over the teeming water; and on the tenth we set foot in the country of the Lotus-eaters, who eat a flowery food. There we went ashore and drew water, and my companions took their meal at once beside the fast ships. And when we had tasted food and drink, I sent some of my companions to go and find out who the men were who ate bread here on the earth. I chose two, and sent a third with them as a herald. They went off and mixed with the Lotus-eaters, and the Lotus-eaters did not plan any death for my companions, but gave them lotus to taste. And whichever of them ate the honey-sweet fruit of the lotus had no more wish to bring back word or to come home, but wanted to stay there among the Lotus-eaters, feeding on lotus, and forgot the way home. So I brought these men back to the ships by force, weeping, and dragged them under the benches and tied them fast in the hollow ships; and I told the rest of my trusty companions to embark quickly on the fast ships, in case anyone should eat the lotus and forget the way home. So they went aboard at once and sat down at the benches, and sitting in their order struck the grey sea with their oars.

From there we sailed on, grieving in our hearts, and we came to the country of the Cyclopes, an overbearing and lawless people, who trust in the immortal gods and plant nothing with their hands and do no ploughing; but everything comes up for them without sowing or ploughing — wheat and barley and vines that carry the rich clusters of wine — and the rain of Zeus makes them grow. They have no assemblies for council and no established laws, but live on the peaks of high mountains in hollow caves, and each of them lays down the law to his own children and wives, and they take no notice of one another.

Now there is a level island stretching aslant outside the harbour, not close to the shore of the country of the Cyclopes and not far off either, a wooded island. Wild goats live there past counting, because the tread of men does not frighten them off, and hunters do not go there, the men who endure hardship in the woodland ranging over the mountain peaks. It is not held with flocks or with ploughland; unsown and untilled all its days, it knows nothing of men and feeds the bleating goats. For the Cyclopes have no ships with vermilion cheeks, and there are no shipwrights in their country who might build them well-benched ships to do everything they need, crossing to the cities of other peoples as men often cross the sea in ships to visit one another — craftsmen, who would have made a fine settlement of this island too. It is not a poor island; it would bear everything in season. There are meadows by the shores of the grey sea, well-watered and soft, where vines would never fail, and level ploughland in it, where they could reap harvests season after season, extraordinarily deep, so rich is the soil underneath. And there is a harbour in it with safe anchorage, where there is no need of moorings, either to throw out anchor-stones or to make fast stern cables; a man can beach his ship and wait until the sailors' minds tell them to put out and the breezes blow fair. And at the head of the harbour a spring of bright water flows out from under a cave, and poplars grow round about it.

That is where we sailed in, and some god guided us through the murky night; there was no light to see by, but a mist lay deep about the ships and the moon gave no light from the sky, because it was shut in by clouds. So nobody's eyes made out that island, and we did not see the long waves rolling in on the beach until we had run our well-benched ships ashore. And when we had beached the ships we lowered all the sails and went out ourselves onto the shore of the sea, and there we fell asleep and waited for the bright Dawn.

As soon as the early dawn appeared, rosy-fingered, we wandered about the island, marvelling at it; and the nymphs, the daughters of Zeus who holds the aegis, roused the mountain goats, so that my companions might have something to make a meal of. Straight away we took our curved bows and long javelins out of the ships and, drawing up in three bands, started shooting; and the god soon gave us game enough to satisfy us. Twelve ships followed me, and nine goats fell to each by lot, but for me alone they picked out ten.

So all day long until sunset we sat feasting on plenty of meat and sweet wine. The red wine was not used up out of our ships; some was still left, because we had drawn plenty of it in jars for each crew when we took the sacred citadel of the Cicones. And we looked across at the land of the Cyclopes, who lived close by, and noticed the smoke, and the voices of men, and of the sheep and the goats. But when the sun set and the dark came on, we lay down to rest on the shore of the sea.

And as soon as the early dawn appeared, rosy-fingered, I called my men together and spoke to them all.

"Stay here now, the rest of you, my trusty companions. I will go with my own ship and my own crew and find out about those men over there — whether they are cruel and wild and unjust, or whether they welcome strangers, and whether their minds fear the gods."

With that I went aboard the ship and told my companions to embark themselves and loose the stern cables. So they went aboard at once and sat down at the benches, and sitting in their order struck the grey sea with their oars.

But when we reached the place, which lay close by, we saw a high cave right at the edge of the land, close to the sea, roofed over with laurels, and a great many flocks, sheep and goats alike, used to sleep there. Round about it a high court was built with stones set deep in the earth, and with tall pines and high-crested oaks. A monstrous man slept there, who pastured his flocks alone and far off, and did not mix with the others but lived apart, with his heart set on lawlessness. He was made a marvellous monster, and was not like a man who lives on bread, but like a wooded peak of a high mountain that stands out alone, apart from the rest.

Then I told the rest of my trusty companions to stay there by the ship and guard the ship, and I picked twelve of my best companions and went off. I took with me a goatskin of the dark sweet wine which Maro, son of Euanthes, had given me, the priest of Apollo, the god who watched over Ismarus. He had given it me because we had protected him and his child and his wife out of respect; for he lived in a wooded grove of Phoebus Apollo. And he gave me splendid gifts: seven talents of well-worked gold, and a mixing bowl all of silver; and besides these, wine, which he filled twelve jars with in all, sweet and unmixed, a divine drink. Not one of his slaves or of the maids in his halls knew about it, only himself and his dear wife and one housekeeper. And whenever they drank that honey-sweet red wine, he would fill one cup and pour it into twenty measures of water, and a smell would rise from the mixing bowl marvellously sweet; and then you would not want to hold back. I filled a great skin with this wine and took it with me, and provisions in a bag as well; because my proud spirit had a feeling that a man would come at me before long clothed in great strength, a savage who knew nothing of justice or of law.

We reached the cave quickly, and did not find him inside; he was pasturing his fat flocks in the fields. So we went into the cave and stared at everything in it. The crates were loaded with cheeses, and the pens were crowded with lambs and kids. Each kind was penned separately: the firstlings by themselves, the later lambs by themselves, and the newly weaned by themselves again. And all the well-made vessels were swimming with whey, the milk-pails and the bowls he milked into.

Then my companions spoke and begged me first of all to take some of the cheeses and go, and after that to drive the kids and lambs quickly out of the pens to the fast ship, and sail over the salt water. But I would not listen to them — it would have been far better if I had — because I wanted to see the man himself, and whether he would give me guest-gifts. As it turned out, his appearing was not going to be a joy to my companions.

Then we kindled a fire and made an offering, and took some of the cheeses ourselves and ate, and sat in the cave and waited for him until he came back with his flocks. He was carrying a huge weight of dry wood to serve him at suppertime, and he flung it down with a crash inside the cave, and we shrank back into a recess of the cave in terror. He drove his fat flocks into the wide cavern, all the ones he milked, but the males, the rams and the goats, he left outside in the deep court. Then he lifted the great doorstone and set it in place, a mighty rock; twenty-two strong four-wheeled wagons could not have lifted it off the ground, such a towering mass of rock he set in the doorway. Then he sat down and milked the ewes and the bleating goats, all in turn, and put her young under each mother. Then he curdled half the white milk and gathered it into wicker baskets and put it away, and the other half he set in vessels, so that he could take it and drink it, and it would do for his supper.

But when he had busily got through his work, he rekindled the fire, and caught sight of us, and asked:

"Strangers, who are you? Where do you sail from over the watery roads? Is it on some business, or are you wandering at random over the sea like pirates, who wander with their lives in their hands and bring harm to people in other countries?"

So he spoke, and our spirits broke in our breasts at the terror of his deep voice and his monstrous self; but even so I answered him and said:

"We are Achaeans from Troy, driven wandering by all kinds of winds over the great gulf of the sea. Trying to get home, we have come by another way, by other roads; that is how Zeus was pleased to arrange it, I suppose. And we say we are the men of Agamemnon, son of Atreus, whose fame is now the greatest under heaven, so great a city did he sack, and killed so many people. But we, coming to you like this, have come as suppliants to your knees, hoping you will give us hospitality, or make us some present of another kind, which is what is due to strangers. Respect the gods, mightiest one; we are your suppliants. Zeus is the avenger of suppliants and strangers — Zeus the god of strangers — who always attends on strangers who deserve respect."

So I spoke, and he answered me straight away out of a pitiless heart: "You are a fool, stranger, or you have come from a long way off, if you tell me to fear the gods or avoid them. The Cyclopes take no notice of Zeus who holds the aegis, or of the blessed gods, since we are far stronger than they are. I would not spare you or your companions to avoid the anger of Zeus, not unless my own heart told me to. But tell me where you moored your well-made ship when you came. Was it somewhere at the far end of the country, or close by? I should like to know."

So he spoke, testing me; but he did not trap me, because I know too much. I answered him with crafty words: "Poseidon the earth-shaker smashed my ship, throwing it on the rocks at the edge of your country. He brought it close to the headland and the wind drove it in from the sea. But I and these men here got clear of destruction."

So I spoke, and out of his pitiless heart he made no answer at all, but sprang up and laid his hands on my companions. He seized two of them at once and dashed them on the ground like puppies, and their brains ran out on the ground and wetted the earth. Then he cut them limb from limb and got his supper ready, and ate them like a mountain-bred lion, leaving nothing — ate the entrails and the flesh and the marrowy bones. And we held our hands up to Zeus, wailing, seeing his cruel work; and helplessness took hold of our souls.

But when the Cyclops had filled his huge belly by eating human flesh and drinking unmixed milk afterwards, he lay down in the cave, stretched out among the sheep. And I made a plan in my great heart to steal up to him and draw my sharp sword from beside my thigh and stab him in the chest, where the midriff holds the liver, feeling for the place with my hand. But a second thought stopped me, because we too would have died right there in utter ruin: we could not have pushed the mighty stone he had set there away from the high door with our hands. So we waited, wailing, for the bright Dawn.

As soon as the early dawn appeared, rosy-fingered, he rekindled the fire and milked his fine flocks, all in turn, and put her young under each mother. And when he had busily got through his work he seized two men again at once and got his meal ready. And when he had eaten he drove his fat flocks out of the cave, moving the great doorstone away easily; and then he put it back in place, the way a man puts the lid on a quiver. Then, whistling loudly, the Cyclops turned his fat flocks towards the mountain, and I was left there, planning evil in the depths of my heart, hoping to take some revenge on him, and that Athena would grant me the glory.

And this seemed to my mind the best plan. There was a great club of the Cyclops lying beside a sheep-pen, a staff of green olive wood, which he had cut to carry when it was dry; and looking at it we thought it was as big as the mast of a black twenty-oared ship, a broad-beamed merchantman that crosses the great gulf: that was how huge it looked in length and thickness. I went to it and cut off about a fathom's length and handed it to my companions and told them to dress it down; and they made it smooth, and I stood by and sharpened it to a point, and then took it and hardened it in the blazing fire. Then I laid it carefully away, hiding it under the dung, which lay in great heaps all through the cave. And I told my companions to cast lots for which of them should have the nerve to lift the stake with me and grind it into his eye when sweet sleep came on him. And the lot fell on the very ones I would have chosen myself: there were four of them, and I made the fifth.

He came at evening, herding his flocks with their fine fleeces, and drove his fat flocks into the wide cave straight away, every one of them, and did not leave one outside in the deep court, either from some foreboding or because a god told him to. Then he lifted the great doorstone and set it in place, and sat down and milked the ewes and the bleating goats, all in turn, and put her young under each mother. And when he had busily got through his work, again he seized two men at once and got his supper ready.

Then I went up to the Cyclops and spoke to him, holding an ivy-wood bowl of the dark wine in my hands:

"Cyclops, here, drink wine after your meal of human flesh, so that you know what kind of drink our ship held. I was bringing it to you as an offering, hoping you might take pity and send me on my way home; but you rage past all bearing. Cruel man — how is anyone of all the crowds of men ever going to come to you again, when what you do is lawless?"

So I spoke, and he took the cup and drained it, and was marvellously pleased as he drank the sweet draught, and asked me for it a second time:

"Give me more, and readily, and tell me your name straight away, so that I can give you a stranger's gift to be glad of. The grain-giving earth bears the rich clusters of wine for the Cyclopes too, and the rain of Zeus makes them grow; but this is a stream of ambrosia and nectar."

So he spoke, and I handed him the flaming wine again. Three times I brought it and gave it him, and three times he drained it in his folly. But when the wine had got round the wits of the Cyclops, then I spoke to him with gentle words:

"Cyclops, you ask my famous name, and I will tell it you; and give me the stranger's gift, as you promised. Nobody is my name. Nobody they call me — my mother and my father and all my companions too."

So I spoke, and he answered me straight away out of a pitiless heart: "Nobody I will eat last among his companions, and the others before him. That shall be your gift."

He spoke, and reeled and fell on his back, and lay there with his thick neck bent aslant, and sleep that conquers everything took hold of him. And wine came up out of his gullet, and bits of human flesh, and he vomited in his drunken sleep.

Then I thrust the stake in under the deep ashes until it should get hot, and heartened all my companions with cheering words, so that no man should flinch from fear. But when the olive stake was about to catch fire, green as it was, and began to glow terribly, then I came close, bringing the stake out of the fire, and my companions stood round me, and a god breathed great courage into us. They took the olive stake, sharp at the point, and drove it into his eye, while I threw my weight on it from above and whirled it round, as a man bores a ship's timber with a drill while the men below keep it spinning with a thong, holding it by either end, and the drill runs round without stopping. So we took the fiery-pointed stake and whirled it round in his eye, and the blood flowed round the hot thing. The flame singed his eyelids and his brows all round as the eyeball burned, and its roots crackled in the fire. As when a smith dips a great axe or an adze in cold water with a loud hissing, to temper it — because that is where the strength of iron comes from — so his eye hissed round the olive stake.

Then he cried out terribly, and the rock rang round us, and we shrank back in terror, while he wrenched the stake out of his eye, all fouled with blood, and flung it away from him, waving his arms wildly. Then he called aloud to the Cyclopes who lived round about him in caves among the windy heights; and they heard his cry and came crowding from every side, and stood round the cave and asked him what was wrong.

"What is so badly wrong with you, Polyphemus, that you shout out like this through the immortal night and keep us from sleeping? Can it be that some mortal man is driving off your flocks against your will, or killing you himself by guile or by force?"

Then mighty Polyphemus answered them from inside the cave: "My friends, it is Nobody who is killing me by guile, and not by force."

And they answered him with winged words: "If nobody is doing you violence when you are alone, then it is a sickness from great Zeus and you cannot escape it. Pray to our father, the lord Poseidon."

So they spoke and went away; and my heart laughed inside me, that my name and my clever trick had taken them in so.

But the Cyclops, groaning and labouring in his pain, groped with his hands and took the stone away from the door and sat down in the doorway himself with his arms stretched out, hoping to catch anyone who tried to go out with the sheep: that was how witless he thought I was in his heart. But I was thinking how it might all turn out for the best, if I could find some way of escaping death for my companions and for myself. And I wove every kind of trick and plan, as a man will when it is a matter of life and death; for the danger was close on us. And this seemed to my mind the best plan.

There were rams, well fed and thick of fleece, fine big beasts with wool dark as the violet. These I bound together silently with the twisted withes the Cyclops used to sleep on, that monster with his heart set on lawlessness. I took them three at a time: the one in the middle carried a man each time, and the other two went one on either side, keeping my companions safe. So every three sheep carried one man. But for myself — there was a ram, far the best of the whole flock. I took hold of him by the back and curled up under his shaggy belly and lay there face upwards, holding on to his marvellous fleece with my hands, with a steady heart. So we waited, wailing, for the bright dawn.

As soon as the early dawn appeared, rosy-fingered, the males of the flock hurried out to pasture, and the females bleated unmilked round the pens, because their udders were bursting. And their master, in terrible pain, felt along the backs of all the sheep as they stood up in front of him; but in his folly he did not notice this, that my men were tied under the breasts of his fleecy sheep. Last of all the flock the ram went out, weighed down by his fleece and by my cunning self. And mighty Polyphemus felt along his back and said to him:

"Good ram, why are you going out through the cave like this, the last of the flock? You never used to lag behind the sheep before; you were always far the first to feed on the tender bloom of the grass, moving with long strides, and always the first to reach the streams of the river, and the first to want to come back to the fold in the evening. But now you are the last of all. Surely you are grieving for your master's eye, which an evil man blinded, with his miserable companions, when he had overpowered my wits with wine — Nobody, who I tell you has not yet escaped destruction. If only you could feel as I do, and could get the power of speech to tell me where he is skulking away from my anger, then his brains should be dashed on the ground here and there through the cave when I had struck him, and my heart should be lightened of the troubles that good-for-nothing Nobody has brought me."

With that he sent the ram out away from him. And when we had got a little way from the cave and the court, I first loosed myself from under the ram and then set my companions free. Quickly we drove off those long-shanked sheep, rich with fat, turning round again and again to look until we reached the ship. And the sight of us who had escaped death was welcome to our dear companions; but for the others they wept and wailed. I would not let them weep, though, but forbade each man with a frown. I told them instead to fling the many fine-fleeced sheep aboard quickly and sail over the salt water. So they went aboard at once and sat down at the benches, and sitting in their order struck the grey sea with their oars.

But when I was as far off as a man's voice carries when he shouts, then I spoke to the Cyclops with mocking words:

"Cyclops, that man was no weakling, it seems, whose companions you meant to eat by brute strength in your hollow cave. Your evil deeds were bound to fall on your own head, you cruel wretch, who did not shrink from eating your guests in your own house. So Zeus has taken his revenge on you, and the other gods."

So I spoke, and he grew angrier still in his heart, and broke off the peak of a high mountain and hurled it at us, and threw it in front of the dark-prowed ship. And the sea surged under the stone as it fell, and the backward flow, like a flood out of the deep, carried the ship quickly landward and drove it onto the shore. But I seized a long pole in my hands and shoved the ship off and along the shore, and roused my companions with a nod of my head and told them to fall to their oars so that we could get out of trouble. And they bent to the oars and rowed.

But when we had gone twice as far over the sea, then I wanted to call to the Cyclops, though my companions, one after another, tried to check me with gentle words: "Reckless man, why do you want to provoke a savage to anger? He has just thrown his missile into the sea and driven our ship back to the land, and we really thought we had died there. If he had heard one of us making a sound or speaking, he would have hurled a jagged rock and crushed our heads and the ship's timbers, he throws so hard."

So they spoke, but they could not persuade my great-hearted spirit, and I answered him again in anger:

"Cyclops, if any mortal man asks you about the shameful blinding of your eye, say that Odysseus blinded it, the sacker of cities, the son of Laertes, whose home is in Ithaca."

So I spoke, and he groaned and answered: "Now the prophecy spoken long ago has come on me. There was a seer here, a good tall man, Telemus, son of Eurymus, who was better than anyone at prophecy and grew old as a seer among the Cyclopes. He told me all this would come to pass in days to come, that I should lose my sight at the hands of Odysseus. But I always looked for some tall handsome man to come here, clothed in great strength; and now a puny thing, a man of no account and a weakling, has blinded me of my eye, after overpowering me with wine. But come here, Odysseus, so that I can set guest-gifts in front of you and speed your sending, and that the glorious earth-shaker may grant it you. I am his son, and he declares himself my father; and he himself will heal me, if he pleases, and nobody else, not one of the blessed gods and not any mortal man."

So he spoke, and I answered him: "I only wish I could rob you of soul and life and send you to the house of Hades, as surely as not even the earth-shaker will heal your eye."

So I spoke, and then he prayed to the lord Poseidon, stretching both his hands out to the starry sky: "Hear me, Poseidon, holder of the earth, dark-haired god, if I really am your son and you declare yourself my father. Grant that Odysseus, sacker of cities, may never reach his home, the son of Laertes, whose home is in Ithaca. But if it is his fate to see his friends and reach his well-built house and his own country, let him come late and in a bad way, after losing all his companions, in another man's ship; and let him find trouble in his house."

So he spoke in prayer, and the dark-haired god heard him. But the Cyclops lifted a far greater stone again and swung and hurled it, putting measureless strength into the throw. He threw it a little behind the dark-prowed ship, and barely missed the end of the steering-oar. And the sea surged under the stone as it fell, and the wave carried the ship on and drove it to the shore.

Now when we had come to the island where our other well-benched ships all lay together, and our companions sat round them weeping, always expecting us, we beached our ship on the sand when we got there, and went out ourselves onto the shore of the sea. Then we took the Cyclops's flocks out of the hollow ship and divided them, so that as far as it lay with me no man should go cheated of an equal share. But my well-greaved companions gave the ram to me alone, as a gift apart, when the flocks were divided; and I sacrificed him on the shore to Zeus, son of Cronos, the god of the dark clouds, who is lord of all, and burned the thigh-pieces. But he took no notice of my offering; he was planning how all my well-benched ships and my trusty companions might be destroyed.

So all day long until sunset we sat feasting on plenty of meat and sweet wine; and when the sun set and the dark came on we lay down to rest on the shore of the sea. And as soon as the early dawn appeared, rosy-fingered, I roused my companions and told them to embark themselves and loose the stern cables. So they went aboard at once and sat down at the benches, and sitting in their order struck the grey sea with their oars.

From there we sailed on, grieving in our hearts, glad to have escaped death, though we had lost our dear companions.
