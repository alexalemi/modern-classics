Book Four (Part 2 of 2)

Meanwhile the suitors in front of the palace of Odysseus were amusing themselves, throwing the discus and the javelin on the levelled ground, in their usual insolence of heart. Antinous and godlike Eurymachus were sitting there, the leaders of the suitors, who were far the best of them in ability. Noemon, son of Phronius, came up to them and put a question to Antinous.

"Antinous, do we know at all, or not, when Telemachus is coming back from sandy Pylos? He has gone off with a ship of mine, and I need her to cross over to spacious Elis, where I have twelve brood mares with sturdy mules still unbroken at the teat. I want to drive one of them off and break him in."

So he spoke, and their hearts were astonished, because they had not supposed Telemachus had gone to Neleian Pylos, but that he was out there somewhere on his lands, among the flocks or with the swineherd. Then Antinous, son of Eupeithes, said to him:

"Tell me the truth: when did he go, and which young men went with him? Were they picked men of Ithaca, or his own hired men and slaves? He would be capable of managing even that. And tell me this truly, so that I know for certain. Did he take the black ship from you by force and against your will, or did you give it him freely of your own accord, because he asked you?"

Then Noemon, son of Phronius, answered him: "I gave it him freely myself. What else could any man do, when a man like that, with his heart full of trouble, comes asking? It would be hard to refuse the gift. The young men who went with him are the best in the country after ourselves; and I noticed one going aboard as their leader — Mentor, or a god who was in every way like Mentor. But that is what puzzles me. I saw noble Mentor here yesterday at daybreak, and yet he had embarked for Pylos before that."

With that he went off to his father's house, and the proud hearts of those two were angry. They made the suitors sit down and leave off their games, and Antinous, son of Eupeithes, spoke among them, in a rage. His black heart was full of it, and his eyes were like blazing fire.

"The insolence of it — a great piece of work has been carried through, this journey of Telemachus, and we thought he would never get it done. Off the boy goes, in spite of all of us here, without a word — launching a ship and picking the best men in the country. He will be starting to be our ruin before long. But may Zeus destroy his strength, to his own undoing, before he reaches the full measure of manhood. Come, give me a fast ship and twenty men, so that I can lie in wait for him and watch as he passes in the strait between Ithaca and rugged Samos. This voyage of his after his father will come to a sorry end."

So he spoke, and they all praised his words and told him to get on with it; and they got up at once and went to the house of Odysseus.

Now Penelope was not long without knowledge of the plans the suitors were plotting in the depths of their hearts, because the herald Medon told her. He had heard what they were deciding, standing outside the court while they were weaving their plot inside. He went through the hall to bring the news to Penelope, and as he stepped over the threshold she spoke to him and said:

"Herald, why have the lordly suitors sent you out? To tell the women of godlike Odysseus to stop their work and get a feast ready for them? I wish they would stop courting and stop gathering here, and that this were the last meal they ever ate — you men who are always crowding in here and wasting so much of the living, the wealth of clear-headed Telemachus. Surely you never listened, in the old days when you were children, when your fathers told you what kind of man Odysseus was among the men who fathered you: how he did no wrong to any man in the country, in word or in deed, though that is the way with kings, who hate one man and love another. He never did anything unjust to anyone at all. But your minds and your unseemly behaviour are there for anyone to see, and there is no gratitude afterwards for good deeds done."

Then Medon, who had good sense, answered her: "I wish, my queen, that this were the worst of it. But the suitors are planning something far greater and far more terrible, which I pray the son of Cronos never brings about. They intend to kill Telemachus with the sharp sword on his way home. He has gone after news of his father to sacred Pylos and to lovely Lacedaemon."

So he spoke, and her knees went slack where she sat and her heart melted. For a long time she could not speak; her eyes filled with tears and the flow of her voice was checked. At last she answered him and said:

"Herald, why has my son gone? He had no need to go aboard the fast-going ships, which serve men as horses of the deep and cross the wide waters of the sea. Was it so that not even his name should be left among men?"

Then Medon, who had good sense, answered her: "I do not know whether some god drove him to it, or whether his own heart moved him to go to Pylos, to learn either of his father's homecoming or what fate he met."

So he spoke, and went off through the house of Odysseus; and a cloud of soul-consuming grief came down on her. She had no heart left to sit on any of the many seats in the room, but sank down on the threshold of her beautifully made chamber, moaning pitifully, and all round her the women wailed, all of them in the house, young and old alike. Penelope spoke among them, sobbing.

"Hear me, my friends. The Olympian has given me sorrow beyond all the women who were born and brought up with me. Long ago I lost my noble husband with the lion heart, who stood out among the Danaans in every kind of excellence, my noble husband, whose fame goes wide through Hellas and the middle of Argos. And now the storm winds have swept my well-loved son away out of the halls without a word, and I never heard he was going. Cruel, all of you — not one of you thought to rouse me out of my bed, though in your hearts you knew perfectly well when he went aboard the hollow black ship. If I had known he was thinking of this journey, he would have stayed here, however eager he was to be gone, or he would have left me dead in the halls.

"Now let one of you hurry and call old Dolius here, my servant, whom my father gave me before I came here, and who keeps my garden with all its trees. Let him go at once and sit down beside Laertes and tell him all this. It may be Laertes can weave some plan in his heart, and come out and make his case with tears to the people, who mean to destroy his line and the line of godlike Odysseus."

Then the good nurse Eurycleia answered her: "Dear lady, you can kill me with the pitiless bronze or let me live on in the house, but I am not going to keep it from you. I knew all this and gave him what he told me to, bread and sweet wine. But he made me swear a great oath not to tell you until at least the twelfth day came, or you missed him yourself and heard he was gone, so that you should not spoil your lovely face with weeping. Now wash, and put clean clothes on, and go up to your room with your women and pray to Athena, the daughter of Zeus who holds the aegis; she may save him even from death. And do not trouble an old man who has trouble enough. I do not think the family of the son of Arceisius is altogether hated by the blessed gods. There will still be one of them, I think, to hold the high-roofed halls and the rich fields lying far off."

So she spoke, and quieted Penelope's laments and stopped her eyes from weeping. She washed and put clean clothes on and went up to her room with her women, and put barley grains in a basket, and prayed to Athena.

"Hear me, child of Zeus who holds the aegis, unwearied one. If ever Odysseus of the many tricks burned fat thigh-pieces of heifer or ewe to you in his halls, remember them now, I beg you, and save my dear son, and keep the suitors off him in their wicked insolence."

With that she raised the sacred cry, and the goddess heard her prayer.

But the suitors broke into uproar through the shadowy halls, and one of the arrogant young men would say: "Our queen, courted by so many, is certainly getting our marriage ready — and she does not know at all that her son has been marked for death."

That is how one of them talked; but they did not know how these things were going to go. Then Antinous spoke to the company and said: "You fools, keep off this high talk, all of you alike, in case somebody reports what you say inside the house. Come, let us get up quietly like this and put into effect the plan that pleased every one of us."

So he spoke, and chose the twenty best men, and they went off to the fast ship and the shore of the sea. First they drew the ship down to deep water and set the mast and sail in the black ship, and fitted the oars in the leather thole-straps, all in their order, and spread the white sail; and proud attendants brought them their weapons. They moored the ship well out in the roadstead and went ashore themselves, and there they took their supper and waited for the evening to come.

But wise Penelope lay up in her room, touching no food, tasting neither meat nor drink, turning over whether her faultless son would escape death or be killed by the insolent suitors. As a lion is seized with fear and broods in the middle of a crowd of men when they are drawing their cunning ring about him, so she was turning it over when sweet sleep came on her. She sank back and slept, and every joint in her went slack.

Then the goddess grey-eyed Athena thought of something else. She made a phantom and gave it the form of a woman, Iphthime, the daughter of great-hearted Icarius, whom Eumelus had married, whose home was in Pherae. And she sent it to the house of godlike Odysseus, to Penelope in the middle of her wailing and crying, to tell her to stop weeping and tearful lamenting. It passed into the room by the thong of the bolt and stood over her head and said to her:

"Are you asleep, Penelope, with your heart so stricken? The gods who live at their ease do not allow you to weep or be distressed, since your son is going to come back after all. He is no sinner in the eyes of the gods."

Then wise Penelope answered her, slumbering very sweetly at the gates of dreams: "Why have you come here, sister? You never used to come before, because your home is a long way off. And you tell me to stop grieving and to stop the many pains that distress me in my mind and heart. Long ago I lost my noble husband with the lion heart, who stood out among the Danaans in every kind of excellence, my noble husband, whose fame goes wide through Hellas and the middle of Argos. And now my well-loved son has gone off in a hollow ship, a mere child, who knows nothing of hardship or of the gatherings of men. I grieve for him even more than for the other one, and I tremble for him, and I am afraid something will happen to him, either in the country he has gone to or on the sea. There are a great many enemies plotting against him, eager to kill him before he comes back to his own country."

Then the dim phantom answered her and said: "Take heart, and do not be too much afraid in your mind. There is such a guide going with him as other men have often begged to have stand beside them, because she has the power — Pallas Athena. And she pities you in your grief. It is she who has sent me out to tell you this."

Then wise Penelope answered her again: "If you are indeed a god, and have heard the voice of a god, come, tell me about that unlucky man too — whether he is still alive and sees the light of the sun, or whether he is dead already and in the house of Hades."

And the dim phantom answered her and said: "About him I cannot speak at length, whether he is alive or dead. It is a bad thing to speak words as empty as wind."

With that the phantom slipped away by the bolt of the door into the breath of the winds. And the daughter of Icarius started up out of her sleep, and her heart was warmed with comfort that so clear a vision had come speeding to her in the dark of the night.

But the suitors embarked and sailed over the watery roads, with utter murder for Telemachus in their hearts. There is a rocky island in the middle of the sea, halfway between Ithaca and rugged Samos — Asteris, not large, but there is a harbour in it where ships can lie, with an entrance on either side. That is where the Achaeans waited, lying in ambush for Telemachus.
