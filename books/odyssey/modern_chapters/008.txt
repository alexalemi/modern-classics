Book Eight

As soon as the early dawn appeared, rosy-fingered, the strong and mighty Alcinous got up from his bed, and Odysseus got up too, born of Zeus, the sacker of cities. And the strong and mighty Alcinous led the way to the meeting place of the Phaeacians, which was built for them close by their ships. They came there and sat down on the polished stones beside one another; and Pallas Athena went through the city in the likeness of the herald of wise Alcinous, planning a homecoming for great-hearted Odysseus. She came up to each man and said:

"Come now, leaders and counsellors of the Phaeacians, come to the meeting place, and hear about the stranger who has just come to the palace of wise Alcinous after his wanderings over the sea, and who is like the immortals in build."

With that she stirred up the spirit and heart of every man, and the meeting place and the seats were quickly filled with the men who gathered. A great many marvelled at the sight of the wise son of Laertes, because Athena poured a marvellous grace over his head and shoulders, and made him taller and sturdier to look at, so that he would be welcomed by all the Phaeacians and win awe and respect, and might carry off the many feats the Phaeacians would test him with.

And when they had come together, Alcinous spoke to the gathering.

"Listen to me, leaders and counsellors of the Phaeacians, and let me say what the heart in my breast tells me. This stranger — I do not know who he is — has come to my house in his wanderings, whether from the men of the east or of the west. He is pressing to be sent on his way, and asks for a promise of it. Let us speed his sending, as we have always done. No man at all who comes to my house sits here long in distress for want of a sending. Come, let us draw a black ship down to the bright sea for her first voyage, and let the people pick fifty-two young men, the best there have been up to now. And when you have all lashed the oars properly to the thole-pins, go ashore and come to my house, and get a feast ready quickly; I will provide generously for everyone. That is my order to the young men. But the rest of you, the sceptred kings, come to my fine palace so that we can entertain the stranger in the halls; and let nobody refuse me. And call the divine singer here, Demodocus, since the god has granted him skill in song beyond anyone else, to give delight in whatever way his spirit prompts him to sing."

With that he led the way, and the sceptred kings followed him, while a herald went for the divine singer. And fifty-two picked young men went, as he ordered, to the shore of the restless sea. And when they had come down to the ship and the sea, they drew the black ship down to deep water and set the mast and sail in the black ship and fitted the oars in the leather thole-straps, all in their order, and spread the white sail. They moored the ship well out in the roadstead, and then went off to the great palace of wise Alcinous.

The porticoes and the courts and the rooms were full of the men who gathered, a great many of them, young and old. Alcinous slaughtered twelve sheep for them, and eight white-tusked boars, and two slow-moving oxen. They skinned them and dressed them and got a splendid feast ready.

Then the herald came up, leading the good singer, whom the Muse loved above all other men, and gave both good and evil: she took away his sight and gave him the gift of sweet song. Pontonous the herald set a silver-studded chair for him in the middle of the banqueters, leaning it against a tall pillar, and hung the clear-toned lyre from a peg just above his head and showed him how to reach it with his hands. And beside him he put a basket and a beautiful table and a cup of wine, to drink when his heart told him to. So they put out their hands to the good food in front of them.

But when they had eaten and drunk as much as they wanted, the Muse moved the singer to sing of the famous deeds of fighting men, from a song whose fame had reached the wide sky in those days: the quarrel of Odysseus and Achilles, son of Peleus, how once they fell out with furious words at a rich feast of the gods, and Agamemnon, king of men, was glad in his heart that the best of the Achaeans were quarrelling — because Phoebus Apollo had told him in his answer that it would be so, in sacred Pytho, when he crossed the threshold of stone to consult the oracle. For then the beginning of the misery was rolling down on Trojans and Danaans alike, through the will of great Zeus.

That was the song the famous singer sang. But Odysseus took hold of his great purple cloak with his strong hands and drew it down over his head and hid his handsome face, because he was ashamed to let the Phaeacians see the tears falling from under his eyebrows. And whenever the divine singer stopped singing, Odysseus would wipe away his tears and pull the cloak off his head and take the two-handled cup and pour an offering to the gods. But every time he began again, and the Phaeacian nobles told him to sing because they enjoyed his song, Odysseus would cover his head again and groan.

He hid his weeping from all the rest of them, but Alcinous alone noticed him and took it in, because he sat beside him and heard him groaning heavily. And at once he spoke among the Phaeacians, who love the oar.

"Listen to me, leaders and counsellors of the Phaeacians. We have satisfied our hearts already with the shared banquet and with the lyre, which is the companion of a rich feast. Now let us go out and try our hands at every kind of game, so that the stranger can tell his friends, when he gets home, how far we are ahead of other men at boxing and wrestling and jumping and running."

With that he led the way and they followed him. The herald hung the clear-toned lyre on its peg and took Demodocus by the hand and led him out of the hall, guiding him by the same road the others had gone, the Phaeacian nobles, to watch the games. They went off to the meeting place, and a huge crowd went with them, past counting; and a good many noble young men stood up. Acroneus stood up, and Ocyalus, and Elatreus, and Nauteus, and Prymneus, and Anchialus, and Eretmeus, and Ponteus, and Proreus, Thoon and Anabesineus, and Amphialus, son of Polyneus, son of Tecton; and Euryalus stood up too, the equal of man-destroying Ares, the son of Naubolus, who was the best-looking and best-built of all the Phaeacians after faultless Laodamas. And the three sons of noble Alcinous stood up, Laodamas and Halius and godlike Clytoneus.

These made trial first in the foot race. A course was marked out for them from the turning point, and they all ran fast, raising the dust of the plain; but noble Clytoneus was far the best of them at running, and by as much as the range of a team of mules in fallow land he shot ahead and reached the crowd, and left the others behind. Then they tried the hard work of wrestling, and there Euryalus was better than all the princes. At jumping Amphialus was best of all, and with the discus Elatreus was far the best again, and at boxing Laodamas, the good son of Alcinous.

But when they had all taken their pleasure in the contests, Laodamas, son of Alcinous, spoke among them. "Come, friends, let us ask the stranger whether he knows any contests, or has learned any. He is certainly no small man in build — look at his thighs and his calves, and his two arms above, and his strong neck, and how powerful he is. He lacks nothing of the strength of youth; he has only been broken by a great deal of trouble. To my mind there is nothing worse than the sea for wrecking a man, however strong he is."

And Euryalus answered him: "Laodamas, that is very properly said. Go and challenge him yourself, and make your point."

When the good son of Alcinous heard this, he went and took his stand in the middle and said to Odysseus: "Come, sir, you try the contests too, if you know any. And you must know some, since there is no greater glory for a man as long as he lives than what he wins with his own hands and feet. Come and try, and throw the care out of your heart. Your journey will not be put off much longer; your ship is launched already and the crew is ready."

Then Odysseus of the many tricks answered him: "Laodamas, why do you mock me with this challenge? There is far more sorrow in my mind than there are contests. I have suffered a great deal and worked hard in the past, and now I sit in the middle of your assembly longing for my homecoming, making my plea to the king and to all the people."

Then Euryalus answered him and taunted him to his face: "No, stranger, I would not compare you to a man skilled in the contests, of which there are plenty among men. I would compare you to a man who goes to and fro with his benched ship as the captain of sailors who are traders, a man with his mind on his freight, keeping an eye on his cargo and the profits of his greed. You do not look like an athlete."

Then Odysseus of the many tricks gave him a black look from under his brows and answered him: "Stranger, that was not well said. You are like a man blinded by his own foolishness. It is true, then, that the gods do not give gracious gifts to everyone alike, not looks or mind or eloquence. One man is poor in appearance, but the god sets a crown of beauty on his words, and men look at him with delight, and he speaks on steadily with sweet modesty and stands out among the gathered people, and as he goes through the city they gaze at him as at a god. Another is like the immortals to look at, and there is no crown of grace set about his words. So with you: your looks are outstanding, and a god himself could not improve them, but in mind you are stunted.

"You have stirred up the spirit in my breast by speaking so rudely. I am not unskilled in sports, as you chatter. I think I was among the first of them, as long as I could trust my youth and my hands. But now I am held down by suffering and pain; I have endured a great deal, passing through the wars of men and the grievous waves. Even so, and though I have suffered so much, I will try the contests. Your words stung me to the heart, and you provoked me with what you said."

He spoke, and jumped up with his cloak still round him as it was, and seized a discus larger than the rest and thick, a good deal heavier than the ones the Phaeacians used against one another. He whirled it and sent it from his strong hand, and the stone hummed as it flew; and down they crouched to the ground, the long-oared Phaeacians, men famous for their ships, under the rush of the stone. It flew past the marks of all of them, running lightly from his hand, and Athena, in the likeness of a man, set the mark, and spoke to him.

"Even a blind man could pick this mark out, stranger, groping for it with his hands, because it is not muddled up in the crowd of the others but far the first. Take heart about this bout at any rate: no Phaeacian will reach this or throw past it."

So she spoke, and much-enduring noble Odysseus was glad, delighted to see a true friend among the contestants. Then he spoke among the Phaeacians with a lighter heart.

"Reach that now, young men. And soon, I think, I shall send another after it, as far or even further. And of the rest of you, if any man's heart and spirit tell him to, let him come here and try me — since you have made me thoroughly angry — at boxing or at wrestling or even at running, I do not mind. Let any of the Phaeacians come, except Laodamas alone. He is my host, and who would quarrel with the man who is entertaining him? That man is a fool and worth nothing who challenges his host to a contest in a strange country; he only spoils his own chances. Of all the rest I refuse nobody and think little of nobody, but I am willing to know them and try them man to man. I am no weakling at anything, at any of the contests men practise. I know well enough how to handle a polished bow, and I would always be the first to shoot and hit my man in a crowd of the enemy, even with a great many companions standing by me shooting at them. Philoctetes alone beat me with the bow in the land of the Trojans, when we Achaeans shot. Of all the others I say I am far the best, of all the mortals now alive on the earth who eat bread. But I will not try to compete with the men of the old days, with Heracles or Eurytus of Oechalia, who competed even with the immortals in archery. That is why great Eurytus died soon, and old age never came on him in his halls, because Apollo grew angry and killed him for challenging him to a contest with the bow. And with the spear I throw further than any other man can shoot with an arrow. It is only in the foot race that I am afraid some Phaeacian may outrun me. I have been cruelly broken among all those waves, since there was no lasting store of provisions in my ship, and my limbs are loosened."

So he spoke, and they were all hushed and silent; and only Alcinous answered him and said: "Stranger, since you speak among us in no ungracious way, but mean to show the ability that goes with you, in anger that this man came up to you among the contestants and taunted you in a way that no mortal would make light of your ability who knew in his heart how to speak properly — come, listen to me now, so that you can tell another hero, when you are feasting in your halls with your wife and children and remembering our skill, what feats Zeus has granted us from our fathers' days until now. We are not faultless boxers or wrestlers, but we run swiftly in the foot race, and we are the best seamen; and the banquet is always dear to us, and the lyre, and the dance, and changes of clothes, and warm baths, and bed.

"But come now, all you who are the best dancers of the Phaeacians, make sport, so that the stranger can tell his friends when he gets home how far we are ahead of others in seamanship and in speed of foot, and in the dance and in song. And let somebody go straight away and fetch the clear-toned lyre for Demodocus, which is lying somewhere in our halls."

So spoke godlike Alcinous, and the herald got up to fetch the hollow lyre from the king's palace. Then the masters of the lists stood up, nine of them, men chosen from the people, who used to put everything in order at their gatherings. They levelled a place for the dance and marked out a fine wide ring, and the herald came up carrying the clear-toned lyre for Demodocus. He moved into the middle, and boys stood round him in the first bloom of youth, skilled in the dance, and they beat the good dancing floor with their feet; and Odysseus watched the twinkling of their feet and marvelled in his spirit.

But the singer struck the chords and began his sweet song, and sang of the love of Ares and Aphrodite of the fair crown: how they first lay together in the house of Hephaestus, secretly, and Ares gave her many gifts, and shamed the bed of the lord Hephaestus. And someone came to him at once with the news, Helios, who had noticed them lying together in love. And when Hephaestus heard the bitter tale, he went off to his smithy turning evil over in the depths of his heart, and set the great anvil on the anvil block and forged bonds that could not be broken or loosed, so that the lovers should stay fast where they were.

And when he had made the snare in his anger against Ares, he went to his room, where his bed was, and spread the bonds all round the bedposts, and hung many of them from above, from the roof-beams, fine as spiders' webs, so that nobody could see them, not even one of the blessed gods; they were made so extraordinarily cunningly. And when he had spread the whole snare round the bed, he pretended he was going to Lemnos, that well-built city, which is far the dearest of all lands in his eyes.

And Ares of the golden rein was keeping no blind watch, and when he saw Hephaestus, famous for his craft, going off, he went to the house of famous Hephaestus, eager for the love of Cytherea of the fair crown. She had only just come from the presence of her father, the mighty son of Cronos, and had sat down. And Ares came into the house and took her hand and spoke to her.

"Come, my love, let us go to bed and take our pleasure lying together. Hephaestus is not in the country any longer; I imagine he has gone off to Lemnos by now, to visit the Sintians with their savage speech."

So he spoke, and it seemed a welcome thing to her to lie with him. So the two of them went to the bed and lay down to sleep, and the cunning bonds of clever Hephaestus closed round them, and they could not stir a limb or lift one. Then at last they understood there was no getting away.

And the famous god of the two strong arms came up close to them, having turned back before he reached the land of Lemnos, because Helios had kept watch for him and brought him word. He went to his house with a heavy heart and stood at the gateway, and fierce anger took hold of him, and he cried out terribly and called to all the gods:

"Father Zeus, and you other blessed gods who live for ever, come here and see something laughable and monstrous — how Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, despises me for being lame, and loves destructive Ares because he is handsome and sound of limb, while I was born misshapen. And there is nobody to blame for that but my own two parents — I wish they had never had me. But look where the two of them have gone up into my bed and are sleeping together in love; and I am sick at the sight. Still, I do not think they will want to lie like that any longer, not for a moment, however much in love they are. Soon they will both lose their appetite for sleeping. But the snare and the bonds will hold them until her father pays me back every one of the wedding gifts I gave him for the sake of his shameless girl. His daughter is beautiful, but she cannot govern her passion."

So he spoke, and the gods gathered at the house of the bronze floor. Poseidon came, who holds the earth, and the helper Hermes came, and the lord Apollo the archer. The goddesses stayed at home, each of them, out of modesty; but the gods, the givers of good things, stood in the gateway; and unquenchable laughter rose up among the blessed gods when they saw the craft of clever Hephaestus. And one of them would say, with a glance at his neighbour:

"Bad deeds do not prosper. The slow one catches the swift — look how Hephaestus, slow as he is, has caught Ares, though Ares is the fastest of all the gods who hold Olympus. Lame as he is, he has caught him by craft. So Ares owes the adulterer's fine."

That is how they talked to one another. But the lord Apollo, son of Zeus, said to Hermes: "Hermes, son of Zeus, messenger, giver of good things — would you really be willing to lie on a bed beside golden Aphrodite, even tangled in strong bonds?"

Then the messenger, the slayer of Argus, answered him: "I only wish it might happen, lord Apollo, you archer god — that three times as many bonds might hold me fast past untying, and you gods, and all the goddesses too, might be looking on, so long as I slept beside golden Aphrodite."

So he spoke, and laughter rose up among the immortal gods. Only Poseidon did not laugh, but kept begging Hephaestus, the famous craftsman, to set Ares free; and he spoke to him with winged words: "Loose him, and I promise, as you tell me, that he will pay you everything that is right himself, in the presence of the immortal gods."

Then the famous god of the two strong arms answered him: "Do not ask this of me, Poseidon, you who hold the earth. Standing surety for a worthless man is a poor sort of security. How could I put you in bonds among the immortal gods, if Ares got out of both the debt and the bonds and went off?"

Then Poseidon the earth-shaker answered him again: "Hephaestus, even if Ares does get out of the debt and run away, I will pay you this myself."

Then the famous god of the two strong arms answered him: "It is not possible for me to refuse you, and it would not be right."

With that mighty Hephaestus loosed the bonds, and the two of them, when they were free of that bond so strong, sprang up at once. Ares went off to Thrace, and she, laughter-loving Aphrodite, went to Cyprus, to Paphos, where her precinct is and her fragrant altar. There the Graces bathed her and rubbed her with immortal oil, the kind that gleams on the gods who live for ever, and dressed her in lovely clothes, a wonder to look at.

That was the song the famous singer sang. And Odysseus was glad in his heart as he listened, and so were the long-oared Phaeacians, men famous for their ships.

Then Alcinous told Halius and Laodamas to dance alone, since nobody could compete with them. And when they had taken in their hands the beautiful purple ball, which clever Polybus had made for them, one of them would lean back and toss it towards the shadowy clouds, and the other would leap up off the ground and catch it skilfully before his feet touched down again. And when they had tried their skill at throwing the ball straight up, the two of them fell to dancing on the bountiful earth, tossing the ball to and fro all the while, and the other young men stood in the ring and beat time, and a great din rose up from it.

Then noble Odysseus said to Alcinous: "Lord Alcinous, renowned above all men, you boasted that your dancers were the best, and your words are made good. Amazement takes hold of me looking at them."

So he spoke, and the strong and mighty Alcinous was glad, and at once he spoke among the Phaeacians, who love the oar: "Listen to me, leaders and counsellors of the Phaeacians. This stranger seems to me a man of understanding. Come, then, let us give him a friendship gift, as is fitting. Twelve glorious kings hold power in our country as rulers, and I am the thirteenth myself. Let each of the twelve of you bring a newly washed cloak and a tunic and a talent of precious gold, and let us bring it all together quickly, so that the stranger may go to his supper with our gifts in his hands and a glad heart. And let Euryalus make amends to the stranger himself, with words and with a gift, since what he said was not at all seemly."

So he spoke, and they all praised his words and said it should be so, and every man sent a herald to fetch the gifts. And Euryalus answered and said: "Lord Alcinous, renowned above all men, I will indeed make amends to the stranger, as you tell me. I will give him this sword, all of bronze, with a silver hilt on it and a scabbard of newly sawn ivory worked round it; and it will be a thing of great worth to him."

With that he put the silver-studded sword into his hands, and spoke to him with winged words: "Greetings, sir stranger. If any word has been spoken that was harsh, may the storm winds snatch it up at once and carry it away. And for yourself, may the gods grant you to see your wife and to come to your own country; you have been suffering a long time, far from your friends."

And Odysseus of the many tricks answered him: "Greetings to you too, my friend, and may the gods grant you happiness; and may you never afterwards miss this sword you have given me, making amends with gentle words."

He spoke, and hung the silver-studded sword over his shoulders. And the sun set, and the splendid gifts were brought to him. The lordly heralds carried them to the palace of Alcinous, and the sons of faultless Alcinous took the beautiful gifts and set them down before their honoured mother. And the strong and mighty Alcinous led the way, and they came in and sat down on the high seats.

Then mighty Alcinous said to Arete: "Bring a fine chest here, wife, the best you have, and put a newly washed cloak and tunic in it yourself. And heat a cauldron on the fire for the stranger and warm some water, so that when he has bathed and seen all the gifts the noble Phaeacians have brought here safely stowed, he can take pleasure in the feast and in hearing the strains of the song. And I will give him this beautiful cup of mine, made of gold, so that he may remember me all his days as he pours offerings in his halls to Zeus and to the other gods."

So he spoke, and Arete told her women to set a great cauldron on the fire as fast as they could. They set the cauldron for the bath water on the blazing fire and poured water in and took billets of wood and kindled them underneath. The fire played round the belly of the cauldron and the water grew warm; and meanwhile Arete brought a beautiful chest out of the treasure chamber for the stranger and put the fine gifts in it, the clothes and the gold the Phaeacians had given. And she put a cloak and a fine tunic in it herself, and spoke to Odysseus with winged words.

"See to the lid yourself now, and throw a cord round it quickly, in case somebody robs you of your goods on the way, when you are lying in sweet sleep later on as you travel in the black ship."

When much-enduring noble Odysseus heard this he fitted the lid on at once and quickly threw a cord round it, in a cunning knot which queenly Circe had once taught him. Then the housekeeper told him to go to the bath and wash; and his heart was glad when he saw the warm bath, because he had not been used to such attention since he left the house of fair-haired Calypso, though up to then he had been attended constantly, like a god.

And when the women had bathed him and rubbed him with oil and put a fine cloak and a tunic on him, he came out of the bath and went to join the men at their wine. And Nausicaa, given her beauty by the gods, stood by the doorpost of the well-built hall, and marvelled at Odysseus as her eyes took him in, and spoke to him with winged words.

"Farewell, stranger. And afterwards, even in your own country, remember me, because it is to me first that you owe your life."

Then Odysseus of the many tricks answered her: "Nausicaa, daughter of great-hearted Alcinous, may Zeus grant it so, the loud-thundering lord of Hera, that I reach my home and see the day of my return. Then I will pray to you there as to a god, all my days; because you gave me my life, girl."

He spoke, and sat down on a chair beside king Alcinous. And now they were serving out the portions and mixing the wine. Then the herald came up leading the good singer, Demodocus, honoured by the people, and seated him in the middle of the banqueters, leaning his chair against a high pillar.

Then Odysseus of the many tricks said to the herald, cutting off a portion of the chine of a white-tusked boar, with more still left on it and rich fat on either side: "Herald, take this portion and give it to Demodocus to eat, and let me greet him, in spite of my grief. Among all the men on earth singers win honour and respect, because the Muse has taught them the paths of song and loves the tribe of singers."

So he spoke, and the herald carried the portion and put it in the hands of the lord Demodocus, and he took it and was glad in his heart. So they put out their hands to the good food in front of them. And when they had eaten and drunk as much as they wanted, Odysseus of the many tricks said to Demodocus:

"Demodocus, I praise you above all mortal men, whether it was the Muse who taught you, the daughter of Zeus, or Apollo. You sing the fate of the Achaeans truly and well, everything they did and suffered and all the labour they endured, as though you had been there yourself or heard it from someone who was. But come now, change your theme and sing of the building of the wooden horse, which Epeius made with Athena's help, the horse Odysseus once led up into the citadel as a thing of guile, when he had filled it with the men who sacked Ilios. If you tell me this tale properly, I will declare to all mankind that the god has granted you the gift of divine song with a ready heart."

So he spoke, and the singer, moved by the god, began and let his song be heard, taking up the tale where the Argives had gone aboard their benched ships and were sailing away, after throwing fire into their huts, while the others, led by glorious Odysseus, were sitting by then in the meeting place of the Trojans, hidden in the horse; because the Trojans had dragged it up to the citadel themselves. So it stood there, while the people talked on and on sitting round it, and could reach no decision. Three courses found favour with them: to split the hollow timber with the pitiless bronze, or to drag it to the height and throw it down the rocks, or to let it stand as a great offering to win the goodwill of the gods — which is what was to be done in the end, because it was their fate to die when their city took in the great wooden horse, with all the best of the Argives sitting inside it, bringing death and doom to the Trojans.

And he sang how the sons of the Achaeans poured out of the horse and left their hollow ambush and sacked the city; how the others laid waste the high city this way and that, and how Odysseus went like Ares to the house of Deiphobus together with godlike Menelaus. That, he said, was where Odysseus faced the most terrible fighting, and in the end conquered with the help of great-hearted Athena.

That was the song the famous singer sang. But the heart of Odysseus melted and the tears wet his cheeks under his eyelids. As a woman wails and flings herself over her dear husband, who has fallen in front of his city and his people, trying to keep the pitiless day away from his city and his children; and as she sees him dying and gasping for breath she clings to him and shrieks aloud, while behind her they strike her back and shoulders with their spears and lead her off into slavery, to hard labour and misery, and her cheeks waste away with the most pitiful grief — so Odysseus let the pitiful tears fall from under his brows.

He hid his weeping from all the rest of them, but Alcinous alone noticed him and took it in, because he sat beside him and heard him groaning heavily. And at once he spoke among the Phaeacians, who love the oar.

"Listen to me, leaders and counsellors of the Phaeacians, and let Demodocus stop his clear-toned lyre now, since this song does not give pleasure to everyone alike. From the moment we began to eat and the divine singer was moved to sing, the stranger there has never stopped his sorrowful lamenting. Grief has taken hold of his heart, I am sure of it. Let the singer stop, so that we can all enjoy ourselves, hosts and guest alike, since that is better. All this has been made ready for the sake of our honoured guest, his sending and the friendship gifts we give him out of our affection. A stranger and a suppliant is as dear as a brother to any man whose wits reach even a little way.

"So do not hide any longer with crafty thoughts whatever I ask you. Speaking out plainly is the better course. Tell me the name they called you by at home, your mother and your father and the others besides, your townsmen and the people living round about. There is nobody among all mankind who has no name, base man or noble, once he has been born; parents give names to all of them when they are born. And tell me your country, your people, and your city, so that our ships can carry you there, working out the course by their own wits. The Phaeacians have no pilots and no steering-oars such as other ships have; their ships understand the thoughts and minds of men by themselves, and they know the cities and the rich fields of every people, and cross the gulf of the sea most swiftly, hidden in mist and cloud, and never have any fear of damage or ruin.

"Yet I once heard this story told by my father Nausithous, who used to say that Poseidon was angry with us because we give safe convoy to everyone. He said that one day, as a well-built ship of the Phaeacians was coming home from a convoy over the misty water, Poseidon would strike her, and would throw a great mountain round our city. That is what the old man said, and the god may bring it about or leave it unfulfilled, whichever pleases him.

"But come, tell me this and tell me the truth. Where have you wandered, and what countries of men have you come to? Tell me about the people and their well-built cities, both those who are cruel and wild and unjust, and those who welcome strangers and whose minds fear the gods. And tell me why you weep and mourn in your spirit when you hear the doom of the Argive Danaans and of Ilios. The gods brought that about, and spun the thread of ruin for men, so that there might be a song for those yet to be born. Did some relative of yours fall before Ilios, some good true man, your daughter's husband or your wife's father, who are the nearest to a man after his own kin and blood? Or was it a companion who was dear to your heart, some good true man? A companion who has an understanding heart is no worse than a brother."
