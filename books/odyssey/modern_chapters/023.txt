Book Twenty-Three

Then the old woman went up to the upper room, laughing aloud, to tell her mistress that her dear husband was in the house. Her knees moved nimbly, and her feet trotted along under her. And she stood over her lady's head and said to her:

"Wake up, Penelope, dear child, and see with your own eyes what you have wanted all your days. Odysseus is here; he has come home, late as his coming has been, and he has killed the proud suitors who troubled his house and ate up his substance and oppressed his son."

Then wise Penelope answered her: "Dear nurse, the gods have made you mad — they who can make even a very wise person foolish, and set the simple-minded on the paths of understanding. It is they who have spoiled your wits, when you were sound in mind before. Why do you mock me, when my heart is full of sorrow, telling me this wild tale, and rouse me out of sleep, the sweet sleep that bound me and wrapped my eyelids? I have never slept so soundly since the day Odysseus went off to see evil Ilios, that should never be named. Go down now and back to the women's hall. If any other of my women had come and told me this and roused me out of sleep, I would have sent her back into the hall in a sorry state at once. But your old age will bring you this much profit."

Then her dear nurse Eurycleia answered her: "I am not mocking you, dear child. Odysseus really is here, and has come home, just as I tell you. He is that stranger they all dishonoured in the halls. But Telemachus knew he was here long ago, and in his prudence hid his father's purpose, until he should take vengeance on the violence of overbearing men."

So she spoke, and Penelope was glad, and leapt out of bed and flung her arms round the old woman and let the tears fall from her eyelids; and she spoke to her with winged words:

"Come now, dear nurse, tell me truly: if he really has come home, as you say, how did he lay his hands on the shameless suitors, all alone as he was, when they stayed in the house always in a body?"

Then her dear nurse Eurycleia answered her: "I did not see, I did not ask; I only heard the groaning of men being killed. We women sat terror-stricken in the innermost part of our well-built rooms, and the close-fitting doors shut us in, until the hour when your son Telemachus called me from the hall — his father had sent him to call me. Then I found Odysseus standing among the bodies of the killed, and they were stretched all round him on the hard floor, lying one on another; the sight would have warmed your heart with cheer.

"And now the bodies are all gathered together at the gates of the court, and he is purging the fine house with sulphur and has kindled a great fire, and he has sent me to call you. Come with me, so that the hearts of you two may come into joy; you have suffered so much. And now at last this long desire of yours has been fulfilled: he has come himself, alive, to his own hearth, and has found both you and his son in the halls; and as for those who did him harm, the suitors, he has taken vengeance on every one of them in his house."

Then wise Penelope answered her: "Dear nurse, do not boast loudly over them with laughter yet. You know how welcome the sight of him in the halls would be to everyone, but most of all to me and to the son we two had. But this is no true tale as you tell it. One of the immortals has killed the lordly suitors in anger at their grievous insolence and their evil doings. They honoured nobody on earth, evil or good, whoever came among them; and so it is through their own wanton folly that they have suffered. But Odysseus has lost his homecoming to the land of Achaea far away, and is lost himself."

Then her dear nurse Eurycleia answered her: "My child, what a word has escaped the barrier of your teeth, saying that your husband, who is here at his own hearth this very moment, would never come home again. Your heart is always unbelieving. Come, I will tell you a plain sign besides: the scar of the wound the boar dealt him long ago with his white tusk. I noticed it while I was washing his feet, and I wanted to tell you as well, but he laid his hand on my mouth and in the great wisdom of his heart would not let me speak. Come with me. And I will stake my very life on it, that if I am deceiving you, you should kill me by the most pitiful death."

Then wise Penelope answered her: "Dear nurse, it is hard for you to understand the counsels of the gods who live for ever, however wise you are. But even so let us go to my son, so that I may see the suitors dead, and the man who killed them."

With that she went down from the upper room, and her heart turned over a great deal, whether to stand apart and question her dear husband, or to go up to him and clasp and kiss his head and hands. But when she had come in and crossed the stone threshold, she sat down opposite Odysseus in the light of the fire, by the further wall. And he was sitting by a tall pillar, looking down, waiting to see whether his noble wife would say anything to him when her eyes took him in. But she sat a long while in silence, and amazement came on her soul. Now with her eyes she would look full at his face, and now again she would fail to know him, because he had mean clothes on.

But Telemachus rebuked her and said to her: "Mother, cruel mother, with your unyielding heart, why do you hold off from my father like this, and not sit beside him and ask him questions? No other woman would harden her heart as you do and stand apart from a husband who has come back to her in the twentieth year to his own country after so much hard suffering. But your heart is always harder than stone."

Then wise Penelope answered him: "My child, the heart in my breast is lost in wonder, and I have no power to speak at all, or to ask a question, or to look him in the face. But if he really is Odysseus and has come home, then the two of us will know each other more surely; because we have signs which the two of us alone know, hidden from everyone else."

So she spoke, and much-enduring noble Odysseus smiled, and at once spoke winged words to Telemachus: "Telemachus, let your mother test me in the halls now; she will come to a surer knowledge presently. As it is, because I am filthy and dressed in mean clothing about my body, she looks down on me and will not yet admit that I am he. But let us think how it may all turn out for the best. When a man has killed only one man in a country, even a man who leaves few behind him to avenge him, he goes into exile and leaves his kinsmen and his own country; and we have killed men who were the very stay of the city, far the noblest of the young men of Ithaca. I ask you to think about that."

Then clear-headed Telemachus answered him: "Look to it yourself, dear father; they say your counsel is the best among men, and no other mortal could compete with you. We will follow with you eagerly, and I do not think we shall be wanting in courage, as far as our strength goes."

Then Odysseus of the many tricks answered him and said: "Then I will tell you what seems to me the best way. First bathe yourselves and put your tunics on, and tell the women in the halls to take their clothes. And let the divine singer, with his clear-toned lyre in his hand, lead us in a cheerful dance, so that any man who hears the sound from outside — a passer-by or one of the neighbours — may say it is a wedding feast; and then the rumour of the killing of the suitors will not spread through the city before we go out to our well-wooded farm. There we will afterwards think out whatever advantage the Olympian may grant us."

So he spoke, and they all listened readily and obeyed. First they bathed and put on their tunics, and the women dressed themselves, and the divine singer took the hollow lyre and roused in them the desire for sweet song and good dancing. So the great hall rang all round with the tread of dancing men and fair-girdled women; and anyone who heard the noise from outside the house would say:

"So somebody has married the queen who was courted by so many. Cruel woman, she had not the heart to keep the great house of the husband she married to the end, until he should come."

That is what they would say; but they did not know how these things were.

Meanwhile Eurynome the housekeeper bathed great-hearted Odysseus in his house and rubbed him with oil and threw a fine cloak and a tunic round him; and Athena shed abundant beauty over his head, making him taller to look at and stronger, and made the hair flow down from his head in curls like the hyacinth flower. As when a man lays gold over silver, a skilled workman whom Hephaestus and Pallas Athena have taught every kind of craft, and the work he turns out is full of grace — so the goddess shed grace on his head and shoulders. And he came out of the bath like the immortals in form.

Then he sat down again on the chair he had got up from, opposite his wife, and said to her: "Strange lady, to you beyond all women the dwellers on Olympus have given a heart that cannot be softened. No other woman would harden her heart as you do and stand apart from a husband who has come to her in the twentieth year to his own country after so much hard suffering. Come then, nurse, strew me a bed, so that I may lie down alone; the heart in her breast is made of iron."

Then wise Penelope answered him: "Strange man, I am not proud in any way, and I do not scorn you, and I am not too greatly astonished either; but I know very well what kind of man you were when you went out of Ithaca on your long-oared ship. Come then, Eurycleia, strew the strong bedstead for him outside the well-built bridal chamber which he made himself. Bring the strong bedstead out there for him and throw bedding on it, fleeces and cloaks and bright coverlets."

So she spoke, and made trial of her husband. But Odysseus spoke to his true-hearted wife in a burst of anger:

"Woman, that is a bitter word you have spoken. Who has moved my bed somewhere else? It would be hard for anybody, however skilled, unless a god came himself and easily set it in another place by his will. Of men there is no mortal alive, not the youngest and strongest, who could easily prise it from its place; because a great token is worked into that fashioned bed, and it was I who built it and nobody else. A bush of long-leafed olive was growing inside the court, strong and vigorous, and thick as a pillar. I built my chamber round it, until I had finished it, with close-set stones, and roofed it over well, and added jointed doors, close-fitting. And then I cut away the leafy branches of the long-leafed olive, and trimmed the trunk from the root, and smoothed it round with the adze, well and cunningly, and made it straight to the line, so making the bed-post; and I bored it all with the auger. Beginning from that, I hewed out my bed until I had finished it, inlaying it with gold and silver and ivory; and I stretched a thong of ox-hide on it, bright with purple. That is the token I declare to you. But I do not know, woman, whether my bedstead is still fast in its place, or whether some man has cut it away from under the olive stump by now and set the bedstead somewhere else."

So he spoke, and her knees were loosened where she sat, and her heart melted, as she knew the sure tokens Odysseus told her. Then she ran straight towards him with a burst of tears, and flung her arms round the neck of Odysseus and kissed his head, and said:

"Do not be vexed with me, Odysseus; in everything else you were always the wisest of men. It was the gods who gave us sorrow, the gods who grudged that we two should stay with each other and enjoy our youth and come to the threshold of old age together. Do not be angry with me now over this, or full of indignation, because when I first saw you I did not welcome you like this. The heart in my breast was always full of dread, in case some man should come and beguile me with his words; there are a great many who plan wicked devices. Argive Helen, daughter of Zeus, would not have lain in love with a man of another people if she had known that the warlike sons of the Achaeans were going to bring her home again to her own dear country. It was a god who drove her to do that shameful thing; she never laid up the thought of that folly in her mind until then, the grievous folly that first brought sorrow on us too. But now, since you have told the clear tokens of our bed, which no mortal has ever seen except you and me alone and one single woman, the daughter of Actor, whom my father gave me before I came here, who kept the doors of our strong bridal chamber — now you convince my heart, unbending as it is."

So she spoke, and roused in him a still greater desire for lamentation; and he wept, holding his dear and true-hearted wife in his arms. And as the sight of land is welcome to men swimming, whose well-built ship Poseidon has smashed on the sea as it was driven on by the wind and the swollen wave, and only a few have escaped from the grey sea to the shore by swimming, and their bodies are thickly crusted with brine, and they set foot on the land gladly, having escaped their trouble — so welcome was her husband to her as she gazed at him, and she could not let her white arms go from his neck at all.

And rosy-fingered Dawn would have risen on their weeping, if the goddess grey-eyed Athena had not thought of something else. She held the long night back at the end of its course, and kept golden-throned Dawn at the streams of Oceanus and would not let her yoke the swift-footed horses that bring light to men, Lampus and Phaethon, the colts that carry the Dawn.

Then Odysseus of the many tricks said to his wife: "Wife, we have not come to the end of all our trials yet; there is still measureless labour ahead, long and hard, which I must carry through to the end. That is what the spirit of Teiresias foretold to me on the day I went down into the house of Hades to ask about the homecoming of my companions and myself. But come, wife, let us go to bed, so that we may take our joy of rest now, lulled by sweet sleep."

Then wise Penelope answered him: "Your bed will be ready for you whenever your heart wants it, since the gods have brought you back to your well-built house and your own country. But since you have thought of this, and a god has put it in your heart, tell me about this trial. I shall learn of it in time to come, I suppose, and it is no worse to know it at once."

And Odysseus of the many tricks answered her and said: "Strange lady, why do you press me so urgently to tell you? Well, I will declare it and hide nothing. Your heart will have no joy of it, and I have none myself. Teiresias told me to go out to a great many cities of men, carrying a well-shaped oar in my hands, until I came to men who know nothing of the sea and do not eat food mixed with salt, and know nothing of ships with purple cheeks, or of the well-shaped oars that serve as wings to ships. And he told me a sign, quite unmistakable, and I will not hide it from you. When another traveller met me and said I had a winnowing fan on my broad shoulder, then he told me to plant my oar in the earth and make fine offerings to lord Poseidon — a ram and a bull and a boar that mates with sows — and to go home and make sacred hecatombs to the immortal gods who hold the wide sky, to each in his order. And death will come to me myself far from the sea, a death so gentle that it will lay me low when I am worn out with a sleek old age, and my people will live in prosperity around me. All this, he said, I should see fulfilled."

Then wise Penelope answered him: "If the gods are really going to bring about a happier old age for you, then there is hope that you will find an escape from your troubles."

So they talked to one another; and meanwhile Eurynome and the nurse made the bed ready with soft coverlets by the light of blazing torches. And when they had busily spread the strong-built bedstead, the old nurse went back to her room to lie down, and Eurynome, the woman of the bedchamber, led them on their way to the bed with a torch in her hands; and when she had brought them to the bridal chamber she went back. And then the two of them gladly came to the place of the bed that had been theirs of old.

But Telemachus and the herdsman and the swineherd stopped their feet from dancing, and stopped the women, and lay down to sleep themselves through the shadowy halls.

But when the two of them had had their fill of the joy of love, they took delight in talking, each telling the other. She, that lovely woman, told of everything she had endured in the halls, looking at the destructive crowd of the suitors, who killed so many beasts for her sake, cattle and fine sheep, and drew off a great store of wine from the jars. And Odysseus, born of Zeus, recounted all the troubles he had brought on men, and all the labour he had endured himself in his sorrow; and she was glad to listen, and sweet sleep did not fall on her eyelids until he had told the whole tale.

He began by telling how at the first he overcame the Cicones, and then came to the rich land of the Lotus-eaters, and everything the Cyclops did, and how he made him pay the price for his mighty companions whom the Cyclops had eaten and shown no pity. Then how he came to Aeolus, who received him with a ready heart and sent him on his way; but it was not yet his fate to come to his own dear country, and the storm wind caught him up again and carried him over the teeming water, groaning heavily. Next how he came to Telepylus of the Laestrygonians, who destroyed his ships and his well-greaved companions, every one of them, and Odysseus alone escaped in his black ship. Then he told of all the tricks and craftiness of Circe, and how he had gone in his benched ship to the dank house of Hades to consult the spirit of Theban Teiresias, and had seen all his companions and the mother who bore him and nursed him when he was a child. And how he heard the voice of the Sirens, who sing without stopping, and had come to the Wandering Rocks, and to dread Charybdis, and to Scylla, whom no men had ever got past unhurt. Then how his companions killed the cattle of Helios, and how Zeus, who thunders on high, struck his fast ship with a flaming thunderbolt, and his fine companions all died together while he alone escaped the evil fates. And how he came to the island of Ogygia and to the nymph Calypso, who kept him there in her hollow caves, wanting him for a husband, and tended him, and said she would make him immortal and ageless all his days; and yet she could never persuade the heart in his breast. Then how he came after many labours to the Phaeacians, who honoured him heartily as if he were a god and sent him in a ship to his own dear country, after giving him stores of bronze and gold and clothing. That was the end of the tale he told, when sweet sleep, that loosens the limbs of men, leapt on him and loosened the cares of his heart.

Then the goddess grey-eyed Athena thought of something else again. When she judged that the heart of Odysseus had had its fill of his wife and of sleep, at once she roused golden-throned Dawn from Oceanus to bring light to men; and Odysseus got up from his soft bed and gave his wife his charge:

"Wife, by now we have had our fill of many trials, you and I — you here, mourning over my troubled journey home, while Zeus and the other gods bound me fast in sorrows far from my own country, eager as I was to come back. But now that we have both come to the bed we wanted, look after the wealth that I have inside the halls. As for the flocks the insolent suitors have wasted, I shall get a great many myself as plunder, and the Achaeans will give others, until they fill all my folds. But I am going to my well-wooded farm to see my noble father, who is in bitter distress for my sake. And on you, wife, I lay this charge, wise as you are. As soon as the sun rises the report will go about concerning the suitors I killed in the halls. So go up to your upper room with your women and stay there. Look at no man, and ask no question."

He spoke, and girded his beautiful armour about his shoulders, and roused Telemachus and the herdsman and the swineherd, and told them all to take weapons of war in their hands. They did not disobey, but clothed themselves in bronze and opened the doors and went out, and Odysseus led the way. There was light over the earth by now, but Athena hid them in night and led them quickly out of the city.
