Book Twenty-Two

Then Odysseus of the many tricks stripped off his rags and sprang to the great threshold with the bow and the quiver full of arrows, and poured the swift arrows out right there in front of his feet, and spoke among the suitors:

"So this decisive contest is over at last. Now I will see whether I can hit another mark, which no man has ever hit yet, and whether Apollo grants me the glory."

He spoke, and aimed a bitter arrow at Antinous. He was on the point of lifting a fine goblet to his lips, a two-eared cup of gold, and was handling it at that moment to drink the wine; and death was not in his thoughts. For who among men sitting at meat could think that one man among so many, however strong, would bring evil death and black fate on him?

But Odysseus took aim and struck him with an arrow in the throat, and the point went clean out through the tender neck. He sank to one side, and the cup fell from his hand as he was struck, and a thick jet of human blood came up at once through his nostrils; and he quickly thrust the table away from him with a kick of his foot and spilled all the food on the floor, and the bread and the roast meat were fouled.

Then the suitors broke into uproar through the halls when they saw the man fall, and they sprang up from their high seats, driven in fear through the hall, looking everywhere along the well-built walls; but nowhere was there a shield or a mighty spear to seize. And they railed at Odysseus with angry words:

"Stranger, you shoot at men to your own cost. You will never take part in any other contest; your utter destruction is certain now. You have killed a man who was far the best of the young men in Ithaca; so vultures will devour you here."

That is what each man said, because they thought he had not killed the man on purpose; and in their folly they did not know this, that the cords of destruction had been made fast over every one of them.

Then Odysseus of the many tricks gave them a black look from under his brows and answered them: "You dogs, you thought I would never come home again from the land of the Trojans, and so you wasted my house and lay with the serving-women by force, and courted my wife by stealth while I was still alive, with no fear of the gods who hold the wide sky, or of the indignation of men that was to come afterwards. Now the cords of destruction have been made fast over every one of you."

So he spoke, and pale fear seized them all, and every man looked about to see how he might escape utter destruction. Only Eurymachus answered him:

"If you really are Odysseus of Ithaca, come home again, what you say is just about everything the Achaeans have done — many deeds of wanton folly in your halls and many in the fields. But the man who was to blame for all of it lies dead now, Antinous. He set these things going, not so much out of desire or need of the marriage but with another purpose, which the son of Cronos did not bring about for him: that he might be king himself in the land of settled Ithaca, and might lie in wait for your son and kill him. Now he has been killed, as he deserved. But spare your own people; and we will go about the land afterwards and get you recompense for everything that has been drunk and eaten in your halls, and each man will bring the worth of twenty oxen for himself in requital, and pay you back in bronze and gold until your heart is warmed. Until then nobody could blame you for being angry."

Then Odysseus of the many tricks gave him a black look from under his brows and answered him: "Eurymachus, not even if you gave me all your fathers' inheritance in requital, everything you have now, and added other wealth to it from wherever you could — not even then would I hold my hands from killing from now on, until the suitors had paid the full price for all their wrongdoing. It lies before you now to fight in open fight or to run, if any man can avoid death and the fates; but I think a good many of you will not escape utter destruction."

So he spoke, and their knees were loosened where they stood and their hearts melted. And Eurymachus spoke among them a second time:

"Friends, this man will not hold his invincible hands. Now that he has got the polished bow and the quiver, he will shoot from the smooth threshold until he has killed us all. So let us think about fighting. Draw your swords and hold the tables in front of you against the arrows that bring swift death, and let us all go at him in a body, hoping we may thrust him from the threshold and the doorway and get out through the city, and raise the alarm quickly; then this fellow would soon have shot his last."

With that he drew his sharp bronze sword, two-edged, and sprang at Odysseus with a terrible cry. But at the same instant noble Odysseus let an arrow fly and struck him on the breast beside the nipple, and fixed the swift shaft in his liver. And Eurymachus let the sword fall out of his hand to the ground, and writhed over the table and bowed and fell, and spilled the food and the two-handled cup on the floor. He beat the earth with his brow in the agony of his soul, and spurned and shook the chair with both his feet; and a mist was shed over his eyes.

Then Amphinomus went at glorious Odysseus, rushing straight at him, and had drawn his sharp sword, hoping Odysseus might give way in front of him from the door. But Telemachus was too quick for him, and cast and struck him from behind with his bronze-tipped spear between the shoulders, and drove it through his breast; and he fell with a thud and struck the ground full with his forehead. And Telemachus sprang back, leaving the long spear where it was, fixed in Amphinomus, because he was badly afraid that as he tried to draw the long spear out one of the Achaeans might rush at him and stab him with his sword, or deal him a blow as he stooped over the corpse.

So he started to run and came quickly to his dear father, and stood beside him and spoke winged words: "Father, now I will bring you a shield and two spears and a helmet all of bronze, well fitted to the temples; and when I come back I will arm myself, and give armour to the swineherd and to the herdsman too; it is better to be clothed in armour."

Then Odysseus of the many tricks answered him and said: "Run and bring them, while I still have arrows to defend me, in case they thrust me from the door, alone as I am."

So he spoke, and Telemachus did as his dear father said, and went to the store-chamber where the glorious arms were kept. He took four shields from there and eight spears and four bronze helmets with thick horse-hair plumes, and carried them out and came quickly to his dear father. Then first of all he girded the bronze about his own body, and the two slaves put the beautiful armour on in the same way, and took their stand on either side of Odysseus, the wise and cunning-minded.

And he, as long as he had arrows to defend him, kept aiming and striking the suitors one by one in his house, and they fell thick and fast. But when the arrows failed the prince as he shot, he leaned the bow against the doorpost of the well-built hall and let it stand against the bright entrance wall. And he put a four-fold shield about his shoulders and set a well-made helmet with a horse-hair plume on his mighty head, and the plume waved terribly above him; and he took two mighty spears tipped with bronze.

Now there was a certain postern door in the well-built wall, and along the topmost level of the threshold of the well-built hall there was a way into a passage, and well-fitting folding doors closed it. Odysseus told the good swineherd to watch this postern, taking his stand close by, because there was only one way to reach it.

Then Agelaus spoke among the suitors and declared his word to them all: "Friends, will not one of us climb up by the postern door and tell the people, so that the alarm may be raised at once? Then this fellow would soon have shot his last."

Then Melanthius the goatherd answered him: "It cannot be done, Agelaus, brought up by Zeus; the fine door of the court is terribly close, and the mouth of the passage is hard. One man could bar the way for everybody, if he were brave. But come, let me bring you arms to put on out of the store-room, because I think it is inside there, and nowhere else, that Odysseus and his glorious son have laid the arms."

With that Melanthius the goatherd climbed up by the steps of the hall to the store-rooms of Odysseus. He took twelve shields from there, and as many spears, and as many bronze helmets with thick horse-hair plumes, and went off and quickly brought them and gave them to the suitors.

Then the knees of Odysseus were loosened and his heart melted, when he saw them putting armour on and brandishing long spears in their hands, and his task looked great to him. And he quickly spoke winged words to Telemachus: "Telemachus, one of the women in the halls is rousing an evil battle against us — or perhaps it is Melanthius."

Then clear-headed Telemachus answered him: "Father, it is I myself who am at fault in this, and nobody else is to blame, because I left the close-fitting door of the store-room open; and their watcher was better than I was. Go now, good Eumaeus, and shut the door of the store-room, and see whether it is one of the women who does this, or Melanthius, son of Dolius, as I suspect."

So they talked to one another. But Melanthius the goatherd went to the store-room again to bring back beautiful armour; and the good swineherd noticed him, and said at once to Odysseus, who was near:

"Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of the many tricks, there is that pestilent fellow again, the one we suspected ourselves, going to the store-room. Tell me truly, shall I kill him if I prove the better man, or bring him here to you, so that the fellow may pay for the many crimes he has planned in your house?"

Then Odysseus of the many tricks answered him and said: "Telemachus and I will keep the lordly suitors inside the hall, however fierce they are; but you two bend his feet and his arms up behind him and throw him into the store-room and tie boards behind his back; and then make a twisted rope fast to his body and hoist him up the tall pillar until you bring him near the roof-beams, so that he stays alive a long while and suffers grievous torment."

So he spoke, and they listened readily and obeyed. They went out to the store-room, unseen by the man inside. He was searching for armour in the innermost part of the store-room, and the two of them lay in wait, standing on either side of the doorposts. And when Melanthius the goatherd was about to cross the threshold, carrying a fine helmet in one hand and a broad old shield in the other, flecked with rust — the shield of lord Laertes, which he used to carry in his youth, but which was laid by now with the seams of its straps loosened — then the two of them sprang on him and seized him. They dragged him in by the hair and flung him down on the ground in bitter terror, and bound his feet and hands with galling bonds, tying them firmly behind his back, as the son of Laertes told them, much-enduring noble Odysseus; and they made a twisted rope fast to his body and hoisted him up the tall pillar until they brought him near the roof-beams.

Then you mocked him, Eumaeus the swineherd, and said: "Now you will keep watch the whole night through, Melanthius, lying on a soft bed, as suits you; and you will not fail to notice the early Dawn, golden-throned, as she comes out from the streams of Oceanus, at the hour when you usually drive your she-goats for the suitors, to prepare a feast in the halls."

So he was left there, stretched in that deadly bond; and the two of them put their armour on and closed the bright door and went to Odysseus, the wise and cunning-minded. And there they stood, breathing fury — those on the threshold only four, while those inside the hall were many and brave.

Then Athena, daughter of Zeus, came near them, like Mentor in shape and voice; and Odysseus saw her and was glad, and said: "Mentor, keep off ruin, and remember me, your dear companion, who often did you good. You are of the same age as I am."

So he spoke, though he believed it was Athena, the rouser of armies. But the suitors on the other side shouted aloud in the hall, and Agelaus, son of Damastor, was the first to rebuke Athena:

"Mentor, do not let Odysseus beguile you with his words into fighting against the suitors and bringing help to himself. This is how our will is going to be brought about, I think: when we have killed these men, father and son, you shall be killed with them afterwards, for the kind of thing you mean to do in these halls; you shall pay the price with your own head. And when we have stripped you of your strength with the sword, all the possessions you have indoors and in the fields we will mingle with those of Odysseus, and we will not let your sons or your daughters live in your halls, or your faithful wife go about the city of Ithaca."

So he spoke, and Athena grew angrier still at heart, and rebuked Odysseus with angry words: "Odysseus, you no longer have the steady might or the courage that was yours when you fought the Trojans for nine years without stopping for high-born Helen of the white arms, and killed a great many men in dreadful fighting, and the broad-wayed city of Priam was taken by your counsel. How is it that now, when you have come to your house and your own possessions, you shrink and wail at playing the man, and against suitors? Come here, friend, and take your stand beside me and watch what I do, so that you may know what kind of man Mentor, son of Alcimus, is at repaying kindness in the middle of the enemy."

She spoke, but did not give him the strength to turn the course of the battle altogether; she was still testing the might and the courage of Odysseus and his glorious son. And for herself, she flew up to the roof-beam of the smoky hall and sat there in the likeness of a swallow, to look on.

Now the suitors were urged on by Agelaus, son of Damastor, and by Eurynomus, and Amphimedon and Demoptolemus and Peisander, son of Polyctor, and wise Polybus; because these were far the best in ability of all the suitors who were still alive and fighting for their lives; the rest the bow and the swiftly falling arrows had laid low by now.

And Agelaus spoke among them and declared his word to them all: "Friends, this man will hold his invincible hands at last. Look, Mentor has gone off from him and has only uttered empty boasts, and they are left alone there at the outer doors. So do not hurl your long spears at them all at once. Come, let six of you throw first, and hope that Zeus grants Odysseus to be hit and that we win the glory. Of the rest there is no need to think, once he has fallen."

So he spoke, and they all hurled their spears as he told them, eagerly. But Athena made them all come to nothing. One man struck the doorpost of the well-built hall, another the close-fitting door, another's ash spear, heavy with bronze, struck the wall. But when they had avoided the suitors' spears, much-enduring noble Odysseus spoke first among his men:

"Friends, now I give the word that we too should cast our spears into the crowd of the suitors, who mean to kill us on top of all their earlier wrongs."

So he spoke, and they all hurled their sharp spears with sure aim. Odysseus struck Demoptolemus, Telemachus struck Euryades, the swineherd struck Elatus, and the herdsman of the cattle killed Peisander. So all of these at the same moment bit the vast floor with their teeth, and the suitors drew back to the innermost part of the hall. And the others sprang forward and drew their spears out of the dead bodies.

Then the suitors hurled their sharp spears again, eagerly; but Athena made them come to nothing, many as they were. One man struck the doorpost of the well-built hall, another the close-fitting door, another's ash spear, heavy with bronze, struck the wall. But Amphimedon struck Telemachus on the hand by the wrist, a grazing blow, and the bronze tore the surface of the skin. And Ctesippus grazed the shoulder of Eumaeus with his long spear, over his shield, but the spear flew on and fell on the ground.

Then once more Odysseus, the wise and cunning-minded, and his company hurled their sharp spears into the crowd of the suitors; and again Odysseus, the sacker of cities, struck Eurydamas, and Telemachus struck Amphimedon, and the swineherd struck Polybus, and then the herdsman of the cattle struck Ctesippus in the breast, and boasted over him:

"Son of Polytherses, you lover of abuse, never speak big again, giving way to folly, but leave the matter to the gods, since they are far mightier. This is my gift of welcome to match the hoof you gave godlike Odysseus lately, when he went begging through the house."

So spoke the herdsman of the sleek cattle. And Odysseus wounded the son of Damastor in close fighting with a thrust of his long spear, and Telemachus wounded Leiocritus, son of Evenor, with a spear-thrust full in the groin, and drove the bronze clean through; and he fell headlong and struck the ground full with his forehead.

Then Athena held her aegis up on high from the roof, the bane of mortals, and the minds of the suitors were panic-stricken, and they fled through the halls like a herd of cattle that the darting gadfly falls on and drives along in the spring season, when the long days come. And as vultures with crooked talons and curved beaks come out of the mountains and dart on smaller birds, which scour the plain flying low beneath the clouds, and the vultures pounce on them and kill them, and they have no defence and no way of escape, and men are glad at the chase — so those others set on the suitors and struck them left and right through the hall. And a hideous groaning rose from it as heads were struck, and the whole floor swam with blood.

But Leiodes rushed forward and took hold of the knees of Odysseus and made his plea to him and spoke winged words: "I beg you by your knees, Odysseus; respect me and have pity. I declare to you that I never wronged one of the women in your halls by a wanton word or deed. I tried to check the other suitors when any of them would do such things. But they would not listen to me and hold their hands from evil, and so through their wanton folly they have met a cruel doom. And I, who was the soothsayer among them and have done no wrong, shall be laid low the same as they are; so true is it that there is no gratitude afterwards for good deeds done."

Then Odysseus of the many tricks gave him a black look from under his brows and answered him: "If you really declare yourself the soothsayer among these men, you must often have prayed in the halls that the outcome of a joyful homecoming should be kept far from me, and that my dear wife should go with you and bear you children. So you shall not escape a grievous death."

With that he seized in his strong hand a sword that lay near, which Agelaus had let fall to the ground when he was killed, and struck him full on the neck with it. And his head was mingled with the dust while he was still speaking.

Now the son of Terpes, the singer, was still trying to escape black fate — Phemius, who sang for the suitors because they made him. He stood with the clear-toned lyre in his hands near the postern door, and was divided in his mind whether to slip out of the hall and sit down by the well-built altar of great Zeus, the god of the court, where Laertes and Odysseus had burned so many thigh-pieces of oxen, or whether to rush forward and take hold of the knees of Odysseus in supplication. And as he turned it over, this seemed the better course, to take hold of the knees of Odysseus, son of Laertes. So he laid the hollow lyre on the ground between the mixing-bowl and the silver-studded chair, and rushed forward himself and took Odysseus by the knees and made his plea to him and spoke winged words:

"I beg you by your knees, Odysseus; respect me and have pity. Sorrow will come to you yourself afterwards if you kill a singer, one who sings to gods and men. I am self-taught, and the god has planted every kind of song in my heart; and I am fit to sing to you as to a god. So do not be eager to cut my throat. And Telemachus will bear witness to this too, your dear son, that it was through no will or wish of mine that I used to come to your house to sing to the suitors at their feasts; they were far more and stronger, and they brought me here by force."

So he spoke, and the strong young Telemachus heard him, and quickly said to his father, who was near: "Hold your hand, and do not wound this guiltless man with the sword. And let us save the herald Medon too, who always cared for me in our house when I was a child — unless Philoetius has killed him already, or the swineherd, or he ran into you as you raged through the house."

So he spoke, and Medon, who had good sense, heard him, because he was lying crouched under a chair and had wrapped himself in the skin of an ox, newly flayed, trying to avoid black fate. He got up at once from under the chair and stripped the ox-hide off, and rushed forward and took Telemachus by the knees and made his plea and spoke winged words:

"Friend, here I am. Hold your hand and tell your father to hold his, in case in the greatness of his strength he harms me with the sharp bronze in his anger against the suitors, who wasted his possessions in the halls and in their folly gave you no honour at all."

But Odysseus of the many tricks smiled and said to him: "Take heart; he has delivered you and saved you, so that you may know in your heart and tell another man too how much better the doing of good deeds is than of evil. But go out of the halls and sit down outside in the court, away from the slaughter, you and the singer of many songs, until I have finished everything I have to do in the house."

So he spoke, and the two of them went out of the hall and sat down by the altar of great Zeus, looking about on every side, expecting death all the time.

And Odysseus too looked about all through his house, to see if any man were still alive and hiding there, trying to avoid black fate. But he found every one of them fallen in the blood and the dust — the whole host of them, like fish that fishermen have drawn out of the grey sea in the meshes of their net onto the curving beach, and they all lie heaped on the sand longing for the waves of the sea, and the bright sun takes their life away: so the suitors lay now, heaped on one another.

Then Odysseus of the many tricks said to Telemachus: "Telemachus, go and call me the nurse Eurycleia, so that I can tell her what is in my mind."

So he spoke, and Telemachus did as his dear father said, and shook the door and said to Eurycleia: "Up and come here, old woman, who have charge of all our serving-women in the halls. Come; my father is calling you, so that he can tell you something."

So he spoke, and her word stayed unwinged. She opened the doors of the stately hall and came out, and Telemachus led the way in front of her.

There she found Odysseus among the bodies of the dead, all fouled with blood and filth, like a lion that comes from feeding on an ox of the farmyard, with all his breast and both his cheeks stained with blood, and he is terrible to look at: that was how Odysseus was fouled, his feet and his hands above.

But she, when she saw the corpses and the great welter of blood, was ready to raise loud cries of joy, seeing what a thing had been done. But Odysseus stopped her and checked her in her eagerness, and spoke to her with winged words:

"Rejoice in your own heart, old woman, but restrain yourself and do not cry out aloud. It is an unholy thing to boast over slain men. The fate of the gods destroyed these men, and their own reckless deeds; they honoured nobody on earth, evil or good, whoever came among them, and so by their wanton folly they brought a shameful death on themselves. But come, name over to me the women in the halls, which of them dishonour me and which are innocent."

Then his dear nurse Eurycleia answered him: "Then I will tell you the whole truth, my child. You have fifty serving-women in the halls, women we have taught to do their work, to card the wool and bear the lot of slaves. Of these, twelve in all have set their feet in the way of shamelessness, and take no notice of me or of Penelope herself. And Telemachus is only newly grown to manhood, and his mother would not let him rule over the serving-women. But let me go up to the bright upper room and take word to your wife, on whom some god has sent sleep."

Then Odysseus of the many tricks answered her and said: "Do not wake her yet; tell those women to come here, the ones who have contrived shameful deeds in the past."

So he spoke, and the old woman went out through the hall to bring the women word and tell them to come. And Odysseus called Telemachus and the herdsman and the swineherd to him, and spoke winged words to them:

"Begin carrying the dead bodies out now, and tell the women to help you; and after that clean the beautiful chairs and the tables with water and porous sponges. And when you have set the whole house in order, lead the women out of the well-built hall to a place between the dome and the good fence of the court, and strike them down there with your long swords, until you have taken the life out of all of them, and they forget the love they had at the suitors' bidding, when they lay with them in secret."

So he spoke, and the women came all in a crowd, wailing terribly and shedding big tears. First they carried out the bodies of the dead and set them down under the portico of the well-fenced court, propping them one against another; and Odysseus gave the orders himself and hurried the work on, and they carried the bodies out because they had to. Then they cleaned the beautiful high seats and the tables with water and porous sponges. And Telemachus and the herdsman and the swineherd scraped the floor of the well-built house with hoes, and the women carried the scrapings out and threw them out of doors.

But when they had set the whole hall in order, they led the women out of the well-built hall to a place between the dome and the good fence of the court, and shut them into a narrow space, where there was no possible way of escape. Then clear-headed Telemachus was the first to speak to the others:

"Let it not be by any clean death that I take the lives of these women, who have poured reproaches on my own head and on my mother, and used to lie with the suitors."

So he spoke, and tied the cable of a dark-prowed ship to a great pillar and flung it round the dome, stretching it high up so that none of them could reach the ground with her feet. And as long-winged thrushes or doves fall into a snare set in a thicket as they try to reach their resting-place, and the bed that welcomes them is hateful — so the women held their heads in a row, and nooses were laid round the necks of all of them, so that they should die most pitifully. And they writhed a little while with their feet, but not long.

Then they led Melanthius out through the doorway and the court, and cut off his nostrils and his ears with the pitiless bronze, and drew out his vitals for the dogs to eat raw, and cut off his hands and his feet in their furious anger.

After that they washed their own hands and feet and went into the house to Odysseus, and the work was done. But Odysseus said to his dear nurse Eurycleia: "Bring sulphur, old woman, to cleanse from pollution, and bring me fire, so that I can purge the hall. And tell Penelope to come here with her women, and order all the women in the house to come."

Then his dear nurse Eurycleia answered him: "Yes, my child, all this you have said rightly. But come, let me bring you a cloak and a tunic for clothes, and do not stand like this in the halls with your broad shoulders wrapped in rags; that would give cause for blame."

Then Odysseus of the many tricks answered her: "First of all let a fire be made for me in the hall."

So he spoke, and his dear nurse Eurycleia did not disobey, but brought fire and sulphur; and Odysseus purged the hall thoroughly, and the house, and the court.

Then the old woman went back through the fine house of Odysseus to bring the women word and tell them to come; and they came out of their hall with torches in their hands. They crowded round Odysseus and embraced him, and clasped and kissed his head and his shoulders and his hands in loving welcome; and a sweet longing seized him to weep and wail, because in his heart he knew every one of them.
