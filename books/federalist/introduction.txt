The Constitution was signed at Philadelphia on 17 September 1787 and then had to be sold. It would take effect only when nine of the thirteen states ratified it, each through a specially elected convention, and New York was one of the states most likely to say no. Its governor was against it. So Alexander Hamilton organised a newspaper campaign, recruited James Madison and John Jay, and on 27 October 1787 the first of these essays appeared in a New York paper addressed to the people of the state. Eighty-five followed over the next ten months, at times three or four a week.

All of them are signed Publius. The pseudonym was standard practice in eighteenth-century newspapers and it was also a claim: Publius Valerius was one of the founders of the Roman republic. Hamilton wrote about fifty-one of the papers, Madison twenty-nine, and Jay five, his share cut short by illness. The three did not agree with one another about everything, and Hamilton and Madison became political opponents within a few years of writing this. Authorship of a dozen papers, numbers 49 to 58 and 62 and 63, was disputed between them for more than a century; this edition follows the attribution to Madison, which is where a statistical study of their word habits came out in the 1960s. Each paper here carries its number, its date, and the name.

They are arguing about a specific document, and the document is short and still in force. Reading the Constitution first, or keeping it open alongside, makes an enormous difference, because much of what looks like abstract theory is a defence of a particular clause against a particular objection that had been printed the week before. It also helps to know that there was no Bill of Rights yet. Hamilton devotes the eighty-fourth paper to arguing that one is unnecessary and would be dangerous, an argument the country did not accept.

These were written fast, for a newspaper, by men with a deadline, and the quality is uneven in the way that implies. The famous ones are famous for good reason. Number 10 is Madison on faction, and the claim that a large republic is safer than a small one because it contains more competing interests. Number 51 is where the separation of powers is explained as a machine that uses ambition to check ambition, and where he observes that if men were angels no government would be necessary. Number 78 is Hamilton on why judges hold office for life and why they must be able to set aside a law that violates the Constitution.

New York ratified in July 1788, by thirty votes to twenty-seven, after the ninth state had already made the question moot. Paine's Common Sense and Locke's Two Treatises of Government are in this collection.
