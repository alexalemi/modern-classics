EURIPIDES: THE NINETEEN PLAYS
Translation strategy. From the GREEK (Perseus' text), with Coleridge's
prose (1891, as Perseus carries it) under reference/ as a per-file crib
ONLY. The third and last volume of the drama shelf: READ
sophocles/text_analysis.txt AND aeschylus/text_analysis.txt FIRST. The
form ruling, the locked vocabulary, the Verne rule and the whole method
carry over; this file records what is DIFFERENT about Euripides.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
1. WHY THIS BOOK -- THE HIGHEST ARCHAISM SCORE IN THE PROJECT
--------------------------------------------------------------------
Screened 2026-09-09 with screen.py:

    Coleridge, Medea (prose, 1891)          arch 60.05
    Murray, Medea (rhymed verse, 1910)      arch 48.24
    Coleridge, Hippolytus (prose)           arch 44.11
    Murray, Trojan Women (verse)            arch 39.96
    Sophocles (Campbell, verse)             arch 39.10   <- the old record
    Augustine (Pusey)                       arch 35.9

Coleridge's Medea at 60 archaisms per thousand words is the highest
score ever measured here -- and it is PROSE. A reader who downloads the
free Euripides gets "thou" in every second line of a man who wrote the
plainest, most conversational Greek of the three tragedians, the one
Aristophanes mocked for putting everyday speech on the tragic stage. So
the nights/ case again: the costume is the translator's, none of it is
in the Greek, and removing it is not simplification.

NOTE ON THE CRIB: Perseus' Coleridge is NOT uniform. Some plays carry
his 1891 text (Medea 60, Hippolytus 44); others were silently modernised
by Perseus' editors and measure 0.0 (Bacchae, Orestes, Hecuba). Do not
be reassured by a clean crib file -- it is the same Victorian prose with
the thou's taken out, still literal and still stiff, and it is prose
throughout, so it is no witness to the form.

2. THE FORM -- DERIVED FROM THE METRE, NOT THE MARKUP
--------------------------------------------------------------------
  SPOKEN, iambic trimeter and trochaic tetrameter  -> MODERN PROSE
  SUNG, lyric metres, anapaests, refrains          -> VERSE, tab-indented

The ruling is Alex's, from sophocles/. What changed is the WITNESS. For
Sophocles the Perseus division labels were right; for Aeschylus they
were wrong in ~30 places and corrected by hand; for Euripides they are
wrong in ~380 places, always the same way (a "lyric" or "anapests"
<div> runs on over the spoken scene after it). So prep.classify decides
each speech from the SYLLABLE COUNT (trimeter is 12-13 a line,
tetrameter 15-16, lyric 6-11) with the label only as tie-breaker, and
three KIND_FIXES cover the chanted metres it cannot know (elegiacs,
hexameters). See euripides/prep.py's docstring and metre.py.
AGENTS FOLLOW chapters/'s [Sung]/[Spoken] MARKERS EXACTLY. If one looks
wrong, REPORT it in the notes; never override it. The Aeschylus lesson:
three agents flagging the same thing is what got prep fixed.

EURIPIDES SINGS DIFFERENTLY. Less chorus than Aeschylus and more ACTOR'S
SONG: the monody (Ion sweeping the temple, Creusa's aria, Hecuba,
Electra at the well, Iphigenia's lament, the Phrygian slave's whole
scene in Orestes) and the lyric duet between actors. These are the
arias of the Athenian stage and the audience went for them. Set them as
verse with real momentum, an image a line, first person, never a crib
in short lines. The choral odes are often DETACHED from the plot (the
escapist ode: "let me be a bird", the ode on a myth two removes away);
render that detachment, do not tie them back to the action.

3. VOICE
--------------------------------------------------------------------
Euripides is the modern one, and the trap is to make him solemn like
Aeschylus or clean like Sophocles. He is neither.

  - HIS PEOPLE TALK LIKE PEOPLE. Nurses, farmers, slaves, a Phrygian
    eunuch, a herdsman -- and kings who argue like litigants. The
    register is conversational Attic and the English must be
    conversational: contractions are fine in speech, short sentences,
    people interrupting themselves. Aristophanes called it "the
    language of the marketplace" as an insult; take it as a
    description.
  - THE DEBATE (agon). Nearly every play has a formal two-speech
    argument -- Medea and Jason, Hecuba and Helen, Pentheus and
    Dionysus, Clytemnestra and Electra over the murder -- and both
    sides are GOOD. Jason is not a fool; Helen's defence is clever
    enough that Hecuba has to work. Never make the loser stupid (the
    sophocles rule, at maximum).
  - THE RHETORIC IS DELIBERATE. Characters make speeches they know are
    speeches; Medea says "I will show you where the argument goes". Keep
    the visible joints -- "first... and second... and then there is
    this" -- because the self-consciousness is the point.
  - WOMEN AND SLAVES ARE THE CENTRE, not the margin: Medea, Phaedra,
    Hecuba, Andromache, Electra, Alcestis, Iphigenia, Creusa, Agave.
    Give them the full argumentative weight the Greek gives them; the
    "misogynist" passages (Hippolytus' speech, Jason's wish that
    children could be got without women) are CHARACTERS' claims and
    stand as such, unhedged and unwinked at.
  - THE GODS ARE UNRELIABLE and the plays say so. Apollo in Ion,
    Aphrodite in Hippolytus, Athena at the end of the Suppliants,
    Dionysus in the Bacchae: characters accuse them, and the accusation
    is often the last word. Render it flat.
  - THE MESSENGER SPEECHES are the best set-pieces in Greek drama --
    Pentheus torn apart, the death of Glauce and Creon, Hippolytus'
    chariot, the sacrifice at Aulis, Neoptolemus at Delphi. Past tense,
    fast, exact, physical, at full length.
  - THE ENDINGS ARE OFTEN ABRUPT OR MACHINE-MADE (the god on the crane,
    the tag lines the chorus repeats from play to play). Keep them as
    written: the deus ex machina speaks in the flat register of a
    decree, and the repeated closing tags ("Many are the shapes of what
    the gods do...") recur VERBATIM across plays (Alcestis, Medea,
    Andromache, Helen, Bacchae) -- the nights/ rule for frame formulas.
  - CYCLOPS IS A COMEDY. The only complete satyr play: Silenus and the
    satyrs are drunk, cowardly and obscene, and Odysseus is a straight
    man. Play it for laughs; the Verne rule covers the obscenity.
  - RHESUS may not be his. Translate it as the play it is: a night
    piece, soldiers and sentries, and the Muse's lament.

4. LOCKED VOCABULARY
--------------------------------------------------------------------
All of sophocles/ section 4 and aeschylus/ section 4 carry over (ate
RUIN, hubris OUTRAGE, dike JUSTICE, philos the people you owe, sophron
"keep yourself in hand", daimon never demon, Hades a name, dmoe SLAVE).
Added:
  sophia/sophos    CLEVER as often as wise -- Medea's sophia is what
                   Creon fears; keep the accusation.
  tolma            "daring"/"the nerve to" -- the word for what Medea
                   does; not "audacity".
  gnome            judgement/opinion; the agon turns on it.
  tyrannos         "king" where it is neutral (Euripides uses it of
                   any monarch), "tyrant" only where it is an
                   accusation. The reverse of the Prometheus lock; say
                   which in the notes when it is a close call.
  xenos            stranger AND guest AND host -- Alcestis and Helen
                   turn on the obligation; keep it visible.
  barbaros         "barbarian" kept, and in Euripides the word is
                   usually an argument (Medea, Andromache, Helen,
                   Iphigenia in Tauris): Greeks call foreigners it and
                   the plays show Greeks behaving worse.
  Names: familiar forms (Medea, Jason, Hippolytus, Phaedra, Theseus,
  Hecuba, Andromache, Helen, Menelaus, Clytemnestra, Iphigenia, Orestes,
  Electra, Pentheus, Dionysus, Agave, Cadmus). Bacchus/Bromius/Dithyrambos
  in the Bacchae are cult titles of Dionysus and are RESOLVED to
  "Dionysus" except where the title is the point (the chorus's invocation
  "Bromius" once, glossed). Loxias -> Apollo; Cypris -> Aphrodite (except
  in Hippolytus, where the goddess names herself and "Cypris" recurs as
  the play's word for love: keep it there, glossed once). Phoebus ->
  Apollo. Zeus's titles resolved as in aeschylus/.

5. STAGE DIRECTIONS ARE COLERIDGE'S, NOT EURIPIDES'
--------------------------------------------------------------------
As in the other two volumes: the Greek carries none, they are ours,
sparse and factual, own paragraph in parentheses, a full sentence so
they do not render as headings. The machine (deus ex machina) gets one:
"(Athena appears above the house.)"

6. THE VERNE RULE
--------------------------------------------------------------------
No editorial note anywhere in the volume. Translated in full, unhedged:
  - MEDEA kills her children and is right about Jason; the play gives
    her the chariot and the last word. Her "I know what I am about to
    do is evil" speech at full length and full contradiction.
  - HIPPOLYTUS' speech wishing women did not exist; Phaedra's letter;
    the Nurse's argument that everyone does it.
  - THE SACRIFICE OF IPHIGENIA is consented to, in a speech that a
    modern reader will find monstrous and that Aristotle thought out
    of character. It stands.
  - HECUBA blinds Polymestor and kills his children; the play does not
    condemn her and neither does the edition.
  - PENTHEUS torn apart by his mother, who carries his head on stage
    thinking it is a lion's, and the slow recognition. Full length.
  - The slavery throughout -- the Trojan Women is a play about women
    being allotted as slaves and says so; Andromache is a slave and a
    concubine; the Phrygian in Orestes is a eunuch slave played for
    laughs. None of it softened. "barbarian" kept.
  - HERACLES kills his wife and children in a madness a goddess sends;
    Theseus' argument that he should go on living anyway is the play.
  - The satyrs' obscenity in Cyclops, including the jokes about
    Ganymede and about raping Helen.
  - Aristophanes' charge that Euripides was an atheist: the plays'
    accusations against the gods stand as the characters make them.

7. RATIO
--------------------------------------------------------------------
Coleridge's prose is 227,000 English against 148,719 Greek, 1.53 --
LOWER than Smyth's 1.70 on Aeschylus, because Euripides' Greek is
plainer and unpacks less. Target 1.50-1.75 per file; check.py
1.40-1.90; verify --min-ratio 1.35 --max-ratio 1.90. UNDER 1.40 MEANS
SUMMARISING. Re-derive the band from the finished files (the purgatorio
rule) and record it in running_notes.txt. This is the collection's
LARGEST from-the-Greek volume by far: 57 files, ~240k English words,
between Plato's Dialogues and Don Quixote in size.
