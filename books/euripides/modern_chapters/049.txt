The Bacchae
(Part 3 of 3)

Dionysus.
Then I will put it back in place. Looking after you is my business
now. Hold your head up.

Pentheus.
There. You dress me. I have handed myself over to you.

Dionysus.
Your belt has come loose, and the pleats of your gown are not hanging
straight all the way down to your ankles.

Pentheus.
I think so too, at least by my right foot. On this side the robe sits
properly along the back of my heel.

Dionysus.
You will count me the first of your friends, I think, when you see the
Bacchants behaving decently, against everything you expected.

Pentheus.
Should I take the thyrsus in my right hand, or this one, to look more
like a Bacchant?

Dionysus.
In the right hand, and you lift it at the same moment as your right
foot. I am glad your mind has changed.

Pentheus.
Could I carry the glens of Cithaeron on my shoulders, Bacchants and
all?

Dionysus.
You could, if you wanted to. The mind you had before was not sound.
Now you have the mind you ought to have.

Pentheus.
Shall we bring levers? Or shall I tear them up with my hands, putting
a shoulder or an arm under the peaks?

Dionysus.
Do not wreck the shrines of the Nymphs, or the places where Pan sits
and plays his pipes.

Pentheus.
Well said. Women are not to be beaten by strength. I will hide myself
in the pines.

Dionysus.
You will be hidden in the hiding place you have earned, coming as a
treacherous spy on the Maenads.

Pentheus.
Yes. I imagine them in the thickets like birds, held in the sweetest
grip of their lovemaking.

Dionysus.
And that is exactly what you are being sent out to watch for. Perhaps
you will catch them. If you are not caught first.

Pentheus.
Take me through the middle of Thebes. I am the only man of them with
the nerve for this.

Dionysus.
You alone wear yourself out for this city, you alone. So the struggle
waiting for you is the one you had coming. Follow me. I am your escort
and I will bring you there safely. Someone else will bring you back.

Pentheus.
My mother, you mean.

Dionysus.
And every eye will be on you.

Pentheus.
That is what I am going for.

Dionysus.
You will come home carried.

Pentheus.
You are promising me luxury.

Dionysus.
In your mother's arms.

Pentheus.
You will force me to be spoiled.

Dionysus.
Spoiling of a kind.

Pentheus.
I am laying my hands on what I deserve.

Dionysus.
You are terrible. Terrible, and the suffering you are walking into is
terrible, and out of it you will find a fame that stands up as high as
heaven. Reach out your hands, Agave, and you too, her sisters, the
daughters of Cadmus. I am taking this young man to a great contest,
and the winner will be me, and Dionysus. The rest the thing itself
will show.

(Dionysus leads Pentheus away towards the mountain.)

Chorus.
	Go, fast hounds of Madness, go to the mountain,
	where the daughters of Cadmus hold their company,
	and sting them into a rage
	against the man in a woman's dress,
	the raving spy on the Maenads.
	His mother will be the first to see him,
	from a smooth rock or a broken stump,
	watching them, and she will call out to the Maenads:
	Who is this who has come to the mountain,
	to the mountain, Bacchants, hunting for
	the mountain-running women of Thebes? Who bore him?
	He was not born out of a woman's
	blood. He is the offspring of some lioness,
	or of the Gorgons of Libya.
	Let Justice go out where all can see her, let her go with a sword,
	killing him straight through the throat,
	the godless, lawless, unjust son
	of Echion, born out of the earth.

Chorus.
	The man who with an unjust mind and a lawless temper
	sets out against your rites, Dionysus,
	and your mother's, with a maddened heart
	and a wrecked purpose,
	meaning to master by force what cannot be mastered:
	death takes those plans in hand
	and listens to no excuses,
	where the business of the gods is concerned.
	To live the way a mortal lives is a life without pain.
	I do not grudge anyone cleverness.
	I am glad to go hunting the other things,
	the great ones, the plain ones: to let a life run
	towards what is fine, to be clean
	and worship, day and night,
	to throw out the customs that lie outside justice,
	and to honour the gods.
	Let Justice go out where all can see her, let her go with a sword,
	killing him straight through the throat,
	the godless, lawless, unjust son
	of Echion, born out of the earth.

Chorus.
	Show yourself as a bull, or a snake with many heads
	to look at, or a lion breathing fire.
	Come, Dionysus, with a laughing face
	throw the noose of death
	round the hunter of the Bacchants
	as he goes down under the herd of Maenads.

(A Second Messenger comes down from the mountain.)

Second Messenger.
House that was fortunate once in all Greece, house of the old man from
Sidon, who sowed the earth-born crop of the snake in this ground: how I
groan for you. I am a slave, but even so, a good slave takes his
masters' troubles for his own.

Chorus.
What is it? Do you have news from the Bacchants?

Second Messenger.
Pentheus is dead. Echion's son.

Chorus.
Lord Dionysus, you are showing yourself a great god.

Second Messenger.
What are you saying? What was that? Are you glad, woman, when things
go badly for my masters?

Chorus.
	I am a stranger here and I shout for joy in a foreign song.
	I do not cower in fear of chains any more.

Second Messenger.
	And do you think Thebes is so short of men?

Chorus.
	Dionysus, Dionysus, not Thebes,
	has the power over me.

Second Messenger.
You can be forgiven. But it is not right, women, to be glad about
things as evil as this, once they have actually been done.

Chorus.
Tell me. Say it. What kind of death did he die, the unjust man who
did unjust things?

Second Messenger.
We left the farms of Theban country behind and crossed the streams of
the Asopus, and started up the crags of Cithaeron, Pentheus and I, for
I was following my master, and the stranger who was guiding us to the
show. First we sat down in a grassy hollow, keeping our feet quiet and
our tongues quiet, so that we could see without being seen. There was
a gorge with cliffs on both sides of it, running with water, shaded
over with pines, and there the Maenads were sitting with their hands
busy at work they enjoyed. Some of them were binding a worn-out
thyrsus and making it leafy with ivy again. Others, like colts let out
of the painted yoke, were singing Bacchic music back and forth to each
other. And poor Pentheus, who could not see the crowd of women, said
this: "Stranger, from where we are standing I cannot reach these fake
Maenads with my eyes. But up on the bank, if I climbed a pine with a
high neck to it, I could see the Maenads and their filthy business
properly."

And then I saw the stranger do a marvellous thing. He took hold of the
topmost branch of a pine, high up in the sky, and pulled it down, and
down, and down to the black ground. It curved like a bow, or like a
wheel whose curve a compass draws round in a circle. That is how the
stranger dragged the mountain branch down with his hands and bent it
to the earth, doing work that is no mortal's work. He set Pentheus on
the pine boughs, and let the trunk go straight back up through his
hands, steadily, taking care not to throw him off, and it stood
upright into the upright sky with my master sitting on its back.

He was seen more than he saw. He had hardly come into view up there
when the stranger was nowhere to be seen at all, and out of the sky a
voice, Dionysus, I suppose, shouted: "Young women, I bring you the man
who made a laughing-stock of you, and of me, and of my rites. Punish
him." And as he was saying it, a light of holy fire stood fixed
between the sky and the ground. The air went silent. The wooded glen
held its leaves silent. You would not have heard an animal's voice.
The women had not caught the sound clearly with their ears, and they
stood up straight and turned their eyes this way and that. He called
again. And when the daughters of Cadmus recognised the clear command
of Dionysus, they shot forward, as fast as doves, running on straining
feet: his mother Agave, her sisters born of the same womb, and all the
Bacchants. Through the torrent gorge, over the broken rocks, they went
leaping, mad with the god's breath in them.

When they saw my master sitting up in the pine, first they climbed a
rock that stood opposite it like a tower and began throwing stones at
him, hard enough to smash him, and they threw pine branches at him
like javelins, and others sent their thyrsus-wands flying through the
air at Pentheus, a miserable target. But they were not getting there.
The poor man was sitting at a height above their eagerness, trapped,
with nothing he could do. In the end they smashed oak branches down
like lightning and began to prise the roots up with levers that were
not iron. When all that work came to nothing, Agave said: "Come,
Maenads, stand round in a circle and take hold of the trunk, so we can
catch the beast that has climbed up there, and stop him telling anyone
about the god's secret dances." They put ten thousand hands on the
pine and tore it up out of the earth, and down from his high seat, all
that way to the ground, Pentheus fell, with ten thousand cries out of
him, because he was close enough to the evil now to understand it.

His mother began the killing, as the priestess of it, and fell on him.
He threw the headband off his hair, so that poor Agave might recognise
him and not kill him, and he touched her cheek and said: "It is me,
mother. I am your son. Pentheus. The one you bore in Echion's house.
Pity me, mother. Do not kill your own child because of my mistakes."
But she was foaming at the mouth, rolling her eyes round in her head,
not thinking what she ought to have thought, and Dionysus had hold of
her, and Pentheus did not persuade her. She took his left arm at the
wrist, and set her foot against the wretched man's ribs, and tore his
shoulder out. Not by her own strength: the god put the ease into her
hands. Ino was working on the other side of him, ripping the flesh
open, and Autonoe, and the whole crowd of Bacchants were pressing in.
Every kind of noise at once: Pentheus groaning for as long as he had
breath left in him, the women shrieking in triumph. One of them was
carrying an arm. Another had a foot with the boot still on it. His
ribs were stripped bare by the tearing. And every one of them, with
blood all over her hands, was tossing Pentheus's flesh about like a
ball.

His body lies in pieces in different places, some of it under the hard
rocks, some in the deep leaves of the wood, and it will not be an easy
thing to look for. His poor head: his mother happened to take that in
her hands, and she has fixed it on the point of a thyrsus, and she is
carrying it through the middle of Cithaeron like the head of a
mountain lion, leaving her sisters behind in the Maenads' dances. She
is coming inside these walls, glorying in her disastrous hunt, calling
on Dionysus, her fellow huntsman, her partner in the chase, the
winner, the beautiful winner. And the victory she wins in his service
is tears.

As for me, I am getting out of the way of this disaster before Agave
reaches the house. To keep yourself in hand, and to reverence what
belongs to the gods, is the finest thing there is; and I think it is
the cleverest thing a mortal can own, for anyone who uses it.

(The Second Messenger goes back towards the mountain, away from the
house.)

Chorus.
	Let us dance for Dionysus,
	let us shout out what has happened
	to Pentheus, the snake's descendant,
	who put on a woman's clothes
	and took up the fennel wand, the fine thyrsus,
	which was death, and could be trusted to be death,
	with a bull walking ahead of him into disaster.
	Bacchants of Thebes,
	you have made your famous victory
	end in groaning, and in tears.
	A fine contest: to close a hand
	dripping with blood round your own son.

Chorus.
But I can see Pentheus's mother Agave hurrying towards the house, with
her eyes rolling in her head. Welcome the reveller of the god of the
cry.

(Agave comes in, carrying the head of Pentheus on a thyrsus.)

Agave.
Bacchants of Asia.

Chorus.
Why do you call me out? Oh.

Agave.
	I am bringing home from the mountain
	a fresh-cut tendril to the house.
	A blessed catch.

Chorus.
	I see it. I will take you in as a fellow reveller.

Agave.
	I caught him without any snares:
	the young cub of a wild lion.
	Look at him.

Chorus.
	Out of what wilderness?

Agave.
	Cithaeron.

Chorus.
	Cithaeron?

Agave.
	Killed him.

Chorus.
	Who struck him first?

Agave.
	Mine was the first honour.
	Blessed Agave, they call me in the company.

Chorus.
	Who else?

Agave.
	Cadmus's.

Chorus.
	Cadmus's what?

Agave.
	Children. After me, after me,
	they got their hands on this animal.
	This was a lucky hunt.

Agave.
	Take your share of the feast, then.

Chorus.
	Share it? Share it, you poor woman?

Agave.
	The bull is young. The down
	is only just coming up on his cheek
	under the soft-haired crest of his head.

Chorus.
	Yes, the hair is like a wild animal's.

Agave.
	Dionysus, the clever huntsman,
	cleverly set the Maenads on
	to this beast.

Chorus.
	Our lord is a hunter.

Agave.
	Do you praise me?

Chorus.
	I praise you.

Agave.
	And soon the Thebans.

Chorus.
	And your son Pentheus too.

Agave.
	Will praise his mother
	for taking this lion of a catch.

Chorus.
	Extraordinary.

Agave.
	Extraordinarily taken.

Chorus.
	Are you proud of it?

Agave.
	I am delighted.
	Great things, great things,
	and plain for anyone to see, done in this hunt.

Chorus.
Then show the citizens, poor woman, the prize you have brought home in
victory.

Agave.
You who live in the fine-towered city of Thebes, come and see this
catch, the animal we hunted down, we the daughters of Cadmus. Not with
the looped javelins of Thessaly, not with nets, but with the sharp
tips of our own white arms. And after that, why should men boast, and
buy the spear-makers' tools for nothing? We took him with our own bare
hands, and we pulled the beast's joints apart. Where is my old father?
Let him come here. And where is my son Pentheus? Let him take a
ladder, and put it up against the house, and climb it, so he can nail
this lion's head to the beam, the head I have come home from hunting.

(Cadmus comes in. Slaves follow him, carrying the pieces of Pentheus's
body on a bier.)

Cadmus.
Follow me. Carry the miserable weight that is Pentheus, follow me,
slaves, and set it down in front of the house. I am bringing his body
here, worn out with searching a thousand times over. I found it in the
folds of Cithaeron, torn to pieces, no two parts of it in the same
place, lying in woodland where nothing is easy to find. I heard what
my daughters had dared to do when I was already back inside the city
walls with old Teiresias, coming home from the Bacchants. So I turned
round and went back up the mountain, and here I am bringing home my
child, killed by Maenads. I saw Autonoe, the woman who once bore
Actaeon to Aristaeus, and Ino with her, still in the thickets, still
stung out of their minds, poor creatures. And somebody told me Agave
was coming this way with her Bacchic step. What I heard was true. I
can see her, and it is not a happy sight.

Agave.
Father, you have the greatest boast in the world to make: that you
fathered daughters who are by a long way the best of all mortal women.
I mean all of us, and myself most of all. I left my shuttle at the
loom and went on to greater things: catching wild animals with my two
hands. And here in my arms, as you can see, I am carrying the prize I
won, to be hung up on your house. Take it, father, take it in your
hands. Be proud of what I hunted, and call your friends to a feast,
because you are blessed. Blessed, now that we have done this.

Cadmus.
Grief past all measuring, and I cannot bear to look at it: a murder,
done by miserable hands. This is the fine victim you have brought down
for the gods, and it is on this that you are inviting Thebes and me to
a banquet. Oh. Your trouble first, and then my own. The god has
destroyed us justly, and much too far: Lord Dionysus, who was born
into our own family.

Agave.
How bad-tempered old age is in a man, and how sour in the face. I hope
my son turns out a good hunter, and takes after his mother's ways,
when he goes after wild animals with the young men of Thebes. But all
he can do is fight with the gods. You must have a word with him,
father. Somebody call him here where I can see him, so he can see how
lucky I am.

Cadmus.
Ah. Ah. When you understand what you have done, you will suffer a
terrible pain. If you stayed in the state you are in now, right
through to the end, you would not be fortunate, but you would not
think you were unfortunate either.

Agave.
What is wrong with any of this? What is there in it to be sad about?

Cadmus.
First lift your eyes up to this sky.

Agave.
There. Why did you tell me to look at it?

Cadmus.
Is it still the same, or does it seem to you to have changed?

Agave.
It is brighter than it was before, and clearer.

Cadmus.
And that fluttering in your mind. Is it still there?

Agave.
I do not know that word of yours. But somehow I am coming to my
senses. Something has shifted out of the mind I had before.

Cadmus.
Can you hear me, then, and answer me clearly?

Agave.
Yes. I have forgotten what we said before, father.

Cadmus.
Whose house did you come to when you married?

Agave.
You gave me to Echion, they say. One of the Sown Men.

Cadmus.
And what son was born in that house to your husband?

Agave.
Pentheus. From me and his father together.

Cadmus.
Then whose face is it you are holding in your arms?

Agave.
A lion's. That is what the women who hunted him said.

Cadmus.
Look at it properly, then. It takes very little effort to see.

Agave.
Ah. What am I looking at? What is this I am carrying in my hands?

Cadmus.
Look hard at it. Learn it more clearly.

Agave.
I see the greatest grief there is. I am ruined.

Cadmus.
Does it still look like a lion to you?

Agave.
No. It is Pentheus's head I am holding, and I am the most miserable
woman alive.

Cadmus.
Yes, and it was mourned over before ever you recognised it.

Agave.
Who killed him? How did he come into my hands?

Cadmus.
Miserable truth, what a moment you have picked to arrive.

Agave.
Say it. My heart is jumping at what is coming.

Cadmus.
You killed him. You and your sisters.

Agave.
Where did he die? At home? In what place?

Cadmus.
Where the dogs once shared Actaeon out between them.

Agave.
And why did the poor man go to Cithaeron?

Cadmus.
He went to mock the god, and to mock your worship.

Agave.
And how did we come to be up there?

Cadmus.
You went mad. The whole city was raving with the god.

Agave.
Dionysus destroyed us. I understand it now.

Cadmus.
Outrage was done to him. You would not count him a god.

Agave.
And my child's body, father, the dearest body there is. Where is it?

Cadmus.
I hunted it out. It was hard. I have brought it here.

Agave.
Is all of it fitted properly together, joint into joint?

Agave.
And what part did Pentheus have in my foolishness?

Cadmus.
He was the same as you. He would not reverence the god. And so the god
joined us all together in one ruin, you and him alike, and destroyed
this house, and me with it. I had no sons of my own, and now I see
this shoot of your womb, poor woman, killed in the most shameful and
the ugliest way there is. You were the eye the house looked out of.
You held my roof together, child, my daughter's son, and the city was
afraid of you. Nobody who saw your face wanted to insult the old man,
because you would have given him the punishment he deserved. And now I
will be thrown out of my own house without honour: Cadmus the great,
who sowed the people of Thebes and reaped the finest crop there ever
was. Dearest of men, and even now you are gone I still count you among
the dearest, child, you will never again put your hand to my chin and
hold me and call me your mother's father and say: "Who is wronging
you, old man? Who is dishonouring you? Who is upsetting your heart and
giving you trouble? Tell me, father, so that I can punish the man who
wrongs you." Now I am wretched, and you have suffered, and your mother
is pitiful, and your aunts have suffered too. If there is anybody who
looks down on the gods, let him look at this man's death and believe
in them.

Chorus.
I am sorry for your part in it, Cadmus. Your daughter's son has the
punishment he deserved, and it is a hard one for you.

Agave.
Father, you can see for yourself how far everything of mine has been
turned upside down.

(Dionysus appears above the ruined house, no longer disguised.)

Dionysus.
Cadmus, hear what is left for you. You will change your shape and
become a snake, and your wife will be turned into an animal and take
the form of a serpent: Harmonia, the daughter of Ares, whom you
married while you were a mortal man. You will drive a wagon of oxen,
with your wife beside you, at the head of a barbarian army. That is
what the oracle of Zeus says. You will sack many cities with a force
nobody can count. And when they plunder the oracle of Apollo, they
will have a miserable journey home. But Ares will rescue you and
Harmonia, and settle your life in the land of the blessed. I say this
as Dionysus, and my father was no mortal. I was born from Zeus. If you
had learned to keep yourselves in hand when you refused to, you would
have had the son of Zeus for an ally, and you would be happy now.

Cadmus.
Dionysus, we beg you. We were wrong.

Dionysus.
You learned what I am too late. When you should have known it, you did
not.

Cadmus.
We understand that now. But you are coming after us too hard.

Dionysus.
I am a god by birth, and you did me outrage.

Cadmus.
Gods should not be like mortals in their anger.

Dionysus.
My father Zeus nodded to all this long ago.

Agave.
Aiai. It is decided, old man. Exile, and misery in it.

Dionysus.
Then why put off what has to happen?

Cadmus.
Child, what a terrible evil we have all come to: you, poor girl, and
your sisters, and me. I will go to a foreign country, an old man
living among strangers. And there is more foretold for me: that I will
bring a mixed barbarian army into Greece. I will lead them under arms,
with Harmonia beside me, the daughter of Ares, my wife, with the wild
nature of a serpent on her, and I a serpent myself, against the altars
and the tombs of Greece. I will never have any rest from my troubles.
Not even when I have sailed down the Acheron will I be at peace.

Agave.
Father, and I will go into exile without you.

Cadmus.
Why do you put your arms round me, poor child, like a young swan round
its useless grey-feathered parent?

Agave.
Where am I to turn, thrown out of my own country?

Cadmus.
I do not know, child. Your father is a poor ally.

Agave.
	Goodbye, house. Goodbye, city
	of my fathers. I am leaving you in disaster,
	an exile out of my own room.

Cadmus.
	Go now, child, to the country of Aristaeus.

Agave.
	I grieve for you, father.

Cadmus.
	And I for you, child,
	and I have wept for your sisters.

Agave.
	Terribly, terribly has lord Dionysus
	carried this humiliation
	into your house.

Dionysus.
	Yes, because I suffered terribly at your hands.
	My name had no honour in Thebes.

Agave.
	Goodbye, father.

Cadmus.
	Goodbye, my miserable
	daughter. You will not easily come to any good.

Agave.
	Take me, escorts, to where I can find
	my sisters, my pitiable companions in exile.
	Let me come somewhere
	where filthy Cithaeron cannot see me,
	and my eyes cannot see Cithaeron,
	and where no thyrsus stands as a memorial.
	Let those be some other Bacchant's business.

(Agave goes out one way, Cadmus the other. Dionysus is gone from above
the house.)

Chorus.
	Many are the shapes of what the gods do,
	and many the things they bring about that no one expected.
	What we counted on did not happen,
	and for what no one counted on, a god found a way.
	That is how this business ended.
