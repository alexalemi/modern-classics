Medea
(Part 3 of 3)

Chorus.
	Now I have no hope left for the children's lives,
	none. They are walking to their murder already.
	The bride will take the golden headband,
	the poor girl will take her ruin in her hands,
	and around her yellow hair she will set
	the finery of Hades, herself, with her own two hands.

Chorus.
	The loveliness of the robe, and its shine like a god's,
	will talk her into putting on the crown of worked gold,
	and she will dress as a bride for the dead below.
	That is the net she will fall into,
	that is the death that is her share, poor girl,
	and she will not slip out from under the ruin.

Chorus.
	And you, poor man, you married badly
	when you married into the king's house, and you do not see
	that you are bringing destruction on your sons' lives
	and a hateful death on your wife.
	Poor man. How wrong you are about what is coming to you.

Chorus.
	And I grieve for your pain too, poor mother
	of these children, who will murder
	your sons over a marriage bed,
	the bed your husband left, against all law,
	to live with another woman.

(The Tutor comes back from the palace with the two children.)

Tutor.
Mistress, these children of yours are let off their exile, and the royal
bride took the gifts gladly, in her own hands. From that side, the
children have peace. Hey. Why are you standing there so upset, when
things are going well for you? Why have you turned your face away, and
why aren't you glad to hear this from me?

Medea.
Aiai.

Tutor.
That does not go with the news I have brought.

Medea.
Aiai, again.

Tutor.
Have I reported some misfortune without knowing it? Was I wrong to think
my news was good?

Medea.
You reported what you reported. I don't blame you.

Tutor.
Then why are your eyes on the ground? Why are you crying?

Medea.
I have to, old man. I have to. The gods and I, in my bad judgement,
arranged all of this.

Tutor.
Take heart. Your children will bring you home again some day too.

Medea.
Before that I will bring others home. Down, into the ground. The misery
of it.

Tutor.
You are not the only woman who was ever parted from her children. When
you are mortal you have to carry your troubles lightly.

Medea.
I will. But go inside, and get the children what they need for the day.

Children, children. You two have a city now, and a house, where you will
live for ever without me, leaving your wretched mother behind, robbed of
her. And I am going to another country, an exile, before I have had any
joy of you, before I have seen you happy, before I have dressed a bride
for you and made the marriage bed beautiful and held up the wedding
torch. Oh, my own stubborn will, and the misery it has brought me. So it
was for nothing that I brought you up, children, for nothing that I
laboured and was worn to shreds with work, and bore the hard pains of
childbirth. There was a time, poor fool that I was, when I had great
hopes of you: that you would look after me when I was old, and when I
died you would wrap my body with your own hands, which is a thing people
envy. Now that sweet thought is dead. Without you two I will drag out a
life that is bitter and full of pain. And you will never see your mother
again with those dear eyes. You are going away into another shape of
life.

Oh. Oh. Why are you looking at me like that, children? Why are you
smiling at me, that very last smile? Aiai. What am I going to do? My
heart has gone out of me, women, now that I have seen my children's
bright faces. I could not do it. Goodbye to the plans I made before. I
will take my children out of the country with me. Why should I hurt
their father through their suffering, and get myself twice as much
suffering as he gets? No. Not I. Goodbye to my plans.

And yet, what is happening to me? Do I want to be laughed at, and let my
enemies go unpunished? I have to have the nerve for this. What a coward
I am, to let soft words into my heart at all. Go into the house,
children. Anyone who has no right to be present at my sacrifice, let
that be his own concern. I will not let my hand go weak.

Ah. Ah. No, my heart, don't. Don't you do this. Let them be, you poor
thing. Spare the children. They will live there with us, and they will
make you glad. No. By the avenging spirits down in Hades, there is no
way, ever, that I will hand my children over to my enemies to be
outraged. They have to die, whatever happens. And since they must, I
will kill them, I who gave them birth. It is all done now, whatever
happens, and she will not escape. The crown is on her head already; the
royal bride is dying in the robe. I know it for certain.

But I am going down the most wretched road there is, and I am sending
these two down one more wretched still, so I want to speak to my
children. Give me, children, give your mother your right hand to kiss.
Dearest hand, dearest mouth, and the shape of you, and the noble faces
of my children. Be happy, both of you. But there, not here. Everything
here your father has taken away. Oh, the sweetness of holding you, the
soft skin, the sweet breath of my children. Go. Go in. I cannot look at
you any longer. The evil is beating me. I know what evil I am about to
do, but my anger is stronger than my plans, and anger is the cause of
the greatest evils human beings suffer.

(The children go into the house. Medea stays where she is.)

Chorus.
	Often before now
	I have gone into finer arguments
	and taken on bigger questions
	than the female sex ought to look into.
	But we have a Muse of our own, too,
	who keeps us company for the sake of understanding.
	Not all of us. It is a small breed
	you would find among many women,
	but you might find it: women the Muse has not passed by.
	And I say this. Of all human beings,
	the ones who have never had children,
	who know nothing at all about it,
	are better off in happiness
	than the ones who are parents.
	The childless never had any, so they never learn
	whether children turn out a sweetness to people
	or a grief,
	and they are spared a great deal of hard work.
	But the ones who have in their house
	the sweet growth of children, I see them
	worn down with worry the whole time.
	First, how to bring them up well,
	and how to leave them enough to live on.
	And after all that, whether it is worthless children
	or good ones
	they have been working for, they cannot tell.
	And there is one evil more, the last of all,
	and I will name it now for every mortal.
	Say they have found enough to live on,
	and the children's bodies have come to their prime,
	and they have turned out good. Then, if their luck
	falls that way, Death is off to Hades,
	carrying the children's bodies away.
	So what is the good of it? Why, on top of everything else,
	should the gods throw this one grief, the bitterest of all,
	for the sake of children,
	on mortal men and women?

Medea.
Friends, I have been waiting a long time for what happens next, watching
for how things turn out over there. And now I see one of Jason's men
coming. He is breathing hard, and that tells me he has some new evil to
report.

(A messenger runs in from the palace, out of breath.)

Messenger.
Medea. You have done a terrible thing, and a lawless one. Run. Run.
Whatever will carry you, take it, a ship over the sea or a wagon over
the plain.

Medea.
And what has happened that deserves this running away?

Messenger.
The princess is dead, just now, and Creon her father with her, killed by
your poisons.

Medea.
That is the most beautiful thing you could have said. From now on you
count among my benefactors and my friends.

Messenger.
What? Are you in your right mind, woman? Not mad? You have desecrated
the king's hearth, and you are glad to hear it, and not afraid?

Medea.
I have something to say to that too, an answer of my own. But don't
rush, friend. Tell me. How did they die? You will give me twice the
pleasure if they died horribly.

Messenger.
When your two children came with their father and went into the bride's
house, we were glad, we slaves who had suffered along with you in your
troubles. At once the word went from ear to ear all through the house
that you and your husband had made up your old quarrel. One of us kissed
the children's hands, another their yellow hair, and I myself was so
happy that I went along with the children into the women's rooms. Our
mistress, the one we honour now in your place, had her eyes fixed
eagerly on Jason, before she caught sight of your two boys. But then she
covered her eyes and turned her white cheek away, disgusted that the
children had come in. Your husband set about taking the girl's anger and
her temper away, saying this: "You won't be unkind to people who love
you, will you? Stop being angry and turn your head back this way. Count
as your friends the people your husband counts. Take these gifts, and
ask your father to let these boys off their exile, for my sake." And
when she saw the finery, she couldn't hold out. She agreed to everything
her husband asked, and before the father and the children were far from
the house, she took the embroidered robe and put it on, and set the
golden crown around her curls, and arranged her hair in a bright mirror,
smiling at the lifeless image of her own body. Then she got up from her
chair and walked through the rooms, stepping delicately on her white,
white feet, overjoyed with the gifts, and over and over again she
stretched out her leg and looked down at the line of her ankle.

But what came after that was a terrible thing to see. Her colour
changed. She staggered back sideways, her legs shaking under her, and
only just managed to fall into the chair instead of onto the floor. One
of the servants, an old woman, thinking, I suppose, that a fit from Pan
or some other god had come on her, raised the cry of prayer. Until she
saw white foam coming out of the girl's mouth, and her eyes rolling back
in their sockets, and no blood left in her skin. Then instead of that
prayer she sent up a great shriek of grief. Straight away one servant
ran for her father's house and another for the new husband, to tell him
what had happened to his bride, and the whole house rang with feet
running back and forth.

By the time a fast walker could have covered the length of a running
track and been reaching the far end of it, the poor girl came to, out of
her silence and her closed eyes, with a terrible groan. Two torments
were attacking her at once. The golden band lying round her head let
loose an astonishing stream of fire that ate everything, and the fine
robe, your children's gift, was eating the poor girl's soft flesh. She
got up from the chair and ran, on fire, shaking her hair and her head
this way and that, trying to throw the crown off. But the gold held its
fastenings tight, and every time she shook her hair the fire blazed
twice as bright. She fell to the floor, beaten by the disaster, and
nobody could have known her except her own father. You could not make
out the set of her eyes any more, or her lovely face. Blood was dripping
from the top of her head, mixed with fire, and the flesh was running off
her bones under the invisible jaws of the poisons, the way resin drips
from a pine. A terrible sight. And everyone was afraid to touch the
body. What had happened had taught us that much.

Then her poor father, knowing nothing of what had happened, came
suddenly into the room and fell on the corpse. He groaned at once, and
wrapped his arms round her and kissed her, and said this to her: "My
poor child. Which of the gods destroyed you like this, so shamefully?
Who is making an old man, with one foot in the grave, an orphan, robbed
of you? Oh. Let me die with you, child." When he had stopped his crying
and his wailing and wanted to lift his old body up, he was stuck to the
fine robe the way ivy sticks to the shoots of a laurel, and then there
was a terrible wrestling. He wanted to get up on his knee, and she held
him back. If he pulled hard, he tore his old flesh off his bones. In the
end he gave out, and the poor man let his life go. He could not rise
above the evil any longer. So they lie there dead, the daughter and the
old father, side by side, a disaster that cries out for tears.

As for you, I leave your part out of my account. You will find out for
yourself how to turn the punishment aside. Not for the first time, I
think human life is a shadow. And I would say without fear that the
people who pass for clever, the ones who work so hard on their words,
are the ones found guilty of the greatest folly. No human being is
happy. When wealth pours in, one man may be luckier than another. But
happy? No.

Chorus.
It looks as if the god is fastening a great many evils on Jason today,
and fastening them with justice. Poor woman, daughter of Creon, how we
pity your disaster, gone to the house of Hades because of your marriage
to Jason.

Medea.
Friends, the thing is decided. I will kill the children as fast as I can
and leave this land. I will not delay and hand my children over to
another hand to murder, a hand that hates them more. They have to die,
whatever happens. And since they must, I will kill them, I who gave them
birth. Come on, then, heart. Arm yourself. Why am I waiting to do the
terrible thing that has to be done? Come, my wretched hand, take the
sword. Take it. Go to the starting line of a life of grief. Do not turn
coward. Do not remember the children, how much you love them, how you
bore them. For this one short day forget your children, and grieve
afterwards. Because even if you kill them, still, they were born your
own dear ones. And I am an unlucky woman.

(Medea goes into the house.)

Chorus.
	Io, Earth, and you, ray of the Sun
	that lights everything, look down, look
	at this ruined woman before she lays
	a bloody hand on her children, her own.
	They grew from your golden seed,
	and for a god's blood to be spilled
	by human hands is a thing to fear.
	Light born of Zeus, hold her back,
	stop her, get her out of the house,
	this pitiful Fury of blood that the spirits of vengeance drive.

Chorus.
	The labour of your children has gone for nothing.
	Was it for nothing you bore your dear sons,
	you who left behind the dark Clashing Rocks,
	the Symplegades, the passage that welcomes no stranger?
	Wretched woman, why does this heavy anger
	fall on your mind, why is murder,
	hateful murder, taking the place of love?
	Pollution from the blood of your own kin
	is hard for human beings to bear on the earth. It brings
	griefs from the gods down on the killers' house, to match.

Children.
	Aiai.

Chorus.
	Do you hear the cry? Do you hear the children?
	Io, wretched, ill-starred woman.

(The children's voices come from inside the house, first one boy and
then the other.)

Children.
Oh. What do I do? Where can I run from my mother's hands?

I don't know, brother, dearest. We are being killed.

Chorus.
	Should I go into the house? I think
	I should keep the children from being murdered.

Children.
Yes, by the gods, keep us from it. We need you now.

The net of the sword is closing on us already. We are that near.

Chorus.
	Wretched woman. Then you were stone,
	or iron, all along, to kill the children you bore,
	the crop of your own body, with a death dealt by your own hand.

Chorus.
	One, only one woman before this
	have I heard of who laid her hand on her own children:
	Ino, whom the gods drove mad, when the wife of Zeus
	sent her out of her house to wander.
	And the poor woman fell into the sea
	for the unholy murder of her children,
	stepping off the sea cliff over the water,
	and died there with her two sons.
	What is left that could be terrible now, after this?
	Oh, the marriage bed of women,
	how much pain it costs, how much evil it has done to people already.

(Jason comes in at a run.)

Jason.
Women, standing near this house. Is she still inside, Medea, the one who
did these terrible things, or has she run for it? She will have to hide
under the earth or lift her body on wings into the depths of the sky, if
she is not going to pay the royal house what she owes it. Does she think
that after killing the rulers of this land she will get away from this
house unpunished? But it is not her I am thinking of. It is the
children. The ones she has hurt will hurt her back. I came to save my
children's lives, in case the family of the dead does something to me,
taking revenge for the unholy murder their mother did.

Chorus.
Poor man. You do not know how far into evil you have come, Jason, or you
would not have said that.

Jason.
What is it? Does she want to kill me too?

Chorus.
Your children are dead. Their mother's hand killed them.

Jason.
Oh. What are you saying? You have destroyed me, woman.

Chorus.
Your children no longer exist. Think of them that way now.

Jason.
Where did she kill them? Inside the house, or outside?

Chorus.
Open the doors, and you will see your children murdered.

Jason.
Slaves, undo the bolts, quick as you can. Unfasten the bars. Let me see
the double evil, the two dead, and her. I will make her pay for it.

(Medea appears above the house, in a chariot drawn by dragons. The
bodies of the children are beside her.)

Medea.
Why are you shaking the doors and prising at the bolts, hunting for the
dead and for me, who did it? Save your effort. If you want something
from me, say it, if you like. But your hand will never touch me. This
chariot is what my father's father, the Sun, gives me: a wall against my
enemies' hands.

Jason.
You hateful thing. Woman most loathed by the gods, and by me, and by the
whole human race. You had the nerve to drive a sword into your own
children, you, their mother, and you have destroyed me, left me
childless. And having done it, you look at the sun and the earth, after
having the nerve to do the most unholy thing there is. Die. I am in my
right mind now. I was not then, when I brought you out of your house and
your barbarian country to a Greek home, a great evil, a traitor to your
father and to the land that raised you. The gods have flung your
avenging spirit at me. You had already killed your brother at the hearth
when you stepped aboard the Argo with its lovely prow. That is how you
began. Then you married me, and bore me children, and for the sake of a
bed, of sex, you have destroyed them. There is not one Greek woman who
would ever have had the nerve to do this, and I passed over every one of
them to marry you, a marriage that was my enemy and my ruin: a lioness,
not a woman, wilder by nature than the monster Scylla in the Tyrrhenian
sea. But I could not bite you with ten thousand insults; that is how
bold you are by nature. Go to hell, you filth, you murderer of your
children. All that is left to me is to weep over my own fortune. I will
get no joy of my new marriage bed, and the children I fathered and
brought up I will never speak to alive again. I have lost them.

Medea.
I would have made a long speech in answer to that, if Zeus the father
did not already know what you had from me and what you did to me. You
were not going to dishonour my bed and then live a pleasant life
laughing at me. Nor was the princess. Nor was Creon, who arranged your
marriage, going to throw me out of this land and get away with it. So
call me a lioness if you like, and Scylla who lives on the Tyrrhenian
shore. I have got my hands on your heart, the way I had to.

Jason.
You are in pain too. You share the evil.

Medea.
Be sure of it. But the pain is worth it, as long as you are not
laughing.

Jason.
Children, what an evil mother you got.

Medea.
Boys, how your father's sickness destroyed you.

Jason.
It was not my right hand that killed them.

Medea.
No. Your outrage did, and your new marriage.

Jason.
You thought a marriage bed a good enough reason to kill them?

Medea.
Do you think that is a small hurt to a woman?

Jason.
To a woman who keeps herself in hand, yes. To you everything is evil.

Medea.
They no longer exist. That will bite you.

Jason.
They exist. Oh, they exist, as avengers on your head.

Medea.
The gods know who began this pain.

Jason.
They know, yes. They know your disgusting mind.

Medea.
Hate me. I loathe the bitter sound of your voice.

Jason.
And I loathe yours. Parting is easy.

Medea.
How, then? What do I do? I want it badly enough myself.

Jason.
Let me bury these dead and weep over them.

Medea.
No. I will bury them myself, with this hand. I will carry them to the
sacred ground of Hera of the Heights, so that none of my enemies can
outrage them by tearing up their graves. And on this land of Sisyphus,
on Corinth, I will lay a solemn festival and rites for all time to come,
to pay for this unholy murder. I myself am going to the land of
Erechtheus, to Athens, to live with Aegeus, the son of Pandion. And you,
as is only right, will die a coward's death, a bad man dying badly, your
head struck by a piece of the wreck of the Argo, after you have seen the
bitter end of your marriage to me.

Jason.
	May the children's Fury destroy you,
	and Justice that avenges blood.

Medea.
	What god or spirit listens to you,
	you who broke your oath and cheated the house that took you in?

Jason.
	Ah. Ah. Filthy woman. Child-killer.

Medea.
	Go home, and bury your wife.

Jason.
	I am going. Without either of my two sons.

Medea.
	You are not mourning yet. Wait until you are old.

Jason.
	Children, dearest children.

Medea.
	To their mother. Not to you.

Jason.
	And then you killed them?

Medea.
	Yes. To hurt you.

Jason.
	Oh. I want to kiss
	my children's dear mouths. The misery of it.

Medea.
	Now you talk to them, now you want to hold them.
	Then, you pushed them away.

Jason.
	By the gods, let me
	touch my children's soft skin.

Medea.
	No. Your words are thrown away on the wind.

Jason.
	Zeus, do you hear how I am driven off,
	what I am suffering from this filthy woman,
	this lioness who kills her children?
	But as far as I can, as far as I am allowed,
	I mourn them, and I call on the gods,
	I call them to witness that you killed
	my children and will not let me
	touch them with my hands or bury their bodies.
	I wish I had never fathered them
	to see them dead at your hands.

(The chariot rises and carries Medea away. Jason is left below.)

Chorus.
	Zeus on Olympus keeps the store of many things,
	and many the things they bring about that no one expected.
	What we counted on did not happen,
	and for what no one counted on, a god found a way.
	That is how this business ended.
