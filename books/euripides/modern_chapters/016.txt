Hecuba
(Part 3 of 3)

Chorus.
	Troy, my country, you will never again
	be counted among the cities nobody has sacked.
	Such a cloud of Greeks has closed over you,
	spear upon spear, and laid you waste.
	Your crown of towers has been cut from your head,
	and you are smeared with soot,
	a filthy, pitiful stain. Poor city,
	I will never walk your streets again.

Chorus.
	It was midnight when my ruin came,
	the hour when sleep after supper
	spreads sweetly over the eyes.
	The songs were over, and the sacrifice, and the dancing,
	and my husband lay in our bedroom
	with his spear hung on its peg,
	no longer watching for the crowd of sailors
	who had set foot on Trojan ground.

Chorus.
	And I was doing up my hair
	in its band, looking into the endless
	gleam of a golden mirror,
	ready to fall into my bed.
	And a noise went up through the city,
	and this was the shout that ran down the streets of Troy:
	"Sons of Greece, when, oh when
	will you sack the citadel of Troy
	and get home to your houses?"

Chorus.
	I left the bed I loved in nothing but a shift,
	like a Dorian girl,
	and sat down at the altar of holy Artemis,
	and it did me no good, poor woman.
	I saw my husband dead, and I am being carried away
	over the salt sea,
	looking back at my city, when the ship
	set its foot for home
	and cut me off from the land of Troy.
	Wretched. I gave in to the pain,

Chorus.
	cursing Helen, the sister of the twin sons of Zeus,
	and the cattleman of Ida,
	Paris, the ruin of us,
	because it was a marriage, a marriage that was no marriage
	but misery sent by some avenging spirit,
	that destroyed me, and tore me from my father's land,
	and threw me out of my home.
	May the salt sea never carry her back again,
	and may she never
	come to her father's house.

(Polymestor comes in with his two young sons and an armed escort.
Hecuba comes out of the tent to meet him.)

Polymestor.
Priam, the dearest man I knew, and you, Hecuba, dearest of women, I
weep to look at you, and at your city, and at your daughter, killed
just now. Pheu. There is nothing a man can trust. Not a good name.
Not the idea that someone doing well will not come to do badly. The
gods themselves stir everything up, backwards and forwards, throwing
in confusion, so that we will worship them because we cannot
understand them. But what is the use of crying over all that? It does
not get us one step ahead of our troubles. As for you, if you have any
complaint about my being away, hold it. I happened to be off in the
middle of Thrace when you came here. When I got back, I was just
lifting my foot over my own doorstep when this slave of yours ran
straight into me with your message, and I heard it and came.

Hecuba.
I am ashamed to look you in the face, Polymestor, lying in troubles
like these. You saw me when I was doing well, and shame takes hold of
me now that I have come to this, to where I am now. I could not look
at you with a steady eye. But do not take it for ill will towards you,
Polymestor. There is another reason besides: the custom that women do
not look men in the face.

Polymestor.
No wonder at all. But what do you need me for? What made you send for
me to come out of my house?

Hecuba.
There is something private, something of my own, that I want to say to
you and to your sons. Order your attendants to stand off, away from
these tents.

Polymestor.
Go. There is no danger in being alone here.

(The escort goes.)

You are my friend, and this Greek army is a friend to me. But you must
say what it is. How should a man who is doing well help friends who are
not? I am ready.

Hecuba.
First tell me about the boy you have in your house, Polydorus, from my
hand and his father's. Is he alive? The rest I will ask you after.

Polymestor.
Certainly. On his account, at least, you are lucky.

Hecuba.
Dearest of friends, how well you speak, and how like you.

Polymestor.
So what is the second thing you want to learn from me?

Hecuba.
Does he remember me at all, his mother?

Polymestor.
Yes. He was looking for a way to slip off and come to you here in
secret.

Hecuba.
And the gold he came from Troy with, is it safe?

Polymestor.
Safe. Under guard in my house.

Hecuba.
Then keep it safe, and do not covet what belongs to your neighbours.

Polymestor.
Never. Let me enjoy what I have, lady.

Hecuba.
Do you know, then, what I want to tell you and your sons?

Polymestor.
I do not. Your words will show me.

Hecuba.
There is, my friend, and you are as dear to me now as I am to you,
there is...

Polymestor.
What is it that I and my children have to know?

Hecuba.
Gold. Old vaults of it, dug down under the ground by Priam's house.

Polymestor.
This is what you want to pass on to your son?

Hecuba.
Yes, and through you. You are a god-fearing man.

Polymestor.
Then why do these children have to be here?

Hecuba.
Better that they know, in case you die.

Polymestor.
Well said. That is the cleverer way, too.

Hecuba.
Do you know where the temple of Athena of Troy stands?

Polymestor.
The gold is there? What is the mark?

Hecuba.
A black rock sticking up out of the ground.

Polymestor.
Is there anything else you want to tell me about that place?

Hecuba.
I want you to keep safe the money I came away with.

Polymestor.
Where is it? Inside your dress, or have you got it hidden somewhere?

Hecuba.
It is kept safe in these tents, among a heap of spoils.

Polymestor.
Where? These are the Greeks' shelters, all round their ships.

Hecuba.
The captive women have tents of their own.

Polymestor.
And inside, can I trust it? No men about?

Hecuba.
No Greeks inside. Only us women. Come into the tent. The Argives are
longing to cast off their ships and get home from Troy. Then, when you
have done everything you need to, you can go back with your sons to
wherever it was you settled my child.

(Hecuba takes Polymestor and his sons into the tent.)

Chorus.
You have not paid yet. But perhaps you will pay.

Chorus.
	Like a man who falls into the bilge with no harbour near,
	you will pitch sideways out of everything your heart holds dear,
	robbed of your life. For where what is owed
	to Justice and what is owed to the gods fall together,
	there is ruin. Deadly, deadly ruin.
	The hope that led you down this road will cheat you.
	It has led you to death and to Hades, poor fool,
	and you will lose your life to a hand that never went to war.

(Polymestor screams from inside the tent.)

Polymestor.
Oh. Oh. I am blinded. The light of my eyes. Misery.

Chorus.
Did you hear the Thracian howling, friends?

Polymestor.
Oh, again, again. My children. Butchered. Misery.

Chorus.
Friends, something new and terrible has been done inside the tent.

Polymestor.
You will not get away from me, not on your quick feet. I will smash my
way in. I will break open the inner rooms of this house.

Chorus.
Look. There goes a missile from a heavy hand. Shall we burst in? This
is the moment. Hecuba and the women of Troy need us at their side.

(Hecuba comes out of the tent.)

Hecuba.
Pound away. Spare nothing. Batter the doors down. You will never put
the bright sight back in your eyes, and you will never see your sons
alive. I killed them.

Chorus.
Have you really brought the Thracian down, mistress? Is the stranger
in your power? Have you done what you say?

Hecuba.
You will see him in a moment in front of the tent, blind, coming out on
blind and stumbling feet, and the bodies of his two sons, whom I
killed, with the best of the women of Troy. He has paid me what he
owed. Here he comes out of the house, as you see. I will get out of his
way and stand clear of the Thracian while his anger is running. There
is no fighting him now.

(Polymestor crawls out of the tent on his hands and knees, blood
running from his eyes.)

Polymestor.
	Oh, oh, where can I go,
	where can I stand, where can I put in to shore?
	Down on all fours like a mountain beast,
	hands and feet on the track? Which way
	shall I take, this one or that one,
	hunting to catch the Trojan women, the killers of men,
	the women who destroyed me?
	Wretched daughters of Phrygia, wretched,
	cursed women,
	which corner have they run to, cowering from me?
	Sun, if only you would heal my eyes,
	heal the bleeding lids, the blindness,
	and give me back the light.
	Ah. Ah.
	Quiet. I can hear them, the women,
	their hidden step. Where can I spring
	and fill myself with flesh and bone,
	and make a wild beast's feast of them,
	and win back my ruin,
	their maiming for mine? Oh, misery.
	Where, where am I rushing, leaving my children
	alone for the Bacchants of Hades to tear in pieces,
	butchered, a bloody meal for dogs,
	thrown out on the mountainside like a savage's leavings?
	Where can I stand, where turn, where go,
	like a ship hauling in its sea ropes, furling
	the linen sail, rushing to that bed
	of death, to guard my children?

Chorus.
Poor man. Unbearable things have been done to you. But you did
shameful things, and the penalty is terrible. The god who is heavy on
you, whoever he is, has given it.

Polymestor.
	Aiai. Io, Thrace, my people, spear in hand,
	armed, riding fine horses, in the grip of Ares.
	Io, Greeks. Io, sons of Atreus. I am shouting, shouting, I shout.
	Come. Come here, by the gods.
	Does anyone hear? Will nobody help? Why are you waiting?
	Women have destroyed me, captive women.
	Terrible, terrible things have been done to us.
	Oh, my ruin.
	Where can I turn, where can I go?
	Shall I fly up to the high roof of heaven,
	where Orion and Sirius shoot
	blazing fire from their eyes,
	or shall I plunge down, wretched, to the black
	ferry-crossing of Hades?

Chorus.
When a man suffers evils too great to bear, it is forgivable if he
takes himself out of a wretched life.

(Agamemnon comes in with his men.)

Agamemnon.
I heard shouting and came. Echo, the daughter of the mountain rock,
was not quiet about it: she shrieked it through the whole army and set
off a commotion. If we did not know that the Phrygians' towers had
fallen to the Greek spear, this noise would have given us a real
fright.

Polymestor.
Dearest of friends. I know you, Agamemnon. I heard your voice. Do you
see what has been done to us?

Agamemnon.
What is this? Polymestor. Poor man, who has destroyed you? Who blinded
your eyes and bloodied the pupils, and killed these children? Whoever
he was, he had a great anger against you and your sons.

Polymestor.
Hecuba, with the captive women. She has destroyed me. Not destroyed.
Worse than that.

Agamemnon.
What are you saying? You did this, as he says? You, Hecuba, had the
nerve for this, a thing nobody could have thought possible?

Polymestor.
Oh. What are you saying? Is she near here somewhere? Show me, tell me
where she is, so that I can grab her in my hands and tear her apart
and bloody her flesh.

Agamemnon.
You. What is the matter with you?

Polymestor.
	By the gods I beg you,
	let me loose my raging hand on her.

Agamemnon.
Stop. Throw the barbarian out of your heart and speak. I will hear you
and her in turn, and judge fairly why you are suffering this.

Polymestor.
I will speak, then. There was a son of Priam's, the youngest,
Polydorus, Hecuba's boy, and his father Priam sent him to me out of
Troy to bring up in my house, because he already suspected Troy would
fall. I killed him. But hear why I killed him, and how well and how
cleverly I thought it out. I was afraid that if the boy was left alive
as your enemy he would gather Troy together and settle it again, and
that the Greeks, once they knew a son of Priam was living, would raise
another expedition against the Phrygians' country, and then they would
be trampling these plains of Thrace and plundering them, and the
neighbours of Troy would have the same trouble we were labouring under
just now, my lord.

Hecuba found out about her son's death, and she brought me here with a
story: she was going to show me the hidden vaults of the house of
Priam's gold in Troy. She took me into the tent alone with my children,
so that nobody else would know about it. I sat down on a couch in the
middle of them, with my knees bent. There were a lot of hands. The
girls of Troy had their seats on my left and on my right, as if beside
a friend, and they were praising the weaving of Edonian hands, holding
this cloak up to the light to look at it. Others were admiring my
Thracian spear, and between them they stripped me of both my defences.
And the ones who were mothers were making a great fuss over my
children, tossing them in their arms and passing them from hand to
hand, to get them away from their father. And then, out of that calm
talk, would you believe it, all at once they pull daggers from
somewhere in their dresses and stab the children, and the rest of them
grab me like enemies and hold my arms and legs. I wanted to help my
sons, and if I lifted my face, they held me down by the hair; if I
moved my hands, I could do nothing, poor man, against that crowd of
women. And at the last, pain on top of pain, they did the worst of
all. They took their brooches and stabbed the pupils of my eyes, my
poor eyes, and made them run with blood, and then they scattered
through the tent and ran. I leapt up like a wild beast and went after
the murdering bitches, hunting along every wall like a huntsman,
striking, battering.

That is what I have suffered for trying to do you a favour,
Agamemnon, and for killing an enemy of yours. And so as not to spin out
a long speech: if any man in the old days has spoken ill of women, or
if any man is speaking ill of them now, or is going to, I will put it
all in one sentence. Neither the sea nor the land breeds a race like
them. Anyone who has ever had dealings with them knows it.

Chorus.
Do not be so bold, and do not lump the whole female sex in with your
own troubles and blame us all together. There are many of us. Some of
us deserve to be hated, and some of us are born to be counted among the
bad. But not all.

Hecuba.
Agamemnon, men's tongues should never have been given more power than
their deeds. If a man had done good, he should have spoken well; if he
had done wrong, his words should have been rotten, and it should never
have been possible to make injustice sound good. The people who have
worked that art out to a fine point are clever, but they cannot go on
being clever to the end. They come to a bad end. Nobody has escaped it
yet.

That is my opening, and it was for you. Now I will go to this man and
answer him. You say you killed my son to save the Greeks a second war,
and for Agamemnon's sake. But first, you worst of men: the barbarian
race never could be friends with the Greeks, and never will be. It is
not possible. And what favour were you so eager to do? Were you
marrying into some family? Were you a relative? What reason did you
have? Or were they going to sail back here and cut down the crops of
your land? Who do you think is going to believe that? If you wanted to
tell the truth, it was the gold that killed my son. The gold, and your
profit.

Then explain this to me. When Troy was doing well, when the wall still
stood round the city, when Priam was alive and Hector's spear was in
flower, why did you not kill the boy then, if you really wanted to do
this man a favour? You had him in your house, you were bringing him up.
Or why not come to the Argives with him alive? No. It was when we were
no longer in the light, when the smoke showed that the city belonged to
the enemy, that you murdered a guest who had come to your hearth.

And on top of that, listen to this, so you can be shown for what you
are. If you were really a friend to the Greeks, you should have taken
the gold, which you say is not yours but his, and brought it and given
it to them, when they were poor and had been exiles from their own
country so long. But even now you cannot bring yourself to let it out
of your hand. You keep hold of it and hang on to it in your house. And
consider: if you had brought up my boy the way you were bound to bring
him up, and kept him safe, you would have had a fine name. It is in
trouble that good men show most clearly as friends; good luck finds
friends of its own accord, every time. If you had run short of money
and he had prospered, my son would have been a great treasure for you
to draw on. As it is, you no longer have him for your friend, the good
of the gold is gone, your sons are gone, and you yourself are as you
are.

And I say this to you, Agamemnon. If you help this man, you will be
shown for a bad man. You will be doing good to a guest who was not
god-fearing, not faithful where he should have been faithful, not
holy, not just. And we shall say that you take pleasure in bad men
because you are one yourself. But I do not abuse my masters.

Chorus.
Pheu, pheu. A good cause always gives a man the start of a good speech.

Agamemnon.
It is a heavy thing for me to judge other people's evils, but I have to
all the same. It would be a disgrace to take this business into my
hands and then push it away. As for my opinion, so that you know it:
it does not look to me as if you killed your guest as a favour to me,
or to the Greeks. It looks as if you did it to have the gold in your
own house. Now that you are in trouble, you say what suits you. Perhaps
among your people it is an easy thing to kill a guest. Among us Greeks
it is a disgrace. How can I judge you not guilty and escape the blame?
I cannot. You had the nerve to do what was not right. Now bear what you
will not like.

Polymestor.
Oh. Beaten by a woman, it seems, and a slave, I am to pay the penalty
to people who are beneath me.

Agamemnon.
And is it not just, if you did the evil?

Polymestor.
Oh, my children. Oh, my eyes. Misery.

Hecuba.
You are in pain? And what about me? Do you think I feel no pain for my
son?

Polymestor.
It makes you happy, does it, this outrage against me, you criminal?

Hecuba.
And why should I not be happy, when I have my revenge on you?

Polymestor.
Not for long. Not once the salt spray of the sea...

Hecuba.
Carries me by ship to the borders of Greece, you mean?

Polymestor.
Closes over you, when you fall from the masthead.

Hecuba.
And who is going to force me to make that jump?

Polymestor.
You will climb the ship's mast yourself, on your own feet.

Hecuba.
With wings on my back? Or how?

Polymestor.
You will turn into a dog, with eyes of fire.

Hecuba.
And how do you know about this change in my shape?

Polymestor.
Dionysus, the prophet of the Thracians, told me.

Hecuba.
And he told you nothing about the evils you have now?

Polymestor.
No. Or you would never have caught me with a trick like this.

Hecuba.
Dead or alive, is it here that I will finish my life?

Polymestor.
Dead. And your tomb will be given a name...

Hecuba.
After my shape, you will say? Or what?

Polymestor.
The Grave of the Miserable Dog. A landmark for sailors.

Hecuba.
I do not care, so long as you have paid me the penalty.

Polymestor.
And your daughter Cassandra will have to die too.

Hecuba.
I spit on that. I give it back to you, to keep for yourself.

Polymestor.
This man's wife will kill her. The bitter keeper of his house.

Hecuba.
May the daughter of Tyndareus never go so mad as that.

Polymestor.
And she will kill this man too, with an axe raised high over her head.

Agamemnon.
You there. Are you mad? Are you asking for trouble?

Polymestor.
Kill me. A bath of blood is waiting for you in Argos.

Agamemnon.
Slaves, drag him out of my sight, by force.

Polymestor.
Does it hurt to hear it?

Agamemnon.
Shut his mouth.

Polymestor.
Shut it. It has been said.

Agamemnon.
Now, as fast as you can, throw him out on some deserted island, since
his mouth is so very bold. And you, Hecuba, poor woman, go and bury
your two dead. And you women of Troy must go to your masters' tents.
I can see the wind already, the wind that will carry us home. May we
sail safely home to our own country, and find everything well in our
houses, set free from all this trouble.

(Agamemnon's men drag Polymestor away. Agamemnon goes. Hecuba goes
to her dead.)

Chorus.
	Go to the harbour, friends, and to the tents,
	to taste the labour of slaves
	under masters. Necessity is hard.
