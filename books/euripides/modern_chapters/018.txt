The Suppliants
(Part 2 of 3)

Herald.
Who is the king of this country? Who do I deliver my message to? I
bring the words of Creon, who rules the land of Cadmus now that
Eteocles is dead at the seven gates, killed by his own brother's hand,
by Polyneices.

Theseus.
You have started off with a mistake, stranger, looking for a king
here. This city is not ruled by one man. It is free. The people rule
it, taking their turn in office year by year, and wealth does not get
the biggest share. The poor man has an equal one.

Herald.
Well, that is one point you hand to us, like a piece given away in a
board game. The city I come from is governed by one man, not by a
crowd. There is nobody there who puffs the people up with speeches and
turns them this way and that for his own private profit, sweet as
honey for the moment and handing out favours, and then doing them harm
later, and then covering his old blunders with fresh slanders and
slipping clean out of justice. Besides, how can the people steer a
city straight when they cannot even judge an argument straight? Time
is a better teacher than haste. A poor man who works the land, even if
he happens not to be stupid, has his hands too full to keep an eye on
public business. And it makes the better sort sick, I can tell you,
when some worthless man who was nothing yesterday gets a position by
getting hold of the people with his tongue.

Theseus.
A polished herald. He does a little speech-making on the side. Well,
since you have entered this contest of your own accord, listen. You
set up the debate. There is nothing more hostile to a city than a
tyrant. Under a tyrant, first of all, there are no laws that belong to
everyone. One man rules, and the law is his private property, and
right there equality is gone. When the laws are written down, the weak
man and the rich man have equal justice. The weaker can answer the man
who is doing well in the same terms he is spoken to in, and the little
man beats the great one if he has right on his side. This is freedom:
"Who has good advice for the city and wants to bring it before us?"
The man who wants to speak, shines. The man who does not, keeps
silent. What is fairer to a city than that?

And there is this. Where the people are masters of the land, they take
pleasure in the young men coming up under them. A king sees them as
enemies. He kills the best men, and any man he thinks has a mind of
his own, because he is afraid for his throne. So how can a city go on
being strong when someone is cutting the daring out of it, like a man
scything the ears off a spring meadow, and plucking its young men
away? Why gather wealth and a livelihood for your children, so a
tyrant can live better on your labour? Why bring your daughters up
decently at home, to be a sweet treat for a tyrant whenever he feels
like it, and tears for the parents who raised them? I would rather be
dead than see my children married off by force.

That is my volley, fired back at yours. Now: what do you want from this
country? If your city had not sent you, you would have paid in tears
for all this extra talking. A messenger's job is to say what he was
told to say and get back as fast as he can. Next time Creon can send
my city a messenger who talks less than you.

Chorus.
Pheu, pheu. When a god gives good fortune to bad men, they turn to
outrage, as if their luck would last forever.

Herald.
Now I will say my piece. On the points we have been arguing, you think
what you think and I think the opposite. I forbid you, I and the whole
people of Cadmus, to let Adrastus into this land. If he is in it, then
before the sun's light goes down, untie the holy suppliant wreaths and
drive him out of it, and do not take up the dead by force. You have no
business with the city of Argos. If you listen to me, you will steer
your city on a calm sea. If you do not, there will be a great surge of
war, for us, for you, and for your allies.

Think about it. Do not lose your temper at my words and, because you
have a free city, answer me with a speech that flexes its arms. Hope
is not to be trusted. It has thrown many cities into war by driving
their spirit too far. When war comes up for a vote before the people,
nobody reckons on his own death. He assumes that piece of bad luck
belongs to somebody else. If death stood before each man's eyes as he
cast his vote, Greece would not be dying of spear-madness. And yet
every one of us knows which of two arguments is the better, and what
is good and what is bad, and how much better peace is for human beings
than war. Peace is the dearest friend the Muses have, and the enemy of
the Spirits of Vengeance. She delights in fine children. She rejoices
in wealth. And we throw all that away, we bad men, and take up wars,
and enslave whoever is weaker, man enslaving man and city enslaving
city.

And you: you are helping men who are your enemies even in death,
burying them and carrying them home, when it was their own outrage
that destroyed them? Then was it wrong that Capaneus's body still
smokes where the lightning struck him? He set the uprights of his
ladder against our gates and swore he would sack the city whether the
god liked it or not. Was it wrong that the earth opened and swallowed
the seer Amphiaraus, chariot and four horses and all? And that the
other captains lie at the gates with the seams of their skulls torn
open by rocks? So either boast that you know better than Zeus, or
admit that the gods are right to destroy bad men.

A sensible man loves his children first, and then his parents and his
country, and his country is something to build up, not to break down.
A bold leader is a dangerous thing. A sailor who keeps quiet and knows
his moment is the wise one. And to my mind that is courage:
forethought.

Chorus.
Zeus has punished us. That was enough. You had no need to add this
outrage on top of it.

Adrastus.
You worthless, worthless —

Theseus.
Quiet, Adrastus. Hold your tongue. Do not put your words in front of
mine. This man has not come with his proclamation to you but to me,
and it is for me to answer him.

I will take your first points first. I am not aware that Creon is my
master, or that he has more power than I have, so that he can force
Athens to do this. Rivers would be running uphill if we took our
orders that way. And I am not the one starting this war. I did not go
with these men to the land of Cadmus. What I claim is the right to
bury the men who have died, doing no harm to your city and bringing no
man-killing battle to it, but keeping the law of all the Greeks. What
is wrong with that? If the Argives did something to you, they are dead
for it. You beat off your enemies with honour, and with shame to them,
and justice has run its course. Let the dead be covered with earth
now. Let each part of them go back where it came from when it came
into the light: the breath to the air, the body to the ground. We do
not own our bodies. We live in them for a while, and afterwards the
earth that fed them has to take them back.

Do you think it is Argos you are hurting by refusing to bury the dead?
Not at all. This is the common business of all Greece, if a man keeps
the dead from what they are owed and leaves them lying unburied. Make
that a law, and it will put cowardice into brave men. And you have
come to me hurling dreadful threats, while your own people are afraid
of a few corpses, if they are hidden in the ground? Afraid of what?
That they will dig up your country from underneath, once they are
buried? Or breed children in the hollows of the earth, and one of them
will grow up to be an avenger? That is a stupid waste of breath, being
frightened of fears that are wretched and empty.

You fools. Learn what human beings are up against. Our life is a
wrestling match. Some men are lucky soon, some later, some right now,
and Fortune is a pampered god. The unlucky man honours her and pays
her court so that his luck will turn, and the prosperous man praises
her to the sky because he is afraid her breeze will drop. Knowing
that, we should bear a moderate wrong without rage, and do only such
wrong as does no harm to a city. So how will it be? Give us the fallen
dead to bury, since we are the ones who want to do what is right. Or
else the next step is plain: I will go and bury them by force. It will
never be reported around Greece that the ancient law of the gods came
to me, and to the city of Pandion, and was destroyed there.

Chorus.
Take heart. Keep the light of Justice burning and you will escape most
of what men find to blame.

Herald.
Do you want me to put your whole speech in a few words?

Theseus.
Say what you like. You are not going to be silent anyway.

Herald.
You will never take the sons of Argos out of our land.

Theseus.
Then hear my answer to that, if you like.

Herald.
I will hear it. Not that I want to, but I have to give you your turn.

Theseus.
I will bury the dead. I will take them out of the land of Asopus and
bury them.

Herald.
You will have to risk it among the shields first.

Theseus.
I have been through plenty of other labours before this.

Herald.
Did your father breed you to be a match for everybody?

Theseus.
For everybody who commits outrage, yes. We do not punish good
behaviour.

Herald.
Meddling in everything. That is your habit, and your city's.

Theseus.
Which is why she works so hard, and why she does so well.

Herald.
Come, then, and let the spear of the men sown in our soil catch you
inside our city.

Theseus.
What kind of furious war god could ever come out of a snake?

Herald.
You will learn that the hard way. You are still a boy.

Theseus.
You will not needle me into losing my temper with your boasting. Get
out of this country, and take the empty words you brought here with
you. We are getting nowhere.

(The Herald goes out, back the way he came.)

Every man in armour must move out now, and every man who rides a
chariot, and the horses' bits must start to move, dripping foam on the
land of Cadmus. I am going to the seven gates of Cadmus myself, with
sharp iron in my hands, and I will be my own herald. You I order to
stay here, Adrastus. Do not mix your luck with mine. I am going to
lead this army with my own fortune beside me, a new general with a new
spear. I need one thing only: to have the gods on my side, the ones
who honour justice. Have those together and you have victory. Courage
means nothing to human beings unless the god is willing.

(Theseus goes out to gather the army.)

Chorus.
	Unhappy mothers of unhappy captains,
	how the pale fear crouches under my heart.
	What is this new cry you are raising?
	Where will the army of Pallas be judged?
	Judged by the spear, do you mean, or by an exchange of words?
	Words would be a gain. But if the killing of battle,
	the fighting, the blows of hands on breasts, break out
	through the city again, then, poor wretch,
	what will they say of me, what blame will I carry, who am the cause?

Chorus.
	But a man who shines with good luck can be taken down
	by fate again. That is the thought that gives me courage.
	You are talking as if the gods were just.
	Who else deals out what happens to us?
	I see a great deal in the gods' dealings with us that does not agree.
	It is the old fear that is destroying you.
	Justice has called for justice, and blood for blood,
	and the gods give human beings a breathing space from evil.
	The end of everything is in their hands.

Chorus.
	How could we reach the plains under those fine towers,
	leaving the goddess's water here, the well of Callichorus?
	If one of the gods would give you wings,
	so I could come to the city of the two rivers, Thebes.
	Then you would know, you would know how your friends have fared.
	What fate, what end
	is waiting for the brave man
	who is king of this land?

Chorus.
	The gods we called on before, we call on again.
	Yes. When we are afraid, that is the first thing we trust.
	Io, Zeus, who fathered a child on the heifer long ago,
	our first mother, the daughter of Inachus:
	be kind to this city of mine. Fight beside it.
	Your pride, your foundation stone,
	the men on whom you built our city,
	I am carrying to the pyre, outraged.

(A Messenger comes in.)

Messenger.
Women, I come with a great deal of good news to tell you, and I have
come through alive myself. I was taken prisoner in the battle that the
seven companies fought beside the stream of Dirce, the companies of
the seven dead masters. And I come to announce the victory of Theseus.
I will save you a long round of questions. I was the servant of
Capaneus, the man Zeus burned to ash with his fiery lightning.

Chorus.
Dearest of men, it is good news that you have come back, and good news
about Theseus. If the army of Athens is safe as well, then everything
you tell us will be good.

Messenger.
Safe, and it has done what Adrastus should have done with the
Argives, when he led them out from the river Inachus against the city
of the Cadmeans.

Chorus.
How did the son of Aegeus and the men who shared the fight with him
set up Zeus's trophy? Tell us. You were there, and we were not, and
you will make us glad.

Messenger.
The bright ray of the sun, a clear straight line, was striking the
earth. I stood by the Electran gates to watch, from a tower with a
clear view. And I saw three divisions of three armies. The men in
armour were stretched out up towards the hill of Ismenus, so I was
told, and the king himself, the famous son of Aegeus, and the men with
him, the people of the old city, were drawn up on the right wing. The
men from the coast were on the left, equal in number, equipped with
spears, right beside the spring of Ares, with the chariots, and the
mass of the cavalry was drawn up on the edges of the camp. The people
of Cadmus were sitting in front of the walls, with the dead, who were
what the fight was about, placed behind them, under the holy tombs of
Amphion. Horsemen were armed against horsemen, and four-horse chariots
stood against chariots.

Then Theseus's herald spoke to everyone. "Silence, peoples. Silence,
ranks of the Cadmeans, and listen. We have come for the dead. We want
to bury them, keeping the law of all the Greeks. We have no wish to
draw out the slaughter." Creon made no proclamation in answer to that.
He sat there by his arms in silence. Then the drivers of the four-horse
chariots began the battle. They drove their chariots through past one
another and set down the fighting men who rode beside them into the
line of battle. Some of these fought it out with iron, while the
drivers wheeled the horses back into the fight to pick their men up
again. Phorbas, who commanded the cavalry for the sons of Erechtheus,
saw the mass of chariots, and so did the men guarding Cadmus's horse,
and they joined battle, and they won and they lost by turns.

I saw all this. I did not hear about it. I was there, right where the
chariots and the men who rode them were fighting, and out of all the
misery that was there I do not know what to tell you first. The dust
going up to the sky, there was so much of it? Or the men being dragged
up and down in their reins, and the streams of red blood, as some fell
and others were thrown headfirst out of their smashed chariots and
slammed into the ground, and left their lives lying among the wreckage
of the cars?

When Creon saw that our side was winning with the horse, he took a
shield in his hand and went forward himself, before despair could
reach his allies. And they crashed together in the middle of the whole
army, and killed and were killed, and passed the order along to one
another with a great shout: "Strike! Hold your spears against the sons
of Erechtheus!" And Theseus's side was not lost through hesitation.
He snatched up his shining armour and went straight at them. The
company that grew to manhood from the serpent's teeth was a terrible
wrestler. It bent our left wing back. But their left was beaten by our
right and ran, so the fight was evenly balanced. And there the general
deserved his praise. He did not simply take his profit from the part
that was winning. He went to the part of his own army that was giving
way, and he broke out in a shout that made the ground ring: "Boys, if
you do not hold the stubborn spear of these sown men, the cause of
Pallas is lost." That put heart into the whole army of Athens. And he
took up his own weapon, the club he carried from Epidaurus, that
terrible thing, and swung it round and round, and swept off necks and
the heads that sat on them together, harvesting helmets and snapping
them off at the stalk with the wood. Even then they barely turned and
ran.

I shouted for joy and danced and clapped my hands. They were streaming
for the gates. There was shouting and wailing through the city, young
and old, and they crowded the temples in their fear. And though he
could have gone inside the walls, Theseus held back. He said he had
not come to sack the city but to ask for the dead. That is the kind of
general a people should choose: a man who is brave in danger, and who
hates a people that turns to outrage, that does well and then goes
looking for the top rung of the ladder, and throws away the good
fortune it could have used.

Chorus.
Now that I have seen this day I never hoped to see, I believe in the
gods. And I think my misfortune weighs less, now that these men have
paid the penalty.

Adrastus.
Zeus, why do they say that wretched human beings have any sense? We
hang from you. We do whatever you happen to want. To us Argos was
irresistible, and we were many, and young, with strong arms. When
Eteocles offered terms, and moderate terms, we would not take them,
and then we were destroyed. And then the winners, the ill-minded
people of Cadmus, behaved like a poor man who has just come into
money: they turned to outrage, and their outrage destroyed them in
their turn. You men who draw the bow past the mark. You empty things,
who suffer so many evils, and deserve to, who will not listen to
friends but only to events. And you cities, who could bend your
troubles aside with talk, and settle your business with slaughter
instead of speech.

But why go on about that? What I want to know is how you got away,
and then I will ask the rest.

Messenger.
When the fighting threw the city into confusion, I walked out through
the gates, the same gates the army was coming in by.

Adrastus.
And the dead, the men the whole fight was about: are you bringing them?

Messenger.
The ones who commanded the seven famous companies, yes.

Adrastus.
What do you mean? The rest of the dead, all those others: where are
they?

Messenger.
They have been given burial in the folds of Cithaeron.

Adrastus.
On that side of the mountain or this? And who buried them?

Messenger.
Theseus. Where the shady rock of Eleutherae stands.

Adrastus.
And the ones he did not bury: where did you leave them when you came?

Messenger.
Close by. Anything you are eager for is close.

Adrastus.
I suppose the servants hated bringing them in from the slaughter.

Messenger.
Not one slave was set to that work.

Messenger.
You would say the same if you had been there, when he tended the dead.

Adrastus.
He washed those poor men's wounds himself?

Messenger.
And laid out their beds, and covered their bodies.

Adrastus.
That was a dreadful load to carry, and there was shame in it.

Messenger.
What is shameful to men about other men's suffering?

Adrastus.
Oh. How gladly I would have died with them.

Messenger.
You are grieving for nothing, and you are drawing tears from these
women.

Adrastus.
I suppose I am. They are the ones who taught me. Well then. I raise my
hand to meet the dead, and I pour out the tearful songs of Hades,
calling to my friends. They have left me behind, and I am the wretch
who weeps alone. This is the one expense human beings can never
recover once it is spent, a human life. For money there are always
ways.

Chorus.
	Some of it is good, some of it is grief.
	For the city, glory,
	and for the generals of the spear,
	honour doubled.
	For me, to see my children's limbs
	is bitter, and a fine sight, if I do see it,
	having seen the day I never hoped for,
	the greatest grief of all.

Chorus.
	Unmarried. Old Time, the father,
	should have made me that,
	and left me so to this day.
	What did I need children for?
	I thought it would be some terrible thing to suffer,
	if I were never yoked in marriage.
	Now I see the evil plain as day,
	stripped of the children I loved most.

(The bodies of the dead captains are carried in.)

Chorus.
	But now I see them, the bodies
	of my children who are gone. Wretched woman,
	how can I die with these children of mine,
	and go down to Hades with them together?
