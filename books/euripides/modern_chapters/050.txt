Iphigenia at Aulis
(Part 1 of 3)

(Night, on the beach at Aulis, in front of Agamemnon's tent. The Greek
fleet lies drawn up along the shore. Agamemnon comes out carrying a
folding writing tablet and a lamp.)

Agamemnon.
Leda, the daughter of Thestius, had three girls: Phoebe, and
Clytemnestra, who is my wife, and Helen. And it was Helen the young men
of Greece came courting, the ones who had had the best of life so far.
There were terrible threats between them, and blood, each one of them
ready to kill the rest if he did not get the girl. Her father Tyndareus
could see no way through it. Give her, do not give her, how to take
hold of his luck and come off best. And then this came to him. The
suitors should swear oaths to one another, and clasp right hands, and
pour offerings over a burnt sacrifice, and bind themselves with this
curse: whichever of them got Tyndareus's daughter for a wife, they
would all stand by that man, if anybody ever took her out of his house
and went off with her and pushed the husband out of his own bed. They
would march against that man in armour and level his city to the
ground, Greek or barbarian, it would make no difference. And once they
had given their word — and the old man had come at them well, and
worked round them with a shrewd mind — he let his daughter choose one
of the suitors for herself, whichever one the sweet breezes of
Aphrodite carried her toward.

And she chose. I wish to god he had never taken her. She chose
Menelaus. And then out of Phrygia came the man who judged between the
goddesses, Paris, the way the story runs among the Argives, and he came
to Sparta: flowering in his clothes, a blaze of gold on him, barbarian
luxury. He was in love with her and she was in love with him, and he
snatched her up and was gone with her to the cattle pens of Ida, and he
took his moment while Menelaus was away from home. And Menelaus went
running the length of Greece like a beast with a sting in it, calling
Tyndareus's old oath to witness, saying they were bound to help a man
who had been wronged.

So the Greeks came at it with the spear. They took up their armour and
came here, to the narrow water at Aulis, with ships and shields
together, with plenty of horses and chariots. And they picked me to be
general, and they did it for Menelaus's sake, because I am his brother.
I wish somebody else had picked up that honour instead of me.

Then, with the army gathered and standing together, we sat here at
Aulis and could not sail. We were at a loss, and Calchas the prophet
told us this: sacrifice Iphigenia, my own daughter, to Artemis, who
lives in this country. If we sacrificed her, we would sail and we would
tear the Phrygians' city down; if we did not sacrifice her, none of it
would happen. And when I heard it, I told Talthybius to give the order
out at the top of his voice and let the whole army go home, because I
would never have the nerve to kill my daughter. And then my brother
came at me with every argument there is, and talked me into daring a
terrible thing. So I wrote it in the folds of a tablet and sent it to
my wife: send our daughter here to be married to Achilles. I made a
great deal of the man's standing, and I said he refused to sail with
the Achaeans unless a bride out of our house went home with him to
Phthia. That was the persuasion I used on my wife. I put a marriage
together for the girl out of nothing.

Of all the Achaeans, only we know how it really stands: Calchas,
Odysseus, Menelaus, and I. And what I decided badly then, I have now
written out again, and written it well, in this tablet here — the one
you saw me untying and tying up again in the shadow of the night, old
man. Come on, take these letters and go to Argos. And what the tablet
keeps hidden in its folds, I will tell you out loud, all of it, every
word that is written there. You are loyal to my wife and to my house.

Agamemnon.
	Old man, come out here, out in front of the tent.

Old Man.
	I am coming. What is the new plan,
	Agamemnon, my lord?

Agamemnon.
	Will you hurry?

Old Man.
	I am hurrying.
	Old age gets very little sleep,
	and it keeps a sharp watch on my eyes.

Agamemnon.
	What star is that, the one steering across up there?

Old Man.
	Sirius, running close to the seven roads
	of the Pleiades, still high at the middle of the sky.

Agamemnon.
	No sound of birds, then, and no sound
	from the sea. The winds are keeping their silence
	over this water, over the Euripus.

Old Man.
	Then why are you rushing about outside the tent,
	Agamemnon, my lord?
	It is still quiet here in Aulis
	and the guard on the walls has not stirred.
	Let us go inside.

Agamemnon.
	I envy you, old man.
	I envy any man who comes to the end of a life
	with no danger in it, unknown, with no fame.
	The ones with honours I envy less.

Old Man.
	And yet that is where the beauty of a life is.

Agamemnon.
	That beauty is a thing a man slips on,
	and being honoured above other men
	is sweet, and it hurts when it stands close to you.
	Sometimes it is the gods' business, not set straight,
	that turns a life over; sometimes it is people,
	their many opinions,
	hard to please, grinding you away.

Old Man.
	I do not admire this in a man who is the best of us.
	Atreus did not father you, Agamemnon,
	for nothing but good things.
	You have to feel joy and you have to feel pain.
	You were born mortal. Even if you do not want it,
	that is what the gods want, and that is how it will be.
	And you have opened out the light of the lamp
	and you are writing that tablet,
	the one you are still carrying there in your hands,
	and then you smear out the same letters again,
	and seal it, and untie it again afterwards,
	and throw the pinewood down on the ground,
	crying, the tears coming thick,
	and there is nothing in the whole list of helpless things
	that you are leaving out. You look like a madman.
	What is the trouble? What is new with you, king?
	Come on, share the story with me.
	You will be telling it to a good man, and a loyal one.
	Tyndareus sent me along with your wife once,
	as part of her dowry,
	to look after the bride, and to do it honestly.

Agamemnon.
	I am sending you word, on top of the letters
	I sent before, daughter of Leda —

Old Man.
	Say it, spell it out, so that my tongue
	can say the same thing your writing says.

Agamemnon.
	Do not send your girl to the
	deep-bayed wing of Euboea,
	to Aulis, where no wave comes.
	We will hold our child's wedding feast
	in some other season.

Old Man.
	And Achilles, cheated of his bride —
	how will he not swell up with a huge anger
	against you and against your wife?
	That is dangerous too. Tell me what you mean.

Agamemnon.
	Achilles is lending a name, not a deed.
	He knows nothing about a wedding, or about what we are doing,
	or that I promised the girl to him,
	that I would give her over to a bridegroom's arms
	and a bridegroom's bed.

Old Man.
	That was a terrible thing to dare, Agamemnon, my lord.
	You said your daughter was a wife for the goddess's son
	and you were bringing her here as a throat to cut for the Greeks.

Agamemnon.
	Oh. I have gone out of my mind.
	Aiai. I am falling into ruin.
	Go on now, row your feet along,
	and give nothing away to your age.

Old Man.
	I am hurrying, king.

Agamemnon.
	Do not sit down beside the springs in the woods
	and do not let sleep charm you.

Old Man.
	Do not say such things.

Agamemnon.
	And wherever the road splits, cross it
	looking hard, keeping watch that no wagon
	slips by you on its rolling wheels
	and gets past, bringing my child here
	to the ships of the Greeks.

Old Man.
	It will be done.

Agamemnon.
	And if you meet her with her escort
	already out through the gates,
	turn them back, shake the reins,
	send them off to the walls the Cyclopes built.

Old Man.
	Tell me, when I say all this,
	how am I to be believed by your daughter and your wife?

Agamemnon.
	Keep the seal, the one on the tablet
	you are carrying. Go. The dawn
	is whitening this light already, burning,
	and so is the fire of the Sun's four horses.
	Take some of this labour off me.
	No mortal is fortunate all the way to the end.
	Nobody is happy.
	No one was ever born without pain.

(The Old Man hurries away. Agamemnon goes into the tent. It is
daylight. A group of young women from Chalcis come along the shore,
looking at the ships.)

Chorus.
	I came down to the sandy beach
	beside the sea at Aulis,
	sailing across the currents
	of the narrow Euripus,
	leaving Chalcis behind me, my own city,
	that feeds the waters of Arethusa,
	the famous spring beside the sea,
	so that I could see the army of the Achaeans
	and the oared ships of the Achaeans
	that carry the half-gods across,
	the ones our husbands say
	fair-haired Menelaus
	and high-born Agamemnon
	are leading to Troy
	in a thousand ships of fir,
	after Helen, whom Paris the herdsman
	took from the reedy banks of the Eurotas,
	a gift to him from Aphrodite,
	when beside the dripping spring
	she stood up against Hera and Pallas
	in a quarrel, a quarrel about beauty.

Chorus.
	I came through the grove of Artemis,
	where so much is killed on the altars,
	running, and my cheek went red
	with a shame that was new to me,
	because I wanted to see the wall of shields
	and the tents of the Greeks under arms
	and the crowd of their horses.
	I saw the two Ajaxes sitting together,
	the son of Oileus and the son of Telamon,
	the crown of Salamis; and Protesilaus
	and Palamedes, whose father was Poseidon's son,
	enjoying themselves over a board
	at a game of counters with complicated shapes;
	and Diomedes, taking his pleasure
	in the throwing of the discus;
	and beside him Meriones,
	a shoot of the war god, a wonder to look at;
	and the son of Laertes, Odysseus,
	down from his island hills,
	and with him Nireus,
	the best-looking man among the Achaeans.

Chorus.
	And Achilles, quick as the wind on his feet,
	the light-running one,
	whom Thetis bore and Chiron finished,
	I saw him along the beach,
	down on the shingle,
	running a race in his armour.
	He was working his feet against
	a four-horse chariot,
	wheeling round the turn for the win.
	And the driver was shouting,
	Eumelus, the grandson of Pheres,
	and I saw his horses, the loveliest,
	their bits worked in gold,
	taking the goad:
	the middle pair, the yoke-horses,
	dappled, with white flecks in their coats,
	and the outside pair, the trace-horses,
	leaning into the bends of the course,
	fire-red, with spotted skin
	above their single hooves. And beside them
	the son of Peleus was springing along in his armour,
	level with the rail and the axle-box of the car.

Chorus.
	And I came to count the ships,
	a sight there are no words for,
	to fill a girl's eyes with looking,
	a sweet pleasure, like honey.
	The right wing was held
	by the war of the Myrmidons out of Phthia,
	fifty ships that come on hard,
	and high on the sterns stood
	Nereid goddesses in gold,
	the badge of Achilles' fleet.

Chorus.
	Next to them the Argive ships lay,
	with as many oars as those,
	and the son of Mecisteus commanded them,
	whom his grandfather Talaus brought up,
	and Sthenelus, the son of Capaneus.
	And next in the line, with sixty ships
	out of Attica, the son of Theseus
	was anchored, and he had the goddess
	Pallas set on a winged chariot
	drawn by whole-hoofed horses:
	a lucky sign for men at sea.

Chorus.
	And I saw the sea-armour of the Boeotians,
	fifty ships dressed out with their signs.
	Theirs was Cadmus, holding a snake of gold,
	up at the beaks of the ships,
	and Leitus, who was born out of the earth,
	was in command of that fleet.
	And from the land of Phocis,
	and from Locris, with as many ships as those,
	came the son of Oileus,
	who had left the famous town of Thronium.

Chorus.
	And from Mycenae, the city the Cyclopes built,
	the son of Atreus sent his crews,
	a hundred ships gathered together,
	and Adrastus was with him
	as a leader, friend beside friend,
	so that Greece could take payment
	for the woman who ran out of her house
	for the sake of a barbarian marriage.
	And out of Pylos I saw, on the sterns
	of Nestor of Gerenia,
	the sign of his neighbour the Alpheus,
	a river drawn with a bull's feet.

Chorus.
	And there was a squadron of twelve
	Aenianian ships, with lord Gouneus
	in command of them. And near those,
	the rulers of Elis,
	whom all the people call the Epeians,
	and Eurytus was their master.
	And he led the white-oared war of Taphos,
	whose lord was Meges, the child of Phyleus,
	who had left the Echinae behind,
	islands where a sailor cannot put in.

Chorus.
	And Ajax, brought up in Salamis,
	was joining his right wing to the left
	of the fleet he was moored nearest to,
	locking them in with his outermost oars,
	twelve ships, the handiest that turn.
	That is what I heard, and I saw
	the men who row them.
	If anyone brings barbarian boats up
	to grapple with that,
	he will carry no homecoming away.
	Such was the fleet under sail
	that I looked at here,
	and the rest I heard at home,
	about the gathered army, and I hold it in my memory.

(The Old Man comes back along the shore, struggling with Menelaus, who
has the tablet in his hand.)

Old Man.
Menelaus, you have the nerve for terrible things, and no right to them.

Menelaus.
Get away. You are far too loyal to your masters.

Old Man.
That is a fine insult you have insulted me with.

Menelaus.
You will be sorry, if you do what is not yours to do.

Old Man.
You had no business breaking open a letter I was carrying.

Menelaus.
And you had no business carrying misery to every Greek alive.

Old Man.
Argue that out with somebody else. Let go of this and give it to me.

Menelaus.
I will not let go.

Old Man.
And I will not let go either.

Menelaus.
Then this staff will have your head bloody in a moment.

Old Man.
It is a fine thing to die for one's masters.

Menelaus.
Let go. For a slave you make long speeches.

Old Man.
Master, I am being wronged. He tore your letter out of my hands by
force, Agamemnon, and he will have nothing to do with what is right.

(Agamemnon comes out of the tent.)

Agamemnon.
Ah. What is this uproar at my door, this disorderly shouting?

Menelaus.
It is my story that has the right to speak first, not his.

Agamemnon.
And you, Menelaus, why have you come to a quarrel with this man, and
why are you dragging him about?

Menelaus.
Look at me. Let that be where my story starts.

Agamemnon.
Do you think I am afraid to lift my eyes? I am a son of Atreus.

Menelaus.
Do you see this tablet? The servant of the worst kind of writing?

Agamemnon.
I see it. And before anything else, get it out of your hands.

Menelaus.
No. Not until I have shown what is written in it to every Greek here.

Agamemnon.
So you broke the seals, and you know what it was no business of yours
to know?

Menelaus.
I opened it, yes. And I know what you have been doing behind our backs,
and it will hurt you.

Agamemnon.
And where did you get hold of it? Gods, what a shameless mind you have.

Menelaus.
I was waiting for your daughter to come from Argos to the camp.

Agamemnon.
And what business have you keeping watch on what is mine? Is that not
shamelessness?

Menelaus.
The itch to do it. That is why. I was not born your slave.

Agamemnon.
Is that not outrageous? Am I not to be allowed to run my own house?

Menelaus.
No, because your mind runs crooked. One thing now, another thing a
while ago, another in a moment.

Agamemnon.
Very pretty polish on a nasty argument. A clever tongue is a hateful
thing.

Menelaus.
And a mind that will not stay put is a dishonest possession, and no
use to the people you owe. I want to put you to the test, and you must
not turn away from the truth out of anger, and I will not strain the
case too hard either.

You remember when you were straining every muscle to lead the Greeks
against Troy — pretending you did not want it, wanting it badly all the
same — how humble you were then? Taking every man by the right hand,
keeping your doors unbolted for any citizen who felt like walking in,
giving every man in turn a chance to speak with you, even the ones who
did not want it, trying with your manners to buy popularity off the
open market? And then, when you had the command in your hands, you
changed your ways. You were no longer the friend to your old friends
that you used to be. You were hard to get at, and hardly ever inside
your own doors. A good man who comes into a great position ought not to
change his manners. That is exactly the moment when he ought to be
steadiest with the people he owes: when his own luck is good and he has
the most power to help them.

That is the first thing I went through, because that is where I first
found you out to be bad.

And then, when you came here to Aulis with the whole army of Greece,
you were nothing. The luck the gods dealt you knocked you off your
feet, and there was no wind to carry us. And the Greeks began saying
you should let the ships go and stop labouring at Aulis for nothing.
And what a wretched, confused look you had on your face, at the thought
that you commanded a thousand ships and were not going to fill Priam's
plain with spears. And you kept calling me in. "What am I to do? What
way out can I find, and where?" So that you would not be stripped of
the command and lose your fine name.

And then, when Calchas said in the middle of the rites that you should
sacrifice your daughter to Artemis, and that the Greeks would sail, you
were delighted in your heart. You gladly promised to sacrifice the
child. And you send word to your wife — willingly, nobody forced you,
do not tell me that — to send your daughter here, on the excuse of a
marriage to Achilles. And then you turn round, and you get caught
changing the writing to something else: that you will not be your
daughter's murderer after all. Exactly. And this is the same air that
heard you say the first thing.

Thousands of men have had this happen to them. They work hard at power
while they hold it, and then they get out of it badly, sometimes
because the citizens have no sense, and sometimes justly, because they
were not able themselves to keep a city safe.

It is Greece I groan for most, poor Greece. She wanted to do something
worth doing, and now she is going to let these barbarian nobodies get
away laughing at her, because of you and because of your daughter. I
would never put a man in charge of a country for his courage, or make
him commander of an army. A general has to have a mind on him. Any man
at all can rule a city, so long as he happens to have some
understanding.

Chorus.
It is a terrible thing when brothers come to words and to blows, once
they have fallen into a quarrel.

Agamemnon.
I want to say hard things to you, and say them well: briefly, and
without lifting my eyelids too high into a shameless stare, but keeping
myself in hand, as one brother should. A decent man knows how to feel
shame.

Tell me. Why are you breathing so hard, with blood in your eyes? Who is
wronging you? What is it you want? A good wife? I cannot supply you
with one. The one you did get, you ruled badly. Am I to pay the penalty
for your troubles, when I never made the mistake? Is it my ambition
that bites you? No. What you want is a good-looking woman in your arms,
and you have thrown reason and decency away to get her. A bad man's
pleasures are bad.

And if I judged wrongly before and have now changed to a better plan,
am I mad? You are the madder one. You lost a worthless wife and you
want her back, when a god handed you good luck by taking her away. It
was the suitors who swore Tyndareus's oath, out of bad judgement,
because they were all in love with the wedding. Hope is a god, I think,
and it was Hope that brought the thing about, rather than you and your
strength. Take them and go to war. They are ready enough, in the folly
of their minds. But the divine is not without understanding. It can
tell an oath that was badly fixed and forced out of a man.

I will not kill my own children. It is not going to happen that your
affairs come out well, against all justice, in a revenge for the worst
of wives, while the nights and the days grind me down in tears for
doing a lawless and unjust thing to children I fathered myself.

There. That is said to you briefly, and it is clear, and it is easy to
follow. If you will not think straight, I will set my own affairs in
order.

Chorus.
This is different from what was said before. But it is good, this:
sparing the children.

Menelaus.
Aiai. So I have no friends after all, wretched man that I am.

Agamemnon.
You have friends, if you are not trying to destroy them.

Menelaus.
And where will you show me that you were born from the same father as
I was?

Agamemnon.
I want to keep myself in hand along with you. I do not want to be sick
along with you.

Menelaus.
The people you owe should hurt along with the people they owe.

Agamemnon.
Call me in by doing me good, not by giving me pain.

Menelaus.
So you do not mean to take on this labour along with Greece?

Agamemnon.
Greece is sick, and so are you, by some god's doing.

Menelaus.
Go on, boast about your sceptre, now that you have betrayed your own
brother. I will go and look for other devices, and other friends—

(A Messenger comes running along the shore.)

Messenger.
Lord of all the Greeks, Agamemnon. I am here, and I have brought your
daughter, the one you called Iphigenia at home. Her mother is with her,
Clytemnestra your wife herself, and the child Orestes, so that you
might have some pleasure in seeing him, after being away from home so
long. But the road was long, and they are cooling their soft feet now
at a spring with a good flow of water, they and the horses; we turned
those loose in the green of the meadow to get a taste of grass. I have
come running ahead so that you could get ready for them.

The army knows already that your daughter has arrived. Rumour went
through it fast. The whole crowd is coming down to the beach at a run
to get a look at your child. People who are fortunate are famous
everywhere, and everyone stares at them. And they are saying: "Is it a
wedding? What is going on? Or has King Agamemnon been missing his
daughter and brought the girl here?" And from others you would have
heard this: "They are giving the girl her wedding-eve rites for
Artemis, the queen of Aulis. Who is going to marry her?"

Come on, then. Start the baskets for what comes next, and put wreaths
on your heads. And you, lord Menelaus, get the wedding song ready. Let
the pipe sound through the tents and let there be a stamping of feet.
This is a blessed day that has come for the girl.

Agamemnon.
Thank you. Now go inside. The rest will be well enough, as fortune
takes its course.

(The Messenger goes into the tent.)

Oh. What am I to say? Where do I begin? What a yoke of necessity I have
fallen under. A god has come at me from underneath and turned out to be
a great deal cleverer than all my cleverness.

Low birth has something useful about it. Those men can cry easily and
say everything they feel. The well-born man has the same troubles and
none of that comfort. We have our dignity standing over our lives like
a master, and we are slaves to the crowd. I am ashamed to let a tear
fall, and then, wretched man, I am ashamed not to cry, when I have come
into the greatest disasters there are.

Well. What am I going to say to my wife? How will I receive her? What
sort of face will I meet her with? She has ruined me, coming when I did
not call for her, on top of everything else. And of course she came,
with her daughter, to give the bride away, to hand over what she loves
most — and she is going to find out what kind of men we are.

And the poor girl. Girl? What girl? It looks as though Hades is going
to marry her soon. How I pity her. I think she will beg me like this.
"Father, are you going to kill me? Then I hope you make a marriage like
this yourself, and everyone who is a friend to you." And Orestes will
be standing there beside her, shouting things that make no sense and
make all the sense in the world, because he is still a baby.

Aiai. Priam's son Paris destroyed me when he married Helen. He is the
one who has done all this.

Chorus.
I pity her myself, as far as a woman from another country can grieve
over the troubles of kings.

Menelaus.
Brother. Give me your right hand to hold.

Agamemnon.
Take it. You have won, and I am the wretched one.

Menelaus.
I swear by Pelops, who was called my father's father and yours, and by
Atreus who fathered us both, that I will tell you what is in my heart,
plainly, nothing said for effect, only what I think.

When I saw you letting a tear fall out of your eyes, I pitied you, and
I let one fall for you in return. I take back what I said before. I am
not going to be a terror to you. I stand where you are standing now.
And this is my advice: do not kill your child, and do not choose my
business ahead of your own. It is not just that you should be groaning
while my affairs go pleasantly, that your children should die while
mine look at the light.

What is it I want, after all? If it is a marriage I am longing for,
could I not get another bride, and a fine one? Was I to destroy my
brother — the last man on earth I ought to destroy — and take Helen,
getting the bad thing in place of the good one? I was out of my mind. I
was young about it. Then I looked at the business from close up and saw
what killing children means.

And besides that, pity for the poor girl came over me, when I thought
about the blood between us. She is going to have her throat cut for the
sake of my marriage. What has your girl to do with Helen?

Let the army break up and go from Aulis. And you, brother, stop soaking
your eyes with tears, and stop calling me into tears with you. Whatever
share you have in the oracles about your daughter, let me have none of
it. I hand over my part to you.

A sudden turn, you will say, after all those terrible words of mine?
What has happened to me is natural. I loved the man born from the same
blood as me, and I changed. These are the turns a man makes who is not
a bad one: to take whatever is best, every time.

Chorus.
That was nobly said, and worthy of Tantalus, the son of Zeus. You do
not shame your ancestors.

Agamemnon.
I praise you, Menelaus, for putting the argument down in the right
place, against everything I expected of you, and worthily of yourself.
Brothers fall into confusion over love, and over greed for the family
property. I spit on a family tie like that, bitter for both of them.
But we have come to a hard necessity: to carry out the bloody murder of
my daughter.

Menelaus.
How? Who is going to force you to kill your own child?

Agamemnon.
The whole gathered assembly of the Achaean army.

Menelaus.
Not if you send her back to Argos.

Agamemnon.
I could do that without being noticed. There is another thing I will
not get away with.

Menelaus.
What thing? You must not be too frightened of a crowd.

Agamemnon.
Calchas will tell his prophecies to the Argive army.

Menelaus.
Not if he dies first. That is easily done.

Agamemnon.
The whole breed of prophets is a bad thing, chasing honour.

Menelaus.
And no use at all, and no good to anybody when they are among us.

Agamemnon.
And the other thing that is coming over me: does that not frighten you?

Menelaus.
How can I take up an argument you do not tell me?

Agamemnon.
The seed of Sisyphus knows all of it.

Menelaus.
Odysseus is not the man to do you or me any harm.

Agamemnon.
He was born shifty, always was, and he goes with the crowd.

Menelaus.
He is held fast by the love of honour. That is a terrible evil.

Agamemnon.
Then do you not think he will stand up in the middle of the Argives and
tell them the prophecies Calchas gave out, and how I promised the
goddess a victim and then broke my word? That I would sacrifice to
Artemis? And that he will carry the whole army away with him, and tell
the Argives to kill you and me and cut the girl's throat? And if I run
for Argos, they will come and take the place and tear it down to the
ground, walls and all, and the Cyclopes built those walls. That is what
my troubles are.

I am wretched. What a helpless place the gods have put me in now.

Do one thing for me, Menelaus, when you go through the army. See that
Clytemnestra does not learn about this, until I have taken my child and
handed her to Hades. Let me do this badly with the fewest tears
possible.

And you, women from abroad, keep silence.

(Menelaus goes off along the shore. Agamemnon goes into the tent.)

Chorus.
	Blessed are the ones who share
	the bed of Aphrodite
	with the goddess in her measure,
	and with themselves in hand,
	who get a calm sea
	instead of the maddening sting,
	there where Love with the golden hair
	draws two arrows of delight:
	one of them for a life of good days,
	one to throw a life into confusion.
	Aphrodite, most beautiful,
	I forbid that arrow my bedroom.
	Let my pleasure be measured,
	and my longings holy,
	and let me have my share of her,
	and put the great share away.

Chorus.
	People's natures are different,
	and their ways of living are different,
	but what is truly good is always plain.
	And a rearing that has been taught
	carries a person a long way toward being good.
	Knowing how to feel shame is wisdom,
	and it has this rare grace with it,
	that judgement lets you see
	what has to be done, and reputation
	sheds a glory over a life
	that never grows old.
	It is a great thing to hunt goodness down:
	for women, in a love kept out of sight,
	and among men, an order inside them,
	present in a thousand ways,
	which makes a city greater.

Chorus.
	You came, Paris, to the place where you were reared
	as a herdsman among the white heifers of Ida,
	piping in a foreign scale,
	breathing into your reeds
	an imitation of the Phrygian pipes of Olympus.
	The cows were grazing with their udders full
	when the judging of the goddesses drove you mad,
	the judging that sends you to Greece.
	And you stood in front of the ivory-inlaid house
	and put love into Helen's eyes
	as they looked back into yours,
	and you were shaken with love yourself.
	And out of that, quarrel brings quarrel
	and brings Greece with her spears and her ships
	against the towers of Troy.

(A carriage comes along the shore, with Clytemnestra and Iphigenia in
it, and an escort of soldiers.)

Argive Soldiers.
	Io, io. Great is the good fortune
	of great people. Look at Iphigenia,
	the king's child, my queen,
	and Clytemnestra, the daughter of Tyndareus.
	See what great stock they grew from,
	and how high a fortune they have come to.
	The powerful, the ones who carry riches,
	are gods to those mortals who have no luck.

Chorus.
	Let us stand here, we children of Chalcis,
	and take the queen down out of her carriage
	to the ground without a stumble,
	gently, with our hands, with a soft strength,
	so that Agamemnon's famous daughter,
	just come to me now, will not be frightened,
	and so that we, strangers to the women of Argos,
	give strangers no confusion and no shock.
