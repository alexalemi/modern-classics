Orestes
(Part 2 of 4)

Orestes.
There is a vote being taken against us. Today.

Menelaus.
On what? Banishment from the city? Or whether you live or die?

Orestes.
Whether I die. At the hands of the citizens, under a heap of stones.

Menelaus.
Then why not get over the border and run for it?

Orestes.
Because we are ringed round with men in bronze armour.

Menelaus.
Your own private enemies, or the armed force of Argos?

Orestes.
Every citizen in the place, so that I die. It is a short story.

Menelaus.
Poor man. You have come to the very end of misfortune.

Orestes.
You are the hope I have left. You are where I run to, out of all
this. You have come home lucky, and you find us in misery. Share your
good fortune with the people you owe. Do not take the good thing you
have got and keep it to yourself. Take your turn at carrying the
trouble too, and pay back what my father did for you to the people you
owe it to. Friends who are not friends when the trouble comes have the
name of friends and nothing else.

Chorus.
And here is Tyndareus the Spartan, hurrying this way as fast as an old
man's legs will carry him, dressed in black, his hair cropped in
mourning for his daughter.

Orestes.
I am finished, Menelaus. This is Tyndareus coming towards us, and of
all men alive he is the one I am most ashamed to look in the face,
after what I have done. He brought me up when I was small. He covered
me with kisses, carrying Agamemnon's boy round in his arms, and Leda
did the same, and the two of them made as much of me as they did of
their own sons, Castor and Polydeuces. And I have paid them back, oh my
wretched heart, my wretched soul, with this. Where is there a darkness
I can pull over my face? What cloud can I put between me and him, so
that I get away from the old man's eyes?

(Tyndareus comes in, an old man in black with his hair cut short, led
by his attendants.)

Tyndareus.
Where is he, where is my daughter's husband? Where can I see Menelaus?
I was pouring the offerings on Clytemnestra's grave when I heard that
he had put in at Nauplia, with his wife, safe after all these years.
Take me to him. I want to stand at his right hand and greet him. It is
a long time since I set eyes on a friend.

Menelaus.
Greetings, old man. You shared a wife with Zeus.

Tyndareus.
Greetings to you too, Menelaus, my daughter's husband. Ah. What an evil
thing it is, not to know the future. There he is, the mother-killer,
in front of the house, a snake, flashing the sick light out of his
eyes. I loathe him. Menelaus, are you speaking to him? To that godless
thing?

Menelaus.
Why not? He is the son of a man I loved.

Tyndareus.
That is Agamemnon's son? This thing here?

Menelaus.
It is. And when he is down on his luck, he is owed respect.

Tyndareus.
You have been too long among barbarians. You have turned into one
yourself.

Menelaus.
It is the Greek way, surely, always to honour your own blood.

Tyndareus.
And it is the Greek way not to want to put yourself above the laws.

Menelaus.
The wise say that anything done under necessity is a slave to it.

Tyndareus.
Then you keep that piece of wisdom. I will not have it.

Menelaus.
No, because you are angry, and old age and anger together are not wise.

Tyndareus.
An argument about wisdom, with him standing there? If everyone can see
what is right and what is not, was there ever a man more senseless than
this one? He never once looked at what justice required. He never went
to the law that all Greeks share. When Agamemnon breathed out his life,
struck over the head by my daughter, a filthy deed, and I will never
defend it, he should have brought a charge for the blood, a holy
prosecution, and thrown his mother out of the house. That would have
been the disciplined course, instead of a disaster: he would have kept
to the law, and he would have kept his hands clean before the gods. As
it is, he has walked into the same fate as his mother. He was right to
think her wicked. He became more wicked than she was, by killing her.

Let me ask you one thing, Menelaus, just this. Suppose this man's wife
kills him. And his son kills his mother to pay for it. And then that
man's son wipes out killing with another killing. Where does it end?
Where is the last of the evil? Our fathers long ago settled this the
right way. A man with blood on his hands was not allowed into their
sight, was not allowed to cross their path; they made him clean by
exile. They did not kill him back. Otherwise there is always going to
be one more man liable for a killing, the one who took the last stain
onto his hands.

Now, I hate godless women, and my own daughter first of all, who killed
her husband. And Helen, your wife: I will never say a good word for
her, and I would not so much as speak to her, and I do not envy you
your trip to Troy for a worthless woman. But the law I will defend with
everything I have, to put a stop to this savagery, this bloodlust,
which is always the ruin of countries and of cities.

(Tyndareus turns away from Menelaus and speaks to Orestes directly.)

What kind of heart did you have in you, you wretched man, when your
mother bared her breast and begged you? I was not there, I did not see
the horror of it, and it still melts these old eyes of mine with tears.
And here is one thing that agrees with what I say: the gods hate you.
You are paying for your mother, wandering about in fits of madness and
terror. What do I need other witnesses for, when I can see it for
myself?

So understand this, Menelaus. Do not set yourself against the gods by
wanting to help this man. Leave him to the citizens, to be stoned to
death, or never set foot on Spartan soil again. My daughter died, and
she deserved to die. But it should not have been by his hand. In
everything else I have been a happy man. Except in my daughters. There
I have had no luck at all.

Chorus.
The one to envy is the man who has been lucky in his children and has
never had a famous disaster in his house.

Orestes.
Old man, I am afraid to speak to you, because whatever I say I am going
to hurt you, and hurt your heart. I know: I am unholy, because I killed
my mother. But there is another name for me too, and it is holy: I
avenged my father. Let your old age get out of the way of my argument.
It is your years that knock the words out of me. If they were not
standing there I would go straight down the road; as it is, I am afraid
of your grey hair.

What should I have done? Set one thing against the other. My father
planted me. Your daughter bore me: she was the field that took the seed
from someone else. Without a father there would never be a child. So I
reasoned it out: I owed more to the founder of my line than to the
woman who took on the job of feeding me. And your daughter, I am
ashamed to call her my mother, went to a man's bed in a private wedding
of her own, and not a decent one. I speak against myself when I speak
badly of her. I know that. I will say it anyway. Aegisthus was the
husband hidden in the house. I killed him. And on top of that I
sacrificed my mother. An unholy thing to do, yes. But I was avenging my
father.

And now, as to the reason you say I should be stoned, hear how I am
doing every Greek a service. If women get the nerve to go this far, to
murder their husbands, and then run to their children for shelter and
hunt for pity with their bared breasts, then killing a husband would be
nothing to them. Any charge that came to hand would do. I did a
terrible thing, as you keep announcing, and I put a stop to that
custom. I hated my mother and I destroyed her, and I was within my
rights. While her husband was away from home under arms, leading the
army for the whole of Greece, she betrayed him. She did not keep his
bed clean. And when she realised she had been caught out, she did not
punish herself. No. To keep from being punished by her husband, she
punished my father. She killed him.

By the gods, and this is not the moment to be bringing up the gods,
when I am arguing a case of murder, but if I had kept my mouth shut and
gone along with what my mother did, what would the dead man have done
to me? Would he not have hated me, and set the Furies dancing round me?
Or are there goddesses on my mother's side and none on his, when he was
wronged far worse than she was? You are the one, old man. You fathered
a wicked daughter, and you destroyed me. It was her nerve that took my
father from me, and made me my mother's killer.

Look at Telemachus. He did not kill the wife of Odysseus, because she
did not marry a second husband on top of the first. The bed in her
house stayed sound. And look at Apollo, who lives at the navel of the
earth and gives mortals the clearest word there is, the god we obey in
everything he says. I obeyed him, and I killed the woman who bore me.
Call him the unholy one. Kill him. It was his mistake, not mine. What
should I have done? Or is the god not good enough to take the pollution
off me when I hand it over to him? Where is anyone supposed to run,
then, if the one who gave the order will not save me from death? So do
not say that what was done was done badly. Say that for the ones who
did it, it has turned out unhappily. When people's marriages are well
made, their life is blessed. When they fall out badly, everything goes
wrong for them, at home and outside it.

Chorus.
Women are always getting in the way of what men do, and always to make
things worse.

Tyndareus.
Since you are so bold about it, since you will not tone down a word,
and you answer me in a way that is meant to hurt, you are making me all
the more eager to go and see to your death. And I will count it a fine
extra piece of work on top of the business I came here for, which was
to dress my daughter's grave. I am going to the assembly of the
Argives, and I will set the city on you, willing or not, on you and on
your sister, to pay the penalty of stoning. She deserves to die more
than you do. She is the one who made you savage against your mother,
always dripping stories into your ear to make you hate her more,
telling you about dreams sent by Agamemnon, and about Aegisthus's bed,
may the gods below loathe it, because it was bitter enough up here,
until she set the house on fire with a fire no forge ever lit.

Menelaus, I say this to you, and I will do it as well. If you count my
hatred for anything, or the marriage that binds us, do not defend this
man from death against the gods. Leave him to the citizens to be stoned
to death, or never set foot on Spartan soil. You have heard it. Now you
know it. And do not choose godless friends and push the god-fearing
ones aside. Take me away from this house, servants.

(Tyndareus goes, led away by his attendants.)

Orestes.
Go, then. Let the rest of what I have to say reach this man without a
disturbance, and without your old age in the way. Menelaus, why are you
pacing round in circles, thinking, going up one road and back down the
other, with two cares pulling at you?

Menelaus.
Leave me alone. I am turning something over in my mind, and I cannot
see which way to turn, the way things stand.

Orestes.
Do not finish deciding yet. Hear what I have to say first, and then
make up your mind.

Menelaus.
Speak, then. You are right. There are times when silence is better than
talk, and times when talk is better than silence.

Orestes.
Then I will speak now. A long speech has the advantage over a short
one, and is clearer to listen to. Give me nothing of your own,
Menelaus. Pay back what you took from my father. I am not talking about
money. Or rather, money is exactly what it is, if you save my life,
because my life is the dearest thing I own.

I did wrong. And I need to get a little wrong from you in return for
that evil. Because my father Agamemnon did wrong too, when he gathered
Greece together and went to Troy. Not a fault of his own. He was trying
to heal your wife's fault, and your wife's wrong. That is one thing,
and you owe me one thing back for it. And he gave up his body, truly,
as friends should for friends, slogging beside your shield so that you
could get your wife back. So pay me back the very thing you got there.
Work hard for one day, standing up as my saviour, not ten full years.
And what Aulis took, my sister's blood: I let you keep that. Do not
kill Hermione. When I am doing as badly as I am doing now, you have to
come out ahead, and I have to let it pass.

But give my life to my poor father, and my sister's life, a girl who
has gone unmarried too long. If I die I leave my father's house with no
one in it. You will say it is impossible. That is just it. It is when
things are bad that friends have to help friends. When the god gives
freely, what do you need friends for? The god is enough by himself,
when he is willing to help.

Every Greek believes you love your wife, and I do not say that to crawl
to you or to flatter you. By her, I beg you. Oh, the misery of my
troubles, and where I have come to. What of it? I have to suffer this.
I am begging for the whole house. Uncle, my father's own brother,
imagine that the dead man under the ground can hear this, that his
spirit is hovering over you and saying what I am saying, the same
words, for tears and groans and disasters. I have spoken. I have asked
for my life. I am hunting what everybody hunts, not only me.

Chorus.
And I beg you too, a woman though I am, to help people who need it.
And you can.

Menelaus.
Orestes, I do respect you, and I want to share the labour of your
troubles. And that is what you are supposed to do: carry the evils of
your own blood together, when a god gives you the power for it, dying
and killing the other side. But the power is what I would need to get
from the gods. I have come home with a spear and no allies behind it,
worn down by ten thousand troubles, with the small strength of the
friends I have left. In a battle, then, we could never beat Pelasgian
Argos. If we could win with soft words, that is where our hope comes
in. How does anyone take a great thing with small means? By hard work?
It is stupid even to want it.

When the people are in the flush of their anger, it is like trying to
put out a raging fire. But if a man goes quietly, slackens off and
gives way while they are pulling hard, and watches for his moment, they
might blow themselves out. And once they let their breath go, you can
easily get whatever you want from them. There is pity in them, and
there is a great temper in them too, and to a man who watches and waits
that is the most valuable possession there is. I will go and try to
persuade Tyndareus and the city for you, to use their excess well. A
ship dips too, when the sheet is hauled hard against the wind, and
rights itself again when the sheet is let go. The god hates too much
eagerness, and so do the citizens. I have to save you, and I say it
straight out, by cleverness, not by force against people who are
stronger than we are. By strength I could not save you, whatever you
may imagine. It is not easy for one spear to set up a trophy over the
troubles you are in. We never approached the land of Argos with
softness before. But now it has to be done. The wise are slaves to
fortune.

(Menelaus goes out, in the direction of the town.)

Orestes.
You are no use for anything except leading an army for a woman's sake.
You worst of men at defending your friends. You turn your back on me
and run, and what Agamemnon did for you is gone with the wind? So you
had no friends after all, father, when things went badly. Oh. I am
betrayed. There is no hope left, nowhere to turn to get away from the
Argives' death sentence. He was my refuge. He was my safety. But look:
here is the man I love best in the world, Pylades, running this way
from Phocis. A sweet sight. A man you can trust when things are bad is
a better sight than a calm sea is to sailors.

(Pylades comes in on foot, out of breath from running.)

Pylades.
I came through the town faster than I should have. I heard the city was
gathering, and then I saw it for myself, plainly: it is against you and
your sister, to kill you at once. What is this? How are you? What is
happening to you, dearest of all the friends my age, dearest of my
friends and my kin? Because you are all of those things to me.

Orestes.
We are finished. That is my trouble for you, in short.

Pylades.
Then you would be pulling me down with you. Friends have everything in
common.

Orestes.
Menelaus is the worst of men, to me and to my sister.

Pylades.
Naturally. A bad woman's husband turns into a bad man.

Orestes.
He came, and he paid me back exactly as if he had never come.

Pylades.
So he really has arrived in this country?

Orestes.
After a long time. But he was found out very quickly as a bad friend.

Pylades.
And did he sail here with that wife of his, the worst of women?

Orestes.
He did not bring her here. She brought him.

Pylades.
Where is she, the one woman who destroyed more Achaeans than anyone?

Orestes.
In my house. If I am supposed to call this house mine.

Pylades.
And what did you say to your father's brother?

Orestes.
Not to stand there and watch me and my sister killed by the citizens.

Pylades.
By the gods, what did he say to that? That is what I want to know.

Orestes.
He was careful. That is what bad friends do to their friends.

Pylades.
And what excuse did he take cover behind? Once I know that, I know
everything.

Orestes.
He arrived. The father who sowed those excellent daughters.

Pylades.
You mean Tyndareus. Angry with you, I suppose, over his daughter?

Orestes.
You have got it. And Menelaus chose the marriage tie to him over the
one to my father.

Pylades.
He did not have the nerve to take up a share of your troubles, when he
was standing right here?

Orestes.
No. He was not born a fighter. He is brave among women.

Pylades.
Then you are in the worst trouble there is. And you have to die?

Orestes.
The citizens have to cast their vote on us, about the killing.

Pylades.
And what does the vote decide? Tell me. I am frightened.

Orestes.
Death or life. A short speech about a long matter.

Pylades.
Then leave the house with your sister and run.

Orestes.
Can you not see? There are guards on us in every direction.

Pylades.
I saw the streets of the town blocked with armed men.

Orestes.
We are besieged, like a city with the enemy round its walls.

Pylades.
Now ask me what is happening to me. I am finished too.

Orestes.
Who did it? That would be a new evil on top of mine.

Pylades.
Strophius, my father. He lost his temper and drove me out of the house,
into exile.

Orestes.
On a charge of his own, or one the citizens share?

Pylades.
That I helped you kill your mother. He calls it unholy.

Orestes.
You poor man. It looks as if my troubles are going to hurt you as well.

Pylades.
I do not go about things the way Menelaus does. This has to be borne.

Orestes.
Are you not afraid Argos will want to kill you too, as well as me?

Pylades.
It is not their place to punish me. I belong to the land of Phocis.

Orestes.
The mob is a terrible thing, when it has villains for leaders.

Pylades.
But when it gets honest ones, its decisions are always honest.

Orestes.
Right. We need to talk this through together.

Pylades.
What is it we have to decide?

Orestes.
Suppose I went to the citizens and told them —

Pylades.
That what you did was just?

Orestes.
Avenging my own father?

Pylades.
They might be only too glad to lay hands on you.

Orestes.
So I cower here in silence and die?

Pylades.
That is cowardly.

Orestes.
Then what should I do?

Pylades.
If you stay here, have you any way of saving yourself?

Orestes.
I have none.

Pylades.
And if you go, is there any hope of coming out of this alive?

Orestes.
With luck, there might be.

Pylades.
Then that is better than staying.

Orestes.
Then I will go.

Pylades.
At least if you die that way, you die better.

Orestes.
You are right. That way I get clear of cowardice.

Pylades.
More than by staying.

Orestes.
And what I did was just.

Pylades.
Just pray it looks that way to them.

Orestes.
And somebody might pity me —

Pylades.
Yes. Your birth counts for a great deal.

Orestes.
— someone angry about my father's death.

Pylades.
All of that is plain to see.

Orestes.
I have to go. To die without honour is not a man's death.

Pylades.
I agree.

Orestes.
Then shall we tell my sister?

Pylades.
No. By the gods, no.

Orestes.
There would be tears, certainly.

Pylades.
And that is a bad omen. A big one.

Orestes.
Clearly better to keep quiet.

Pylades.
And you will gain time.

Orestes.
There is only one thing in my way.

Pylades.
What is this new thing now?

Orestes.
That the goddesses might take hold of me and drive me mad.

Pylades.
But I will take care of you.

Orestes.
It is an unpleasant business, handling a sick man.

Pylades.
Not for me. Not when it is you.

Orestes.
Be careful. You could catch my madness.

Pylades.
Let that go.

Orestes.
Then you will not hesitate?

Pylades.
No. Hesitation is a great evil between friends.

Orestes.
Then walk on, and steer my feet.

Pylades.
Gladly, when the man I am caring for is a friend.

Orestes.
And take me to my father's tomb.

Pylades.
Whatever for?

Orestes.
So that I can beg him to save me.

Pylades.
Yes. That is right and proper.

Orestes.
But my mother's grave: I do not even want to see it.

Pylades.
No. She was your enemy. But hurry, or the vote of the Argives will get
to you first. Put your side against my side, slow as it is with the
sickness. I will carry you through the town, and think very little of
the crowd, and not be ashamed of it. Where would I ever show myself a
friend, if I did not stand by you when you are in terrible trouble?

Orestes.
That is the old saying exactly: get yourself friends, not only
relations. A man whose ways are melted into yours, even if he is from
outside the house, is a better friend for a man to have than ten
thousand of his own blood.

(Orestes and Pylades go out together, Orestes leaning on his friend.)

Chorus.
	The great wealth and the courage
	that held its head high through Greece
	and by the streams of the Simois
	turned back, for the sons of Atreus, out of good fortune,
	long ago, from an old disaster in the house,
	when the quarrel over a golden lamb
	came to the sons of Tantalus:
	the most pitiful feasts,
	and the slaughter of high-born children.
	And from that day killing pays for killing
	through blood, and does not let go
	of the two sons of Atreus.

Chorus.
	The fine thing was not fine:
	to cut a parent's flesh with a hand and a forged blade,
	and hold the sword up black with blood
	to the rays of the sun.
	Doing evil well is a many-coloured impiety,
	the madness of men whose minds have gone wrong.
	In her terror of death
	the poor daughter of Tyndareus screamed:
	"Child, this is not holy, what you dare,
	killing your own mother. Do not, for honouring
	your father, fasten a disgrace
	on yourself for ever."

Chorus.
	What sickness, what tears,
	what pity is greater anywhere on earth
	than to take a mother's blood on your hands?
	Look at what he did,
	and now he is raving in fits of madness,
	the Furies' prey, whirling murder
	in his racing eyes,
	Agamemnon's son.
	Poor man. When he saw his mother's breast
	rising out of her gold-threaded dress,
	he saw it, and he made his mother
	the sacrifice, to pay back
	what was done to his father.
