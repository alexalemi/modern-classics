Ion
(Part 1 of 3)

(Delphi, in front of the temple of Apollo. It is not yet light. Hermes
comes in.)

Hermes.
Atlas, who wears down the sky, that old house of the gods, on his bronze
back, fathered Maia on one of the goddesses, and Maia bore me, Hermes,
to great Zeus, to run the gods' errands. I have come to this country,
Delphi, where Apollo sits on the navel of the world and sings to
mortals, forever telling them what is and what is coming. There is a
city in Greece that is not without a name, the city called after Pallas
Athena of the golden spear. There Apollo took Creusa, the daughter of
Erechtheus, by force, under the hill of Pallas in the land of Athens, at
the rocks that face north, the ones the lords of Attica call the Long
Rocks, the Macrae. Her father never knew. The god wanted it that way.
She carried the weight in her belly to term, and when her time came she
gave birth to a boy in the house, and then she carried the baby out to
the same cave where the god had bedded her, and she put him out there to
die, in the rounded hollow of a cradle, keeping an old custom of her
ancestors and of Erichthonius, who was born from the earth. When Athena,
the daughter of Zeus, gave that child to the daughters of Aglaurus to
keep safe, she set two snakes beside him to guard his body, and that is
why the descendants of Erechtheus still have a custom of raising their
children with snakes of beaten gold. But Creusa tied what finery a girl
had on her baby, and left him to die.

Then Apollo, who is my brother, asked me this. "Brother, go to the
people who grew out of the soil of famous Athens, you know the goddess's
city, and take a newborn baby out of a hollow rock, cradle and swaddling
clothes and all, and bring him to my oracle at Delphi, and set him down
right at the entrance to my house. The rest, because the boy is mine,
you should know that, will be my business." So I did my brother Apollo
the favour. I picked up the woven cradle and brought it here, and I set
the boy down on the steps of this temple, and I opened the lid of the
curved wicker cradle, so the child could be seen.

And it happened that just as the sun's chariot was setting out on its
round, the priestess was going into the god's shrine. Her eye fell on
the little baby, and she was amazed that any girl of Delphi would have
the nerve to throw the child of a secret labour into the god's house,
and she was all for putting him out beyond the altar. But pity got the
better of her hardness, and the god was working alongside her, so that
his child would not be thrown out of his own house, and she took him up
and raised him. She does not know that Apollo fathered him, or who the
mother was, and the boy does not know his parents either. While he was
small he wandered and played round the altars that fed him, and when his
body grew to a man's, the Delphians made him keeper of the god's gold
and trusted steward of everything, and he lives here in the god's halls
to this day, a holy life.

Creusa, meanwhile, the young man's mother, is married to Xuthus, and
this is how that came about. A wave of war broke between Athens and the
people of Chalcodon, who hold the land of Euboea. Xuthus fought it
through with the Athenians and helped them win it with his spear, and
his reward was the honour of marrying Creusa, though he was not
Athenian-born but an Achaean, a son of Aeolus, who was a son of Zeus. He
has been sowing that marriage bed for years and has no child, and
neither has Creusa. That is why they have come here, to this oracle of
Apollo: they want children. And Apollo is driving their luck to this
point. Nothing has escaped him, whatever people think. When Xuthus goes
into this oracle, the god will give him his own son and say the boy is
Xuthus's, so that the boy can go to his mother's house and be known to
Creusa, and Apollo's bed stays a secret, and the boy gets what is coming
to him. And he will see to it that all Greece calls the boy Ion, the
founder of a country in Asia.

But I will step into this laurel grove and find out what is decided
about the boy. I can see Apollo's son coming out now, to make the
doorway in front of the temple bright with sprigs of laurel. And the
name he is about to have, Ion: I am the first of the gods to call him by
it.

(Hermes hides among the laurels. Ion comes out of the temple with a
broom made of laurel branches and a bow over his shoulder. Temple
servants follow him out.)

Ion.
	Here it comes, the bright chariot and the four horses:
	the Sun is already shining down on the earth,
	and the stars are running from this fire in the sky
	into holy night.
	The peaks of Parnassus, where nobody may walk,
	are catching the light, and taking the first arch of day
	on behalf of mortals.
	The smoke of dry myrrh flies up
	to the roof of Apollo.
	The woman of Delphi sits on the holy tripod,
	singing out to the Greeks whatever cries
	Apollo sounds in her.
	But you, Delphians, servants of Apollo,
	go down to the silver swirl of Castalia,
	wash yourselves in its clean dew,
	and then walk up to the temple.
	Guard your mouths. Say nothing of bad omen.
	Give a good word from your own tongues
	to the people who want to ask the god a question.
	And I will do the work I have done since I was a child,
	the work I never stop doing. With sprigs of laurel
	and holy garlands I will make Apollo's doorway clean,
	and wet the floor with drops of water,
	and the flocks of birds
	that foul the sacred offerings
	I will put to flight with my bow.
	Because I was born with no mother and no father,
	and it is the temple of Apollo I serve,
	the temple that brought me up.

Ion.
	Come, fresh-grown shoot
	of the most beautiful laurel,
	come and do your service to the god.
	You sweep the altar of Apollo under his temple.
	You come from gardens that never die,
	where holy dews are always wetting
	the sacred foliage of the myrtle,
	sending out a stream from a spring that never fails.
	With you I sweep the god's floor
	all day long, from the moment the sun
	lifts its swift wing,
	my service, every day.
	Healer, Healer,
	be blessed, be blessed,
	son of Leto.

Ion.
	It is fine work I do for you, Apollo,
	here in front of your house,
	honouring the seat of your prophecy.
	And it is a glorious thing to me, this work,
	to give my hands as a slave gives them, to gods,
	not to mortals, but to the deathless ones.
	I never tire of working
	at work that is holy.
	Apollo is my father. It was Apollo who begot me,
	because I bless the one who feeds me,
	and it is that helpful name, father,
	that I give to Apollo, who lives in the temple.
	Healer, Healer,
	be blessed, be blessed,
	son of Leto.

Ion.
	But now I will let the laurel broom rest,
	and from golden jugs I will fling out
	the spring water of Earth,
	the water the eddies of Castalia
	pour down. I throw the wet drops
	and I am clean: no one has ever shared my bed.
	Let me go on serving Apollo like this forever,
	and never stop, or stop only when my luck is good.
	Ah. Ah.
	Here they come already, the birds,
	leaving their beds on Parnassus.
	I am telling you, do not come near the cornices,
	do not go into the golden house.
	I will catch you with my arrows again, herald of Zeus,
	you who beat the strength of every other bird
	with those hooked claws.
	Here is another one rowing towards the altar,
	a swan. Will you not take
	those red feet somewhere else?
	Apollo's lyre, that sings along with your song,
	will never save you from my bow.
	Turn your wings aside.
	Go and settle on the lake at Delos.
	If you do not do as I say, you will bleed,
	and your beautiful songs will end in blood.
	Ah. Ah.
	What is this? A new bird coming?
	Are you going to build a nest of dry straw for your chicks
	under the eaves?
	The twang of my bowstring will keep you off.
	Will you not listen? Go and raise your young
	in the swirling water of the river Alpheus,
	or in the wooded valley at the Isthmus,
	so that the offerings and Apollo's temple
	come to no harm.
	I hate to kill you.
	You carry the gods' messages to mortals.
	But I am bound to my work.
	I will slave for Apollo, and I will never stop
	serving the ones who feed me.

(The Chorus, Creusa's Athenian serving women, come in as sightseers, and
go from sculpture to sculpture on the temple front.)

Chorus.
	So it is not only in holy Athens
	that the gods have courtyards with fine columns,
	and the god of the roadways gets his worship.
	Here too, at the house of Apollo, Leto's son,
	the two faces of the temple
	shine with the light of beautiful eyes.
	Look at this one. Look:
	the son of Zeus is killing the water snake of Lerna
	with a golden sickle.
	Look at it, my dear, with your own eyes.

Chorus.
	I see it. And next to him another man
	is lifting a blazing torch.
	Is it the one whose story I weave at my loom,
	Iolaus with his shield,
	who took up the labours alongside the son of Zeus
	and bore them with him?
	And look at this one too,
	the man on the winged horse.
	He is killing the fire-breathing thing
	with the three bodies.

Chorus.
	I am chasing my eyes everywhere.
	Look at the battle of the Giants
	on the stone walls.
	We are looking, friends, right here.
	Do you see her, then, over Enceladus,
	shaking her shield with the Gorgon's face on it?
	I see Pallas, my own goddess.
	And what about this? The thunderbolt, fire at both ends,
	heavy, in the far-shooting hands of Zeus?
	I see it. He is burning Mimas, his enemy, to ash.
	And Dionysus is killing another of Earth's children
	with his staff of ivy,
	a stick that was never made for war.

Chorus.
	You there, by the temple. I am talking to you.
	Is it allowed to step inside the shrine,
	with our white feet?

Ion.
	It is not allowed, strangers.

Chorus.
	Could we have a word from you, then,
	and ask you something?

Ion.
	What is it you want?

Chorus.
	Is it true that the house of Apollo
	really holds the very navel of the earth?

Ion.
	It does. Wrapped in garlands, with Gorgons all round it.

Chorus.
	That is what people say, too.

Ion.
	If you have offered the holy cake in front of the house
	and there is something you want to ask Apollo,
	come up to the altar. But do not go
	into the inner house unless sheep have been slaughtered.

Chorus.
	I understand. We will not step over
	the god's law.
	What is outside will be a feast for the eyes.

Ion.
	Look at everything your eyes are allowed to see.

Chorus.
	Our masters let me come
	and look at these shrines of the god.

Ion.
	And whose house do you belong to? Whose slaves are you called?

Chorus.
	The house that raised the lords I serve
	shares its roof with Pallas.
	But the one you are asking about is here herself.

(Creusa comes in. She stops at the sight of the temple, in tears.)

Ion.
There is breeding in you, and the way you carry yourself is proof of
your character, whoever you are, lady. You can usually tell about a
person, whether they were born well, just by looking at how they hold
themselves. Oh, but you have startled me. You shut your eyes, and your
well-bred cheek is wet with tears, the moment you saw Apollo's holy
oracle. What is it, lady, that has brought you to this point of worry?
Everyone else who looks at the god's shrine is glad. And here your eye
is streaming.

Creusa.
Stranger, there is nothing ill-bred in your surprise at my tears. When I
saw this house of Apollo, I measured out an old memory all over again,
and my mind was at home, though I am standing here. Poor women. Poor us.
And the gods, and the things they have the nerve to do. What then? Where
are we to take our case for justice, if it is the injustice of the ones
in power that destroys us?

Ion.
What is this, lady? Why so low, over something you will not explain?

Creusa.
Nothing. I have shot my arrow, and that is all. About the rest I say
nothing, and you stop thinking about it.

Ion.
Who are you? Where have you come from? What country were you born in?
What name should we call you by?

Creusa.
My name is Creusa. I am the daughter of Erechtheus, and my country is
the city of Athens.

Ion.
A famous city to live in, and noble fathers to be raised by. How I
admire you, lady.

Creusa.
That is as far as my luck goes, stranger. No further.

Ion.
By the gods, is it true, the story mortals tell?

Creusa.
What are you asking, stranger? What do you want to know?

Ion.
Your father's grandfather grew out of the earth?

Creusa.
Erichthonius did, yes. My family is no help to me.

Ion.
And Athena really lifted him up out of the ground?

Creusa.
Into the hands of unmarried girls, yes. She had not given birth to him.

Ion.
And she handed him over, the way the paintings usually show it?

Creusa.
To the daughters of Cecrops, yes, to keep him and never look at him.

Ion.
I have heard that the girls opened the goddess's chest.

Creusa.
And so they died, and their blood stained the face of the rock.

Ion.
I see. And what about this one? Is it true, or an empty story?

Creusa.
What are you asking? I have the time. I am not tired of it.

Ion.
Your father Erechtheus sacrificed your sisters?

Creusa.
He had the heart to kill them, girls, as victims for the country's sake.

Ion.
And how was it that you alone of the sisters were saved?

Creusa.
I was a newborn baby in my mother's arms.

Ion.
And is it true that a chasm in the earth hides your father?

Creusa.
The blows of the sea god's trident destroyed him.

Ion.
And there is a place there they call the Long Rocks?

Creusa.
Why are you asking about that? You have reminded me of something.

Ion.
The Pythian god honours it, and the Pythian lightning.

Creusa.
Honours it. Honours it. I wish I had never set eyes on it.

Ion.
What? You hate the thing the god loves most?

Creusa.
No. But that cave and I know something shameful together.

Ion.
And which Athenian married you, lady?

Creusa.
Not a citizen. A man brought in from another country.

Ion.
Who? He must be someone of good birth.

Creusa.
Xuthus. He is descended from Aeolus, and from Zeus.

Ion.
And how did he, a foreigner, get you, when you were born in the country?

Creusa.
Euboea is a city next door to Athens.

Ion.
With a boundary of water between them, so they say.

Creusa.
He sacked it, sharing the war with the sons of Cecrops.

Ion.
He came as an ally? And then married into your bed?

Creusa.
Yes. It was his dowry from the war, the prize for his spear.

Ion.
Have you come to the oracle with your husband, or alone?

Creusa.
With him. But he is going round the shrine of Trophonius, the oracle in
the cave.

Ion.
As a sightseer, or for the sake of an oracle?

Creusa.
He wants to hear one and the same word from him and from Apollo.

Ion.
Have you come about the crops of the land, or about children?

Creusa.
We have no children, though we have shared a bed for years.

Ion.
You have never given birth? Never at all? You are childless?

Creusa.
Apollo knows about my childlessness.

Ion.
Poor woman. Lucky in everything else, and not in this.

Creusa.
But who are you? What a lucky woman your mother is.

Ion.
I am called the god's slave, lady, and that is what I am.

Creusa.
An offering from a city, or sold to him by someone?

Ion.
I know only one thing. I am called Apollo's.

Creusa.
Then it is my turn to pity you, stranger.

Ion.
Because I do not know who bore me or who my father is.

Creusa.
Do you live in this temple, or under a roof of your own?

Ion.
The whole house of the god is mine, wherever sleep catches me.

Creusa.
Did you come to the temple as a child, or as a young man?

Ion.
A baby, say the people who think they know.

Creusa.
And which woman of Delphi fed you her milk?

Ion.
I never knew a breast. But the one who raised me —

Creusa.
Who, poor boy? I came here sick, and I have found sickness.

Ion.
Apollo's priestess. I think of her as my mother.

Creusa.
And till you came to be a man, how did you live?

Ion.
The altars fed me, and whatever stranger came along.

Creusa.
Poor woman, your mother. Who could she have been?

Ion.
Some wrong done to a woman, perhaps. That is what I was born from.

Creusa.
But you have a living. You are well dressed.

Ion.
I am dressed in the god's clothes. I am his slave.

Creusa.
And you never rushed to search out your birth?

Ion.
I have nothing to go on, lady. No proof at all.

Creusa.
Ah. Another woman has suffered what your mother did.

Ion.
Who? If she would share the work with me, I would be glad.

Creusa.
She is the reason I came here ahead of my husband.

Ion.
What does she want? Tell me, lady, and I will help.

Creusa.
She wants to learn a secret answer from Apollo.

Ion.
Tell me, then. I will see to the rest, as your host here.

Creusa.
Then listen to the story. But I am ashamed.

Ion.
Then you will get nothing done. Shame is a lazy goddess.

Creusa.
A friend of mine says she went to bed with Apollo.

Ion.
Apollo, with a mortal woman? Do not say that, stranger.

Creusa.
And she had a child by the god, without her father knowing.

Ion.
Impossible. Some man wronged her, and she is ashamed of it.

Creusa.
She says not. And she has suffered terribly.

Ion.
Suffered how, if a god took her to bed?

Creusa.
The child she bore, she put out of the house.

Ion.
And the child who was put out, where is he? Is he looking at the light?

Creusa.
Nobody knows. That is exactly what I am here to ask the oracle.

Ion.
And if he is not alive, how did he die?

Creusa.
She supposes that wild animals killed him, poor thing.

Ion.
What proof did she use to decide that?

Creusa.
She went to the place where she left him, and he was not there any more.

Ion.
Was there any drop of blood on the path?

Creusa.
She says not. And she went over the ground many times.

Ion.
How long is it since the boy was done away with?

Creusa.
If he were alive, he would be just your age.

Ion.
The god has wronged her. And the mother, poor woman.

Creusa.
And she never had another child after that.

Ion.
But what if Apollo took him and is raising him in secret?

Creusa.
Then he is doing wrong, enjoying alone what should be shared.

Ion.
Oh. Her luck sings the same tune as my own trouble.

Creusa.
And you too, stranger, I think: somewhere a poor mother misses you.

Ion.
No. Do not lead me back to a grief I had forgotten.

Creusa.
I am quiet. Finish what I am asking you about.

Ion.
Do you know where your story limps worst?

Creusa.
What is there in that poor woman's case that is not sick?

Ion.
How is the god going to prophesy the very thing he wants hidden?

Creusa.
He will, if he sits on a tripod that belongs to all Greece.

Ion.
He is ashamed of the business. Do not put him on trial.

Creusa.
And the woman it happened to is in pain at her luck.

Ion.
There is nobody who will prophesy this for you. If Apollo were shown up
as a villain in his own house, he would be right to do some harm to the
man who gave you the answer. Go away, lady. We must not ask the oracle
for things the god does not want. Think how far we would have gone in
stupidity, if we worked the gods over against their will to tell us what
they do not want to tell, whether by slaughtering sheep in front of the
altars or by the flight of birds. The good things we chase by force,
when the gods do not want it, we hold against their will, lady. What
they give us willingly, that does us good.

Chorus.
Many are the things that happen to the many kinds of mortals, and the
shapes of them are all different. But one piece of good luck in a human
life: you would hardly find that anywhere.

Creusa.
Apollo, there and here you are unjust to the woman who is not here,
whose words are. You did not save your son, when you were bound to save
him, and you are a prophet, and you will not tell his mother when she
asks, so that if he is no longer alive he could be given a mound of a
grave, and if he is alive —

But I have to let this be, if the god blocks me from learning what I
want to know. Stranger. I can see my husband, the noble Xuthus, close
by, coming from the chambers of Trophonius. Say nothing to him about
what has been said. I do not want the shame of being caught running
secret errands, or the story going off in some direction other than the
one we have been unwinding it in. Things go hard for women with men. The
good ones are mixed in with the bad, and we are hated for it. That is
the luck we were born to.

(Xuthus comes in.)

Xuthus.
First, the god: let him have the first fruits of my greeting, and good
day to him. And to you, wife. Did I take so long that I frightened you?

Creusa.
Not at all. But you have come at an anxious moment. Tell me, what oracle
do you bring from Trophonius? How are we two going to mix our seed and
make children?

Xuthus.
He did not think it right to get ahead of the god's prophecies. But he
did say one thing: that I will not go home from the oracle childless,
and neither will you.

Creusa.
Lady Leto, mother of Apollo, let us come to a good end, and let the
dealings we had with your son before change to something better.

Xuthus.
It will be so. But who speaks for the god here?

Ion.
I do, stranger, for the outside. Inside is other men's business, the
ones who sit close to the tripod, the best men of Delphi, the ones the
lot chose.

Xuthus.
Good. Then I have everything I needed. I will go in. I am told a victim
has been slaughtered in front of the temple, on behalf of all the
visitors together, and I want to take the god's oracle today, because it
is a lucky day. You, wife, take laurel branches and go round the altars,
and pray to the gods that I bring an answer out of Apollo's house that
promises children.

Creusa.
It will be so. It will. And if Apollo is willing now, at last, to make
up for his old wrongs, he could never be a complete friend to us. But
whatever he is willing to give, he is a god, and I will take it.

(Xuthus goes into the temple. Creusa goes off to pray at the altars.)

Ion.
Why does this foreign woman keep dropping hints against the god, in
secret words, abusing him? Is it out of love for the woman she is
consulting the oracle for? Or is she keeping quiet about something that
had better be kept quiet? But what is the daughter of Erechtheus to me?
Nothing. She is nothing to do with me. I will go and fill the basins for
the sprinkling, with water from the golden jugs.

But I have a word to say to Apollo. What is the matter with him? He
rapes girls and then abandons them? He fathers children in secret and
lets them die, and does not care? Not you. No. You have the power, so go
after goodness. Any mortal who turns out bad, the gods punish him. So
how can it be just that you, who write the laws for mortals, should
stand guilty of breaking the law yourselves? And if — it will never
happen, but I will follow the argument — you paid mortals the penalty
for rape, you and Poseidon and Zeus who rules the sky, you would empty
your temples paying the fines for your injustice. You chase pleasure
before you think, and that is wrong. It is no longer fair to call human
beings bad, when we are only copying the fine things the gods do. Blame
the ones who teach us.

(Ion goes.)

Chorus.
	You who were born without the pains of labour,
	with no goddess of childbirth to help,
	my Athena, I beg you,
	you whom the Titan Prometheus
	delivered from the very crown
	of the head of Zeus. Blessed Athena, Lady of Victory,
	come to the Pythian house.
	Fly from the golden chambers of Olympus
	down to these streets,
	where Apollo's hearth, at the navel of the earth,
	beside the tripod and the dancing round it,
	makes the prophecies come true.
	You, and Leto's daughter with you,
	two goddesses, two virgins,
	the holy sisters of Apollo.
	Girls, pray with me
	that the old house of Erechtheus
	hears at last, in clear oracles,
	that children are coming.

Chorus.
	Because there is a happiness beyond all others,
	a footing that does not shift, for mortals
	in whose fathers' halls
	the young lives of children
	shine, ripening like fruit,
	to take over the wealth from their fathers
	and pass it on to children of their own.
	A child is a defence in trouble,
	a joy when luck is good,
	and brings his father's country
	the saving strength of a spear.
	Give me, before wealth,
	before the bedchambers of kings,
	the loving work of raising good children.
	The childless life I loathe,
	and anyone who thinks well of it, I blame.
	Let me have what I need, and no more,
	and a life with good children in it.

Chorus.
	Seat of Pan, and rock
	that stands beside the caves,
	the hollows of the Long Rocks,
	where the three daughters of Aglaurus
	tread out their dances with their feet
	on the green ground in front of Pallas's temple,
	to the shifting cry of your pipes,
	when you, Pan, are piping
	in your sunless caves:
	there a girl who had a baby by Apollo,
	poor girl, put it out
	as a feast for the birds,
	a bloody meal for the beasts,
	the outrage of a bitter bed.
	Not at the loom, not in any story,
	have I heard it said that children
	born to mortals from the gods
	get any share of happiness.

(Ion comes back.)

Ion.
Women in service, you who stand guard here at the steps of this house of
incense and keep watch for your mistress: has Xuthus left the holy
tripod and the oracle yet, or is he still inside, asking about his
childlessness?

Chorus.
He is inside, stranger. He has not crossed this threshold yet. But he
must be at the door. We can hear the doors knocking, and now you can see
the master coming out.

(Xuthus comes out of the temple and goes straight to Ion.)

Xuthus.
My child. Good day to you. That is the right way for me to begin.

Ion.
Good day to you too. Keep your head, and we will both do fine.

Xuthus.
Give me your hand to kiss, and your body to put my arms round.

Ion.
Are you in your right mind? Or has some god struck you and driven you
mad, stranger?

Xuthus.
I am in my right mind. I have found what I love most, and I am not going
to run from it.

Ion.
Stop. Do not touch the god's garlands. You will tear them with your
hand.

Xuthus.
I will touch. I am not stealing. I am finding what is mine, and I love
it.

Ion.
Get away from me, before you get an arrow in the lungs.

Xuthus.
Why are you running from me? When you have found what is dearest to you
—

Ion.
I do not enjoy teaching manners to boorish strangers who have gone mad.

Xuthus.
Kill me, then, and burn me. If you kill me, you will be your father's
murderer.

Ion.
Where is my father? You? Is that not a joke, to make me laugh?

Xuthus.
No. If the story runs on, it will show you what I mean.

Ion.
And what are you going to tell me?

Xuthus.
I am your father, and you are my child.

Ion.
Who says so?

Xuthus.
Apollo, who raised you, when you were mine.

Ion.
You are your own witness, then.

Xuthus.
I have learned the god's oracle, yes.

Ion.
You heard a riddle and got it wrong.

Xuthus.
Then I am not hearing straight.

Ion.
And what did Apollo say?

Xuthus.
That the man who met me —

Ion.
Met you how?

Xuthus.
— as I came out of this house of the god —

Ion.
What was going to happen to him?

Xuthus.
— he was my son by birth.

Ion.
Born yours, or a gift from somewhere else?

Xuthus.
A gift. But born from me.

Ion.
And I am the first person you set foot near?

Xuthus.
Nobody else, child.

Ion.
Where does this luck come from?

Xuthus.
Two of us wondering at the same thing.

Ion.
Ah. And what mother did you have me by?

Xuthus.
I cannot tell you that, child.

Ion.
Apollo did not say either?

Xuthus.
I was so pleased with this that I did not ask that.

Ion.
Then I was born from the earth, and she is my mother.

Xuthus.
The ground does not bear children.

Ion.
Then how could I be yours?

Xuthus.
I do not know. I refer it to the god.

Ion.
Come. Let us take hold of the question another way.

Xuthus.
This way is better, child.

Ion.
Did you ever go to a bed that was not your wife's?

Xuthus.
In the foolishness of youth, yes.

Ion.
Before you married the daughter of Erechtheus?

Xuthus.
Never since, anyway.

Ion.
So that is where you fathered me?

Xuthus.
The time fits, at any rate.

Ion.
And then how did I get here —

Xuthus.
That I cannot work out at all.

Ion.
— travelling such a long road?

Xuthus.
That puzzles me just as much.

Ion.
Did you ever come to the Pythian rock before?

Xuthus.
Yes. For the torchlight festival of Dionysus.

Ion.
Did you stay with one of the official hosts?

Xuthus.
Yes. A man who, with some girls of Delphi —

Ion.
Got you into the revels? Or what are you saying?

Xuthus.
Into the Maenads of Dionysus, yes.

Ion.
Were you sober, or in your wine?

Xuthus.
In the pleasures of Dionysus.

Ion.
That was it. That is where I was sown.

Xuthus.
Fate found you out, child.

Ion.
And how did I get to the temple?

Xuthus.
Some girl's cast-off, perhaps.
