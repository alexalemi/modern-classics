The Bacchae
(Part 1 of 3)

(Thebes, in front of the royal house of Pentheus. To one side is the
tomb of Semele, fenced off, still smoking, with vines trained over it.
Dionysus comes in, in the shape of a young man with long hair, a
fawnskin over his shoulders and an ivy wand in his hand.)

Dionysus.
I have come to this land of Thebes, and I am the son of Zeus. I am
Dionysus. Semele, Cadmus's daughter, gave birth to me once, in a labour
brought on by a flash of lightning. I have changed my shape from a god's
to a man's, and here I am, at the springs of Dirce and the waters of the
Ismenus. And I can see my mother's tomb, the mother the thunderbolt
struck, right here beside the house, and the ruins of her home, still
smoking with the living flame of Zeus's fire: Hera's outrage against my
mother, and it never dies. I give Cadmus credit. He has made this ground
off limits, a shrine for his daughter, and I have covered it over with
the green of the vine and its clustering grapes.

I left the gold-rich fields of Lydia and Phrygia behind me, and the
sun-beaten plains of Persia, and the walled towns of Bactria, and the
bitter winter country of the Medes, and Arabia the blessed, and all of
Asia that lies along the salt sea with its fine-towered cities full of
Greeks and barbarians living side by side. I set every one of those
places dancing and established my rites there, so that mortals could see
me for the god I am. And then I came to this city, the first in Greece.
Thebes was the first town in this Greek land that I made shriek my cry.
I tied the fawnskin on their bodies and put the thyrsus in their hands,
the ivy-tipped wand that is my weapon. Because my mother's sisters, the
last women on earth who should have said it, said that I, Dionysus, was
no son of Zeus at all. They said Semele had been to bed with some mortal
man and was laying the blame for it on Zeus, and that the whole story
was a clever invention of Cadmus's. That was why Zeus killed her, they
crowed: for lying about who was in her bed.

So I have driven those same women stinging mad out of their houses. They
are living on the mountain, out of their minds, and I have made them
wear the dress of my rites. Every woman of the seed of Cadmus, every one
of them, I have sent raving out of her home. They are sitting up there
with the daughters of Cadmus among them, under the green firs, on rocks
with no roof over them. Because this city has to learn, whether it likes
it or not, what it means to be left outside my rites, and I have to
speak up for my mother Semele by showing myself to mortals as the god
she bore to Zeus.

Now Cadmus has handed his honours and his throne to Pentheus, his
daughter's son. And Pentheus is fighting a god, where I am concerned. He
shoves me away from his sacrifices, and there is no mention of me
anywhere in his prayers. So I am going to show him, and every Theban
with him, that I was born a god. And when I have put things right here,
I will move on to another country and reveal myself there. But if the
city of Thebes loses its temper and tries to bring the Bacchants down
off the mountain by force of arms, I will take the field against it with
the Maenads as my army. That is why I have taken on a mortal appearance
and changed my shape into a man's.

But you, women who left Tmolus behind, the mountain fortress of Lydia.
My own company. I brought you from among the barbarians to sit at my
side and travel with me. Take up the drums, the drums that belong to the
Phrygian towns, that Mother Rhea and I invented between us, and come
round this royal house of Pentheus and beat them, so that Cadmus's city
can see. As for me, I am going to the folds of Mount Cithaeron, where
the Bacchants are, to join in their dances.

(Dionysus goes. The Chorus come in, Asian women in fawnskins, carrying
ivy wands and drums.)

Chorus.
	From the land of Asia,
	leaving holy Tmolus behind, I hurry
	to do sweet work for Bromius, the Roarer,
	which is one of the names of Dionysus,
	labour that is no labour at all,
	crying his cry, Euoi.
	Who is in the road? Who is in the road? Who?
	Get indoors, out of the way. Let every mouth
	keep itself clean and holy,
	because I am going to sing the hymn to Dionysus
	the way it has always been sung.

Chorus.
	Oh, happy is the man who is blessed,
	who knows the rites of the gods,
	who keeps his life pure
	and gives his soul to the company,
	dancing for Dionysus on the mountains
	in holy purifications,
	who keeps the mysteries of Cybele,
	the Great Mother, as they should be kept,
	shaking the thyrsus high,
	crowned with ivy,
	serving Dionysus.
	Go, Bacchants, go, Bacchants,
	bring Dionysus home, the god who is a god's son,
	down from the mountains of Phrygia
	into the wide streets of Greece,
	the god who roars.

Chorus.
	Once his mother carried him,
	and her time came on her,
	the pains of birth forced on her early
	by the thunder of Zeus flying at her,
	and she pushed him out of her womb
	and left her life behind under the stroke of the lightning.
	And at once Zeus, the son of Cronus,
	took him into a birth-chamber of his own.
	He hid him in his thigh
	and pinned him in with golden clasps,
	out of Hera's sight.
	And when the Fates had brought him to his term,
	Zeus gave birth to him, the god with the horns of a bull,
	and crowned him with crowns of snakes.
	That is why the Maenads catch wild snakes
	and twist them into their hair.

Chorus.
	Thebes, you who nursed Semele,
	crown yourself with ivy.
	Burst into leaf, burst into leaf, with green
	bryony and its lovely berries,
	and go mad for Dionysus with branches
	of oak or fir.
	Trim your spotted fawnskins
	with tufts of white wool,
	and make yourselves holy with the wild fennel wands.
	In a moment the whole earth will be dancing,
	and whoever leads the dance is Dionysus himself,
	to the mountain, to the mountain, where the crowd of women waits,
	driven from their looms and their shuttles,
	stung mad by Dionysus.

Chorus.
	Cave where the Curetes hid the child,
	holy caverns of Crete
	where Zeus was born:
	there, in their caves, the Corybantes in their triple helmets
	invented this for me,
	this circle of stretched hide, the drum.
	And in the strain of their Bacchic dance
	they blended its beat with the sweet-voiced breath
	of Phrygian pipes, and put it in the hand
	of Mother Rhea, to pound out time for the Bacchants' cries.
	And the Satyrs in their madness
	got it from the Mother goddess,
	and joined it to the dances
	of the festival that comes every second year,
	the festival Dionysus loves.

Chorus.
	He is sweet on the mountains, when he drops to the ground
	out of the running companies,
	wearing the holy fawnskin,
	hunting the blood of the slaughtered goat,
	the joy of raw flesh,
	racing for the mountains of Phrygia, of Lydia,
	and the leader of the dance is Dionysus.
	Euoi.
	The ground runs with milk, it runs with wine,
	it runs with the nectar of bees.
	And the god of the Bacchants lifts high
	the blazing flame of the pine torch on his wand,
	like the smoke of Syrian frankincense,
	and he darts, running and dancing,
	stirring up the stragglers,
	tossing them with his shouts,
	flinging his soft hair into the air.
	And over the cries he roars out this:
	"Oh, go, Bacchants,
	go, Bacchants,
	pride of Tmolus where the river runs gold,
	sing Dionysus
	to the deep growl of the drums,
	Euoi, glorying in the god of Euoi
	with Phrygian shouts and calls,
	when the holy pipe, sweet-sounding,
	sounds its holy games,
	keeping time with the women who wander to the mountain,
	to the mountain." And then she is glad,
	like a foal at pasture beside its mother,
	and the Bacchant throws her quick feet into leaps.

(Teiresias comes in, blind, very old, dressed in a fawnskin with ivy on
his head and a wand in his hand.)

Teiresias.
Who is at the gates? Call Cadmus out of the house, Agenor's son, the man
who left the city of Sidon and built this towered town of Thebes.
Somebody go. Tell him Teiresias is looking for him. He knows what I have
come about, and what I agreed with him, one old man with an older one:
to wind the ivy on the thyrsus, to wear the skins of fawns, and to crown
our heads with sprigs of ivy.

(Cadmus comes out of the house, dressed the same way.)

Cadmus.
My dear old friend. I was inside, and I heard your voice and knew it, a
clever voice from a clever man. And here I am, ready, with the god's
outfit on. He is my daughter's son, this Dionysus who has shown himself
to mankind as a god, and we have to make him as great as we possibly
can. Where do we dance? Where do I put my feet and shake this grey head?
You show me, Teiresias, one old man leading another. You are the clever
one. I could keep this up all night and all day and never get tired,
beating the ground with the thyrsus. It is a pleasure to forget how old
we are.

Teiresias.
Then you feel exactly what I feel. I am young again too, and I am going
to try the dancing.

Cadmus.
So shall we go up the mountain in a carriage?

Teiresias.
No. The god would not get the same honour from that.

Cadmus.
One old man leading another by the hand like a schoolboy. That will be
me, leading you.

Teiresias.
The god will lead us both up there, and it will cost us nothing.

Cadmus.
Are we the only men in the city who are going to dance for Dionysus?

Teiresias.
Yes. We are the only ones thinking straight. The rest are thinking
wrong.

Cadmus.
We are wasting time. Here, take my hand.

Teiresias.
There. Take hold, and yoke your hand to mine.

Cadmus.
I was born a mortal. I do not look down on the gods.

Teiresias.
We do not play clever with the gods. The traditions our fathers handed
down to us, as old as time itself, no argument is going to knock down,
not even if some brilliant mind has thought its way to the very top of
cleverness. Will somebody say I have no shame about my old age, going
out to dance with ivy on my head? Let him. The god drew no line. He did
not say the young should dance and the old should not. He wants his
honours from everybody alike. He wants to be made great, and he counts
nobody out.

Cadmus.
Since you cannot see this daylight, Teiresias, I will be your prophet
and tell you what is happening. Here comes Pentheus, hurrying to the
house, Echion's son, the man I gave the rule of this land to. Look how
worked up he is. What is the news this time?

(Pentheus comes in with his attendants. At first he does not notice the
two old men.)

Pentheus.
I happened to be out of the country, and I hear there is new trouble in
this city. The women have left our homes for a Bacchic worship they have
made up themselves, and they are running about in the shadowy mountains,
honouring this new god with dances, this Dionysus, whoever he is. They
set up mixing bowls full of wine in the middle of their gatherings, so I
hear, and then they slink off one by one to some lonely spot to serve a
man in bed. Maenads at their sacrifices, that is the story. Aphrodite
comes before Dionysus with them.

Every one I have caught, my servants are holding in the public prison
with her hands tied. The ones still out I will hunt down off the
mountain. Ino, and Agave, who bore me to Echion, and Actaeon's mother, I
mean Autonoe. I will clamp them in iron nets, and that will put a quick
stop to this vicious Bacchic business.

And they say some stranger has come, a sorcerer, a chanter of spells
from the land of Lydia, with golden curls and scented hair and the
wine-dark charms of Aphrodite in his eyes, who spends his days and his
nights with young girls, dangling his rites of joy in front of them. If
I catch him inside this house, I will stop him banging his thyrsus and
tossing his hair. I will cut his head off his neck. This is the man who
says Dionysus is a god. He says he was once stitched into the thigh of
Zeus. Dionysus, who was burnt to nothing by the lightning along with his
mother, because she lied about being Zeus's bride. Does that not deserve
a hanging, and a hard one, whoever this stranger is? Outrage on top of
outrage.

But here is another wonder. I see the prophet Teiresias in a spotted
fawnskin, and my own mother's father, and this is too funny, playing the
Bacchant with a fennel wand. Father, I am ashamed to look at you. Two
old men, and no sense between you. Shake that ivy off. Let go of the
thyrsus, grandfather. Empty your hand.

You talked him into this, Teiresias. You want to bring in one more new
god for mankind so that you can watch more birds and rake in more fees
from burnt offerings. If your grey hairs were not protecting you, you
would be sitting in chains among the Bacchants for importing these
vicious rites. Because when women get the gleam of the grape at a feast,
I say there is nothing healthy left in any of their worship.

Chorus.
What blasphemy. Stranger, have you no respect for the gods, or for
Cadmus, who sowed the crop of men that grew out of the earth? You are
Echion's son, and you are shaming your own family.

Teiresias.
When a clever man has a good case to argue, it is no great feat to speak
well. You have a tongue that runs smoothly, as if you had sense, but
there is no sense in what it says. A man who is strong in nerve and
knows how to speak, and has no judgement, makes a bad citizen.

This new god you are laughing at: I could not begin to tell you how
great he is going to be all over Greece. Two things, young man, come
first for human beings. The goddess Demeter, and she is the earth, call
her by whichever name you like, feeds mortals on dry food. And then the
one who came after her, Semele's son, found the match to it, the wet
drink of the grape, and brought it to mortals. It puts an end to grief
for poor suffering men, when they are filled with the stream of the
vine, and it gives them sleep, and forgetfulness of the day's troubles,
and there is no other medicine for hardship. He is a god, and he is
poured out in offering to the gods, so that through him mankind gets its
good things.

And you laugh at him for being stitched into Zeus's thigh? I will teach
you how that story makes good sense. When Zeus snatched him out of the
lightning-fire and carried the baby up to Olympus as a god, Hera wanted
to throw him out of heaven. Zeus made a counter-plan, the way a god
does. He tore off a piece of the sky that wraps round the earth and gave
that to Hera as a hostage, a Dionysus for her to quarrel with, and kept
the real one safe. And in time people said he had been stitched into
Zeus's thigh. They changed a word. The god had been a hostage, a
homeros, to the goddess Hera, and the word for a thigh is meros, and the
one slid into the other and they made a story out of it.

And this god is a prophet. Bacchic frenzy and madness have a great deal
of prophecy in them. When the god comes into a body in full force, he
makes the people he has maddened tell the future. He has taken over a
share of Ares's business too. An army standing armed and in its ranks
has been scattered by panic before a spear was touched, and that is
madness too, and it comes from Dionysus. One day you will see him on the
rocks of Delphi as well, leaping across the plateau between the two
peaks with pine torches, whirling and shaking the Bacchic branch, great
all over Greece.

Listen to me, Pentheus. Do not go boasting that power is what counts
among men, and do not imagine you are thinking straight when your
thinking is sick. Take the god into your land. Pour him wine, be a
Bacchant, crown your head. It is not Dionysus who will force women to
keep themselves in hand where Aphrodite is concerned. That lives in a
woman's nature, always, in everything she does, and that is where you
have to look. Even in the middle of a Bacchic revel, a woman who keeps
herself in hand will not be ruined.

You see? You enjoy it when a crowd stands at your gates and the city
makes the name of Pentheus great. Well, he enjoys being honoured too, I
imagine. So Cadmus, whom you are laughing at, and I are going to put ivy
on our heads and dance, a grey team in harness, but dance we must. And I
will not fight a god because you talked me into it. You are mad, in the
most painful way there is. No drug will cure you, and yet it is a kind
of drug that has made you ill.

Chorus.
Old man, you do no shame to Apollo with those words, and when you honour
Dionysus you are keeping yourself in hand. He is a great god.

Cadmus.
My boy, Teiresias has given you good advice. Live with us, inside the
laws, not outside them. Right now you are all up in the air. You think
you are thinking, and you are thinking nothing. Even if this god is no
god, as you say, tell people he is. Tell the lie, and tell it well, so
that Semele can be said to have given birth to a god, and the honour
comes to our whole family. You know how Actaeon died, poor man, torn to
pieces in the meadows by the flesh-eating hounds he had raised himself,
because he boasted he was a better hunter than Artemis. Do not let that
happen to you. Come here, let me crown your head with ivy. Give the god
his honour, along with us.

Pentheus.
Do not lay a hand on me. Go and play the Bacchant, and do not wipe your
idiocy off on me. As for the man who taught you this folly, I am going
to make him pay. Somebody go, quickly, to the seat where he sits and
watches his birds. Lever it up with crowbars, tip it over, turn the
whole place upside down, everything in a heap, and throw his holy
ribbons to the winds and the storms. That is where I can hurt him most.
And the rest of you, go through the city and track down this womanish
stranger who is bringing a new disease to our women and fouling our
beds. If you catch him, tie him up and bring him here, so that he can be
sentenced to death by stoning and die that way. He will find his Bacchic
revels in Thebes a bitter business.

(Pentheus's men go off in different directions. Pentheus goes into the
house.)

Teiresias.
You poor fool, you have no idea what you are saying. You are mad now,
and you were out of your mind before. Let us go, Cadmus, and pray to the
god for this man, savage as he is, and for the city, that he does
nothing terrible. Follow me, with your ivy staff. Try to hold me up, and
I will hold you up. It would be a disgrace if two old men fell over. But
never mind that. We have to serve Dionysus, the son of Zeus. And Cadmus,
watch that Pentheus does not bring grief into your house. His name means
grief. I am not saying that as a prophet. I am looking at the facts. A
fool talks folly.

(The two old men go off together, slowly, holding each other up.)

Chorus.
	Holiness, queen among the gods,
	Holiness, who carry your golden wing
	low over the earth,
	do you hear what Pentheus is saying?
	Do you hear his unholy outrage
	against Dionysus, Semele's son,
	the god who comes first among the blessed
	at the feast where the garlands are lovely?
	This is his office:
	to dance with the company,
	to laugh with the pipe,
	to put an end to worry,
	whenever the gleam of the grape comes
	to the feast of the gods,
	and at the ivy-wearing banquets
	the wine bowl wraps men in sleep.

Chorus.
	Mouths without a bridle
	and folly without a law
	end in disaster.
	But the quiet life
	and good sense
	stay unshaken, and hold a house together.
	The gods live far off in the sky,
	but they still see what mortals do.
	Being clever is not being wise,
	and neither is thinking thoughts too big for a mortal.
	Life is short. A man who chases
	great things on that short lease
	loses what is in his hands.
	That is how madmen behave,
	people whose judgement is bad,
	if you ask me.

Chorus.
	Let me go to Cyprus,
	Aphrodite's island,
	where the Loves live
	who charm the hearts of mortals,
	and to Paphos, where no rain falls
	and the hundred mouths of a foreign river
	make the land fruitful.
	And where Pieria is, the loveliest,
	the seat of the Muses,
	the holy slope of Olympus:
	take me there, Dionysus, Dionysus,
	leader of the Bacchants, god of the cry.
	There are the Graces,
	there is Desire, and there
	the Bacchants are allowed their rites.

Chorus.
	The god, the son of Zeus,
	is glad at the feast,
	and he loves Peace, the giver of riches,
	the goddess who rears the young.
	To the rich man and to the poorer one alike
	he gives the joy of wine, that has no grief in it.
	And he hates the man who does not care for this:
	to live a life of happiness, by daylight
	and through the dear nights,
	and to keep a wise heart and mind
	clear of men who want too much.
	Whatever the ordinary crowd
	believes and lives by,
	that is good enough for me.

(Pentheus comes out of the house. A Servant arrives with other guards,
leading the stranger, whose hands are bound.)

Servant.
Pentheus, here we are. We have caught the prey you sent us after, and we
did not go out for nothing. But this animal was gentle with us. He did
not pull his foot back and run. He held out his hands, and not
unwillingly either. He did not go pale. He did not lose the wine colour
in his cheeks. He laughed, and told us to tie him up and take him away,
and he stood still and made my job easy. I felt ashamed, and I said to
him, "Stranger, I am not doing this by choice. Pentheus sent me. These
are his orders."

And the Bacchants you locked up, the ones you rounded up and chained in
the public prison: they are gone. They are loose, and they are skipping
off to the meadows calling on their god Dionysus. The chains fell off
their feet by themselves, and the keys turned in the doors without a
mortal hand touching them. This man has come to Thebes full of wonders.
What happens next is your business.

Pentheus.
Let go of his hands. He is in my net now, and he is not so quick that he
can get away from me. Well. You are not bad-looking, stranger, not to a
woman's eye, which is what you came to Thebes for. That long hair, for
one thing, and no wrestler's crop either, spilling down along your
cheek, full of longing. And the white skin, carefully kept. You have
stayed out of the sun, in the shade, hunting Aphrodite with your good
looks. First of all, then, tell me who your people are.

Dionysus.
No boasting about it. It is easy to say. You have heard of Tmolus, I
expect, the mountain covered in flowers.

Pentheus.
I know it. It circles the city of Sardis.

Dionysus.
That is where I am from. Lydia is my country.

Pentheus.
And why are you bringing these rites into Greece?

Dionysus.
Dionysus sent us on our way. The son of Zeus.

Pentheus.
Is there some Zeus over there who breeds new gods?

Dionysus.
No. The same one who married Semele here.

Pentheus.
And did he force this on you at night, in a dream, or with your eyes
open?

Dionysus.
Face to face. He saw me and I saw him, and he gave me the rites.

Pentheus.
These rites of yours. What form do they take?

Dionysus.
That may not be spoken to any mortal who is not a Bacchant.

Pentheus.
And what good do they do the people who hold them?

Dionysus.
It is not lawful for you to hear it. But it is worth knowing.

Pentheus.
Nicely faked. Now I want to hear it.

Dionysus.
The god's rites hate a man who practises impiety.

Pentheus.
You say you saw the god plainly. What was he like?

Dionysus.
Whatever he wanted to be like. I did not give the orders.

Pentheus.
You have channelled that one round me too, very neatly, and said
nothing.

Dionysus.
Talk wisdom to an ignorant man, and he will think you a fool.

Pentheus.
Is this the first place you have come to, bringing your god?

Dionysus.
Every barbarian is dancing these rites already.
