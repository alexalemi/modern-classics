Orestes
(Part 4 of 4)

Electra.
Stand there, some of you on this cart road, and the rest of you over
here on the other path, to keep watch over the house.

(The Chorus divides. Half of them go to watch each road.)

Chorus.
	Why are you calling on me for this duty?
	Tell me, my dear.

Electra.
	I am afraid that someone will come to the house
	and stand over the fresh blood
	and find trouble on top of trouble.

First Half-Chorus.
Go on, hurry. I will keep guard on this road, the one toward the rising
sun.

Second Half-Chorus.
And I on this one, the one that leads toward the west.

Electra.
Now turn your eyes sideways, this way and that.

Chorus.
	From there to here, and then back again
	we turn to look, as you tell us.

Electra.
	Roll your eyes about now,
	look at everything, through your hair.

First Half-Chorus.
Someone is coming along the road. Who is this country man wandering
about your house?

Electra.
Then we are finished, friends. Any moment now he will show our enemies
the armed beasts we have hidden.

First Half-Chorus.
	Do not be afraid. The path is empty, my dear.
	It is not what you think.

Electra.
	And you? Is your side still safe for me?
	Give me some good news,
	tell me the ground in front of the courtyard is deserted.

Second Half-Chorus.
All is well on this side. But watch your own. Not one of the Argives is
coming near us.

First Half-Chorus.
Same here. There is no crowd on this side either.

Electra.
Well then, let me put my ear to the gates.

Chorus.
	You inside the house, why are you so slow
	to redden the blade with the victim's blood, while all is quiet?

Electra.
They do not hear me. Oh, I am wretched. Has her beauty blunted their
swords? Any moment now some Argive will come running in full armour to
the rescue and close in on the house. Watch more carefully. This is no
contest for sitting still. Turn about, some of you this way, the rest
of you that way.

Chorus.
I am going up and down the road, watching everywhere.

(Helen screams from inside the house.)

Helen.
Io, Pelasgian Argos! I am being murdered, foully murdered.

Chorus.
Did you hear? The men have their hands in the killing. That is Helen
screaming, if I can judge.

Electra.
Zeus, oh Zeus, power that never runs dry, come and help the people I
love, whatever it takes.

Helen.
Menelaus, I am dying, and you are right here and do nothing for me.

Electra.
	Murder her, kill her,
	destroy her, drive the two double-edged swords
	out of your hands
	into the woman who left her father, left her husband,
	who killed so many Greeks
	by the spear, dead beside the river,
	where tears fell on tears
	from the iron arrows
	around the eddies of Scamander.

Chorus.
Quiet, quiet. I heard a footstep. Someone is coming down the road to
the house.

Electra.
Dearest women, it is Hermione, walking right into the middle of the
killing. Let us stop shouting. She is stepping into the meshes of the
net. A fine catch she will make, if she is taken. Back to your places,
with calm eyes, and nothing in your faces to show what has been done. I
will keep my own eyes down and gloomy, as if I knew nothing of what has
happened here.

(Hermione comes in from the direction of Clytemnestra's tomb.)

Girl, are you back from wreathing Clytemnestra's grave and pouring
drink-offerings to the dead below?

Hermione.
I am back. I won her goodwill. But a kind of fear has come over me. I
heard a cry from the house while I was still a long way off.

Electra.
Why not? What is happening to us deserves groans.

Hermione.
Say something better than that. What is the news?

Electra.
This country has voted that Orestes and I must die.

Hermione.
No. Not my own kin.

Electra.
It is fixed. We are standing under the yoke of necessity.

Hermione.
Is that the reason for the cry inside the house?

Electra.
Yes. A suppliant fell at Helen's knees and was crying out—

Hermione.
Who? I know nothing more unless you tell me.

Electra.
Poor Orestes. Begging not to die, and for me too.

Hermione.
Then the house has good reason to be shouting.

Electra.
What else would anyone cry out about, if not that? But come and join
your friends in begging. Fall at your mother's feet, blessed as she is,
so that Menelaus does not have to watch us die. You were nursed in my
mother's arms. Pity us, and lift some of this evil off us. Come here,
into the struggle, and I will lead the way. You alone hold the end of
our safety in your hands.

Hermione.
Look, I am hurrying my feet into the house. As far as it lies with me,
count yourselves saved.

Electra.
You inside the house, friends with your swords, will you not seize the
prey?

Hermione.
Oh, no. Who are these men I see?

(Armed men seize Hermione at the door. Orestes speaks from inside.)

Orestes.
Quiet. You have come here for our safety, not for yours.

Electra.
Hold her, hold her. Put a sword to her throat and then keep quiet, so
that Menelaus learns he has found men here, not Phrygian cowards, and
has been treated the way cowards deserve.

(They drag Hermione into the house. Electra goes in after them, and the
doors are shut.)

Chorus.
	Io, io, friends,
	raise a noise, a noise and a shout
	in front of the house, so that the murder that has been done
	does not throw the Argives into a terrible panic
	and bring them running to the royal house,
	before I see for certain that Helen lies
	murdered and bloody in the house,
	or hear the story from one of her servants.
	Part of what has happened I know. The rest I do not know for sure.
	Through justice the vengeance of the gods
	has come to Helen.
	She filled all Greece with tears
	because of that cursed, cursed Paris of Ida,
	who brought Greece to Troy.

Chorus.
But the bolts of the royal doors are rattling. Be quiet. One of the
Phrygians is coming out. We can ask him how things stand inside.

(A Phrygian slave scrambles down from the roof of the porch and tumbles
out in front of the house.)

Phrygian.
	I have escaped the Argive sword, out of death,
	in my barbarian slippers,
	over the cedar beams that roof the porch,
	over the Doric triglyphs,
	gone, gone, oh Earth, Earth,
	a barbarian's flight.
	Aiai.
	Where can I run, strangers? Into the grey air,
	fly up, or into the sea, that the Ocean
	with the bull's head
	winds round the earth in his arms?

Chorus.
	What is it, Helen's servant, you head from Ida?

Phrygian.
	Ilium, Ilium, oh, oh,
	city of Phrygia, and holy Ida
	with your rich soil, how I groan for your ruin,
	a chariot-song, a chariot-song of grief
	in my barbarian cry, over the beauty born of a bird,
	the swan-feathered face, Leda's
	cub, Helen the ruin,
	Helen the ruin,
	a Fury for Apollo's towers of polished stone.
	Ottotoi.
	Wailing, wailing,
	poor Dardania, for Ganymede
	and his horses, the boy who shared the bed of Zeus.

Chorus.
Tell us clearly, one thing after another, what happened in the house.
Up to now I have only been guessing, and I cannot make it out.

Phrygian.
	Ailinon, ailinon: that is how the barbarians
	begin a death, aiai,
	in the tongue of Asia, whenever
	the blood of kings is poured on the ground
	by the iron swords of Hades.
	They came into the house, to tell you
	one thing after another, two twin lions
	of Greece.
	One of them, they said, had the general for his father.
	The other was the son of Strophius, a man of evil cunning,
	like Odysseus, treacherous in his silence,
	but true to his friends, bold in a fight,
	clever in war, and a deadly snake.
	Curse him for his quiet
	planning, the villain.
	They went in to the throne
	of the woman Paris the archer married,
	their eyes smeared with tears,
	and sat down, humble,
	one on this side, one on that,
	each guarding his own flank.
	And they threw, they threw their suppliant hands
	round Helen's knees, both of them.
	And up leapt the Phrygian slaves,
	leapt running,
	and each one said to the next, dropping in fear,
	that this might be a trick.
	Some thought not,
	but others thought the snake who killed his mother
	was tangling the daughter of Tyndareus
	in the meshes of a trap.

Chorus.
	And where were you then? Or had you already run away in fear?

Phrygian.
	It happened I was, in the Phrygian way, the Phrygian way,
	stirring the air, the air beside Helen's curl,
	Helen's, with a round fan
	of feathers, well made, in front of her cheek,
	waving it, the way barbarians do.
	And she was twisting flax on her spindle
	with her fingers,
	letting the thread fall to the floor,
	because she wanted to stitch ornaments for the tomb
	out of the Phrygian spoils, with her flax,
	purple robes, a gift for Clytemnestra.
	Orestes spoke to the Spartan girl:
	"Daughter of Zeus, set your foot
	on the floor here, get up off your chair,
	and come to the seat of Pelops our forefather,
	the old hearth,
	so that you can hear what I have to say."
	And he led her, led her, and she followed,
	no prophet of what was coming.
	And his partner in the work was busy elsewhere,
	the evil Phocian, going about:
	"Out of the way! Phrygians. Cowards, always."
	And he locked us up, one here and one there,
	all over the house: some in the horse
	stables, some in the outer rooms,
	one this way and another that,
	posting each of us far away from our mistress.

Chorus.
	And what came next, in this disaster?

Phrygian.
	Mother of Ida,
	mother, mighty, mighty,
	aiai, the bloody sufferings, the lawless
	evils I saw, I saw
	in the house of kings.
	From under the shadow of their purple-bordered cloaks
	they pulled their swords,
	and each one spun his eye
	a different way, in case anyone was near.
	Like boars of the mountain
	they stood facing the woman
	and said: "You will die,
	you will die.
	Your coward husband is killing you,
	because he betrayed his brother's son
	to die in Argos."
	And she screamed, she
	screamed: "Oh, oh."
	She struck her white arm on her breast
	and beat her poor head with the blow,
	and turned her gold-sandalled foot
	to flight, to flight. But Orestes
	stepped after her in his Mycenaean boot,
	threw his fingers into her hair,
	bent her neck back over her left shoulder,
	and was about to drive the black sword
	into her throat.

Chorus.
	And where were you Phrygians in the house, to defend her?

Phrygian.
	With a scream
	we broke out the doors and doorposts
	with bars, from the rooms where we were waiting,
	and came running to help from every corner of the house,
	one with stones, one with javelins,
	one with a sword drawn in his hands.
	And against us came Pylades,
	unflinching, like, like Hector
	the Phrygian, or Ajax of the triple crest,
	whom I saw, I saw at the gates
	of Priam. And we met, point to point
	of our swords.
	And then, then it was very plain what Phrygians are,
	how much less than Greek spears
	we were in the strength of Ares:
	one gone, running; one a corpse;
	one carrying a wound; one begging,
	holding that up against death.
	We ran for cover in the dark.
	Corpses were falling, others were about to fall, others lay still.
	And poor Hermione came into the house
	just as her mother fell to the floor, murdered,
	the poor woman who bore her.
	And like Bacchants without their wands,
	the two of them ran at her and caught her up,
	a mountain cub in their hands,
	and turned back to the daughter of Zeus
	to slaughter her. But she,
	out of the bedroom,
	straight through the house, she
	vanished, oh Zeus and Earth
	and light and night,
	by drugs, or the arts
	of magicians, or the gods stealing her away.
	What came after I no longer know.
	I stole my runaway foot out of the house.
	So Menelaus, after all his troubles, all his troubles,
	took his wife Helen back from Troy
	and got nothing for it.

Chorus.
And here is a new thing on top of new things. I see Orestes coming out
in front of the house with a sword in his hand, walking with a wild
step.

(Orestes comes out of the house with a drawn sword.)

Orestes.
Where is the man who ran out of the house to get away from my sword?

Phrygian.
I bow down to you, my lord, falling at your feet the way barbarians do.

Orestes.
This is not Ilium. This is the land of Argos.

Phrygian.
Everywhere, a man with sense finds living sweeter than dying.

Orestes.
You did not by any chance raise that shout for Menelaus to come
running?

Phrygian.
No, for you. To help you. You deserve it more.

Orestes.
So the daughter of Tyndareus died justly?

Phrygian.
Most justly. Even if she had three throats to die with.

Orestes.
It is cowardice that makes your tongue so obliging. You do not think so
inside.

Phrygian.
Don't I? The woman who ruined Greece, and the Phrygians along with it?

Orestes.
Swear it, or I kill you. Swear you are not saying it to please me.

Phrygian.
I swear by my life. That is an oath I would keep.

Orestes.
Was it like this at Troy too? Were all the Phrygians this afraid of
iron?

Phrygian.
Take the sword away. This close, it flashes back a terrible death.

Orestes.
Afraid you will turn to stone, as if you had looked at a Gorgon?

Phrygian.
Into a corpse, more likely. I do not know anything about the Gorgon's
head.

Orestes.
You are a slave, and you are afraid of Hades, who would set you free
from your troubles?

Phrygian.
Every man, even a slave, likes looking at the light.

Orestes.
Well said. Your good sense saves you. Go inside the house.

Phrygian.
You are not going to kill me, then?

Orestes.
You are let off.

Phrygian.
That is a fine thing to say.

Orestes.
But we may change our minds.

Phrygian.
That is not a fine thing to say.

Orestes.
Fool. Do you think I could bring myself to bloody your throat? You were
not born a woman, and you do not count among men either. I came out of
the house to stop your shouting, and for nothing else. Argos wakes up
fast when it hears a cry for help. As for Menelaus, I am not afraid to
take him on within reach of my sword. Let him come, proud of the yellow
curls on his shoulders. If he gathers the Argives and brings them
against this house to avenge Helen's murder, and refuses to save me,
and my sister, and Pylades who did this with me, he will have two
corpses to look at, his daughter's and his wife's.

(Orestes drives the Phrygian into the house ahead of him. The doors are
shut.)

Chorus.
	Io, io, fortune.
	Into another struggle, another one, the house
	is falling, a fearful one, around the sons of Atreus.
	What do we do? Report this to the city?
	Or keep quiet? That is safer, friends.
	Look, in front of the house, look, smoke
	is rushing up into the air. It is telling the story already.
	They are lighting torches to burn the house
	of Tantalus, and they do not hold back from murder.
	A god holds the end for mortals,
	the end where he wants it.
	Great is the power. Through avenging spirits
	this house has fallen, fallen, through blood,
	ever since Myrtilus was thrown from the chariot.

Chorus.
But here comes Menelaus too. I see him near the house, moving fast. He
has heard, I suppose, about the luck that stands here now. You sons of
Atreus inside, you had better hurry and fix the bolts across the doors.
A man who is doing well is a terrible thing to men who are doing badly,
and you are doing badly now, Orestes.

(Menelaus comes in with armed servants.)

Menelaus.
I have come because I heard of the terrible, violent deeds of a pair of
lions. I will not call them men. What I heard was that my wife is not
dead but has vanished, gone out of sight. An empty rumour, that.
Someone brought it to me who had lost his head from fear. No, this is
one of the mother-killer's tricks, and a great joke. Someone open the
house. I am telling my servants: force these gates, so that at least we
can rescue my daughter out of the hands of these blood-stained men, and
take up my poor, wretched wife, and the men who destroyed her can die
alongside her, by my hand.

(Orestes appears on the roof of the house, holding Hermione with a
sword at her throat. Pylades and Electra are beside him with torches.)

Orestes.
You there. Keep your hand off those bolts. I mean you, Menelaus,
towering up there in your nerve. Or I will smash your skull with this
coping-stone, tearing off the old cornice, the masons' work. The doors
are fixed fast with bars, which will keep your rescuing zeal locked out
and stop you getting inside the house.

Menelaus.
Ah. What is this? I see the glare of torches, and men on top of the
house, standing at bay as if it were a fort, and a sword guarding my
daughter's throat.

Orestes.
Do you want to ask questions, or to hear me?

Menelaus.
Neither. But I have to hear you, it seems.

Orestes.
I am going to kill your daughter, if you want to know.

Menelaus.
You murdered Helen, and now you are adding murder to murder?

Orestes.
If only I had done it, instead of being robbed by the gods.

Menelaus.
You deny killing her? You say this to outrage me?

Orestes.
It is a painful denial. If only I had—

Menelaus.
Had done what? You are frightening me.

Orestes.
Thrown the polluter of Greece down into Hades.

Menelaus.
Give me my wife's body back, so I can heap a tomb over her.

Orestes.
Ask the gods for it. But I will kill your daughter.

Menelaus.
The mother-killer adds murder to murder?

Orestes.
The man who avenged his father, whom you betrayed to death.

Menelaus.
Was your mother's blood not enough for you, the blood you have on you
already?

Orestes.
I would never get tired of killing evil women.

Menelaus.
And you, Pylades, are you a partner in this killing too?

Orestes.
He says yes by his silence. I will do the talking, and that will be
enough.

Menelaus.
You will not enjoy it, unless you can fly away on wings.

Orestes.
We will not run. We will set the house on fire.

Menelaus.
What, you will destroy your father's house?

Orestes.
So that you cannot have it. And I will cut her throat over the fire.

Menelaus.
Kill her, then. Kill her, and you will pay me for it.

Orestes.
So be it.

Menelaus.
Ah, ah. No. Do not do it.

Orestes.
Be quiet, then, and bear it. You are suffering justly.

Menelaus.
Is it just that you should live?

Orestes.
Yes, and rule a country.

Menelaus.
Which country?

Orestes.
This one. Pelasgian Argos.

Menelaus.
You would be a fine one to touch the holy water—

Orestes.
Why ever not?

Menelaus.
And to kill the victims before a battle.

Orestes.
And you would be fit for it?

Menelaus.
My hands are clean.

Orestes.
But not your mind.

Menelaus.
Who would speak to you?

Orestes.
Anyone who loves his father.

Menelaus.
And anyone who honours his mother?

Orestes.
Was born lucky.

Menelaus.
Not you, then.

Orestes.
No. I do not like evil women.

Menelaus.
Take that sword away from my daughter.

Orestes.
You were born a liar.

Menelaus.
So you will kill my daughter?

Orestes.
Now you are not lying.

Menelaus.
Oh, what am I to do?

Orestes.
Go to the Argives and persuade them—

Menelaus.
Persuade them of what?

Orestes.
Not to kill us. Beg the city.

Menelaus.
Or you will murder my child?

Orestes.
That is how it is.

Menelaus.
Poor Helen—

Orestes.
And I am not poor?

Menelaus.
I brought you back from Phrygia to be a victim—

Orestes.
If only that were true.

Menelaus.
After a thousand labours.

Orestes.
Except for me. Not one on my account.

Menelaus.
I have suffered terribly.

Orestes.
Yes. Because back then you were no use.

Menelaus.
You have me.

Orestes.
You caught yourself, by being a coward. Come on, Electra, set fire to
the house from below. And you, Pylades, the truest friend I have, burn
the cornice of these walls.

Menelaus.
Land of the Danaans, you who founded Argos, city of horses, come on,
will you not come running in arms to help? This man is forcing your
whole city to let him live, when he has shed his mother's polluted
blood.

(Apollo appears above the house, with Helen beside him.)

Apollo.
Menelaus, stop. Put away that sharpened temper. I am Apollo, the son of
Leto, here close beside you, and I am calling you by name. And you,
Orestes, standing over this girl with your sword, listen, so that you
know what words I have brought. Helen, whom you were so eager to
destroy, to make Menelaus angry, and failed: here she is, the woman
you see in the folds of the sky, saved, and not killed by you. I
rescued her. I snatched her from under your sword at the order of Zeus
my father. She is Zeus's child, and she must live and never die. She
will sit with Castor and Polydeuces in the folds of the sky, a saviour
to sailors. Take another bride into your house, Menelaus. The gods used
her beauty to bring Greeks and Phrygians together in one place and set
deaths going, to drain off from the earth the outrage of mortals
swarming in numbers without end.

That is how it stands with Helen. As for you, Orestes, you must cross
the borders of this land and live on the soil of Parrhasia for the
circle of one year. The Azanians and the Arcadians will name it after
your exile and call it Oresteion. From there go to the city of the
Athenians and stand trial for your mother's blood before the three
Kindly Ones, the Furies. The gods will be the judges of your trial, on
the hill of Ares, and they will cast a most righteous vote, and there
you must win. And the girl at whose throat you are holding the sword,
Orestes, Hermione: it is fated that you will marry her. Neoptolemus
thinks he will marry her; he never will. It is his fate to die by a
Delphian sword when he comes to demand satisfaction from me for his
father Achilles. Give your sister to Pylades in marriage, as you once
promised. A happy life waits for him. Menelaus, let Orestes rule Argos.
Go and be king in the land of Sparta, and keep it as the dowry of a
wife who never stopped handing you troubles by the thousand, until this
very day. I will set things right between him and the city. It was I
who forced him to murder his mother.

Orestes.
Apollo, prophet, your oracles. You were no lying prophet after all, but
a true one. And yet a fear had crept into me, that I was listening to
some avenging spirit and only thought I heard your voice. But it is
ending well, and I will obey your words. Look: I release Hermione from
the slaughter, and I agree to the marriage, whenever her father gives
her.

Menelaus.
I greet you, Helen, daughter of Zeus, and I envy you your home in the
blessed house of the gods. Orestes, I pledge my daughter to you, since
Apollo says so. Well born yourself, marrying a well-born wife, may you
have joy of her, and I too, who give her.

Apollo.
Go now, each of you, where I have ordered, and let your quarrels
dissolve.

Menelaus.
We must obey.

Orestes.
I am of the same mind. I make my peace with what has happened,
Menelaus, and with your oracles, Apollo.

Apollo.
	Go your ways now, and honour Peace,
	the most beautiful of the gods. And I
	will bring Helen to the halls of Zeus,
	when I have crossed the vault of the bright stars.
	There, beside Hera and Hebe,
	the wife of Heracles, she will sit enthroned, a goddess
	to men, honoured with drink-offerings forever,
	with the sons of Tyndareus, the sons of Zeus,
	keeping watch over the sea for sailors.

(Apollo and Helen vanish above. The others go their separate ways.)

Chorus.
	Great Victory, holy one,
	stay with me all my life,
	and never stop crowning me.
