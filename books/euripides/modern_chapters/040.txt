The Phoenician Women
(Part 2 of 4)

Polyneices.
The words of truth are simple. What is just does not need elaborate
explanations; it carries its own fitness inside it. It is the unjust
argument that is sick inside, and needs clever medicines. I looked
ahead, for my father's house, to what was mine and what was his. I
wanted to escape the curses Oedipus once spoke against us, so I left
this country of my own free will, and I let him rule our homeland for
the circle of one year, on the understanding that I would take my turn
and rule again after him, and not come at him in hatred and envy, and do
some harm and suffer some, which is what happens. He agreed to it. He
swore it by the gods. And he has done nothing that he promised. He is
holding the throne himself, and my share of our house along with it.

And I am ready, even now: give me what is mine, and I will send the army
out of this country, and live in my own house for my turn, and hand it
back to him for an equal time, turn and turn about. I will not sack my
homeland, and I will not bring the fixed ladders up against its towers.
Only if I do not get justice will I try to. I call the gods to witness
this: that I am acting justly in everything, and it is without justice
that I am being robbed of my homeland, the most unholy thing there is.
That is the whole of it, mother, point by point. I have not wrapped it
in twisted phrases. It is just, to the clever and to the simple alike,
or so it seems to me.

Chorus.
I was not brought up in the land of the Greeks. But to me, what you say
makes sense.

Eteocles.
If the same thing were fine and clever to everyone, there would be no
disputed quarrels among human beings. As it is, there is nothing alike
and nothing equal among mortals, except in the names. The thing itself
does not exist. I will hide nothing from you, mother. I would go to
where the stars and the sun rise, and under the earth, if I were able to
do it, to hold Power, the greatest of the gods. So this good thing,
mother, I have no wish to hand to another man rather than keep it for
myself. It is cowardice to lose the greater and take the less. And there
is this besides: I would be ashamed to let him come with weapons and
ravage the land and get what he wants. That would be a disgrace to
Thebes, if I gave up my sceptre to him out of fear of a Mycenaean spear.
He should not have tried to make terms with weapons, mother. Words can
do everything that an enemy's iron could do. Still, if he wants to live
in this land on any other footing, he may. But that one thing I will not
willingly let go. When I can rule, am I to be his slave? So let the fire
come, let the swords come, harness your horses, fill the plains with
chariots: I will not hand my power to him. If a man must do wrong, then
to do wrong for the sake of power is the finest wrong there is. In
everything else, let him be pious.

Chorus.
Fine words should not be spent on deeds that are not fine. That is not
fine. It is bitter to justice.

Jocasta.
My child, Eteocles, not everything about old age is bad. Experience has
something to say that is wiser than the young. Why are you reaching for
Ambition, the worst of all the gods, my boy? Do not. She is an unjust
goddess. She has gone into many homes and many prosperous cities, and
come out again, and left the people who dealt with her in ruins. And she
is the one you are mad for. It is a finer thing, my child, to honour
Equality, who binds friend to friend, city to city, ally to ally,
always. Equality is what holds steady among human beings, whereas the
less is always the enemy of the more, and that is where the day of
hatred begins. It was Equality that laid down measures for men, and the
divisions of weights, and marked out numbers. The lightless eye of night
and the light of the sun walk their yearly circle as equals, and neither
of them is jealous when it has to give way. So the sun and the night are
servants of mortals, and you will not put up with holding an equal share
of the house and giving the same to him? Then where is justice?

Why do you honour Power so far beyond what she deserves, injustice with
luck on its side, and think her such a great thing? Is it worth so much
to be stared at? That is empty. Or do you want a great deal of trouble
to go with a great deal of property in your house? What is "more"? It is
only a name. What is enough is plenty, for people who keep themselves in
hand. Mortals do not own their possessions. We hold what belongs to the
gods, and look after it, and when they want it, they take it back.
Wealth is not sure. It lasts a day.

Come, suppose I put two questions to you at once and ask you: do you
want to be king, or do you want to save your city? Will you say, to be
king? And what if he beats you, and the Argive spears beat down the
spear of Cadmus's men? You will see this city of Thebes broken, and you
will see many captive girls forced by enemy men and ravaged. So this
wealth you are looking to hold will turn out to be a pain to Thebes. And
you, you have your Ambition.

That is what I say to you. And to you, Polyneices, I say this. Adrastus
did you a stupid favour, and you were a fool too, coming to lay waste
your own city. Come, if you take this land, which may it never happen,
then by the gods, how will you set up a trophy to Zeus? How will you
begin the sacrifice after conquering your own homeland, and what will
you write on the spoils by the streams of Inachus? "Polyneices burned
Thebes and dedicated these shields to the gods"? My child, may you never
win a name like that from the Greeks. And if instead you are beaten, and
his side comes out on top, how will you go back to Argos, leaving ten
thousand dead behind you? Someone is sure to say, "Adrastus, that was an
evil match you made. For one bride's wedding, we are all destroyed." You
are chasing two evils, my child: to lose what you have there, and to
fall in the middle of what you are trying here.

Let it go, both of you, this too much. Let it go. When the folly of two
men meets in the same place, it is the most hateful evil there is.

Chorus.
Gods, turn these evils away, and give the sons of Oedipus some way to
come to terms.

Eteocles.
Mother, this is no longer a contest of words. The time we spend in
between is wasted, and all your good will accomplishes nothing. We will
never come to terms on anything but what has been said already: that I
hold the sceptre and am king of this land. So spare me your long
lectures, and let me be. And you: get outside these walls, or die.

Polyneices.
At whose hands? Who is so impossible to wound that he could drive a
murderous sword into me and not carry off the same death himself?

Eteocles.
He is near. Not far off. Do you see my hands?

Polyneices.
I see them. But wealth is a coward, and it loves its own life. That is
the evil in it.

Eteocles.
Then why did you bring so many men to fight a man who is nothing?

Polyneices.
Because a safe general is better than a bold one.

Eteocles.
You are a boaster, trusting in the truce that keeps you from dying.

Polyneices.
It keeps you alive too. For the second time, I demand the sceptre and my
share of the land.

Eteocles.
I take no demands. I am going to live in my own house.

Polyneices.
Holding more than your share?

Eteocles.
I am. Now get out of the country.

Polyneices.
Altars of my fathers' gods —

Eteocles.
Which you are here to sack.

Polyneices.
Hear me —

Eteocles.
Who would hear you? You have marched against your homeland.

Polyneices.
And temples of the gods who ride white horses —

Eteocles.
Who hate you.

Polyneices.
I am being driven out of my homeland —

Eteocles.
Yes. Because you came to drive us out.

Polyneices.
Unjustly, gods.

Eteocles.
Call on the gods in Mycenae, not here.

Polyneices.
You were born unholy —

Eteocles.
But not my homeland's enemy, as you are.

Polyneices.
You, who are driving me out with no share.

Eteocles.
And I will kill you as well.

Polyneices.
Father, do you hear what is being done to me?

Eteocles.
Yes. And he hears what you are doing.

Polyneices.
And you, mother?

Eteocles.
It is forbidden for you to speak the name of your mother.

Polyneices.
My city.

Eteocles.
Go to Argos, and call on the waters of Lerna.

Polyneices.
I am going. Do not trouble yourself. Mother, I thank you.

Eteocles.
Get out of the land.

Polyneices.
I am going. But let me see my father.

Eteocles.
You will not get that.

Polyneices.
Then my sisters, the unmarried girls.

Eteocles.
You will never see them either.

Polyneices.
My sisters.

Eteocles.
Why are you calling on them? You are their worst enemy.

Polyneices.
Mother, at least you: be well.

Jocasta.
Well. Yes, I am doing wonderfully well, child.

Polyneices.
I am your son no longer.

Jocasta.
I was born to a great deal of misery.

Polyneices.
Because this man is treating me with outrage.

Eteocles.
And I am being outraged in return.

Polyneices.
Where will you take your stand in front of the towers?

Eteocles.
Why do you ask me that?

Polyneices.
I will set myself opposite you, to kill you.

Eteocles.
I have the same desire.

Jocasta.
Oh, I am wretched. What are you going to do, my children?

Polyneices.
It will show for itself.

Jocasta.
Will you not run from your father's Furies?

Eteocles.
Let the whole house go to ruin.

Polyneices.
Yes. Soon enough my sword will not lie idle any longer. It will be red
with blood. I call the land that raised me to witness, and the gods,
that I am being driven out of this country dishonoured, suffering
pitiful things, like a slave, and not a son of the same father, Oedipus,
just as much as he is. And if anything happens to you, my city, do not
blame me. Blame him. I did not come willingly, and unwillingly I am
driven out of the land. And you, Lord Apollo, guardian of the streets,
and these halls, goodbye, and the friends I grew up with, and the
statues of the gods where the sheep are offered. I do not know whether I
will ever speak to you again. But my hopes are not asleep yet. I trust
in them, and in the gods, that I will kill this man and be master of
this land of Thebes.

Eteocles.
Get out of the country. It was a true name our father gave you, with a
god's foresight: Polyneices, the man of many quarrels.

(Jocasta goes into the house. Polyneices goes out toward the Argive
camp, and Eteocles into the palace.)

Chorus.
	Cadmus came to this land
	from Tyre, and a four-footed
	heifer, never broken, dropped itself down
	at his feet, and fulfilled the oracle,
	for the god's word had told him
	to settle there, on the plains
	that carry wheat, and make his home
	where the lovely river water
	comes up over the fields,
	the fields of Dirce, green,
	the deep-sown fields.
	There his mother bore Dionysus,
	from her marriage to Zeus,
	and the ivy wound its curling wreath
	round him, still a baby,
	and covered him with its green shoots,
	shade over shade, and blessed him:
	a Bacchic dance for the girls of Thebes
	and the women who cry Euoi.

Chorus.
	There was the murderous dragon
	of Ares, a savage-hearted guard,
	watching the running springs
	and the green streams
	with the wandering pupils of its eyes.
	Cadmus came for water to wash his hands
	and destroyed it with a lump of marble,
	striking the murderous head
	with a throw of his beast-killing arm,
	and at the word of Pallas,
	Zeus's daughter who had no mother,
	he threw the teeth
	into the deep-sown fields, to fall on the ground.
	And out of it the earth sent up
	a sight in full armour, over the topmost
	edge of the soil, and iron-hearted
	slaughter joined them to the dear earth again.
	It wet the ground with their blood, the ground
	that had shown them to the sunlit breath of the air.

Chorus.
	And you, Epaphus, born long ago
	from Io, our first mother,
	child of Zeus:
	I called you, I called you, with a barbarian cry,
	io, with barbarian prayers.
	Come, come to this land.
	Your descendants founded it,
	and the goddesses of the two names,
	Persephone and dear
	Demeter, the goddess who is queen of all,
	Earth, the nurse of all,
	took it for their own. Send the goddesses
	who carry the torches, and defend this land.
	Everything is easy for the gods.

(Eteocles comes out of the palace with a servant.)

Eteocles.
You, go and fetch Creon, the son of Menoeceus, my mother Jocasta's
brother. Tell him this: that I want to put my head together with his
about the city's business, private and public, before we go into battle
and set the army in its ranks. But no. He is sparing your feet the
trouble. He is here; I can see him walking toward my house.

Creon.
I have been a long way round looking for you, my lord Eteocles. I have
been all round the gates of Thebes and the guard posts, hunting for you.

Eteocles.
And I wanted to see you, Creon. When I went and put it to Polyneices
face to face, I found the terms of peace a long way short.

Creon.
I hear he has his mind set on more than Thebes, trusting in his marriage
tie with Adrastus and in his army. But that we must leave hanging on the
gods. What stands most in our way is what I have come to tell you.

Eteocles.
What is it? I do not follow you.

Creon.
Someone has come in, a prisoner taken from the Argives.

Eteocles.
And what does he say is the latest news from there?

Creon.
That the Argive army means to wrap the city of Cadmus's people in
weapons, right round the towers, at once.

Eteocles.
Then the city of Cadmus must carry its weapons out.

Creon.
Out where? Are you so young you cannot see what you should see?

Eteocles.
Out beyond those trenches, to fight straight away.

Creon.
This country's numbers are small. Theirs are endless.

Eteocles.
I know those men. They are bold in words.

Creon.
Argos carries some weight among the Greeks.

Eteocles.
Do not worry. I will soon fill the plain with their dead.

Creon.
I would like that. But I see a great deal of hard work in it.

Eteocles.
I will not keep my army inside the walls, I tell you.

Creon.
And yet winning is all a matter of good planning.

Eteocles.
So you want me to turn to some other road?

Creon.
Every road, before the whole risk is run at once.

Eteocles.
What if we attacked them at night, from ambush?

Creon.
Yes, if you can get back here safely when it goes wrong.

Eteocles.
Night is even-handed, but it gives more to the man who dares.

Creon.
The dark of night is a terrible place to meet disaster.

Eteocles.
Then shall I attack them with the spear while they are at their dinner?

Creon.
That would give them a fright. But we need to win.

Eteocles.
The ford of Dirce is deep, though, if they have to fall back across it.

Creon.
Nothing is as good as keeping a good guard.

Eteocles.
What if we rode the Argive army down with the cavalry?

Creon.
There too the army is fenced all round with chariots.

Eteocles.
Then what am I to do? Hand the city to the enemy?

Creon.
Certainly not. Think it through. You are a clever man.

Eteocles.
And what forethought is cleverer than mine?

Creon.
Seven men, they say, or this is what I have heard —

Eteocles.
Assigned to do what? Seven is not much strength.

Creon.
To command companies, one posted at each of the seven gates.

Eteocles.
Then what do we do? I will not sit and wait for the moment when there is
nothing left to be done.

Creon.
You choose seven men too, to set against them at the gates.

Eteocles.
To lead companies, or to fight alone with the spear?

Creon.
To lead companies. Pick out the very bravest.

Eteocles.
I understand. To stop them scaling the walls.

Creon.
And second-in-commands with them. One man does not see everything.

Eteocles.
Do I pick them for courage, or for a clear head in planning?

Creon.
Both. Either one, left on its own, is nothing.

Eteocles.
It will be done. I will go to the city of seven towers and post the
captains at the gates, as you say, matching them man for man against the
enemy. To tell you each man's name would take a long time, with the
enemy camped under our very walls. But I will go, so that my hands do
not lie idle. And may I get my brother face to face, stand up to him in
battle, take him with my spear, and kill him, for coming to sack my
homeland.

The marriage of my sister Antigone and your son Haemon: if I stumble in
my luck somehow, that is yours to see to. The promise I made before, I
confirm now, as I go out. You are my mother's brother; why make a long
speech of it? Look after her in a way that is worthy of you, for your
own sake and for mine. As for my father, he has been convicted of folly
against himself, blinding his own eyes. I do not have much praise for
him. And if he gets the chance, he will kill us with his curses.

One thing we have left undone: to find out from the bird-reader
Teiresias whether he has any prophecy to tell. I will send your son
Menoeceus, who carries your father's name, to bring Teiresias here,
Creon. He will come and talk to you gladly. I have found fault with his
prophet's craft before this, to his face, so he has a grievance against
me.

And this charge I lay on the city and on you, Creon: if my side wins,
the body of Polyneices is never to be buried in this Theban soil, and
whoever buries him dies, even if it is one of our own. That is what I
have to say to you. And to my servants I say this: bring out my weapons
and the armour that goes all round me, so that we can set out now for
the contest of the spear that is waiting for us, with Justice on our
side to bring the victory. And to Caution, the most useful of the gods,
we pray: bring this city safely through.

(Eteocles goes out with his servants. Creon stays.)

Chorus.
	Ares, god of so much suffering, why, why
	are you possessed by blood and death,
	out of tune with the feasts of Dionysus?
	Not for the dances where the young girls
	wear their garlands, in the beauty of their youth,
	do you let your hair fly loose
	and sing to the breath of the pipe
	a song with the Graces in it, who make the dance.
	No. You breathe on the Argive army, armed men,
	with a thirst for the blood of Thebes,
	and lead your revel out in front of them,
	the revel with no flute at all.
	You do not come in the whirl of the fawnskin
	and the madness of the wands. You come
	on chariots, with bridles, four-hooved, uncut horses,
	riding down to the waters of Ismenus,
	breathing into the Argives hatred of the Sown Men,
	dressing an armed troop of shield-bearers in bronze
	to face our walls of stone.
	Strife is a terrible god,
	who planned these sorrows for the kings of this land,
	the sons of Labdacus, who have suffered so much.

Chorus.
	Cithaeron, valley of holy leaves,
	full of wild beasts, the snow-fed eye
	of Artemis: if only you had never
	raised the child put out to die, Jocasta's baby,
	Oedipus, thrown out of his home,
	his ankles marked by gold-bound pins.
	And if only the winged girl had never come,
	that monster from the mountains, a grief to this land,
	the Sphinx with her songs that had no music in them,
	who once came right up to our walls
	and with her four clawed feet carried off
	the sons of Cadmus into the untrodden light of the sky,
	sent up against the people of Cadmus by Hades under the ground.
	And now another strife, god-cursed, is coming into flower
	between the children of Oedipus, in the house and in the city.
	What is not fine can never be fine,
	and neither can children born against the law,
	a mother's labour, a father's pollution.
	She came to the bed of her own blood.

Chorus.
	You bore them, Earth, you bore them once,
	I learned it, I learned it once at home,
	the story told in a barbarian tongue,
	the race grown from the teeth
	of the dragon with the red crest, the eater of beasts:
	the finest disgrace Thebes ever had, its glory and its shame.
	Once the children of heaven came
	to the wedding of Harmonia, and to the lyre,
	to Amphion's lyre, the walls of Thebes rose,
	and its towers stood up
	between the crossing of the two rivers,
	where Dirce waters the green plain
	in front of Ismenus.
	And Io, the horned first mother,
	gave birth to the kings of Cadmus's line.
	And this city, changing from one blessing
	to another, ten thousand of them,
	stands on the heights,
	crowned with the wreaths of Ares.

(Teiresias comes in, old and blind, led by his daughter, with Creon's
son Menoeceus beside them. He wears a golden crown.)

Teiresias.
Lead the way, daughter. You are the eye for my blind feet, the way a
star is to sailors. Set my steps here, on the level ground, and walk
ahead, so we do not stumble; your father is weak. And keep the lots safe
for me in your girl's hand, the ones I drew when I read the signs of the
birds, sitting in the holy seat where I give my prophecies. Menoeceus,
my boy, son of Creon, tell me: how much of the road through the city is
left, to reach your father? My knees are failing me. I have come a long,
hard walk, and I can barely get through it.

Creon.
Courage, Teiresias. You are near your friends. You have brought your
foot into harbour. Take hold of him, child. Every wagon, and every old
man's foot, likes to wait for a hand from outside to lighten the load.

Teiresias.
Well, then. Here we are. Why did you send for me in such a hurry, Creon?

Creon.
I have not forgotten. But first gather your strength and get your breath
back, and shake off the steep climb of the road.

Teiresias.
I am worn through with tiredness, I admit it. I was brought here only
yesterday from the land of Erechtheus's sons, from Athens. There was a
war there too, against the spear of Eumolpus, and I gave the people of
Cecrops their glorious victory. And this golden crown, which you can
see, I have as the first fruits of the spoils taken from the enemy.

Creon.
I take your crown of victory as an omen. We are lying in the surge of a
war, as you know, against the spear of the Argives, and it is a great
contest for Thebes. Our king, Eteocles, has already gone, dressed in his
armour, to fight the strength of Mycenae. He has charged me to learn
from you what we should do to have the best chance of saving the city.

Teiresias.
For Eteocles' sake I would have shut my mouth and kept my prophecies to
myself. But you want to learn, so I will tell you. This land has been
sick a long time, Creon, ever since Laius fathered a child in the teeth
of the gods, and begot poor Oedipus to be his own mother's husband. The
bloody destruction of his eyes was the gods' clever device, and a lesson
held up to Greece. The sons of Oedipus wanted to cover it over with
time, as if they could outrun the gods, and they made a stupid mistake.
They gave their father no honours and no way out of the house, and they
turned an unlucky man savage; so he breathed terrible curses at them, in
his sickness, and in the disgrace they had added to it. And I, what did
I not do, what words did I not say, that I ended up hated by the sons of
Oedipus?

Death is close to them, Creon, each at the other's hand. Many dead will
fall on top of many dead, Argive weapons mixed with the weapons of
Cadmus's men, and they will give the land of Thebes bitter cause for
mourning. And you, poor city, you are being dug up by the roots along
with them, unless someone listens to what I say. The first thing would
have been for none of Oedipus's blood to be a citizen of this land, or
its king, since they are possessed by a god and will turn the city over.
But since the evil is stronger than the good, there is one other way to
save it. But no. It is not safe for me to say it, and it is bitter for
the people whose luck it is to supply the city with its saving medicine.
I am going. Goodbye. I am one man among many, and what is coming I will
suffer, if I have to. What else can I do?
