Orestes
(Part 1 of 4)

(Argos, in front of the palace of Agamemnon. Orestes lies asleep on a
bed that has been carried out of the house, wrapped in his cloak.
Electra sits beside him.)

Electra.
There is nothing so terrible you could put it into words, no suffering,
no disaster the gods send down, that human nature cannot take the weight
of. Take Tantalus, the blessed one, and I am not mocking his bad luck.
He was born from Zeus, they say, and now he floats in the air with a
rock hanging over his head, in terror of it. That is the price he pays,
and the reason, they say, is this: he was a man, and the gods let him
sit at their table as an equal, and he could not keep his tongue under
control. The most shameful sickness there is. Tantalus was the father of
Pelops, and Pelops of Atreus, and for Atreus the goddess carded her wool
and spun out strife: a war with his own brother, Thyestes. Why should I
go back over things that are better not said? Atreus killed Thyestes's
children and served them to him at dinner. And from Atreus, passing over
what came in between, came Agamemnon the famous, if he really was
famous, and Menelaus, and their mother was Aerope, from Crete. Menelaus
married Helen, whom the gods hate, and lord Agamemnon married
Clytemnestra, a marriage all Greece knew about. And she bore him three
daughters, Chrysothemis, Iphigenia, and me, Electra, and one son,
Orestes. All from one mother, and no mother was ever less holy. She
wrapped her husband in a robe he could not find his way out of, and
killed him. Why she did it is not a thing an unmarried girl should say.
I leave it unclear, for people to work out for themselves.

And Apollo. What is the point of accusing Apollo of injustice? But he
did persuade Orestes to kill the mother who gave birth to him, and not
everyone thinks that a glorious thing to have done. Still, he killed
her, because he would not disobey the god. And I had a share in the
killing, as much as a woman can. And Pylades, who did the work with us.
Since then poor Orestes here has been melting away in a savage sickness.
He fell on this bed and he lies there, and his mother's blood drives him
round and round in fits of madness. I am ashamed to name the goddesses
who are racing him to death with terror. The Kindly Ones, people call
them. This is the sixth day since our mother's throat was cut and her
body was purified with fire, and in all that time he has not let a
mouthful of food down his throat or put water on his skin. He hides
inside his cloak, and when his body gets some relief from the sickness
he comes to his senses and cries, and then at other times he leaps off
the bed and runs, like a colt getting out from under the yoke.

And this city of Argos has decreed that no one is to take us in under a
roof or at a fire, and no one is to speak to us. We killed our mother.
And today is the day that decides it. Today the city of Argos votes on
whether the two of us must die, under a pile of stones, or by sharpening
a sword and driving it into our necks. There is one hope we have of not
dying. Menelaus has landed here from Troy. His oars fill the harbour at
Nauplia and he is riding at anchor off the shore, after wandering a long
time on his way home from Troy. As for Helen, the woman so many people
have groaned over, he waited for night and sent her on ahead to our
house, in case somebody whose sons died at Troy saw her walking about in
daylight and started throwing stones. She is inside, crying for her
sister and for the ruin of the house. She does have one comfort in her
grief. The daughter she left at home when she sailed for Troy, Hermione,
whom Menelaus brought from Sparta and gave to my mother to bring up, is
here. Helen is happy in her, and forgets her troubles.

So I am watching every road, for the moment I see Menelaus coming.
Because otherwise we are riding on very little strength, unless he is
the one who saves us. A house that has run out of luck has run out of
everything.

(Helen comes out of the house.)

Helen.
Daughter of Clytemnestra and Agamemnon. Electra. Still unmarried, after
all this time. How are you, you poor thing, you and your brother, poor
Orestes here, who killed his mother? I am not polluted by speaking to
you, because I put the blame for the sin on Apollo. And yet I do grieve
for the death of Clytemnestra, my sister. I never saw her again after I
sailed for Troy, the way I did sail, because a god had driven me out of
my mind. And now I am left behind without her, and I weep for what has
happened to us.

Electra.
Helen, why should I tell you what you can see for yourself, standing
here? The house of Agamemnon in ruins. I sit up all night beside a
miserable corpse, because a corpse is what he is, for all the little
breath that is in him. I am not blaming him for his troubles. But you
are blessed, and your husband is blessed, and the two of you have come
to us when we are as badly off as we could be.

Helen.
How long has he been lying on that bed?

Electra.
Ever since he shed the blood he was born from.

Helen.
Oh, poor man. And his mother. What a way to die.

Electra.
That is how it is. He has given in to his troubles.

Helen.
By the gods, would you do something for me, girl?

Electra.
As far as I have time, sitting here with my brother.

Helen.
Will you go to my sister's tomb for me?

Electra.
To my mother's tomb, you mean? What for?

Helen.
To take her a lock of my hair, and my offerings to pour.

Electra.
Is it not allowed for you to go to the tomb of your own family yourself?

Helen.
I would be ashamed to show my face to the Argives.

Electra.
A little late to be thinking straight, when you left your home in such a
shameful way back then.

Helen.
That is true. But it is not kind of you to say it.

Electra.
What is this shame that comes over you in front of the people of
Mycenae?

Helen.
I am afraid of the fathers of the men who died at Troy.

Electra.
And you should be. In Argos your name is shouted from every mouth.

Helen.
Then do me this favour and set me free from my fear.

Electra.
I could not bring myself to look at my mother's tomb.

Helen.
But it would be a disgrace to have slaves carry these things.

Electra.
Then why not send your daughter Hermione?

Helen.
It is not nice for an unmarried girl to go creeping into a crowd.

Electra.
And yet she would be paying the dead woman back for bringing her up.

Helen.
That is true, and you have convinced me, girl. Yes, I will send my
daughter. You are quite right. Hermione, child, come out here in front
of the house.

(Hermione comes out.)

Take these offerings in your hands, and this hair of mine, and go to
Clytemnestra's tomb, and pour round it a mixture of honey and milk, and
the foam of wine, and stand on the top of the mound and say this: "Your
sister Helen sends you these offerings as a gift. She is afraid to come
to your grave herself, because she is frightened of the Argive crowd."
And ask her to keep kind thoughts towards me, and towards you, and
towards my husband, and towards these two poor things, whom a god has
destroyed. And whatever it is right for me to do for a sister, promise
it all, every gift that is owed to the dead. Go on, my child, hurry.
Give the offerings at the tomb, and then remember the road back as fast
as you can.

(Hermione goes off with the offerings. Helen goes back into the house.)

Electra.
Nature. What a great evil you are among human beings, and what a
salvation, to the people who were born with a good one. Did you see how
she cut her hair just at the very tips, to keep it beautiful? She is the
same woman she always was. May the gods hate you, for what you have done
to me, and to him, and to the whole of Greece. Oh, I am so tired of
this. And here they are again, my friends, the women who sing my laments
with me. They will shake him out of his sleep in a moment, this quiet
man lying here, and melt my eyes with tears, when I see my brother mad.
Dearest women, walk softly. Make no noise. Not a sound. Your friendship
is kind, I know, but if you wake him it will be a disaster for me.

(The Chorus, women of Argos, come in on tiptoe.)

Chorus.
	Quiet. Quiet. Set the soles of your shoes down
	lightly. Not a sound.

Electra.
	Further off. Over there. Away from the bed, I beg you.

Chorus.
	There. I am doing as you say.

Electra.
	Ah. Ah. Like the breath through a pipe
	of thin reed, my dear. Speak to me like that.

Chorus.
	Listen. I am bringing my voice down,
	low as a whisper under a roof.

Electra.
	Yes. Like that.
	Down, keep it down. Come close, gently. Gently, come.
	Now tell me what it is you have come here for.
	Because at last, after so long, he has fallen down and is asleep.

Chorus.
	How is he? Tell me, my dear.
	What am I to say has happened? What disaster?

Electra.
	He is still breathing. But his groans are getting faint.

Chorus.
	What are you saying? Oh, the poor man.

Electra.
	You will kill him, if you make his eyelids move
	while he is being carried on the sweetest grace sleep gives.

Chorus.
	Poor man. For the most hateful deeds,
	and a god behind them. Poor man.

Electra.
	Ah, the pain of it.
	Unjust. It was an unjust thing he cried out then, cried out,
	when Apollo sat on the tripod at Delphi that was Themis's once
	and decreed it: the killing of my mother. A killing against nature.

Chorus.
	Do you see? He is moving under his clothes.

Electra.
	That was you, you poor fool.
	You shouted, and threw him out of his sleep.

Chorus.
	No. I think he is sleeping.

Electra.
	Will you not go away from us, away from the house,
	turn round on your heel and go back,
	and stop that noise?

Chorus.
	He is dozing.

Electra.
	You are right.
	Lady. Lady Night,
	who gives sleep to human beings who labour so hard,
	come up from Erebus, from the dark below, come, come on your wings
	to the house of Agamemnon.
	Because under our griefs, under our disaster,
	we are going, we are gone. You made a noise. Will you not be quiet,
	quiet, keep a guard on your mouth,
	keep your clatter away from his bed,
	and let him have the quiet grace of sleep, my dear?

Chorus.
	Tell me. What end is waiting for his troubles?

Electra.
	Death. Death, what else?
	He does not even want food any more.

Chorus.
	Then his end is plain to see.

Electra.
	Apollo sacrificed us,
	when he handed us the blood of a mother who killed our father.
	Pitiful blood. Blood against nature.

Chorus.
	It was just.

Electra.
	But it was not well done.
	You killed, and you died, mother
	who gave birth to me, and you destroyed
	a father, and these children of your own blood.
	We are gone, as good as corpses, gone.
	You are among the dead, and the greater part
	of my life is gone too, in groaning and wailing
	and tears in the night, unmarried,
	childless, dragging out a miserable life
	for ever and ever.

Chorus.
Electra, you are close to him. Look and make sure your brother has not
died without your noticing. I do not like how limp he has gone.

Orestes.
Sweet spell of sleep, the sick man's helper, how sweetly you came to me,
and just when I needed you. Lady Forgetfulness, who takes away our
troubles, what a clever goddess you are, and how the unlucky pray to
you. Where did I come here from? How did I get here? I cannot remember.
The mind I had before has gone from me.

Electra.
My dearest, how happy you made me when you fell asleep. Do you want me
to take hold of you and lift you up?

Orestes.
Yes, take hold of me, take hold, and wipe the crust of foam off my poor
mouth, and off my eyes.

Electra.
There. It is a sweet service. I do not mind nursing a brother's body
with a sister's hand.

Orestes.
Put your side under mine, and get this filthy hair off my face. I can
hardly see.

Electra.
Oh, your poor head, your hair all matted and dirty. How wild you look,
after so long without washing.

Orestes.
Lay me back on the bed again. When the fit of madness lets go of me
there is no strength in my joints and my limbs go weak.

Electra.
There. A sick man loves his bed. It is a painful thing to own, but he
cannot do without it.

Orestes.
Sit me upright again. Turn my body round. Sick people are hard to
please. It is because they cannot help themselves.

Electra.
Do you want to set your feet on the ground and take a step, after all
this time? Any change is sweet.

Orestes.
Yes, very much. It has the look of health about it. And looking well is
better than being well, even if the truth is nowhere near.

Electra.
Then listen to me now, brother, while the Furies are letting you think
straight.

Orestes.
You have some news. If it is good, I will thank you for it. If it is
going to hurt, I have had enough of bad luck.

Electra.
Menelaus has come. Your father's brother. His ships are moored
at Nauplia.

Orestes.
What did you say? He has come, a light in my troubles and yours, a man
of our own blood, who owes our father his thanks?

Electra.
He has come, and here is your proof that I am telling the truth: he is
bringing Helen with him, from the walls of Troy.

Orestes.
If he had come home safe by himself, he would be more to be envied. If
he is bringing his wife, he has come home with a great evil in his
luggage.

Electra.
Tyndareus fathered a brood of daughters marked out for blame, and
infamous all through Greece.

Orestes.
Then you be different from the bad ones. You can. And do not just say
it. Think it.

Electra.
Oh, no. Brother, your eyes are going strange. You were sane a moment
ago, and now you are turning back into madness so fast.

Orestes.
Mother, I beg you, do not shake them at me, those girls with the blood
in their faces and the snakes in their hair. There they are. There,
right beside me, jumping at me.

Electra.
Stay where you are, you poor man. Lie still on your bed. You are seeing
nothing of what you think you see so clearly.

Orestes.
Apollo. They are going to kill me, the dog-faced ones, with the Gorgon's
stare, the priestesses of the dead. The terrible goddesses.

Electra.
I will not let you go. I am going to wind my arm round you and stop you
making these miserable leaps.

Orestes.
Let go of me. You are one of my Furies. You have got me round the middle
to throw me into Tartarus, into the pit.

Electra.
Oh, what am I going to do? Where can I turn for help, when it is the
gods themselves we have against us?

Orestes.
Give me the bow, the horn-tipped bow Apollo gave me. He told me to fight
the goddesses off with it if they tried to frighten me out of my mind
with their raving. One of the gods is going to be shot by a mortal hand,
if she does not get out of my sight. Do you not hear me? Do you not see
the feathered notches of the arrows springing out from my bow, the bow
that shoots so far? Ah. Ah. What are you waiting for? Up. Skim the sky
on your wings, and take it up with Apollo's oracles.

Ah. What is this? What am I raving about, gasping the breath out of my
lungs? Where, where did I jump to, off my bed? The waves have gone down.
I can see calm water again. Sister, why are you crying with your head
inside your dress? I am ashamed in front of you, giving you a share in
my troubles, making a girl who has never married carry the weight of my
sickness. Do not waste away because of my troubles. You nodded yes to
it, but the blood of our mother was shed by me. And I blame Apollo. He
set me on to the most unholy deed there is, and he cheered me on with
words, and with deeds he has done nothing. I believe that if I had asked
my father to his face whether I had to kill my mother, he would have
begged me, over and over, by this chin of mine, never to push a sword
into the throat of the woman who bore me. Because he was never going to
get back into the light either way, and I, poor fool, would be filling
myself up with evils like these.

So now, sister, uncover your head, and come away from the tears, however
badly off we are. And when you see me lose heart, it is your job to calm
down the terrible broken part of my mind and soothe it, and when you are
the one groaning, I have to be there and talk you kindly out of it. That
is the kind of help that is good between people who belong to each
other. Now, my poor sister, go into the house. Lie down and give your
sleepless eyes some sleep. Reach for some food, and put some water on
your skin. Because if you leave me before the end, or catch some
sickness from sitting up with me, we are finished. You are the only
helper I have. Everyone else has left me, as you can see.

Electra.
No. I am not going. I will choose to die with you, and to live with you,
because it comes to the same thing. If you die, what am I going to do, a
woman? How will I survive on my own, with no brother, no father, no
friends? But if you think it is right, I must do as you say. But lie
down on the bed, and do not take the thing that terrifies you and scares
you up off your bed so seriously. Stay on your bed where it is made up.
Because even if you are not sick, and only think you are sick, that
alone wears a person out and leaves them helpless.

(Electra goes into the house.)

Chorus.
	Aiai.
	Running goddesses, wings on your backs,
	goddesses of frenzy,
	whose company is a company that has nothing to do with Dionysus,
	a company of tears and groans,
	black-skinned Kindly Ones, who go bounding
	across the long stretch of the sky, exacting
	the price of blood, exacting the price of murder,
	I beg you, I beg you,
	let the son of Agamemnon
	forget the madness that hunts him,
	the raving and the wandering. Ah, the pain,
	what pains you reached for, poor man, and are lost,
	when you took the word from the tripod that Apollo cried out
	on the floor of his house at Delphi,
	where they say the hollows are, at the navel of the earth.

Chorus.
	Io, Zeus.
	What pity is this, what struggle
	for blood is this coming on,
	driving you, poor man, on whom some avenging spirit
	is heaping tears on tears,
	carrying into the house your mother's blood,
	and the blood is what sends you raving?
	Great good fortune does not stay put among human beings.
	I mourn for it, I mourn for it.
	Some god shook it like the sail of a fast boat
	and sank it under terrible labours,
	as the sea sinks a boat
	under its wild and deadly waves.
	Because what other house should I honour above this one,
	the house that came from a marriage with a god,
	the house of Tantalus?

Chorus.
	And look, here comes the king,
	Menelaus the king, and anyone can see
	from all that luxury
	that he is of the blood of Tantalus.
	You who launched an army in a thousand ships
	against the land of Asia,
	welcome. Good fortune is your companion.
	The gods have given you everything you prayed for.

(Menelaus comes in, with his attendants.)

Menelaus.
My house. In one way I am glad to look at you, coming home from Troy. In
another I look at you and groan. Because I have never seen another
hearth so wretchedly wrapped round with evils. I knew what had happened
to Agamemnon, and how he died, at his wife's hands, as I was bringing my
prow in to Cape Malea. The sailors' prophet told me it out of the waves,
Glaucus, the seer of Nereus, a god who does not lie. He stood up in
plain view and said this to me: "Menelaus, your brother is lying dead.
His wife gave him his last bath, and he fell into it." He filled me and
my sailors with a great many tears. And when I touched land at Nauplia,
with my wife already setting out for this house, I was expecting to
throw my arms round Orestes, Agamemnon's son, and round his mother, and
find them doing well, when I heard from one of the fishermen about the
unholy murder of Tyndareus's daughter. So now tell me where he is, young
women, Agamemnon's son, the one who had the nerve to do these terrible
things. He was a baby in Clytemnestra's arms when I left the house on my
way to Troy, so I would not know him if I saw him.

Orestes.
Here he is, Menelaus. I am Orestes, the one you are asking about. I will
tell you my troubles myself, and willingly. But first, before anything
else, I touch your knees as a suppliant, and I hang my prayers from a
mouth that has no olive branch to hold. Save me. You have come, in
person, at the crisis of my troubles.

Menelaus.
Gods, what am I looking at? Which of the dead is this I see?

Orestes.
Well said. I am not alive, for all my troubles. But I see the light.

Menelaus.
How wild you look, poor man, with your hair all dry and filthy.

Orestes.
It is not my looks that torture me. It is what I did.

Menelaus.
That is a terrible stare, out of eyes with no tears in them.

Orestes.
The body is gone. The name has not left me yet.

Menelaus.
I never expected you to look like this. What a ruin.

Orestes.
Here I am, the man who killed his poor mother.

Menelaus.
I have heard. Spare me. Evils should be spoken of as little as possible.

Orestes.
I will spare you. But the god who has me is rich in evils, and spends
them on me.

Menelaus.
What is wrong with you? What is this sickness that is destroying you?

Orestes.
Understanding. I understand what I have done, and it was terrible.

Menelaus.
What do you mean? Being clear is clever. Being unclear is not.

Orestes.
Grief, mostly. That is what is destroying me.

Menelaus.
Grief is a terrible goddess. But she can be cured, all the same.

Orestes.
And fits of madness. My mother's blood, taking its revenge.

Menelaus.
When did the madness begin? What day was it?

Orestes.
The day I was heaping up the earth on my poor mother's grave.

Menelaus.
In the house, or sitting up by the pyre?

Orestes.
At night. Waiting to gather up her bones.

Menelaus.
Was anyone else there, to hold you up?

Orestes.
Pylades. He did the blood with me. My mother's murder.

Menelaus.
So it is visions that make you sick. What kind?

Orestes.
I thought I saw three girls, as dark as night.

Menelaus.
I know the ones you mean. I would rather not name them.

Orestes.
No. They are holy. You were well brought up, to turn away from saying
it.

Menelaus.
And these are what drive you mad, over the blood of your own family?

Orestes.
Oh, the hunting. The way they hunt me, and drive me. Poor fool that I
am.

Menelaus.
It is not strange for people who have done terrible things to suffer
terrible things.

Orestes.
But there is somewhere I can shift this disaster.

Menelaus.
Do not say death. That is not clever.

Orestes.
Apollo. He ordered me to carry out my mother's murder.

Menelaus.
Then he knew less than he should about what is right and what is just.

Orestes.
We are slaves to the gods. Whatever the gods are.

Menelaus.
And then Apollo does nothing to defend you in your troubles?

Orestes.
He is taking his time. That is what the divine is like, by nature.

Menelaus.
How long is it since your mother stopped breathing?

Orestes.
This is the sixth day. The pyre at her grave is still warm.

Menelaus.
How fast the goddesses came after you for your mother's blood.

Orestes.
I am not clever. But I was born a true friend to the people I owe.

Menelaus.
And your father? Does it do you any good that you avenged him?

Orestes.
Not yet. And I call what is still to come the same thing as nothing.

Menelaus.
And the city? How do you stand with it, after doing this?

Orestes.
They hate us so much that nobody will speak to us.

Menelaus.
You have not even washed the blood off your hands, as the custom is?

Orestes.
Wherever I go, the door is shut in my face.

Menelaus.
Which of the citizens are pushing to have you driven out of the country?

Orestes.
Oeax. He has a grudge from Troy, and he lays it on my father.

Menelaus.
I understand. He is making you pay for his brother Palamedes, who was
killed there.

Orestes.
Which I had nothing to do with. But there are three things destroying
me.

Menelaus.
Who else? Some of Aegisthus's friends, I suppose?

Orestes.
Those are the ones. They are treating me with outrage, and it is them
the city listens to now.

Menelaus.
And will the city let you hold Agamemnon's sceptre?

Orestes.
How would it, when they will not even let us go on living?

Menelaus.
What are they doing? Is there anything you can tell me plainly?
