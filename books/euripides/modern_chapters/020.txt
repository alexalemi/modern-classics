Electra
(Part 1 of 3)

(Before a farmer's cottage in the hill country on the border of Argos.
It is still dark. The Farmer comes out of the house.)

Farmer.
Old plain of Argos, streams of Inachus. This is where King Agamemnon set
out from, when he raised an army and sailed a thousand ships to make war
on the land of Troy. He killed Priam, who ruled the land of Ilium, and
took the famous city that Dardanus founded, and came home here to Argos,
and hung the high temples with more plunder from the barbarians than any
man before him. Over there he did well. At home, he died. His wife
Clytemnestra laid the trap, and Aegisthus, the son of Thyestes, did it
with his own hand. So he is gone, and the old sceptre of Tantalus went
with him, and Aegisthus is king of this country, with Agamemnon's wife,
the daughter of Tyndareus, for his own.

As for the children he left in the house when he sailed for Troy, a boy,
Orestes, and a girl, the young shoot Electra: Orestes was about to die
at Aegisthus's hands, but his father's old tutor stole him away and gave
him to Strophius to bring up in the land of the Phocians. Electra stayed
on in her father's house, and when she came into the full bloom of her
youth, the first men of Greece were asking for her. Aegisthus was afraid
she would bear a son to one of those lords, an avenger for Agamemnon, so
he kept her in the house and would not match her with any bridegroom.
And when even that turned out to be full of fear, because she might bear
children to some nobleman in secret, he made up his mind to kill her.
Her mother stopped him. Cruel as she is, her mother saved her from
Aegisthus's hand. For killing her husband she had an excuse. For killing
her children, she was afraid, she would be hated.

So out of all this, here is what Aegisthus worked out. Agamemnon's son
had got away, an exile out of the country; Aegisthus put up gold for any
man who kills him. And Electra he gave to me, to have as my wife. My
people were Mycenaeans, and on that score nobody can fault me. The
family was bright enough in blood, but poor in money, and that is where
good birth goes to die. He gave her to a weak man so that his fear would
be weak too. Because if a man of standing had got her, he would have
woken the murder of Agamemnon out of its sleep, and justice would have
come for Aegisthus then.

And this man here has never shamed her in bed. Aphrodite is my witness:
she is still a virgin. I would be ashamed to take a rich man's daughter
and outrage her, when I was not born her equal. And I grieve for poor
Orestes, my brother-in-law in name, if he ever comes home to Argos and
sees what a miserable marriage his sister has made. Whoever calls me a
fool for having a young girl in my house and not touching her can know
this: he is measuring good sense with a crooked ruler, and he is the
fool himself.

(Electra comes out of the house with a water jar on her head.)

Electra.
Black night, nurse of the golden stars. Under you I go to the river for
water with this jar balanced on my head, not because I have come down to
such need, but to show the gods what Aegisthus's outrage looks like, and
I send my cries up into the wide sky, to my father. Because that
murderous daughter of Tyndareus, my mother, has thrown me out of the
house to please her husband. She has other children now, by Aegisthus,
and Orestes and I are the leftovers of the family —

Farmer.
Why do you do this, you poor thing? Why work yourself like this for my
sake, taking all this labour on, when you were brought up soft? And why
don't you stop when I tell you to?

Electra.
To me you are as good as a god, the friend you have been to me. You did
not take advantage of me when I was down. When things go badly, it is a
great piece of luck for a mortal to find a healer for it, and that is
what I have found in you. So even if nobody asks me to, I ought to do
all I have strength for to lighten your load and carry the work
alongside you, so you bear it more easily. You have enough to do out of
doors. The house is my business, to keep it straight. When a working man
comes in from outside, it is a pleasure to find things right indoors.

Farmer.
Go, then, if that is what you want. The springs are not far from this
house anyway. And I will go out at first light and put the oxen in the
fields and sow the ground. No idler, however much he keeps the gods'
names in his mouth, ever got a living together without work.

(The Farmer goes off to his fields, and Electra towards the river.
Orestes and Pylades come in along the road.)

Orestes.
Pylades, you are the first man I would name as loyal to me, a friend and
a guest. You alone of my friends have stood by Orestes here, treated the
way I have been treated by Aegisthus, who killed my father, he and my
mother, that destroyer. I have come from the god's holy shrine to Argive
soil, and nobody knows it, to pay my father's murderers back murder for
murder. Last night I went to my father's grave and gave him my tears,
and cut him the first lock of my hair, and killed a sheep over the mound
and let its blood run, unseen by the tyrants who hold this land. And I
am not setting foot inside the walls. I have come to the edge of the
country with two aims at once. If some watcher recognises me, I can step
over the border into another country. And I am looking for my sister.
They say she is living here, yoked in marriage, no virgin any more. I
want to meet her and get her to work with me on the killing, and find
out for certain how things stand inside the walls.

So now, since dawn is lifting her white eye, let us step off this path.
Some ploughman or some slave woman will show herself, and we can ask
whether my sister lives round here. And look. I can see one now, some
servant, carrying a jar of water on her cropped head. Let us sit down,
and put our question to this slave woman, Pylades, in case she gives us
a word about the business we came to this country for.

(They step aside and sit down out of sight. Electra comes back with
the jar of water on her head, singing.)

Electra.
	Move your feet. It is time. Faster.
	Go on, go on, and weep as you go.
	Io moi moi.
	I was born Agamemnon's child,
	and Clytemnestra bore me,
	the hateful daughter of Tyndareus,
	and the people of the town call me
	Electra, the wretched one.
	Pheu, pheu, for the hard work
	and the life I loathe.
	Father, you lie in Hades,
	butchered by your wife
	and Aegisthus. Agamemnon.

Electra.
	Come, wake the same cry again,
	raise up the pleasure of long weeping.

Electra.
	Move your feet. It is time. Faster.
	Go on, go on, and weep as you go.
	Io moi moi.
	What city, what house, my poor brother,
	are you wandering in,
	when you left your sister behind
	in her father's rooms, to be pitied,
	with the worst of griefs on her?
	Come, and set me free from this work,
	miserable as I am.
	Zeus, Zeus, come and stand by my father
	against that most hated blood. Bring the wanderer
	to shore in Argos.

Electra.
	Take this jar off my head and set it down,
	so I can cry the night-cries for my father
	before the dawn,
	a wail, a song of Hades,
	Hades, father, for you
	under the earth. I say the laments
	I live in every day, all day,
	tearing my dear throat with my nails
	and beating my hand on my cropped head,
	for your death.

Electra.
	Ai ai. Tear your face.
	Like a swan crying clear
	by the river's streams,
	calling to its dearest father,
	dead in the treacherous meshes of a net,
	so I cry for you, my wretched
	father,

Electra.
	washed in your last bath,
	in the most pitiful bed of death.
	Io moi, io moi,
	for the bitter cut of the axe on you,
	father, and the bitter plan
	that met you on the road home from Troy.
	Not with garlands did your wife receive you,
	not with crowns,
	but with a two-edged sword. She made you
	the sorry victim of Aegisthus
	and took a traitor into her bed.

(The Chorus, country women of Argos, come in.)

Chorus.
	Daughter of Agamemnon,
	Electra, I have come
	to your country yard.
	A man has come, a man has come from Mycenae,
	a milk-drinker who walks the mountains,
	and he brings news: the Argives are announcing
	a sacrifice for the day after tomorrow,
	and all the unmarried girls
	are to walk in procession to Hera's temple.

Electra.
	It is not for finery, friends,
	that my heart goes flying out of me,
	not for necklaces of gold,
	and I will not stand in the dance
	with the young women of Argos
	and beat out the turns with my foot.
	I pass my nights in tears,
	and tears, poor thing that I am,
	are all my care by day.
	Look at my hair, filthy,
	and these rags I wear for a dress,
	and ask if they are fit for a princess,
	Agamemnon's daughter,
	or for Troy, which fell to my father once
	and has not forgotten him.

Chorus.
	The goddess is great. Come.
	Borrow from me a thick-woven robe to wear,
	and gold, do me the favour,
	to set off your finery.
	Do you think your tears will beat your enemies,
	if you give the gods no honour?
	Not with groans, child. Honour the gods
	with prayers, and you will see good days.

Electra.
	No god hears the cry
	of a woman the gods hate,
	or remembers my father, slaughtered so long ago.
	Oh, for the dead man,
	and for the living one, the wanderer,
	who is somewhere in another country,
	poor man, drifting
	to a hired man's hearth,
	and he the son of that famous father.
	And I live in a labourer's house,
	my life melting away,
	driven out of my father's home
	onto these mountain crags.
	And my mother, in a bed stained with blood,
	lives married to another man.

Chorus.
Your mother's sister Helen is to blame for a great deal of evil, both to
the Greeks and to your own house.

Electra.
Oh. Women, I have broken off my lament. There are strangers by the
house. They were lying low at the altar and now they are getting up out
of their hiding place. Run. You down the path, and I into the house. Let
us get away from these criminals while we can.

(Orestes and Pylades come out of hiding. Orestes has his sword drawn.)

Orestes.
Stay where you are, poor woman. You have nothing to fear from my hand.

Electra.
Apollo, help me. I am on my knees to you. Do not kill me.

Orestes.
There are others I would rather kill. People I hate more than you.

Electra.
Get away. Do not touch what you have no right to touch.

Orestes.
There is nobody I have a better right to touch.

Electra.
Then why are you lying in ambush at my house with a sword?

Orestes.
Stay and listen, and you will soon say the same.

Electra.
I am standing still. I am yours in any case. You are the stronger.

Orestes.
I have come with a message for you from your brother.

Electra.
Dearest of men. Is he alive, or dead?

Orestes.
Alive. I want to give you the good news first.

Electra.
Good luck to you, for the sweetest words I ever heard. That is your fee.

Orestes.
I will take that as a gift for the two of us to share.

Electra.
Where on earth is he, poor man, in his poor exile?

Orestes.
He wears himself out drifting, with no one city's laws to live by.

Electra.
Surely he is not short of his daily bread?

Orestes.
He has that. But a man in exile is a weak man.

Electra.
And what message have you come with from him?

Orestes.
Whether you are alive, and if you are, how you are faring.

Electra.
Well, you can see for yourself, first, how my body has dried up.

Orestes.
Yes, wasted with grief. It makes me groan to look at it.

Electra.
And my head, my hair, cropped with a razor like a Scythian's.

Orestes.
Your brother gnaws at you, I suppose, and your dead father.

Electra.
Oh. What is dearer to me than those two?

Orestes.
Pheu, pheu. And what do you think you are to your brother?

Electra.
He loves me from far away. He is not here to be a friend to me.

Orestes.
And why do you live out here, so far from the town?

Electra.
I am married, stranger. A marriage that is death.

Orestes.
I groan for your brother. To which of the Mycenaeans?

Electra.
Not to the one my father ever hoped to give me to.

Orestes.
Tell me, so I can hear it and tell your brother.

Electra.
This is his house. I live in it, far from everything.

Orestes.
A ditch-digger or a cowherd would be the right man for a house like
this.

Electra.
A poor man, but well born, and he treats me with reverence.

Orestes.
What kind of reverence does this husband of yours have?

Electra.
He has never brought himself to touch my bed.

Orestes.
Some holy vow he keeps? Or does he think you beneath him?

Electra.
He did not think he had the right to outrage my parents.

Orestes.
How could he get a marriage like that and not be delighted?

Electra.
He does not think the man who gave me away had the right to, stranger.

Orestes.
I see. He is afraid Orestes will make him pay for it one day.

Electra.
He is afraid of that, yes. But he is also a man who keeps himself in
hand.

Orestes.
Pheu. That is a noble man you are describing. He should be treated well.

Electra.
Yes, if the one who is away ever comes home.

Orestes.
And your mother, who bore you, put up with this?

Electra.
Women love their husbands, stranger, not their children.

Orestes.
And why did Aegisthus do you this outrage?

Electra.
He wanted me to bear weak children, so he gave me to a man like this.

Orestes.
So you would never bear sons to punish him, of course.

Electra.
That was his plan. And may he pay me for it.

Orestes.
Does your mother's husband know you are a virgin?

Electra.
He does not. We keep quiet and steal that from him.

Orestes.
These women listening to us, are they friends of yours?

Electra.
Friends enough to keep your words and mine well hidden.

Orestes.
Then what could Orestes do about it, if he came to Argos?

Electra.
You ask that? Shame on you for asking. Is now not the moment?

Orestes.
And if he did come, how could he kill his father's murderers?

Electra.
By having the nerve to do to them what his enemies had the nerve to do
to my father.

Orestes.
And would you have the nerve to kill your mother, with him?

Electra.
Yes. With the same axe my father died by.

Orestes.
Am I to tell him that? And that you will not waver?

Electra.
Let me die, once I have spilled my mother's blood.

Orestes.
Pheu. If only Orestes were standing here to hear that.

Electra.
But stranger, I would not know him if I saw him.

Orestes.
No wonder. You were both children when you were torn apart.

Electra.
There is only one of my friends who would recognise him.

Orestes.
The man they say stole him away from being murdered?

Electra.
Yes. My father's tutor. An old man now, from the old days.

Orestes.
And your father, when he died, did he get a grave?

Electra.
He got what he got. Thrown out of the house.

Orestes.
Oh. What a thing to say. It bites a man, hearing of suffering, even when
the trouble is somebody else's. Tell me, so that I can know it and carry
the story to your brother: not pleasant to hear, but he has to hear it.
There is no pity in a stupid man. Pity lives in the wise. And even for
the wise it costs something, to have a mind that is too wise.

Chorus.
And we have the same longing in our hearts as this man. We live far from
the town, and we do not know the evils in the city. Now we would like to
learn them too.

Electra.
I will tell it, if I must, and one must, to a friend: the heavy luck of
my father and me. And since you are the one setting the story going, I
beg you, stranger, tell Orestes what has happened to me, and to him.
First, the clothes I bed down in, like an animal in a stall. The dirt I
am weighed down with. The kind of roof I live under, after a king's
palace. I weave my own clothes at the loom, working myself to the bone,
or else I go naked and do without. I carry the water from the river
myself. I have no festivals, no holy rites, no share in the dances. I
turn away from the married women, because I am a virgin, and I turn away
from Castor, who courted me before he went up among the gods, because I
was his kin.

And my mother sits on her throne among the plunder of Troy, and beside
her seat stand slave women from Asia, my father's prizes of war, their
Trojan robes pinned with golden brooches. And my father's blood is still
there on the walls of the house, gone black and rotten, and the man who
killed him climbs into the same chariot my father rode and drives out in
it, and takes the sceptre my father commanded the Greeks with in his
bloodstained hands and swaggers with it. Agamemnon's grave lies
dishonoured. It has never once had a drink poured on it, or a sprig of
myrtle. His pyre is bare of every offering. And my mother's husband, the
famous one, when he is soaked with drink, jumps on the grave, so they
say, and throws stones at my father's stone monument, and has the nerve
to say this about us: "Where is your boy Orestes? Is he here doing a
fine job of guarding your tomb?" That is how Orestes is outraged, and he
is not here to hear it.

So, stranger, I beg you, take this report back. Many voices are sending
it, and I am only their interpreter: these hands, this tongue, this
worn-out heart, my shaven head, and the man who fathered him. Because it
is a disgrace if his father wiped out the Trojans and he cannot kill one
man, one against one, when he is young and comes from a better father.

Chorus.
And look, we can see him, your husband, I mean, on his way home, his
work done for the day.

(The Farmer comes back from the fields.)

Farmer.
What is this? Who are these strangers I see at my door? What brings them
to a farmyard gate like mine? Do they want something from me? It does
not look right, you know, for a woman to stand about with young men.

Electra.
Dearest, do not get suspicious of me. You will hear the true story.
These strangers have come to me as messengers, with news of Orestes. And
strangers, forgive him for what he said.

Farmer.
What are they saying? The man is alive? He sees the light of day?

Electra.
So they say, and I do not think they are lying to me.

Farmer.
And does he remember anything of your father's wrongs, and yours?

Electra.
That is something to hope for. A man in exile is weak.

Farmer.
What message from Orestes have they come to deliver?

Electra.
He sent them to see for themselves how badly I am treated.

Farmer.
Then they see some of it, and I suppose you are telling them the rest.

Electra.
They know. There is nothing they lack of it.

Farmer.
Then should the doors not have been opened to them long ago? Go inside,
both of you. In return for your good news you will find whatever
hospitality my house has in it. Servants, take their baggage inside. And
do not argue with me: you are friends, come from a friend. I may be
poor, but I will not show you the manners of a low-born man.

Orestes.
By the gods, is this the man who keeps up the pretence of your marriage,
the one who does not want to bring shame on Orestes?

Electra.
This is the man they call my husband. Mine, in my misery.

Orestes.
Pheu. There is no exact way to measure a man's worth. Human nature is a
muddle. I have seen the son of a noble father turn out to be nothing,
and good children come out of bad parents. I have seen famine in a rich
man's mind and a great judgement in a poor man's body. So how does
anyone sort them out and judge them rightly? By wealth? Then he is using
a rotten judge. By having nothing? But poverty has a sickness in it: it
teaches a man to be bad because he is in need. Shall I go to war for my
test, then? Who, with a spear pointed at him, could stand witness to
which man is brave? Best to let all that go, and leave it to chance.

Because this man here is nobody great among the Argives, and he is not
puffed up with the reputation of his family. He is one of the many, and
he has turned out to be the best. Will you not stop being fools, you
people who wander about stuffed with empty notions, and judge men by the
company they keep, and judge the noble by their habits? Men like that
run cities well, and households too. The lumps of flesh with no mind in
them are statues in the marketplace. A strong arm does not stand up to
the spear any better than a weak one: that is a matter of a man's
nature, and of his courage.

But since the son of Agamemnon deserves it, the one who is here and not
here, the one we have come for, let us accept the shelter of this house.
Inside, slaves. We must go in. I would rather have a poor man for my
host who wants to have me than a rich one. So I am grateful for the
welcome this man gives me into his house, though I would have wished
your brother could have led me, prosperous himself, into a prosperous
house. And perhaps he will come. Apollo's oracles are sure. It is the
prophecy of men I say goodbye to.

(Orestes and Pylades go into the house with their servants.)

Chorus.
Now more than ever before, Electra, our hearts are warm with joy.
Perhaps our luck, after its hard slow progress, is coming to a good
standing place.

Electra.
You reckless man. You know how poor your house is. Why did you take in
these strangers, men so far above you?

Farmer.
Why not? If they are as well born as they look, will they not be just as
content with small things as with great?

Electra.
Well, since you have made this mistake, small man that you are, go to my
father's dear old tutor. He is out by the river Tanaus, where it cuts
the border between the land of Argos and the land of Sparta. He was
thrown out of the city, and he keeps sheep there. Tell him these men
have arrived at my house, and to come, and bring something for the
strangers' meal. He will be glad, and he will pray to the gods when he
hears that the child he saved once is alive. From my father's house,
from my mother, we would get nothing. And it would be bitter news we
brought, if that hard woman ever found out that Orestes is still alive.

Farmer.
All right. If that is what you want, I will take the message to the old
man. But you get into the house as fast as you can and get things ready
inside. A woman who wants to can always find plenty to add to a meal.
And there is still enough in the house to fill these men with food for
one day at least. It is at times like this, when I fall short of what I
meant to do, that I see what great power money has: to give to
strangers, and to spend on saving a body when it falls ill. But when it
comes to the day's food, money does not go far. Every man who has eaten
his fill, rich or poor, carries the same load.

(The Farmer goes off towards the river Tanaus. Electra goes into the
house.)

Chorus.
	Famous ships, that went to Troy once
	with oars past counting,
	leading the dance with the Nereids,
	where the dolphin that loves the pipe
	leapt at your dark-blue prows
	and rolled,
	carrying Thetis's son,
	Achilles, light on his leaping feet,
	with Agamemnon to the banks
	of Simois, the river of Troy.

Chorus.
	And the Nereids left the headlands of Euboea
	and carried the shield, the toil of Hephaestus's anvils,
	the golden armour,
	up Pelion, up the glens
	at the foot of holy Ossa,
	the Nymphs' lookouts, searching for the place
	where Chiron, the horseman who was a father to him,
	was raising him, the light of Greece,
	Thetis's son from the sea,
	swift-footed for the sons of Atreus.

Chorus.
	I heard it from a man who came from Troy
	and landed in the harbour at Nauplia:
	that on the round of your famous shield,
	son of Thetis,
	these were the signs worked in it,
	terrors for the Phrygians.
	Running round the rim, on its base,
	Perseus the throat-cutter, over the sea
	on his winged sandals,
	holding up the Gorgon's head,
	with Zeus's messenger Hermes beside him,
	Maia's son, the god of the countryside.

Chorus.
	And in the middle of the shield blazed
	the bright circle of the sun
	on his winged horses,
	and the dancing stars of the sky,
	the Pleiades and the Hyades,
	to turn Hector's eyes and put him to flight.
	On the helmet of beaten gold
	were Sphinxes carrying in their claws
	the prey their singing had won,
	and on the breastplate, round his ribs,
	a lioness breathing fire ran hard,
	claws out,
	at the sight of Pegasus, the colt of Pirene's spring.

Chorus.
	And on the murderous sword
	horses pranced on four feet,
	and black dust rose about their backs.
	The lord of warriors like these, spearmen like these,
	you killed, daughter of Tyndareus,
	with your adultery, evil-minded woman.
	For that the gods of heaven
	will send you one day to your death.
	Yes, and I will see it yet, I will see
	the blood run under your throat,
	spilled by the iron.
