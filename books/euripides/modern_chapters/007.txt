The Children of Heracles
(Part 2 of 2)

Chorus.
	No man, I say, is blessed without the gods,
	and no man is crushed by fate without them either.
	And no house walks forever
	on the road of good fortune. One fate after another
	comes hunting it down.
	One man it brings down from a height to a small place;
	the wanderer it makes prosperous.
	What is fated, no one is allowed to escape.
	No one will push it away by being clever,
	and the man who keeps on trying will only
	have the labour of it, forever, for nothing.

Chorus.
	So do not throw yourself down. Carry what the gods send,
	and do not let grief wear your mind past bearing.
	The poor girl has a death that is honoured,
	dying for her brothers and for this land,
	and the name she has
	among people will not be one without glory.
	Courage walks through hardship.
	What she has done is worthy of her father,
	worthy of her noble birth. And if you honour
	the deaths of the good, I share that with you.

(A servant of Hyllus comes in and finds the old man on the ground with
his cloak over his face.)

Servant.
Children, good day to you. Where is old Iolaus? And your father's
mother, why has she left her place at this altar?

Iolaus.
I am here. Such as my being here amounts to.

Servant.
Why are you lying there? Why is your face down like that?

Iolaus.
A grief came, one of the family's own, and it has hold of me.

Servant.
Then lift yourself up. Get your head up.

Iolaus.
I am old. There is no strength left in me at all.

Servant.
Well, I have come bringing you a great piece of joy.

Iolaus.
Who are you? Where did I meet you? I do not remember.

Servant.
I am one of Hyllus's serfs. You are looking right at me and do not
know me?

Iolaus.
Dearest man. Have you come to save the two of us from harm, then?

Servant.
I have. And more than that: things are going well for you now.

Iolaus.
Mother of a noble son, Alcmene, I am calling you: come out, and hear
the best words you could hear from this man. You have been in labour a
long time over the people who have come, wasting your heart away over
whether they would ever get here.

(Alcmene comes out of the temple.)

Alcmene.
What is this shouting? The whole house is full of it. Iolaus, is there
another herald here from Argos, using force on you? My strength is
feeble, but there is one thing you should know, stranger: you will
never take these children while I am alive. If you do, let nobody
count me his mother any more. And if you lay a hand on them, you will
have a fight on your hands with two old people, and it will not do you
any credit.

Iolaus.
Courage, old woman, do not be frightened. It is not a herald with
hostile words from Argos.

Alcmene.
Then why did you raise a shout? A shout is a messenger of fear.

Iolaus.
For you. So that you would come out here in front of the temple.

Alcmene.
I do not understand any of this. Who is this man?

Iolaus.
He brings news that your son's son has come.

Alcmene.
Then good day to you too, for that news. But if he has set foot in
this country, where is he now? Why is he not here? What has happened
to keep him from appearing here with you and gladdening my heart?

Servant.
He is settling the army he brought with him, and setting it in order.

Alcmene.
Then that is a matter that no longer concerns us.

Iolaus.
It concerns us. And it is my job to find out about it.

Servant.
What is it you want to know about what has been done, then?

Iolaus.
How large a force of allies has he brought with him?

Servant.
A lot of men. I cannot give you a number beyond that.

Iolaus.
The Athenian commanders know this, I suppose?

Servant.
They know. And their left wing is already in position.

Iolaus.
So the army is already armed and ready for action?

Servant.
Yes. And the animals for the sacrifice have already been brought up, a
little way off from the ranks.

Iolaus.
And how far away is the Argive army?

Servant.
Close enough for their general to be seen plainly.

Iolaus.
Doing what? Marshalling the enemy's lines?

Servant.
That was our guess. We could not hear. But I am going. I would not
want my masters going into battle without me, whatever my part in it
is worth.

Iolaus.
And I am coming with you. We think alike, it seems: stand by your
friends and help them.

Servant.
Of all people, you are the last who should be talking nonsense.

Iolaus.
And the last who should stay out of a hard fight when my friends are
in it.

Servant.
Looks do not wound anyone. Only a hand that does something.

Iolaus.
What? Could I not put a spear through a shield, even now?

Servant.
You could. But you would fall first yourself.

Iolaus.
Not one of the enemy will be able to look me in the face.

Servant.
My friend, the strength you once had is not there any more.

Iolaus.
Well, at any rate they will have one more man to fight.

Servant.
It is a small weight you are adding to your friends' side of the
scale.

Iolaus.
Do not hold me back. I am ready to act.

Servant.
Act, you cannot. Want to, perhaps.

Iolaus.
Say anything else you like to me, but not that I am staying here.

Servant.
Then how will you show yourself to the soldiers with no armour?

Iolaus.
There are captured arms inside this temple. I will use those, and if I
live I will give them back. If I die, the god will not ask for them.
Go inside, take a full set of armour down off the pegs and bring it
out to me as fast as you can. This is a shameful way to keep house:
some men fighting, and others hanging back out of cowardice.

(The Servant goes into the temple.)

Chorus.
	Time has not flattened your spirit yet.
	That is still young. It is your body that is gone.
	Why work for nothing at what will hurt you
	and do our city very little good?
	At your age you should know when you are beaten
	and let go of what cannot be done. There is no way
	you will get your youth back again.

Alcmene.
	What are you doing? Have you lost your mind,
	to leave me here alone with my children?

Iolaus.
Fighting is men's work. Your job is to look after them.

Alcmene.
And what if you are killed? How am I to be kept safe?

Iolaus.
Your son's sons, the ones who are left, will look after you.

Alcmene.
And if something happens to them, which the gods forbid?

Iolaus.
These strangers will not give you up. Do not be afraid.

Alcmene.
That is all the courage I have. I have nothing else.

Iolaus.
Zeus cares about your troubles too, I know it.

Alcmene.
Ah. Zeus will hear no bad word from me. But whether he has dealt
righteously with me, he knows himself.

(The Servant comes back out with a full set of armour.)

Servant.
Here is your full suit of armour, you can see it. Now hurry and get
your body inside it. The fight is close, and Ares hates a man who
dawdles more than anything. But if you are afraid of the weight, walk
there without it for now, and put this gear on when you are in the
ranks. I will carry it in the meantime.

Iolaus.
Good idea. Carry the armour and keep it ready to my hand. Put the
spear in my grip, and take my left arm to steady me, and guide my
feet.

Servant.
So I am to lead a soldier about like a child?

Iolaus.
For the omen's sake, we must get there without a stumble.

Servant.
If only you could do as much as you want to.

Iolaus.
Hurry. If I miss the battle it will be terrible for me.

Servant.
You are the one who is slow, not me. You just think you are doing
something.

Iolaus.
Can you not see how my legs are hurrying?

Servant.
I see you thinking you are hurrying. That is not the same thing.

Iolaus.
You will not say that when you see me there.

Servant.
Doing what? Well, I hope it is winning, whatever it is.

Iolaus.
Hitting one of the enemy straight through his shield.

Servant.
If we ever get there. That is what worries me.

Iolaus.
Ah. Arm of mine. If only you could be the ally to me now that I
remember you being in your youth, when you sacked Sparta at the side
of Heracles. What a rout I would make of Eurystheus. He is too much of
a coward to stand and face a spear. There is this, too, that is wrong
with prosperity: it gets a man a reputation for courage. We think that
someone who is doing well must know how to do everything.

(Iolaus goes off toward the battle, leaning on the Servant.)

Chorus.
	Earth, and moon that shines all night,
	and brightest rays of the god
	that bring the light to mortals,
	carry the news to me.
	Shout it to the sky,
	and beside the throne of our ruler,
	and in the house of grey-eyed Athena.
	For my fatherland,
	for my home, because I took in
	people who came begging, I am about to cut
	through the danger with grey iron.

Chorus.
	It is a fearful thing that a city
	as prosperous as Mycenae, so much praised
	for the strength of its spear,
	should hide its anger against my land.
	But it would be a wrong, my city,
	to hand over strangers who came begging
	because Argos ordered it.
	Zeus fights on my side. I am not afraid.
	Zeus is grateful to me, and rightly.
	Never, because of me, will the gods
	be shown weaker than mortals.

Chorus.
	Lady, this ground is yours,
	and the city is yours, and you are its mother,
	its mistress and its guard,
	so turn him away down some other road,
	the man who is wrongly leading an army
	of shaking spears here from Argos. After what I have done,
	I do not deserve to be thrown out of my house.

Chorus.
	For you are honoured, always, with many sacrifices,
	and the waning day of the month is never forgotten,
	nor the songs of the young men, nor the music of the dancers.
	On the windy hill
	the cries of joy ring out all night
	to the beat of girls' feet.

(The Servant comes back.)

Servant.
Mistress, I bring you a report. It is very short for you to hear, and
for me, standing here, it is the finest news there is. We have beaten
the enemy, and trophies are being set up with the full armour of your
enemies hung on them.

Alcmene.
Dearest man. This day has brought you your freedom, for that news. But
there is one trouble you have not set me free from yet. I am afraid:
are the people I want alive, alive?

Servant.
They are alive, and covered in glory throughout the army.

Alcmene.
And the old man, Iolaus. Is he alive too?

Servant.
Very much so. And the gods have done the finest thing of all for him.

Alcmene.
What is it? Did he do something brave in the fight?

Servant.
He has turned from an old man back into a young one.

Alcmene.
That is an astonishing thing to say. But first I want you to tell me
about the battle, and how our friends won it.

Servant.
One speech of mine will make all of it plain to you.

When we had stretched out our lines of armed men face to face with
theirs, Hyllus stepped down from his four-horse chariot and stood in
the middle, in the ground between the two armies, and he said:
"General, you who have come from Argos, why do we not leave this land
alone? You will do Mycenae no harm by losing it one man. Fight me, one
man against one. If you kill me, take the children of Heracles and go.
If you die, leave me my father's honours and my father's house." The
army roared its approval: that was well said, as a way out of the
trouble and as a piece of courage too. But he, with no shame in front
of the people who had heard the challenge, and none at his own
cowardice, and he the general, did not have the nerve to come anywhere
near a good spear. He was a coward, plain and simple. And that is the
sort of man who had come to make slaves of Heracles's children.

So Hyllus went back to his place in the line. And the prophets, when
they saw there would be no settlement by single combat, shield against
shield, did not wait: they cut the victims' throats and let the blood
that promised good luck run straight from a human throat. Then some
were climbing into chariots, and others were tucking flank against
flank under the shelter of their shields. And the king of the
Athenians gave his army the order a well-born man ought to give:
"Fellow citizens, the land that feeds you and the land that bore you
needs every man's help today." And the other one, for his part, was
begging his allies not to let Argos and Mycenae be shamed. Then the
signal went up, shrill, on the Etruscan trumpet, and they came
together in battle, and how loud a crash of shields do you think there
was, how much groaning and screaming all at once? At first the surge
of the Argive spears broke us. Then they gave way in their turn. In
the second round it was foot planted against foot, man standing
against man, and the battle held hard, and many were falling. And the
shouts went up: "Men of Athens!" and "You who sow the fields of
Argos! Will you not keep disgrace off your city?" And by doing
everything, and not without hard work, we finally turned the Argive
spear to flight.

At that point the old man, Iolaus, saw Hyllus charging out, and he
held out his right hand and begged him to take him up into his
chariot. He took the reins in his hands and went after Eurystheus's
horses. From here on I can tell you what I heard from others; up to
here I saw it myself. As he was crossing the sacred hill of Pallene,
which belongs to Athena, he saw Eurystheus's chariot, and he prayed to
Hebe and to Zeus to let him be young for one single day and make his
enemies pay. Now here is a marvel for you to hear. Two stars settled
on the yoke of the horses and hid the chariot in a dark cloud. Your
son and Hebe, the cleverer people say. And out of that murky darkness
he showed the shape of a young man, with young arms. And the famous
Iolaus caught Eurystheus's four-horse chariot at the Scironian rocks,
and bound his hands in chains, and is coming here bringing the general
who was so prosperous before, as the finest first-fruits of the
battle. And with what has happened to him now he announces plainly,
for every mortal to learn: do not envy the man who seems to be doing
well until you have seen him dead. Fortune lasts a day.

Chorus.
Zeus, who turns the battle, now I can look on a day that is free of
terrible fear.

Alcmene.
Zeus, you took your time to look at my troubles, but I thank you all
the same for what has been done. And my son: I did not believe before
that he lived among the gods. Now I know it for certain. Children,
now, now at last you will be free of your troubles, free of
Eurystheus, who is going to die a bad death. You will see your
father's city, and walk onto the land that is yours by lot, and
sacrifice to your fathers' gods, which you were shut out from, living
the miserable life of wanderers in foreign places. But tell me: what
clever plan was Iolaus keeping to himself when he spared Eurystheus
and did not kill him? Tell me. Because the way we see it, that is not
clever, to take your enemies and not make them pay.

Servant.
He was thinking of you: so that you could see him with your own eyes,
beaten and under your hand. Not that Eurystheus came willingly. He
yoked him by force, whether he liked it or not, because he did not
want to come alive into your sight and pay the penalty. But goodbye,
old lady, and remember what you said to me first, when I was beginning
my story: that you would set me free. In matters like this, the mouth
of the well-born should tell no lies.

(The Servant goes off to rejoin the army.)

Chorus.
	Dancing is sweet to me, when the clear grace
	of the pipe is at the feast,
	and may Aphrodite be there, with her grace.
	And it is a sweet thing, too, to see
	friends doing well
	who never expected to before.
	For Fate, who brings things to pass,
	and the long years, the children of Time,
	give birth to a great deal.

Chorus.
	You have a road that is just, my city,
	and you must never let go of it:
	honouring the gods. Anyone who says you do not
	is driving close to madness,
	with proof like this on show.
	For the god gives clear notice:
	he strips the unjust
	of their pride, always.

Chorus.
	Your son is in heaven, old woman.
	He has gone there. He escapes the story
	that he went down to the house of Hades,
	his body eaten by the terrible flame of the fire.
	He has Hebe's lovely bed
	in the golden court.
	Hymen, god of weddings, you have honoured
	two children of Zeus.

Chorus.
	Many things fall in with many others.
	They say Athena stood by the father
	of these children, and now the goddess's city
	and the goddess's people have saved them too,
	and checked the outrage of a man
	whose temper put violence before justice.
	May my heart never be like that,
	may my spirit never want more than it has.

(Eurystheus is brought in, bound and guarded. The Servant is with him.)

Servant.
Mistress, you can see for yourself, but I will say it anyway: we have
come bringing Eurystheus here to you, a sight you never hoped for, and
a turn of fortune he never expected either. He never imagined he would
fall into your hands when he marched out of Mycenae with his
long-suffering shield-men, thinking himself too big for justice, to
sack the city of Athena. But a god set things the other way round and
turned his fortune upside down. Hyllus and brave Iolaus were setting
up a statue of Zeus who turns the battle, for a glorious victory, and
they sent me to bring this man to you, wanting to gladden your heart.
It is the sweetest thing there is: to see an enemy who was prosperous
fall into misery.

Alcmene.
You. Hateful thing. Have you come? Has Justice caught you at last?
First, turn your head this way to me, and bear to look your enemies in
the face. You are the one who is ruled now, not the ruler. Are you the
man, I want to know, who thought it right to heap so many outrages on
my son, wherever he is now, you villain? What outrage did you not have
the nerve to put on him? You sent him down alive into Hades. You sent
him out with orders to kill hydras and lions. The other evils you
devised I pass over; it would make a long story. And it was not enough
for you to have dared that much: no, you drove me and the children out
of the whole of Greece, when we were sitting as suppliants of the
gods, some of us old, some still babies. But here you found men, and a
free city, who were not afraid of you. You have to die, and die badly,
and even then you come out ahead every way: you ought not to die just
once, after all the suffering you have caused.

Chorus.
It is not possible for you to kill this man.

Servant.
Then we took him prisoner for nothing?

Alcmene.
What law is there that stops this man from dying?

Chorus.
The rulers of this land do not approve of it.

Alcmene.
What does that mean? They do not think it right to kill enemies?

Chorus.
Not one they have taken alive in battle.

Alcmene.
And Hyllus put up with a decision like that?

Chorus.
He should have disobeyed this land, I suppose?

Alcmene.
This man should not be alive, or looking at the light any more.

Chorus.
The first wrong done to him was that he did not die then.

Alcmene.
So is it no longer the right moment for him to pay the penalty?

Chorus.
There is no one who would kill him.

Alcmene.
I would. And I count myself as someone, too.

Chorus.
You will get a great deal of blame if you do it.

Alcmene.
I love this city; there is no arguing with that. But this man, now
that he has come into my hands, there is no mortal alive who will take
him out of them. Let anyone who likes call me the bold woman, the one
who thinks bigger than a woman should. This piece of work will be done
by me.

Chorus.
Your quarrel with this man is a terrible one, lady, and it is
forgivable. I understand that well.

Eurystheus.
Woman, understand this clearly: I am not going to flatter you, or say
anything else to save my life that would earn a man the name of
coward. I did not take up this quarrel by my own choice. I knew I was
your cousin, and a blood relation of your son Heracles. But whether I
wanted it or not, and she is a god, Hera made me sick with this
sickness. And once I had taken up hostility against him, and knew that
this was the contest I was going to have to fight, I became an expert
in troubles. I sat up with the night and gave birth to plan after
plan, for pushing my enemies aside and killing them, so that I would
not have to live with fear for the rest of my life. Because I knew
your son was not a mere number: he was a real man. Enemy or not, he
will hear good things from me, because he was a good man.

And once he was gone, hated as I was by these children, and knowing
the feud I had inherited from my father, was I not forced to move
every stone, killing, banishing, plotting? As long as I did that, my
position was safe. And you: if you had taken over my fortunes, would
you not have hounded the hostile lion's cubs, that bad brood, with
every evil you could? Would you have kept yourself in hand and let
them live in Argos? You will not convince anyone of that. So now,
since they did not destroy me then, when I was ready to die, by the
laws of the Greeks my death is a pollution on the man who kills me.
The city let me go, and kept itself in hand when it did: it honours
the god far more than its hatred of me. You have spoken; you have had
your answer. From here on you will have to call me two things: the
avenging ghost, and the man of honour. That is how it stands with me.
I do not want to die. But if I leave my life behind, I will not mind
at all.

Chorus.
I want to give you a small piece of advice, Alcmene. Let this man
go. It is what the city wants.

Alcmene.
And what if he dies, and we obey the city too?

Chorus.
That would be best of all. But how could that happen?

Alcmene.
I will teach you, easily. I will kill him, and then I will give the
body to whichever of his friends come for it. As far as his body goes,
I will not disobey this land. But he will pay me the penalty by dying.

Eurystheus.
Kill me. I am not begging you. But this city, since it let me go and
was too decent to kill me, I will give a present: an old oracle of
Apollo, which will do it more good in time than you would think. When
I am dead, you will bury me where it is fated, in front of the temple
of the divine virgin at Pallene. And I will lie under the ground there
forever, a foreigner settled among you, kind to you and a protection
to the city, and the deadliest enemy of these children's descendants
when they come here with a great army, betraying this kindness you
have done them. That is the kind of strangers you have stood up for.
Then why did I come here, if I knew all this, instead of respecting
the god's oracle? Because I thought Hera was far stronger than any
prophecy, and would not give me up. But do not let them pour drink
offerings on my grave, or let blood drip on the place where I lie. I
will give them a bad homecoming in return for all this. And you will
have a double profit from me: by dying I will help you and I will hurt
them.

Alcmene.
Then what are you waiting for, when you have heard this, if you have
to make the city safe, and our children after us? Kill the man. He is
showing you the safest road himself. He is an enemy, and dead he is
useful. Take him away, slaves. Then kill him and throw him to the
dogs. Do not hope that you will live to throw me out of my father's
land again.

(Eurystheus is led away.)

Half-Chorus.
	I think the same. Go, attendants.
	Nothing that comes from us
	will leave a stain on our kings.
