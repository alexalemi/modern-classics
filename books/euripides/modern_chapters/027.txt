The Trojan Women
(Part 2 of 3)

Chorus.
	Sing me a song of Troy, Muse,
	a new kind of song, a song for a funeral, with tears in it.
	Now I will raise my voice for Troy
	and tell how the four-footed thing on wheels, the Argives' horse,
	destroyed me, a wretched woman, taken by the spear:
	when the Achaeans left that horse at the gates,
	rumbling up to the sky, its harness gold,
	and armed men inside it.
	And the people shouted, standing up on the rock of Troy,
	"Go on, your troubles are over,
	take this holy carving up the hill
	to the virgin daughter of Zeus, who watches over Troy."
	Which of the young women did not go?
	Which old man stayed in his house?
	And singing for joy
	they took in the trick that was their ruin.

Chorus.
	Every soul in Phrygia hurried to the gates
	to give the goddess her present:
	the Argive ambush, hidden in the polished mountain pine,
	the ruin of Dardanus's land,
	a gift for the virgin goddess whose horses never die.
	They put twisted ropes of flax around it
	and hauled it, like the dark hull of a ship,
	up to the stone seat and the stone floor
	of Pallas's temple, the floor that was going to drink
	our country's blood, and set it there.
	And on top of the work and the joy
	the darkness of night came down,
	and the Libyan pipe was sounding,
	and Phrygian songs, and the girls
	lifted their feet high and beat the ground and sang
	a happy song, and in the houses
	the bright blaze of the fire
	threw its dark glow
	and gave everyone over to sleep.

Chorus.
	And I was dancing then, round the house,
	singing for the virgin of the mountains,
	the daughter of Zeus, when a shout,
	a shout of blood, went through the town
	and took hold of the citadel of Pergamum.
	Babies threw their frightened arms
	round their mothers' dresses.
	War came out of its hiding place:
	the work of the virgin Pallas.
	Phrygians were slaughtered round the altars,
	and in the beds, where men lay with their heads cut off,
	the emptiness gave Greece a crown
	of young women, to breed her sons,
	and gave the fatherland of the Phrygians grief.

(A cart is drawn in, piled with Hector's bronze armour and the spoils of
Troy. Andromache sits on it, holding the child Astyanax.)

Chorus.
	Hecuba, do you see Andromache there,
	carried along in a foreigners' cart?
	And at her heaving breast, held close,
	dear Astyanax, Hector's son.
	Where are they taking you on the back of that cart,
	poor woman, sitting beside Hector's
	bronze armour and the spoils of the Phrygians,
	hunted down at spear-point,
	the ones Achilles's son will hang up
	in the temples of Phthia, brought home from Troy?

Andromache.
	The Achaeans, my masters, are taking me away.

Hecuba.
	Oh.

Andromache.
	Why are you groaning? That is my song of grief, not yours.

Hecuba.
	Aiai.

Andromache.
	For these pains.

Hecuba.
	Oh, Zeus.

Andromache.
	And for this disaster.

Hecuba.
	Children.

Andromache.
	We were your children, once.

Hecuba.
	Gone, the happiness. Gone, Troy.

Andromache.
	Poor Troy.

Hecuba.
	And the noble blood of my children.

Andromache.
	Pheu, pheu.

Hecuba.
	Pheu, yes, for my

Andromache.
	sufferings.

Hecuba.
	Pitiful, the luck

Andromache.
	of the city,

Hecuba.
	which is going up in smoke.

Andromache.
	Come to me, husband, come to me.

Hecuba.
	You are calling for my son, and he is in Hades,
	poor woman.

Andromache.
	Come and shield your wife.

Hecuba.
	And you, scourge of the Achaeans,
	master of my children and their father,
	old Priam, first of my house,
	lay me down to sleep in Hades.

Andromache.
	This is a great longing,

Hecuba.
	Poor woman. These are the pains we suffer,

Andromache.
	for a city that is gone,

Hecuba.
	and pain lies on top of pain.

Andromache.
	because the gods turned against us, from the day that son of yours, Paris,
	cheated the death he was meant for, the one who, for a hateful bed,
	destroyed the towers of Troy. And the bloody bodies of the dead
	lie stretched out beside the goddess Pallas
	for the vultures to carry off, and Troy has been brought
	under the yoke of slavery.

Hecuba.
	Oh, my country. Oh, poor

Andromache.
	I am crying for you, left behind,

Hecuba.
	now you see the pitiful end,

Andromache.
	and for my house, where I gave birth.

Hecuba.
	Children, your mother has no city, and now she is losing you.
	What a wailing there is, what grief,
	tears falling out of tears
	in our houses. But the dead
	forget their pain. The dead do not cry.

Chorus.
How sweet tears are to people who have come to grief, and the wailing
of the dirge, and the music that has sorrow in it.

Andromache.
Mother of the man who once killed more Argives with his spear than
anyone alive, mother of Hector, do you see this?

Hecuba.
I see the way the gods work. What was nothing they build up into a
tower, and what looked like something they destroy.

Andromache.
We are being led away as plunder, my child and I. Nobility has come
down to slavery. That is how great the change is.

Hecuba.
Necessity is a terrible thing. Just now Cassandra was torn from me too,
dragged off by force.

Andromache.
	Pheu, pheu.
	It seems your daughter has found a second Ajax, another man
	to drag her off by force. But you are sick in other ways as well.

Hecuba.
Yes, and there is no measure to my sicknesses, and no number. Evil comes
racing against evil.

Andromache.
Your daughter is dead. Polyxena. They cut her throat at Achilles's tomb,
a gift to a dead man who cannot feel it.

Hecuba.
Oh, I am wretched. So that is what Talthybius meant, the riddle he told
me a while ago, so unclearly, and so clearly.

Andromache.
I saw her myself. I got down from this cart and covered her body with
my dress and beat my breast for her.

Hecuba.
Aiai, child, for that unholy slaughter. Aiai, again and again, for the
evil way you died.

Andromache.
She died the way she died. But even so, dying like that, she got a
happier fate than I have, alive.

Hecuba.
Being dead and looking at the light are not the same thing, my child.
One is nothing. The other has hope in it.

Andromache.
Mother, you who gave birth to her, listen to the best argument I have.
I want to put a little pleasure into your heart. I say that never to
have been born is the same thing as being dead, and that dying is
better than living in misery. The dead feel no pain. They know nothing
of their troubles. But the man who was happy once and has fallen into
unhappiness, his mind wanders off, back to the good life he had
before. Polyxena is dead, and it is as if she had never seen the light:
she knows nothing of what has happened to her. But I aimed at a good
name, and I hit it, and I won more of it than most women ever do, and
that is exactly how I missed my luck.

Everything that has ever been found out to help a woman keep herself in
hand, I worked at, in Hector's house. First: whether or not there is
any real blame in it for a woman, the mere fact of not staying at home
brings a bad name with it, so I put the wish to go out of my mind and
stayed in the house. And I did not let the clever talk of women in
through the door either. I had my own mind as a teacher, a good one,
from home, and that was enough for me. I gave my husband a quiet tongue
and a calm eye. I knew where I should win against my husband, and where
I should let him have the victory.

And the report of all this went out to the Achaean army and destroyed
me. Because when I was taken, Achilles's son wanted me for his wife. I
am going to be a slave in the house of the men who murdered mine. And
if I push away my dear Hector's face and open my heart to the husband I
have now, I will look like a traitor to the dead man. And if I hate
this one, my own masters will hate me. And yet they say a single night
undoes a woman's dislike of a man's bed. I spit on the woman who throws
away the husband she had for a new bed and loves another man. Not even
a horse, if it is separated from the one it grew up with, will pull the
yoke easily. And a horse is a dumb beast: it has no reason, and nature
has given it less than us.

But you, Hector, my dear, you were a husband who was enough for me,
great in understanding, in birth, in wealth, in courage. You took me
untouched from my father's house, and you were the first to yoke my
virgin bed. And now you are dead, and I am being shipped off to Greece,
a prisoner of war, to the yoke of slavery. So tell me: the death of
Polyxena, that you are groaning for, is it not less to bear than what I
have? I do not even have the one thing that is left to every human
being alive, hope. I do not fool myself into thinking that I will ever
do well again. And even the thinking would have been sweet.

Chorus.
You have come to the same point of disaster we have. Mourning your own
troubles, you are teaching us where we stand in ours.

Hecuba.
I have never set foot on the hull of a ship myself, but I have seen
them in pictures and heard about them, and so I know. When the storm is
moderate enough to bear, sailors have the will to work their way out of
trouble: one at the tiller, one up on the sails, one keeping the bilge
water out of the ship. But when the sea is thrown into a great
confusion and comes over them, they give in to luck and let themselves
go to the running of the waves. And that is how I am. I have so many
troubles that I am dumb. I have let go. I leave my mouth alone. The
wretched wave the gods have sent is too strong for me.

But, my dear child, let Hector's fate alone. Your tears will not save
him. Honour the master you have now. Offer the man the sweet bait of
your ways. If you do that, you will make everyone who loves you glad,
all of us together, and you may bring up this child, my son's son, to
be the greatest help Troy could have, so that one day, perhaps,
children born from you might settle Troy again, and there might still
be a city. But one thing leads to another. Who is this Achaean servant
I see coming now, bringing news of new decisions?

(Talthybius comes in, with soldiers.)

Talthybius.
Wife of Hector, who was once the best of the Phrygians, do not hate me.
I am not bringing this news because I want to. The Greeks, and the sons
of Pelops with them, have a joint decision to announce.

Andromache.
What is it? The way you are starting sounds like the beginning of
something bad.

Talthybius.
It has been decided that this boy, how am I going to say this?

Andromache.
Not that he is to have a different master from mine?

Talthybius.
No Achaean will ever be this boy's master.

Andromache.
Then they are leaving him here, the last remnant of the Phrygians?

Talthybius.
I do not know how to tell you the bad news gently.

Andromache.
I thank you for your decency. Unless what you have to tell is good.

Talthybius.
They are going to kill your son. There. Now you know the worst of it.

Andromache.
Oh. That is a worse evil than my marriage.

Talthybius.
Odysseus won the argument, speaking in front of the whole Greek army.

Andromache.
Aiai, again. What we suffer is beyond all measure.

Talthybius.
He said that the son of so great a father should not be brought up.

Andromache.
May he win arguments like that about his own children.

Talthybius.
And that he must be thrown from the towers of Troy. Let it happen that
way, and you will show yourself the wiser for it. Do not cling to him.
Grieve for your troubles like the well-born woman you are, and do not
imagine you are strong when you have no strength at all. You have no
defence anywhere. You must look at it clearly: your city is destroyed
and your husband is dead, you are in our power, and we are quite able
to fight one woman. So I do not want you to want a fight, or to do
anything shameful or hateful, or to throw curses at the Achaeans. If
you say anything that angers the army, this child will not be buried,
and there will be no pity for him. But if you keep quiet and take what
has happened to you as it should be taken, you will not leave his body
unburied, and you will find the Achaeans kinder to you.

Andromache.
My darling. My child, honoured beyond all children. You are going to
die at the hands of your enemies and leave your miserable mother
behind. Your father's nobility is killing you, the thing that saves
other people. Your father's greatness came at the wrong time for you.
My unlucky bed, my unlucky marriage, that brought me once into Hector's
house. I did not come there to bear a son for the Greeks to slaughter,
but a king of Asia with its rich harvests.

Child, are you crying? Do you understand what is happening to you? Why
are you gripping me with your hands and holding on to my dress, like a
chick huddling under my wings? Hector is not going to come up out of
the ground with his famous spear in his fist and save you. No kinsman
of your father's is coming, no Phrygian army. You will fall from the
height, a hideous drop, headfirst onto your neck, with nobody to pity
you, and the breath will be broken out of you. Little body in my arms,
the dearest thing your mother has. The sweet smell of your skin. You
smell so sweet. So it was for nothing that this breast fed you in your
baby clothes. For nothing I laboured and wore myself to rags with work.
Now, never again, hold your mother. Fall on the woman who bore you,
wind your arms round my back, and put your mouth to mine.

Greeks. You have invented barbarian cruelties. Why are you killing this
child? He has done nothing. And you, seed of Tyndareus, you were never
Zeus's daughter. I say you had many fathers: the Avenging Spirit first,
and then Envy, and Murder, and Death, and every evil the earth breeds.
I will never boast that Zeus fathered you, you who have been death to so
many barbarians and Greeks. Damn you. Out of those beautiful eyes of
yours you have brought a shameful ruin on the famous plains of Phrygia.
Go on, then. Take him. Carry him off. Throw him, if throwing him is
what you have decided. Feast on his flesh. The gods are destroying us,
and I cannot keep death away from my own child. Cover up my wretched
body and throw it in the ship. I am going to a fine wedding, now that I
have lost my child.

Chorus.
Poor Troy. You have lost thousands of men, for one woman and her
hateful bed.

Talthybius.
	Come, boy. Let go of your poor mother,
	let go of her arms around you, and walk
	to the very top of your father's towers,
	where the vote has decided
	you are to let your breath go.
	Take him.
	This kind of message should be carried
	by a man with no pity in him,
	a man who is a better friend to shamelessness
	than my mind is.

(The soldiers take the child from her. Talthybius goes out with
Andromache and Astyanax.)

Hecuba.
	Child. Son of my poor son.
	They are robbing your mother and me
	of your life, and it is not just.
	What can I do? What can I do for you,
	unlucky boy? All we can give you
	is this: blows on the head, fists on the breast.
	That much is ours to give.
	Oh, for my city. Oh, for you.
	What do we not have?
	What is missing now, to make our ruin
	total, and complete, and fast?

Chorus.
	Telamon, king of Salamis, where the bees are kept,
	you made your home on an island the waves wash round,
	leaning against the holy hills
	where Athena first showed the branch of the grey olive,
	a crown from heaven and a glory for shining Athens.
	You came, you came, sharing the glory
	with the archer, the son of Alcmene, Heracles,
	to sack Troy, Troy, our city,
	long ago, when you came from Greece.

Chorus.
	That was when he led the first flower of Greece,
	furious over the horses Laomedon had promised him and never paid,
	and at the broad-flowing Simois he rested the oar
	that had carried him across the sea, and tied the ship's cables to the stern,
	and took out of the ship the sure aim of his hand, his bow,
	to be the death of Laomedon.
	And the stonework Apollo had squared to the line
	he brought down with the red breath of fire
	and sacked the land of Troy.
	Twice now, with two strokes, around the walls
	of Dardanus's country, the bloody spear has brought them down.

Chorus.
	So it was for nothing, then, that you walk so delicately
	among the golden wine-jugs, Ganymede, Laomedon's boy,
	filling the cup of Zeus, the loveliest of services.
	The city that bore you is burning in the fire,
	and the shores of the sea
	cry out, the way a bird
	shrieks over her young:
	here for husbands, here for children,
	here for old mothers.
	Your dewy baths, the running tracks
	where you exercised, are gone,
	and you, beside the throne of Zeus,
	keep your young face calm and lovely
	with its graces, while the spear of Greece
	has destroyed the land of Priam.

Chorus.
	Love, Love, who came once to the halls of Dardanus,
	when the gods in heaven had you on their minds,
	how high you built Troy then,
	binding her to the gods by marriage.
	I will not say it as a reproach to Zeus any more.
	But the light of the white-winged Dawn,
	which is dear to mortals, looked on this land
	and saw it die, saw the towers destroyed,
	she who had a husband from this land
	in her bedroom, Tithonus, the father of her children,
	the one the golden chariot of the stars, four horses,
	snatched up and carried off,
	a great hope for his fatherland. But the love the gods had
	for Troy is gone.

(Menelaus comes in, with his men.)

Menelaus.
Bright light of the sun, this day, when I get my hands on my wife.
Helen. I am Menelaus, the man who has done all the work, and this is
the Achaean army. I came to Troy not, as people think, for a woman, but
after a man: the man who cheated his host and robbed my house of my
wife. Well, he has paid the price, with the gods' help, he and his
country, fallen to the Greek spear. And I have come to take the
wretched woman away. I do not enjoy saying the word "wife" of a woman
who was once mine. She is in these prisoners' tents, counted in with
the other Trojan women. The men who fought for her with the spear have
given her to me to kill, or, if I choose not to kill her, to take back
to the land of Argos. And I have decided to let Helen's death in Troy
go, and to carry her by ship to Greece, and there hand her over to be
killed, as payment to all the men whose friends died at Troy. Right. In
you go, men, into the tent. Bring her out here by that hair of hers,
foul with murder. And when the winds blow fair, we will send her to
Greece.

Hecuba.
You who carry the earth, and have your seat on the earth, whoever you
are, hard to guess at and hard to know, Zeus, whether you are the
necessity of nature or the mind of mortal men, I pray to you. You go
along a soundless road, and you guide everything human toward justice.

Menelaus.
What is this? You have found a new way to pray to the gods.

Hecuba.
I praise you, Menelaus, if you will kill your wife. But do not look at
her. Run from the sight of her, or she will take you with longing. She
takes men's eyes. She takes cities. She burns down houses. That is the
kind of spell she has. I know her. So do you. So does everyone who has
suffered by her.

(Helen is brought out of the tent by Menelaus's men.)

Helen.
Menelaus, this is an opening that might well frighten me. Your servants
have laid hands on me and driven me out in front of these tents by
force. Still, I am fairly sure you hate me. But I want to ask, all the
same: what have the Greeks decided, and you, about my life?

Menelaus.
Your case did not need a precise ruling. The whole army, the army you
wronged, has given you to me to kill.
