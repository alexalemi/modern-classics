Hippolytus
(Part 2 of 3)

Chorus.
	Eros, Eros, you who drip desire
	down through the eyes, who bring sweet pleasure
	into the souls of the people you march against,
	never come to me with evil in your hands,
	never come out of measure.
	No shaft of fire, no shaft of the stars, hits harder
	than the arrow of Aphrodite
	that Eros, the child of Zeus,
	lets fly from his hands.

Chorus.
	It is all for nothing, all for nothing, that beside the Alpheus
	and at Apollo's Pythian shrine
	the land of Greece piles up the slaughtered oxen,
	while Eros, the tyrant of men,
	who holds the keys
	to Aphrodite's dearest bedrooms, we do not worship,
	though he lays waste, and drags mortals
	through every kind of disaster
	when he comes.

Chorus.
	The girl in Oechalia,
	a filly never yoked to a bed,
	no husband yet, no wedding:
	Cypris unhitched her from the house of Eurytus
	and drove her off like a running Naiad, like a Bacchant,
	with blood, with smoke,
	with murder for her wedding song,
	and gave her to Heracles, Alcmene's son.
	What a wretched wedding that was.

Chorus.
	Holy wall of Thebes,
	and mouth of the spring of Dirce, you could tell
	how Cypris comes creeping in.
	With the double-flamed thunderbolt
	she put Semele to bed, the mother of Dionysus,
	the god who was born twice, and the bed she made her
	was a bloody death.
	Terrible, the breath she blows on everything,
	and she flits from one to the next like a bee.

(Phaedra has gone to the door of the house and is listening. She turns
to the women.)

Phaedra.
Be quiet, women. It is all over for me.

Chorus.
What is it, Phaedra? What is so terrible inside your house?

Phaedra.
Wait. Let me hear what they are saying in there.

Chorus.
I am quiet. But this is a bad beginning.

Phaedra.
Io moi. Aiai. Oh, what I am suffering. What a wretched woman I am.

Chorus.
	What is that cry? What are you shouting?
	Tell me, lady, what word has rushed in
	on your mind and frightened you?

Phaedra.
I am destroyed. Stand here at the doors and listen to the noise that is
breaking over the house.

Chorus.
	You are at the door. It is for you to catch
	the words that the house sends out.
	Tell me, tell me, what evil has happened?

Phaedra.
It is Hippolytus, the son of the horse-loving Amazon. He is shouting.
He is calling my servant terrible names.

Chorus.
	I hear a cry, but nothing I can make out.
	Call out to me
	what came, what came through the door to you.

Phaedra.
Clearly enough. He is calling her a matchmaker for evil, a woman who
has betrayed her master's bed.

Chorus.
	Oh, this is evil. You are betrayed, my dear.
	What can I plan for you now?
	What was hidden is out in the light. You are destroyed by it,

Phaedra.
Aiai. E, e.

Chorus.
betrayed by the people who love you.

Phaedra.
She has destroyed me by telling my trouble. She meant it kindly. It was
no good way to cure this sickness.

Chorus.
What now, then? What will you do? There is no way out of this.

Phaedra.
I only know one thing. To die, as fast as I can. It is the only cure
for the pain I am in now.

(Hippolytus bursts out of the house. The Nurse comes after him,
clutching at him.)

Hippolytus.
Mother Earth. Open sky, and the sun. What words have I heard? Words
that should never be said.

Nurse.
Quiet, boy, before somebody hears you shouting.

Hippolytus.
I cannot hear things like that and keep quiet.

Nurse.
Please. By your right hand, your beautiful arm.

Hippolytus.
Take your hand off me. Do not touch my clothes.

Nurse.
By your knees, I beg you. Do not destroy me.

Hippolytus.
Why should I, if what you said was nothing evil, as you claim?

Nurse.
This story, boy, is not one for everybody to hear.

Hippolytus.
Good things sound even better said in front of a crowd.

Nurse.
Child, do not dishonour your oath. Never.

Hippolytus.
My tongue swore the oath. My mind swore nothing.

Nurse.
Boy, what are you going to do? Destroy the people who love you?

Hippolytus.
I spit on them. No one who does wrong is a friend of mine.

Nurse.
Forgive. People make mistakes. That is what people are, child.

Hippolytus.
Zeus, why did you ever settle women in the light of the sun, this
counterfeit coin of an evil, for men to live with? If you wanted to sow
the human race, you should not have had it come from women. Men should
have been able to put down gold or iron or a weight of bronze in your
temples and buy the seed of children, each man a child worth what he
paid, and then live in their houses free, with no females in them. As
it is, the first thing we do is fetch the evil home, and to do it we
drain the wealth out of the house.

Here is the proof that a woman is a great evil. The very father who
sired her and raised her adds a dowry and ships her off, to be rid of
the trouble. And the man who takes the ruinous plant into his house is
delighted, and hangs ornaments on his statue, a pretty face on a
worthless thing, and works himself to the bone dressing it in robes,
poor fool, draining his house of its wealth. And he is caught either
way. Either he has married into a good family, and he is so pleased
with his in-laws that he holds on to a wife who is bitter to him; or he
has a good wife and useless in-laws, and presses the bad luck down with
the good. The man who has it easiest is the one with a nobody in his
house, a useless woman set in her home by her own simplicity.

I hate a clever woman. I will not have one in my house who thinks more
than a woman should. Cypris breeds mischief more readily in the clever
ones. A woman who is helpless, with only a little judgement, is kept
out of folly by her own lack of wit.

Servants should never have been allowed near a wife. Women should be
housed with dumb beasts that bite, so they could not speak to anyone
and no one could answer them back. As it is, the bad women inside plot
their bad plans, and the servants carry them out of doors. And that is
how you came to me, you evil head, to bargain over my father's bed,
which no one may touch. I will wash it off with running water, pouring
it into my ears. How could I be evil, when just hearing about it makes
me feel unclean?

Understand this, woman: it is my piety that saves you. If you had not
caught me off my guard with oaths to the gods, nothing would have
stopped me telling all of this to my father. As it is, I am leaving the
house for as long as Theseus is out of the country, and I will keep my
mouth shut. When my father comes home, I will come back with him, and I
will watch how you look at him, you and your mistress. I have had a
taste of your nerve already. I will know it when I see it.

Damn you both. I will never get my fill of hating women, not even if
somebody says I never talk about anything else. Because they are always
evil too, one way or another. Either let somebody teach them to keep
themselves in hand, or let me go on trampling on them forever.

(Hippolytus goes out.)

Phaedra.
	Wretched, ill-fated,
	the lot of women.
	What skill do we have now, what words,
	when we have slipped, to untie the knot?
	I have got what I deserved. Io, earth and light.
	Where can I escape what has happened to me?
	How do I hide my pain, friends?
	What god would come to help me, what mortal
	would sit beside me, or lend a hand
	in wrong that I have done? This suffering of mine
	has come to the edge of my life, and there is no crossing it.
	Of all women I am the most unlucky.

Chorus.
Pheu, pheu. It is done. Your servant's schemes have gone wrong,
mistress, and things are bad.

Phaedra.
You worst of all women, you destroyer of the people who loved you, look
what you have done to me. May Zeus, my ancestor, blast you with his
fire and wipe you out, root and all. Did I not tell you, did I not see
your mind coming, to keep quiet about the very thing that is now my
shame? But you could not hold back. And so I will not die with a good
name after all. Now I need new plans. That man, with his mind whetted
sharp on anger, will tell his father your sins and call them mine. He
will tell old Pittheus what has happened. He will fill the whole
country with the ugliest talk. Damn you, and damn everyone who is so
eager to do good to people who do not want it, and does it badly.

Nurse.
Mistress, you can blame me for the harm I have done. The sting of it is
stronger than your judgement. But I have something to say to that, if
you will let me. I brought you up, and I want the best for you. I went
looking for a medicine for your sickness and I found what I was not
looking for. If it had worked, I would have been counted very clever.
We are as wise as our luck lets us be.

Phaedra.
Is that fair? Is it enough for me, that you wound me first and then
want to argue about it?

Nurse.
We are talking too long. I did not keep myself in hand, I admit it. But
there is a way to be saved even from this, child.

Phaedra.
Stop talking. Your advice before was bad, and what you tried was evil.
Get out of my way and go and look after yourself. I will put my own
affairs in order.

(The Nurse goes into the house.)

And you, noble daughters of Troezen, grant me this one thing I ask of
you. Cover what you have heard here in silence.

Chorus.
I swear by holy Artemis, the daughter of Zeus, that I will never bring
any of your troubles into the light.

Phaedra.
Well said. I have turned it over and over, and I have found one way out
of this disaster: to leave my children a life with a good name, and to
get some good for myself out of the way things have fallen. I will
never shame my Cretan house, and I will never come before Theseus's
face with this ugly business on me, to save one life.

Chorus.
What do you mean to do? Something there is no curing?

Phaedra.
To die. How, I will work out for myself.

Chorus.
Do not say such things out loud.

Phaedra.
And you, give me good advice. As for me, I will give Cypris, the
goddess who is destroying me, her pleasure today, when I am rid of my
life. I will be beaten by a bitter love. But in dying I will be an evil
to someone else as well, so that he learns not to stand tall over my
misfortunes. When he has his share of this sickness of mine, he will
learn to keep himself in hand.

(Phaedra goes into the house.)

Chorus.
	I wish I could hide under the sheer cliffs,
	I wish a god would make me a bird with wings
	and set me among the flying flocks.
	I would rise over the sea-wave
	of the Adriatic shore
	and the water of the Eridanus,
	where the poor girls, Phaethon's sisters,
	grieving for their brother,
	drip into their father's dark swell
	the amber-shining light
	of their tears.

Chorus.
	I wish I could reach the apple-sown shore
	of the Hesperides, the singers,
	where the lord of the sea
	grants sailors no passage over the dark water any more,
	where he touches the holy edge of the sky,
	the one Atlas holds up,
	and springs of ambrosia pour
	beside the beds in the halls of Zeus,
	where the holy earth that gives life
	makes the happiness
	of the gods grow.

Chorus.
	White-winged ship from Crete,
	that carried my queen
	through the salt sea, the beating waves,
	away from a happy house,
	to the joy of a wedding that was the worst of weddings.
	Bad omens flew with her from both shores,
	from the land of Crete to glorious Athens,
	and at the shore of Munychus, the harbour,
	they tied the twisted ends of the cables
	and stepped out
	onto the mainland.

Chorus.
	And that is why her mind was broken
	by the terrible sickness of Aphrodite,
	an unholy love.
	She is swamped by hard disaster,
	and from the beams of her bridal room
	she will fasten a hanging noose
	and fit it round her white neck,
	in shame before her hateful fate,
	choosing a good name instead,
	and ridding her heart
	of a love that hurts.

(The Nurse cries out from inside the house.)

Nurse.
Iou, iou. Everyone near the house, come and help. The mistress has
hanged herself, the wife of Theseus.

Chorus.
Pheu, pheu. It is done. The queen is no more. She is hanging in a
noose.

Nurse.
Hurry, will you? Will somebody bring a two-edged blade, so we can cut
this knot from her neck?

Chorus.
Friends, what do we do? Do we think we should go into the house and get
the queen down out of the noose she pulled tight?

Why should we? Are there not young servants in there? Doing too much is
not a safe way to live.

Nurse.
Lay her out straight, the poor dead woman. This is a bitter piece of
housekeeping for my master to come home to.

Chorus.
She is dead, the poor woman, from what I hear. They are already laying
her out like a corpse.

(Theseus comes in, a garland of leaves on his head, back from the
oracle.)

Theseus.
Women, do you know what that shouting is, in the house? A heavy sound
of servants has reached me. The house does not think it worth opening
its doors and greeting me kindly, a man coming home from the oracle.
Has something happened to old Pittheus? His life is far along already,
but even so, it would hurt me if he left this house.

Chorus.
This blow of yours is not aimed at the old, Theseus. It is the young
who have died and will give you pain.

Theseus.
Oh. Not my children. Nobody has robbed me of their lives?

Chorus.
They are alive. Their mother is dead. That is the worst of it for you.

Theseus.
What are you saying? My wife is dead? How did it happen?

Chorus.
She fastened a hanging noose round her neck.

Theseus.
Frozen with grief? Or did something happen to her?

Chorus.
That is all we know. I have only just come to the house myself,
Theseus, to mourn your troubles.

Theseus.
Aiai. Then why is my head crowned with these woven leaves? I went to
the oracle and I have come back to this. Undo the bolts on the doors,
servants, unfasten them, and let me see the bitter sight of the woman
who has destroyed me by dying.

(The doors open. Phaedra's body is carried out and laid down.)

Chorus.
	Io, io. Poor woman, your misery, your evil.
	What you suffered, what you did,
	was enough to bring this whole house down.
	Aiai, the daring of it. Killed by violence,
	an unholy end, wrestled down
	by your own poor hand.
	Who put your life out, poor woman?

Theseus.
	Oh, the pain. I have suffered, my city,
	the greatest of my evils. Fortune,
	how heavily you have set your foot on me and on my house,
	a stain from some avenging spirit, past all telling.
	It is the destruction of my life. No life is left.
	I see an ocean of evils, wretched man that I am,
	so wide I will never swim out of it again,
	never cross the wave of this disaster.
	What word for you, poor woman, what name for your fate,
	so heavy, could I find to say?
	You are gone from my hands like a bird,
	with one quick leap away from me into Hades.
	Aiai, aiai. Misery, misery, this.
	From somewhere far back I am reaping
	a fate the gods sent
	for the sins of somebody who came before me.

Chorus.
Lord, these evils have not come to you alone. Many other men have lost
a good wife.

Theseus.
	Under the earth, under the earth, in the dark,
	that is where I want to live, dead, in my misery,
	robbed of your company, the dearest I had.
	You have destroyed me more than you have died.
	Who can tell me? Where did the deadly stroke,
	poor woman, come from, that reached your heart?
	Will somebody say what happened, or is it for nothing
	that my royal house shelters a crowd of servants?
	Oh, oh, for you.
	The misery, what pain I have seen in this house,
	not to be borne, not to be spoken. I am destroyed.
	The house is empty, and the children are orphans.
	You left them, the children you bore, you left them, dearest
	and best of women, of all
	the light of the sun looks on,
	and the starry glow of the night.

Chorus.
	Io, poor man. Poor man. How much evil this house holds.
	My eyes are wet,
	flooded with tears at your fate.
	And the trouble after this, I have been shuddering at it a long time.

Theseus.
Wait. What is this? What is this letter, hanging from her dear hand?
Does it want to tell me something new? Did she write me instructions,
poor woman, about my marriage and the children, asking me for
something? Do not be afraid, poor girl. No woman will ever come into
Theseus's bed, or into his house. But look, the marks pressed by her
gold ring, and she is gone, are fawning on me, coaxing me to read.
Come, let me unwind the wrapping from the seals and see what this
letter wants to say to me.

Chorus.
	Pheu, pheu. One more new evil the god brings on,
	one after another. As for me, after what has happened,
	life is not worth living. Let it come, if it comes.
	I say the house of my king and queen is destroyed,
	pheu, pheu, it is no more.
	God, if there is any way, do not bring the house down.
	Hear me, I am begging you. I see something coming,
	like a prophet reading a bird, an omen of evil.

Theseus.
Oh. Here is another evil on top of the evil, one that cannot be borne,
cannot be spoken. Misery. I am a wretched man.

Chorus.
What is it? Tell us, if we may share in it.

Theseus.
	It cries out, the letter cries out things that cannot be forgotten.
	Where can I run from the weight of these evils?
	I am gone, destroyed, finished.
	Such a song, such a song I have seen
	speaking in the writing, poor wretch that I am.

Chorus.
	Aiai. The word you bring into the light begins the evil.

Theseus.
	I will not hold it any longer behind the gates of my mouth,
	this evil there is no crossing over,
	this evil that destroys. Io, my city.
	Hippolytus has dared to touch my bed
	by force. He has dishonoured the holy eye of Zeus.
	Father Poseidon, you promised me once
	three curses. Use one of them
	to finish my son. Let him not escape this day,
	if the curses you gave me were real.

Chorus.
Lord, by the gods, take that prayer back. You will find out later that
you were wrong. Listen to me.

Theseus.
It cannot be done. And more: I will drive him out of this country as
well, and one of two fates will strike him down. Either Poseidon will
honour my curses and send him dead to the house of Hades, or he will be
thrown out of this land to wander, and drain out a miserable life on
foreign soil.

Chorus.
And here he is himself, your son Hippolytus, just in time. Let go of
this evil anger, lord Theseus, and think what is best for your house.

(Hippolytus comes in with his companions.)

Hippolytus.
I heard you shouting, father, and I came as fast as I could. But what
you are groaning about, I do not know, and I would like to hear it from
you. Wait. What is this? Your wife, father. I see her dead. This is
past all wondering at. I left her only a moment ago; she was looking at
this light not long since. What happened to her? How did she die?
Father, I want to hear it from you. You say nothing. Silence does no
good when things are bad. The heart that wants to know everything is
caught being greedy even in trouble, I know. But it is not right,
father, to hide your misfortunes from the people who love you, and
more than love you.

Theseus.
Human beings, you make so many mistakes, and all for nothing. Why do
you teach ten thousand skills, and contrive everything, and discover
everything, and there is one thing you do not know and have never yet
hunted down: how to teach sense to people who have none?

Hippolytus.
That would be a formidable expert, the man who could force sense into
people who have none. But this is no time for hairsplitting, father. I
am afraid your tongue is running away with you, in your trouble.

Theseus.
Pheu. There should be some clear test for men to tell their friends by,
some way of seeing into their minds, which one is a true friend and
which one is not. Every man should have two voices, an honest one and
the other one, whatever it happened to be, so that the one thinking
dishonest thoughts could be caught out by the honest one, and we would
never be deceived.

Hippolytus.
Has one of your friends been whispering slander about me in your ear?
Am I sick with it, when I have done nothing? I am stunned. Your words
are stunning me. They wander, they are off the track of any sense.

Theseus.
Pheu, the human mind. How far will it go? What limit will there be to
daring and to sheer face? If it swells like this with each generation
of men, and every one that comes after outdoes the one before in
wickedness, the gods will have to bolt another earth onto this one to
hold the people born unjust and evil.

Look at this man. My own son, and he has shamed my bed, and the dead
woman convicts him, openly, as the worst of men. Show your face, then,
since you have already come into pollution. Show it here, to your
father. So you are the man who is too good for the rest of us, and
keeps company with gods? You are the one who keeps himself in hand and
is untouched by evil? I will not believe your boasting and think so
badly of the gods as to credit them with stupidity. Go on, then, boast
now. Peddle your meatless diet, take Orpheus for your master and rave
in his rites, honouring the smoke of all his books, now that you have
been caught. I warn everyone to run from men like that. They hunt you
down with holy words while they are plotting filth.

She is dead. Do you think that saves you? That is what convicts you
most of all, you worst of men. What oaths, what words could be
stronger than she is, so that you could escape the charge? You will
say she hated you, and that a bastard is born the natural enemy of the
legitimate children. Then you are calling her a bad trader with her
own life, if she destroyed what she loved most out of hatred of you.
Or will you say that folly is not in men, but is born into women? I
know young men. They are no steadier than women when Cypris shakes a
young mind. Being male helps them; it is on their side. But why am I
arguing against your words like this, when the corpse is here, the
clearest witness there could be? Get out of this land, into exile, as
fast as you can. Do not go to god-built Athens, and do not go to the
borders of any land my spear rules. If I let you beat me after what
you have done to me, Sinis, the robber I killed on the Isthmus, will
never bear witness that I killed him. He will say I was bragging, and
it was nothing. And the rocks of Sciron that live beside the sea will
never say I am a hard man to the wicked.
