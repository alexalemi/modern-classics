Iphigenia at Aulis
(Part 2 of 3)

(The carriage has drawn up in front of the tent. Clytemnestra is in it
with Iphigenia, the baby Orestes and their attendants. Agamemnon stands
at the door.)

Clytemnestra.
I take that as a good omen: your kindness, and the good words you have
for us. I have some hope that the wedding I am here to lead a bride to
is a fine one. Now, take the dowry I have brought for the girl out of
the carriages and carry it into the tent, and be careful with it. And
you, my child, get down from the carriage. Put your foot down gently;
it is a delicate foot, and not strong. You girls, take her in your arms
and help her down. And somebody give me a hand to lean on, so I can get
out of the carriage seat without making a spectacle of myself. Some of
you stand in front of the horses, by the yoke. A horse's eye is a
nervous thing, and there is no reasoning with it. And take this boy,
Agamemnon's son, Orestes. He is still only a baby. Child, are you
asleep? Did the carriage rock you off? Wake up. It is your sister's
wedding day, and a lucky one. You are a good man's son and you are
going to have a good man for a brother-in-law: the child of the
Nereid's daughter, a family that is very nearly gods. Sit here by my
feet, child. Iphigenia, come and stand close to your mother, and let
these foreign women see what a happy woman I am. And here he is. Go and
greet your father. You love him.

Iphigenia.
Mother, I am going to run on ahead of you. Do not be angry. I want to
throw my arms round my father's chest, and hold him.

Clytemnestra.
Agamemnon, my lord, the man I honour above everyone. We have come. You
gave your orders and we did not doubt them.

Iphigenia.
I want to run to you, father, and throw myself against your chest. It
has been so long. I have missed your face. Do not be angry.

Clytemnestra.
Go on, child, you should. Of all the children I have given him you were
always the one who loved her father most.

Iphigenia.
Father. It is so good to see you. It has been so long.

Agamemnon.
And your father is glad to see you. What you said goes for both of us.

Iphigenia.
Hello, father. You did a good thing, bringing me here to you.

Agamemnon.
I do not know how to say yes to that, child, and I do not know how to
say no.

Iphigenia.
Ah. You are glad to see me, and you do not look it. You look troubled.

Agamemnon.
A king and a general has a great deal on his mind.

Iphigenia.
Be with me now. Do not go off into your worries.

Agamemnon.
But I am with you now. All of me. I am nowhere else.

Iphigenia.
Then let go of that frown, and look at me the way you love me.

Agamemnon.
There. I am glad to see you. Exactly as glad as I am, child.

Iphigenia.
And then you have tears running down your face?

Agamemnon.
Yes. Because we are going to be apart a long time.

Iphigenia.
I do not know what you mean. I do not know, father, my dearest father.

Agamemnon.
The more sense you talk, the more you make me pity you.

Iphigenia.
Then I will talk nonsense, if that will cheer you up.

Agamemnon.
Papai. I have not got the strength to keep this in. Good girl.

Iphigenia.
Stay at home, father, with your children.

Agamemnon.
I want to. And I cannot have what I want, and it hurts.

Iphigenia.
Curse the spears, and Menelaus and his troubles.

Agamemnon.
They will destroy other people first. They have already destroyed me.

Iphigenia.
You have been away so long, tucked into this bay at Aulis.

Agamemnon.
And even now there is something holding me back from sending the army
out.

Iphigenia.
Where do they say the Phrygians live, father?

Agamemnon.
Somewhere Priam's son Paris should never have lived at all.

Iphigenia.
You are going a long way, father, and leaving me behind.

Agamemnon.
You have come to the same place as your father, daughter.

Iphigenia.
Pheu. If only it were proper for you to take me on the voyage with
you.

Agamemnon.
There is a voyage ahead of you too. And on it you will remember your
father.

Iphigenia.
Do I sail with my mother, or do I make the journey alone?

Agamemnon.
Alone. On your own, without your father and without your mother.

Iphigenia.
You are not settling me in some other house, are you, father?

Agamemnon.
Enough. Girls should not know about such things.

Iphigenia.
Hurry back from the Phrygians for me, father, once you have settled
everything there.

Agamemnon.
First I have a certain sacrifice to make here.

Iphigenia.
Yes. You have to see to what is holy, with the proper rites.

Agamemnon.
You will know all about it. You will be standing right beside the holy
water.

Iphigenia.
Then are we going to have dances round the altar, father?

Agamemnon.
I envy you. I envy you more than I do myself, for knowing nothing. Go
inside the tent now; it is not good for girls to be looked at. Give me a
kiss first, and your right hand, because you are going to live a long
time away from your father. Oh, this chest, and these cheeks, and this
golden hair. What a weight the Phrygians' city has turned out to be for
you, and Helen. I will stop talking. The moment I touch you the tears
come too fast for me. Go into the tent.

(Iphigenia goes into the tent.)

And you, daughter of Leda, forgive me if I was too full of pity just
now, when I am about to give my daughter to Achilles. Sending a daughter
off is a happy thing, and all the same it bites the parents, when a
father who has laboured so long over his children hands them over to
another house.

Clytemnestra.
I am not so stupid. Count on it, I will feel exactly the same, so I am
not going to lecture you, when I lead the girl out to the wedding songs.
But it is what people do, and time wears it down. Now, the man you have
promised our child to: I know his name. I want to hear about his family
and where he comes from.

Agamemnon.
There was a girl called Aegina, and her father was the river Asopus.

Clytemnestra.
And who married her? A mortal, or a god?

Agamemnon.
Zeus. Their son was Aeacus, the lord of the island of Oenone.

Clytemnestra.
And which of Aeacus's sons took over his house?

Agamemnon.
Peleus. And Peleus got the daughter of Nereus for a wife.

Clytemnestra.
With a god's blessing? Or did he take her by force, against the gods?

Agamemnon.
Zeus made the match, and her father, who had the right to, gave her
away.

Clytemnestra.
And where did he marry her? Under the swell of the sea?

Agamemnon.
Where Chiron lives, at the holy foot of Mount Pelion.

Clytemnestra.
Where they say the race of Centaurs has its home?

Agamemnon.
That is where the gods held the wedding feast for Peleus.

Clytemnestra.
And who brought Achilles up? Thetis, or his father?

Agamemnon.
Chiron. So he would not pick up the habits of bad men.

Clytemnestra.
Pheu. A wise man, the one who raised him. And wiser still the one who
gave him to be raised.

Agamemnon.
That is the kind of man your daughter is going to have for a husband.

Clytemnestra.
No fault to find there. And what city in Greece does he live in?

Agamemnon.
By the river Apidanus, inside the borders of Phthia.

Clytemnestra.
And that is where he will take your girl and mine?

Agamemnon.
That will be his business. She will belong to him.

Clytemnestra.
Well, good luck to them both. What day does he marry her?

Agamemnon.
When the circle of the moon comes round full, and lucky.

Clytemnestra.
And have you already killed the first offerings to the goddess, for the
girl's wedding?

Agamemnon.
I am about to. That is exactly the business we are standing in the
middle of.

Clytemnestra.
And then you will hold the wedding feast afterwards?

Agamemnon.
Yes. Once I have made the sacrifice I have to make to the gods.

Clytemnestra.
And where am I to lay out the feast for the women?

Agamemnon.
Here. Beside the fine sterns of the Argive ships.

Clytemnestra.
Fine. Since it has to be. Well, may it turn out for the best all the
same.

Agamemnon.
Do you know what you should do, wife? Do as I say.

Clytemnestra.
What is it? I am used to doing as you say.

Agamemnon.
I will stay here, where the bridegroom is, and I will—

Clytemnestra.
You will do what, without her mother? What is mine to do?

Agamemnon.
Give your child away, with the Greeks looking on.

Clytemnestra.
And where am I supposed to be while that happens?

Agamemnon.
Go to Argos and look after your other girls.

Clytemnestra.
And leave my child? Then who is going to hold up the torch?

Agamemnon.
I will provide whatever light a wedding needs.

Clytemnestra.
That is not the custom. And these are not small things, to be brushed
aside.

Agamemnon.
It is not right for you to be mixing with a mob of soldiers.

Clytemnestra.
It is right for me to give my own children away. I bore them.

Agamemnon.
And right for the girls at home not to be left on their own.

Clytemnestra.
They are well guarded, behind strong walls, in the women's rooms.

Agamemnon.
Do as I say.

Clytemnestra.
	No. By the queen of heaven, the goddess of Argos, no.
	Go and manage what is outside. What is inside the house is mine,
	and a girl on her wedding day needs her mother there.

(Clytemnestra goes into the tent.)

Agamemnon.
Oh. I rushed at that and got nowhere. My hope has slipped through my
fingers. I wanted my wife out of my sight. I make clever plans, I
devise tricks, against the people I love most in the world, and I am
beaten at every turn. All the same, I will go and find Calchas the
priest, and together we will work out what the goddess wants, which is
no good luck for me, and is the labour of all Greece. A clever man
should keep a good and useful wife in his house, or no wife at all.

(Agamemnon goes out.)

Chorus.
	So they will come to the Simois
	and its silver-flashing eddies,
	the gathered army of the Greeks,
	in ships and in armour,
	to Ilium, to the plain of Troy
	that belongs to Apollo,
	where I hear Cassandra
	tosses her golden hair,
	crowned with a wreath of green laurel,
	whenever the god's prophecy
	comes on her, and she cannot fight it.

Chorus.
	And the Trojans will stand on the citadel,
	on the towers of Troy and all round the walls,
	when Ares in his bronze shield comes up from the sea,
	rowed in on the oars of fine-prowed ships,
	to the channels of the Simois,
	wanting to take Helen back from Priam,
	Helen, whose brothers are the twin Dioscuri,
	the stars in the sky,
	and carry her home to the land of Greece
	by the toil of Achaean shields
	and Achaean spears.

Chorus.
	And Pergamum, the Phrygians' city,
	he will ring round with murdering war,
	its walls and its stone towers,
	the son of Atreus, and drag back Paris's head
	and cut his throat,
	and sack the city from the top down,
	and leave the girls weeping,
	and Priam's wife weeping.
	And Helen, the daughter of Zeus,
	will sit and weep and weep
	for leaving her husband.
	Never on me,
	never on my children's children
	may that expectation fall,
	the one the Lydian women, heavy with gold,
	and the wives of the Phrygians
	will stand at their looms
	and talk over with each other:
	Who is the man who will wind my lovely hair
	round his fist and drag me off in tears,
	and pluck me like a flower from my country as it dies?
	Because of you, child of the long-necked swan,
	if the story is true
	that Leda lay with a bird on the wing
	when Zeus had changed his body,
	or whether it is all in the poets' tablets,
	tales carried to men
	for nothing, out of season.

(Achilles comes in.)

Achilles.
Where is the general of the Achaeans? Which of his servants will tell
him that Achilles, the son of Peleus, is at his gates looking for him?
Because this waiting beside the Euripus is not the same for all of us.
Some of us are not married, and have left empty houses behind to sit
here on the beach. Others have wives, and no children yet. That is how
fierce the passion for this expedition is that has fallen on Greece,
and the gods had a hand in it. I have a just claim of my own, and I am
the one who has to state it; anyone else who wants to can speak for
himself. I left my country, Pharsalus, and I left Peleus, and I am
waiting here for these thin breezes on the Euripus, holding my
Myrmidons back. And they are at me the whole time. "Achilles, what are
we waiting for? How much longer do we have to count off the days before
the fleet sails for Ilium? Do something, if you are going to do
anything, or take the army home, and stop waiting on the sons of Atreus
and their dithering."

(Clytemnestra comes out of the tent.)

Clytemnestra.
Son of the Nereid goddess. I heard you talking from inside, and I have
come out in front of the tent.

Achilles.
Holy Modesty. Who is this woman I am looking at? And such a beautiful
woman.

Clytemnestra.
No wonder you do not know me. You never had anything to do with me
before. And I like that in you, that you honour a proper reserve.

Achilles.
Who are you? And why have you come to the muster of the Greeks? A
woman, among men fenced in with shields.

Clytemnestra.
I am Leda's daughter. My name is Clytemnestra. And my husband is
Agamemnon, the king.

Achilles.
Well said. Short, and everything that matters. But it is shameful for me
to stand here talking with women.

Clytemnestra.
Stay. Why are you running away? Put your right hand in mine. It is the
happy beginning of a marriage.

Achilles.
What are you saying? I, give you my hand? I would be ashamed to face
Agamemnon, if I touched what I have no right to touch.

Clytemnestra.
Every right. You are marrying my child, son of the sea-goddess, the
daughter of Nereus.

Achilles.
What marriage are you talking about? I am speechless, lady. Unless you
have lost your mind, and are making up a story?

Clytemnestra.
Everyone is like this. They go shy when they meet their new relations
and someone mentions the wedding.

Achilles.
I have never once courted your daughter, lady. And no word about any
marriage has ever come to me from the sons of Atreus.

Clytemnestra.
Then what can this be? You wonder at what I say. Well, it is my turn.
What you say is a wonder to me.

Achilles.
Guess. We can both guess at this. It may be neither of us is lying.

Clytemnestra.
Then something dreadful has been done to me. I am courting a marriage
that does not exist, by the look of it. I am ashamed.

Achilles.
Perhaps somebody has made fools of both of us, you and me. Pay no
attention to it. Take it lightly.

Clytemnestra.
Goodbye. I cannot look you in the eye any longer. I have been made a
liar, and I have been treated as I do not deserve.

Achilles.
And goodbye to you, from me. I am going into the tent to look for your
husband.

(The Old Man opens the door of the tent a little and calls out.)

Old Man.
Stranger, descendant of Aeacus, wait. You, yes, you, I am talking to
you, the goddess's son. And you, Leda's daughter.

Achilles.
Who is that calling, with the door half open? How frightened he sounds.

Old Man.
A slave. I am not proud of it, but my luck does not allow me to be
anything else.

Achilles.
Whose slave? Not mine. What is mine and what is Agamemnon's are two
separate things.

Old Man.
This lady's, standing in front of the tent. Her father Tyndareus gave me
to her.

Achilles.
We are standing still. Say what you want, if you want something. Tell me
what you stopped me for.

Old Man.
Is it really just the two of you here at the doors?

Achilles.
You can talk as if it were just the two of us. Come out of the king's
tent.

(The Old Man comes out of the tent, looking around him.)

Old Man.
Fortune, and my own foresight. Save the people I want saved.

Achilles.
That is a speech that is putting something off. It sounds important,
though.

Clytemnestra.
Never mind kissing my hand. Do not waste time, if you have something to
tell me.

Old Man.
You do know me, do you not? Who I am, and that I have always been loyal
to you and your children?

Clytemnestra.
I know you. You are an old servant of my house.

Old Man.
And that King Agamemnon got me as part of your dowry?

Clytemnestra.
You came to Argos with us, and you have always been mine.

Old Man.
That is right. And I am loyal to you. Less so to your husband.

Clytemnestra.
Then out with it, at last. Whatever it is you have to tell us.

Old Man.
Your child. Her father, the man who gave her life, is going to kill her
with his own hand.

Clytemnestra.
What? I spit that story out, old man. You are out of your mind.

Old Man.
With a sword. Cutting the poor girl's white throat.

Clytemnestra.
Oh, no. Oh, no. Has my husband gone mad, then?

Old Man.
He is in his right mind, except where you and your daughter are
concerned. There he has lost it.

Clytemnestra.
On what grounds? What avenging spirit is driving him to it?

Old Man.
Oracles. So Calchas says, anyway. So the army can sail.

Clytemnestra.
Sail where? Oh, this is misery, for me, and for the girl her father is
going to kill.

Old Man.
To the house of Dardanus. So that Menelaus can get Helen back.

Clytemnestra.
So Helen's homecoming was fated to cost Iphigenia?

Old Man.
You have the whole of it. Her father is going to sacrifice your child
to Artemis.

Clytemnestra.
And the marriage? What was that for? The excuse that brought me from
home?

Old Man.
So that you would come gladly, bringing your daughter to marry
Achilles.

Clytemnestra.
Daughter, you have come here to be destroyed. You, and your mother with
you.

Old Man.
It is pitiful, what the two of you are suffering. And Agamemnon has
dared a terrible thing.

Clytemnestra.
I am finished. My eyes cannot hold the tears back any more.

Old Man.
If losing a child hurts, then cry.

Clytemnestra.
And you, old man. Where did you hear this? How do you come to know it?

Old Man.
I was on my way to you, carrying a letter about what he had written
before.

Clytemnestra.
Telling me not to bring the child to her death, or telling me again to
bring her?

Old Man.
Telling you not to bring her. Your husband was in his right mind then.

Clytemnestra.
Then if you were carrying a letter, how is it you are not giving it to
me?

Old Man.
Menelaus took it off me. He is the cause of all this.

Clytemnestra.
Child of the Nereid, son of Peleus. Do you hear this?

Achilles.
I hear that you are a wretched woman. And I do not take my own part in
it lightly.

Clytemnestra.
They are going to kill my child. They tricked her here with a marriage
to you.

Achilles.
I blame your husband too. And I do not simply let it go at that.

Clytemnestra.
I will not be ashamed to fall at your knees, a mortal at the knees of a
goddess's son. Why should I stand on my dignity? Whose business should I
work harder for than my own child's?

(She kneels and clasps his knees.)

Defend me, son of the goddess, in my misfortune. And defend her, the
girl who was called your wife. Falsely, yes, but called it all the same.
For you I put the wreath on her head and brought her here to be
married, and now I am bringing her to have her throat cut. And the
disgrace will fall on you, if you do not defend her. You were never
yoked to her in marriage, but you were called the poor girl's loving
husband all the same. By your beard I beg you, by your right hand, by
your mother. Your name destroyed me, and your name is what has to save
me. I have no altar to run to but your knee. I have no friend to smile
on me. You have heard what Agamemnon has done: savage, and stopping at
nothing. And I have come, as you can see, a woman, into a camp of
sailors with no one in charge of them, bold for any mischief, though
useful enough when they want to be. If you have the nerve to stretch out
your hand over me, we are saved. If you do not, we are not saved.

Chorus.
Motherhood is a strange thing. It carries a great love-charm with it,
and every woman shares it: they will go through anything for their
children.

Achilles.
My spirit is a proud one, and it is rising in me now. But I have learned
to grieve at bad fortune in moderation, and to take joy in good fortune
in moderation too. Men like that are the ones who have thought their
lives through, and live them rightly, with judgement. There are times
when it is pleasant not to think too much, and there are times when it
is useful to have some judgement. I was brought up in the house of the
most god-fearing man alive, Chiron, and I learned to keep my ways
simple. As for the sons of Atreus: when they lead well, I will follow
them, and when they lead badly, I will not. Here, and in Troy, I will
show what a free nature is, and honour Ares with my spear as far as it is
in me to do it.

And you, who have suffered so monstrously at the hands of the people
closest to you: as far as a young man can, I will wrap you round in my
pity and set you right. Your daughter was called mine, and she will
never have her throat cut by her father. I am not going to lend your
husband my body to weave his plots with. Because it is my name that
will kill your child, even if it never lifts the iron. The cause is your
husband. But my body is no longer clean, if because of me, because of
my marriage, this girl dies, who has suffered terribly, unbearably, who
has been dishonoured so astonishingly and so undeservedly. Then I would
be the worst man in the Argive army. I would be nothing, and Menelaus
would count as a man. I would be no son of Peleus but the son of some
avenging fiend, if my name does your husband's murdering for him.

By Nereus, who was bred in the wet waves, the father of Thetis, who bore
me: King Agamemnon will not lay a hand on your daughter. Not a fingertip.
Not so much as to brush her dress. Or else Sipylus will be a real city,
that barbarian outpost these generals' family comes from, and the name
of Phthia will never be spoken anywhere again. Calchas the prophet will
find his barley and his holy water bitter when he lifts them. And what
is a prophet? A man who says a few true things and a great many false
ones when his luck is in, and when his luck is out, he is finished.

It is not for the sake of the marriage that I say this. There are ten
thousand girls hunting for a place in my bed. No. King Agamemnon has
committed an outrage against me. He should have asked me for the use of
my name, if he wanted bait for his child. It was mostly on my account
that Clytemnestra was persuaded to give her daughter away to a husband.
I would have given it to the Greeks, if that was where the voyage to
Ilium was breaking down. I would not have refused to build up the common
cause of the men I marched with. But as it is, I am nothing. To the
generals it is all one whether they treat me well or badly. My sword
will soon know the answer. Before it ever gets to the Phrygians I will
stain it with the marks of slaughter, with blood, if anyone tries to take
your daughter away from me. Now calm yourself. I have appeared to you as
a very great god. I am not one. But I will become one, all the same.

Chorus.
Son of Peleus, you have spoken as you should, worthy of yourself and of
the goddess of the sea, your holy mother.

Clytemnestra.
Pheu. How can I praise you without overdoing it in words, and yet not
fall short and lose your goodwill? When good men are praised, they
somehow come to hate the people praising them, if the praise goes too
far. And I am ashamed to be bringing in my pitiful story, when the
sickness is mine alone and you are untouched by my troubles. But still,
it looks well when a good man, even from a distance, helps people in
trouble. Pity us. What we are suffering is pitiable. First I thought I
had you for a son-in-law, and the hope I held was empty. And then,
perhaps, if my child dies it might turn into a bad omen for your own
marriage, when it comes; you should guard against that. But you have
spoken well from the beginning, and well at the end. If you are
willing, my child will be saved.

Do you want her to come and clasp your knee as a suppliant? It is not
what a girl should do. But if you think she should, she will come, with
the modest look of a free-born girl. And if I can get the same from you
without her here, then let her stay inside. She has a proud sense of her
own dignity. Still, modesty only goes as far as it can.

Achilles.
Do not bring your daughter out where I can see her, lady. Let us not
come in for the reproach of ignorant men. An army massed together, idle
and away from home, loves nasty, foul-mouthed gossip. In any case, you
will get the same from me whether the two of you beg me or nobody begs
me at all. I have one great contest in front of me: to get you out of
your troubles. Hear one thing and know it. I will not lie to you. If I am
lying to you, or mocking you for nothing, let me die. And let me not die,
if I save the girl.

Clytemnestra.
Bless you, for always helping people in trouble.

Achilles.
Then listen to me now, so that the thing goes well.

Clytemnestra.
What do you mean? I certainly have to listen to you.

Achilles.
Let us try again to persuade her father to think better of it.

Clytemnestra.
He is a coward, and he is far too afraid of the army.

Achilles.
But arguments wrestle arguments down.

Clytemnestra.
Cold hope. But tell me what I have to do.

Achilles.
Beg him first not to kill his children. If he resists, then you come to
me. Because if he does what you ask, there is no need for my part in
this to move at all: his consent is the rescue by itself. And I will be
on better terms with a friend, and the army will have nothing to blame
me for, if I settle things by reasoning rather than by force. And if it
turns out well, it would come out to the liking of your family and you,
even without me.

Clytemnestra.
How sensibly you talk. We must do what you think best. But if we do not
get what I want, then where do I see you again? Where? Where do I have
to go, poor woman that I am, to find your hand to help me?

Achilles.
I will keep watch to guard you, where you need guarding, so that nobody
sees you rushing in a panic through the crowd of Greeks. Do not shame
your father's house. Tyndareus does not deserve to be spoken of badly.
He is a great man among the Greeks.

Clytemnestra.
So be it. Give the orders. I have to be your slave now. If there are
gods, then you are a just man, and you will find the gods good to you.
And if there are none, why should anyone take the trouble?

(Achilles goes out. Clytemnestra goes into the tent.)

Chorus.
	What was the wedding cry Hymen raised
	to the Libyan flute,
	and the lyre that loves the dance,
	and the pipes of reed,
	when over Pelion the Muses,
	the lovely-haired daughters of Pieria,
	came to the feast of the gods,
	striking the ground with their golden sandals,
	came to the wedding of Peleus,
	and sang Thetis and the son of Aeacus in their music
	across the Centaurs' mountains,
	down through the woods of Pelion?
	And the Dardanian boy,
	the darling of the bed of Zeus,
	drew off the wine in the golden hollows
	of the mixing bowls,
	Ganymede the Phrygian.
	And along the white-shining sand
	the fifty daughters of Nereus
	wheeled in their circles
	and danced the wedding.

Chorus.
	And up through the firs and the green of their wreaths
	came the troop of Centaurs on their horse-feet,
	to the feast of the gods
	and the mixing bowl of Dionysus.
	And they shouted out loud: Daughter of Nereus,
	the prophet who knows Apollo's music,
	Chiron, has named the son you will bear,
	a great light for Thessaly,
	who will come to the famous land of Priam
	with Myrmidon spearmen and Myrmidon shields
	to burn it to the ground,
	his body cased in golden armour,
	the work of Hephaestus,
	a gift from his goddess mother,
	from Thetis, who bore him.
	That was the day the gods made blessed
	the marriage of the high-born bride,
	the first of the daughters of Nereus,
	and the wedding of Peleus.

Chorus.
	But you. The Argives will crown your head,
	crown your lovely hair,
	like a dappled heifer brought down
	unblemished from the mountains,
	from the rocky caves,
	and they will bloody your throat, a human throat,
	you who were never raised to the pipe
	or the whistling of herdsmen,
	but at your mother's side,
	dressed for a bride's wedding to a son of Inachus.
	Where does the face of Modesty
	or the face of Virtue
	have any strength now,
	when what is unholy has the power
	and Virtue is left behind
	and mortals take no notice of her,
	and Lawlessness rules over the laws,
	and mortals no longer stand together
	to keep the anger of the gods away?

(Clytemnestra comes out of the tent.)

Clytemnestra.
I have come out of the tent to look for my husband. He went off a long
while ago and left the place empty. And my poor child is in there in
tears, pouring out one lament after another, since she heard about the
death her father is planning for her. It seems I was talking about a man
who was not far away. Here is Agamemnon now, and in a moment he is going
to be caught in the act, doing something unholy to his own children.

(Agamemnon comes in.)

Agamemnon.
Daughter of Leda. Good, I have found you outside the tent, so I can say
some things away from the girl, things a bride should not hear.

Clytemnestra.
And what is it? What is so pressing that the moment has caught you?

Agamemnon.
Send the child out of the tent, to be with her father. The holy water is
standing ready, and the barley to throw with our own hands onto the
cleansing fire, and the heifers that have to fall to the goddess before
a wedding. To Artemis. With the black blood spurting out of them.
