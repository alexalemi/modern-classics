The Phoenician Women
(Part 3 of 4)

Creon.
Stop there, old man.

Teiresias.
Do not grab at me.

Creon.
Wait. Why are you running away?

Teiresias.
It is your luck that is running from you. Not me.

Creon.
Tell the citizens and the city how they can be saved.

Teiresias.
You want that now. Very soon you will not want it.

Creon.
How could I not want to save my father's land?

Teiresias.
You really want to hear it, then? You are set on it?

Creon.
What should I be more eager about than that?

Teiresias.
Then you may hear my prophecies now. But first there is one thing I
want to know for certain. Where is Menoeceus, who led me here?

Creon.
He is not far off. He is right beside you.

Teiresias.
Then let him go away, well out of hearing of my prophecies.

Creon.
He is my son. He will keep quiet about what needs keeping quiet.

Teiresias.
You want me to tell you with him standing here?

Creon.
Yes. He will be glad to hear how the city is going to be saved.

Teiresias.
Then hear the road my prophecies take. This is what you must do to save
the city of Cadmus. You must cut the throat of Menoeceus here, your
own son, for his country. You asked for your luck yourself.

Creon.
What are you saying? What is this story you are telling me, old man?

Teiresias.
What is so, is so. And you must do it.

Creon.
So much evil, said in so short a time.

Teiresias.
Evil for you. For your country, great good, and her salvation.

Creon.
I did not hear it. I did not listen. Goodbye to the city.

Teiresias.
This is not the same man. He is backing away from it.

Creon.
Go, and good luck to you. I do not need your prophecies.

Teiresias.
Has the truth died, because you have had bad luck?

Creon.
By your knees, by your grey hair, I beg you—

Teiresias.
Why are you falling at my feet? You are asking for evils that cannot
be kept off.

Creon.
Be quiet. Do not tell the city any of this.

Teiresias.
You are telling me to do wrong. I will not be quiet.

Creon.
What are you going to do to me, then? Kill my child?

Teiresias.
That will be other people's business. Mine is to speak.

Creon.
And where did this evil come from, on me and on my son?

Teiresias.
You are right to ask me that, and to come into the argument. This boy
must be killed in the den where the earth-born dragon was born to keep
watch over the springs of Dirce, and his throat must give its blood to
the ground as a drink-offering to Cadmus's earth. It is because of the
old anger of Ares, who is avenging the killing of his earth-born
dragon. Do this and you will have Ares as your ally. If the earth takes
fruit for fruit and human blood for blood, you will have her kind to
you again, the earth who once sent up for us a crop of Sown Men in
golden helmets. But one of that race has to die, one who was born from
the dragon's jaw. And you are the last of the Sown Men left to us here,
pure on both sides, your mother's and your father's, you and your sons.
Haemon's marriage keeps him from the knife. He is not unmarried: even
if he has not touched the bed yet, he has a wife. But this boy is a
colt still loose, and he belongs to this city. If he dies, he can save
his father's land. He will make a bitter homecoming for Adrastus and
the Argives, throwing a black death over their eyes, and he will make
Thebes famous. Choose one of these two fates. Save your son, or save
the city.

That is everything I have for you. Lead me home, child. Anyone who
practises the art of reading the burnt offerings is a fool. If he
happens to see something his clients hate, he is hated by the people
he reads the birds for. And if he takes pity on the people consulting
him and lies to them, he wrongs the gods. Apollo should have been the
only prophet men had. He is afraid of nobody.

(Teiresias goes, led by his daughter.)

Chorus.
Creon, why are you silent? Why have you let your voice fall dead? I am
no less stunned than you are.

Creon.
What is there to say? It is plain enough what my answer is. I will
never come to such a pass that I cut my son's throat and hand him to
the city. Every human being alive loves his children, and nobody
would give his own son to be killed. Let nobody praise me for it while
he kills my children. I myself, since I am standing in the ripe part
of my life, am ready to die to set my country free.

Come on, boy. Before the whole city hears this, forget the wild
prophecies of seers, and run. Get out of this country as fast as you
can. He will go to the rulers and the generals and the captains at the
seven gates and tell them all of it. If we are ahead of him, you are
safe. If you are late, we are finished, and you die.

Menoeceus.
Where am I to run to, then? What city? Which of our guest-friends?

Creon.
Wherever you will be furthest out of the way of this country.

Menoeceus.
Then it is for you to say where, and for me to get there.

Creon.
Go past Delphi—

Menoeceus.
And where do I go then, father?

Creon.
Into the land of Aetolia.

Menoeceus.
And from there, where do I cross to?

Creon.
The soil of Thesprotia.

Menoeceus.
The holy floor of Dodona?

Creon.
You have it.

Menoeceus.
And what protection will that be for me?

Creon.
The god will send you on your way.

Menoeceus.
And money? Where does that come from?

Creon.
I will send the gold along.

Menoeceus.
Good, father. That is a good plan. Go now. I am going to your sister,
Jocasta, I mean, the woman whose breast I first sucked when I lost my
mother and was left an orphan, unyoked. I will say goodbye to her and
then I will save my life. Go on, go. Do not let anything of yours hold
me back.

(Creon goes.)

Women, how neatly I took my father's fear away, stealing it with
words, so that I get what I want. He is smuggling me out of the
country, robbing the city of its luck and handing me over to
cowardice. In an old man that is forgivable. In me there is no
forgiveness for it: to turn traitor to the country that gave me birth.
So that you know, then: I am going to save the city, and I am going to
give my life and die for this land. It would be shameful otherwise.
Men who are free of any prophecy, who have not come under any
necessity laid on them by the gods, stand shield to shield and are not
afraid to die, fighting for their country in front of her towers. And
I, betraying my father and my brother and my own city, walk out of the
country like a coward? Wherever I lived, I would be shown for a bad
man. No. By Zeus among the stars, and by Ares the blood-red, who set
the Sown Men up as lords of this land when they rose out of the earth,
I will go, and I will stand on the top of the battlements, and I will
cut my own throat over the dragon's deep black den, where the seer
said, and I will set this land free. That is the whole speech. I am
going now, to give the city a gift that is nothing to be ashamed of,
my death, and to rid this country of its sickness. If every man took
whatever good he could do and worked it through to the end and brought
it in to the common store for his country, cities would have fewer
evils to live through, and they would do well from then on.

(Menoeceus goes.)

Chorus.
	You came, you came,
	winged thing, child of the earth
	and of the snake-woman down below,
	to snatch the sons of Cadmus,
	all death, all groaning,
	half a girl,
	a monster made to kill,
	with wings that never rested
	and claws that ate raw meat.
	From the country round Dirce's stream
	you lifted young men into the air,
	with a song no lyre would play,
	a Fury of destruction,
	and you brought, you brought grief to our country,
	murderous grief. Murderous was the god
	who did this to us.
	Mothers' wailing,
	girls' wailing,
	went groaning through the houses.
	A cry of woe,
	a song of woe,
	one after another took it up
	in turn across the town.
	The groaning, and the echo of it,
	were like thunder
	every time the winged girl
	carried one more man out of the city.

Chorus.
	And in time there came,
	sent out from Delphi,
	Oedipus, the long-suffering man,
	to this land of Thebes.
	We were glad then. Then came the grief.
	He won the riddle, and the prize
	for winning was a marriage,
	an unholy marriage, with his mother,
	poor wretch,
	and he stained the city with it.
	And through blood he passes on
	into a filthy contest, throwing his sons
	down into it with his curses,
	the miserable man. We admire him, we admire him,
	the boy who is going to his death
	for his father's land,
	leaving Creon his tears,
	but making the seven-towered gates of this country
	victorious.
	Let us be mothers like that,
	let us have children like that, dear
	Pallas, you who brought the dragon's blood
	to the ground with a thrown stone,
	when you set Cadmus's mind
	moving to the deed,
	from which some ruin sent by the gods came rushing
	on this land, to snatch and tear.

(A Messenger, one of Eteocles's men, comes in and beats on the door.)

Messenger.
Hey. Who is at the gates of the house? Open up. Bring Jocasta out of
the house. Hey, again. It is taking a long time, but come out all the
same. Listen to me, famous wife of Oedipus. Stop your mourning and
your tears of grief.

(Jocasta comes out.)

Jocasta.
My dear friend, you have not come with news of disaster, have you?
Eteocles dead? You have always marched beside his shield, keeping the
enemy's spears off him. What is this new word you have come to bring
me? Is my son dead or alive? Tell me.

Messenger.
He is alive. Do not be afraid of that. Let me take that fear off you.

Jocasta.
Well? How are the walls with the seven towers holding?

Messenger.
They stand unbroken. The city has not been taken.

Jocasta.
Did they come into danger from the Argive spear?

Messenger.
To the very edge of it. But the fighting men of Cadmus proved stronger
than the spear of Mycenae.

Jocasta.
Tell me one thing, by the gods, if you know anything about
Polyneices. That matters to me too, whether he still sees the light.

Messenger.
Your sons are alive, the pair of them, as of today.

Jocasta.
Bless you. How did you drive the Argive spear back from the gates,
shut in behind the towers as you were? Tell me, so that I can go into
the house and cheer the old blind man, now that this land is safe.

Messenger.
After Creon's son, the boy who died for his country, had stood on the
top of the towers and driven a black-hilted sword through his throat to
save this land, your son divided the army into seven companies with
seven captains and posted them at the seven gates to guard against the
Argive spear. He set horsemen in reserve behind horsemen, and armed
men behind the shield-bearers, so that wherever the wall was sick,
help with the spear would be close at hand. From the high citadel we
watched the Argive army with its white shields leave Teumessus, and
near the ditch they closed on the town of Cadmus's land at a run. The
war-cry and the trumpets rang out together from their side and from
our walls.

First to the Neistan gate came Parthenopaeus, the huntress's son,
leading a company bristling with close-packed shields. He had his own
family emblem in the middle of his shield: Atalanta bringing down the
Aetolian boar with a long-range bow. To the Proetid gate went the
prophet Amphiaraus, with the animals for sacrifice on his chariot. He
carried no boastful emblem. His weapons were modest and plain. To the
Ogygian gate marched lord Hippomedon, with an emblem in the middle of
his shield: Argus who sees everything, staring out of eyes stippled
all over him, some of the eyes open with the rising of the stars and
some closing as they set, as we could see afterwards, when he was
dead. At the Homoloid gate Tydeus had his post, a lion's skin on his
shield with the mane bristling, and in his right hand the Titan
Prometheus carried a torch, as if to burn the city. Your own
Polyneices brought the fighting to the Crenaean gate. The emblem on
his shield was the mares of Potniae, running mad and leaping with
fright, turned somehow by pivots on the inside, right under the
handgrip, so that they seemed to have really gone mad. And Capaneus,
who thinks himself no less than Ares when it comes to a fight, led his
company against the Electran gate. On the iron back of his shield was
stamped an earth-born giant carrying a whole city on his shoulders,
levered up off its foundations with crowbars: a hint to us of what
our city was going to suffer. And at the seventh gate was Adrastus,
with a hundred snakes painted to fill his shield, the hydra on his
left arm, the pride of Argos, and out of the middle of the walls the
snakes were carrying off the children of Cadmus in their jaws. I had a
view of every one of them, because I was carrying the password along
the line to the captains of the companies.

To begin with we fought them with bows and thonged javelins, with
long-range slings and crashing stones. And as we were winning the
fight, Tydeus and your son suddenly shouted out: "Sons of Danaus,
before you are shredded by their missiles, why are you waiting? All of
you, together, throw yourselves at the gates, light troops, horsemen,
the men on the chariots." When they heard that cry there was not one
of them idle. Many fell with their heads streaming blood, and on our
side too you could have seen men in front of the walls tumbling to the
ground like acrobats, thick on the ground, breathing their last, and
wetting the dry earth with streams of blood.

Then the Arcadian, not an Argive, Atalanta's son, threw himself at the
gates like a whirlwind and shouted for fire and pickaxes, to dig the
city up out of the ground. But Periclymenus, the sea-god's son,
stopped him in his frenzy by dropping a stone on his head that would
have filled a wagon, a coping-stone off the battlements. It smashed
his fair-haired head to powder and burst the seams of the bones of his
skull, and the cheek that had only just turned red with youth ran
with blood. He will not carry his life home to his mother with the
lovely bow, the girl of Maenalus.

When your son saw that this gate was doing well, he went on to the
others, and I followed him. I saw Tydeus and a close-packed line of
his shield-men hurling their Aetolian spears up into the very mouth
of the towers, so that our men left the edge of the battlements and
ran. But your son gathered them again, like a huntsman calling in his
dogs, and posted them back on the towers. And we hurried on to other
gates, once we had stopped the sickness there.

And Capaneus. How can I tell you how mad he was? He came on carrying
the climbing-rungs of a long-necked ladder, and this was his boast:
that not even the holy fire of Zeus would keep him from taking the
city from the top of its citadel. And he was still shouting it as he
climbed, under a rain of stones, with his body wound up under his
shield, changing his footing rung by smooth rung up the ladder. And
just as he was stepping over the coping of the wall, Zeus struck him
with a thunderbolt. The earth boomed, so that everyone was afraid, and
from the ladder his limbs were slung apart from each other like
slingshot, his hair to Olympus and his blood to the ground, and his
arms and legs went spinning round like the wheel of Ixion, and the
body fell to the earth on fire.

When Adrastus saw that Zeus was his army's enemy, he pulled the Argive
force back outside the ditch. But our men had seen the sign from Zeus
was on our side, and they drove out the chariots, horsemen and armed
men together, and closed their spears with the Argives in the middle
of their ranks. And every kind of evil was there at once. Men were
dying, men were pitched over chariot-rails, wheels leaped into the
air, axles piled on axles, and the dead were heaped on the dead. So we
have kept the towers of this land from being dug up, for today. But
whether this country will have good luck from now on rests with the
gods. Even today it was one of the gods who saved her.

Chorus.
Winning is a fine thing. And if the gods have a better judgement in
store for us, I would be a lucky woman.

Jocasta.
The gods and luck have done well by us. My sons are alive, and the land
has escaped. But it is Creon who seems to have got the bitter fruit of
my marriage to Oedipus, poor man, and lost his son: good luck for the
city, and grief for him alone. But go back over it for me. What do my
two sons mean to do next?

Messenger.
Leave the rest alone. So far you are lucky.

Jocasta.
Now you have made me suspicious. I cannot leave it alone.

Messenger.
What more do you want than your sons safe?

Jocasta.
To hear whether I am going to be lucky from here on too.

Messenger.
Let me go. Your son is out there without his shield-bearer.

Jocasta.
You are hiding some evil, and covering it up in the dark.

Messenger.
I would not tell you bad news on top of your good news.

Jocasta.
You will, unless you sprout wings and fly off into the sky.

Messenger.
Aiai. Why did you not let me go after my good news, instead of making
me tell the bad? Your two sons mean to do a thing of the most
shameful daring: to fight one against one, apart from the whole army.
They said this to the Argives and the men of Cadmus together, a
speech I wish they had never made. Eteocles began it. He stood up on a
high tower, ordered silence to be proclaimed to the army, and said:
"Generals of the land of Greece, chiefs of the Danaans who came here,
and people of Cadmus, do not sell your lives for Polyneices' sake, or
for mine. I am going to take this danger off you myself, and fight my
brother alone. If I kill him, I will keep my house alone. If I lose, I
will hand it over to him alone. Argives, give up the contest and go
home to your country, and do not leave your lives here. And there are
enough of the Sown Men lying dead already." That was his speech. Your
son Polyneices came running out of the ranks and approved it. And all
the Argives roared their approval, and the people of Cadmus too,
because they thought it was fair. On those terms they made a truce,
and in the ground between the two armies the generals swore oaths to
keep it.

And then the two young men, old Oedipus's sons, were covering
themselves in bronze armour, and their friends were dressing them: the
best of the Sown Men dressing the champion of this land, and the
leading Argives dressing the other. There they stood, shining, and
their colour did not change, and they were wild to let fly their
spears at each other. Their friends came up to them from either side,
one after another, and encouraged them with words, and this is what
they said: "Polyneices, it rests with you to set up a statue of Zeus
as a trophy and to give Argos a glorious name." And to Eteocles: "Now
you are fighting for your city. Now, win this and you hold the
sceptre." That is what they said, urging them on to the fight. And the
prophets were killing sheep, and watching the points of the flames on
the fire, and the cracks, and the wet reek that means defeat, and the
peak of the flame, which marks two things at once: the sign of victory
and the sign of the losers.

So if you have any strength, or any clever words, or any love-spell of
a charm, go. Hold your children back from this terrible contest. The
danger is very great, and the terrible prize you will win for it is
tears, if you are robbed of both your sons on this one day.

(The Messenger goes back the way he came, toward the field.)

Jocasta.
Antigone, my child, come out of the house. The way the gods have set
things for you now is no time for dancing or the things girls do.
There are two brave men, and they are your brothers, and they are
swerving toward death, and you and your mother must stop them from
dying at each other's hands.

(Antigone comes out of the house.)

Antigone.
Mother who bore me, what new terror are you crying out to your friends
in front of this house?

Jocasta.
Daughter, your brothers' lives are going down.

Antigone.
What do you mean?

Jocasta.
They have set themselves to fight one spear against one.

Antigone.
Oh, no. What are you going to tell me, mother?

Jocasta.
Nothing kind. Follow me.

Antigone.
Where? Leaving the women's rooms?

Jocasta.
Through the army.

Antigone.
I am ashamed to face the crowd.

Jocasta.
There is no room for shame in your position now.

Antigone.
But what am I to do?

Jocasta.
You will end your brothers' quarrel.

Antigone.
Doing what, mother?

Jocasta.
Falling at their knees, with me.

Antigone.
Take me to the ground between the armies. We must not wait.

Jocasta.
Hurry, hurry, daughter. If I can get to my sons before the spears, my
life stays in the light. If they die, I will lie down dead with them.

(Jocasta and Antigone hurry out.)

Chorus.
	Aiai, aiai. My heart is shaking,
	shaking with the cold of fear. Through my flesh
	pity has come, pity
	for the wretched mother.
	Twin sons. Which of them, then,
	will bloody the other,
	io, for the pain of it, io Zeus, io Earth,
	a brother's throat, a brother's life,
	through the shields, through the blood?
	I am wretched, wretched. Which of them
	will I cry over as a corpse when he is gone?

Chorus.
	Pheu, Earth. Pheu, Earth. Twin beasts,
	two murderous souls with spears shaking in their hands,
	in a moment now will bloody
	the enemy fallen, fallen.
	Poor wretches, that it ever came into their heads
	to fight one against one.
	In a foreign voice I will raise the groaning cry
	that is owed to the dead, and weep it through.
	Fortune stands close, close to murder.
	The light of this day will decide it. Doomed,
	doomed is this killing, for the Furies' sake.

(Creon comes in, carrying the body of Menoeceus.)

Chorus.
But here is Creon. I see him coming this way to the house, with a
cloud on his face, and I will stop the grieving I am in the middle of.

Creon.
Oh. What am I going to do? Which do I cry for, myself or the city,
which has a cloud round it thick enough to be going through the river
of Acheron? My son is dead. He died for this land, and he won a noble
name for it, and a painful one for me. I have just taken him from the
dragon's cliffs, where he cut his own throat, and I carried him here
in my own arms, wretched as I am, and the whole house is crying out.
And now I have come, an old man, for my old sister, for Jocasta, so
that she can wash my boy and lay him out. He is gone now. Those who
are not yet dead have to pay the dead their honours. It is how we
show respect to the god under the ground.
