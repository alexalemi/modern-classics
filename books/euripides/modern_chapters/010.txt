Hippolytus
(Part 3 of 3)

Chorus.
I don't know how I could ever call any mortal happy. What was on top
has been turned over and is upside down again.

Hippolytus.
Father, the anger in you, the way your mind has locked itself shut, is
frightening. But this business of yours, for all it has fine arguments
on its side, is not fine at all once somebody unfolds it. I am no good
at making speeches to a crowd. Among people my own age, and few of
them, I am cleverer. And there is a fairness in that too: the men who
are nothing among the wise are the ones who sing sweetest to a mob.
Still, with disaster here, I have no choice. I have to let my tongue
go. I will begin where you first came at me, meaning to finish me off
without my getting a word in. You see this light, and this earth. In
all of it there is no man, whatever you say, who keeps himself in hand
better than I do. I know, first, how to revere the gods. I know how to
have friends who do not try to do wrong, friends who would be ashamed
to propose anything bad or to pay back the people they deal with in
shameful favours. I am not a man who laughs at his companions, father.
I am the same man to my friends when they are away as when they are
standing next to me. And there is one thing I have never touched, and
it is the very thing you think you have caught me in. To this day my
body is clean of the bed. I do not know that act at all, except by
hearing it talked about and seeing it in pictures, and I am not eager
to look even at those. I have a virgin's soul.

Very well. Suppose my self-control does not convince you. Let it go.
Then you have to show how I was corrupted. Was her body more beautiful
than every other woman's? Or did I hope to live in your house by
marrying into a bed with an inheritance attached? Then I was a fool.
No, worse: I was out of my mind entirely. Or is it that "being king is
sweet"? Not to a man who keeps himself in hand, not in the least,
unless being sole ruler has already rotted the minds of the mortals it
pleases. I would want to be first at winning the games of Greece, but
second in the city, and happy for ever there with the best men for my
friends. You can get things done in that position, and the absence of
danger gives a pleasure that no throne can match.

One of my points has not been made. You have all the others. If I had
a witness to the kind of man I am, and if I were fighting this case
with her alive and looking at the light, you would have gone through
the facts and seen who the guilty were. As it is, I swear to you by
Zeus, who keeps oaths, and by the floor of the earth, that I never
touched your marriage, never wanted to, never once had the thought. If
I am a bad man, let me die without fame and without a name, with no
city and no home, an exile wandering the earth, and let neither sea
nor land take my flesh when I am dead. Whether she was frightened and
destroyed her own life, I do not know. I am not allowed to say more
than that. She kept herself in hand, though she did not have it in her
to. I had it in me, and I have not used it well.

Chorus.
You have given a good enough answer to the charge. You have offered
oaths by the gods, and that is no small pledge.

Theseus.
Isn't this man a spell-singer and a sorcerer? He has dishonoured the
father who made him, and he is confident he can master my spirit with
his even temper.

Hippolytus.
And I am astonished at the very same thing in you, father. If you were
my son and I were your father, I would have killed you, not punished
you with exile, if I thought you had laid a hand on my wife.

Theseus.
How fitting, what you have just said. But you will not die like that.
A quick death is the easiest thing for a man in trouble. No. You will
wander in exile from your father's land, exactly the sentence you laid
down for yourself, and drain out a bitter life on foreign soil. That is
the wage for a man who has no piety.

Hippolytus.
Oh. What are you going to do? You won't even wait for time to give its
evidence against me? You will drive me out of the country?

Theseus.
Beyond the sea. Beyond the lands of Atlas, if I had the power. That is
how much I hate the sight of your face.

Hippolytus.
Without testing my oath, or my pledge, or a word from the seers, you
will throw me out of the land untried?

Theseus.
This tablet here drew no lot for the right to speak, and it accuses
you, and it is believable. As for the birds that flit about over our
heads, I wish them a long goodbye.

Hippolytus.
Gods. Why don't I unlock my mouth, when I am being destroyed by you,
the very ones I revere? No. I won't. I would not persuade the people I
need to persuade in any case, and I would be breaking the oaths I
swore for nothing.

Theseus.
Oh, this holiness of yours will be the death of me. Get out of your
father's land. Now.

Hippolytus.
Where am I supposed to turn, in the state I am in? Whose house will
take me in as a guest, an exile on a charge like this?

Theseus.
Somebody who enjoys entertaining the corrupters of other men's wives,
and keeping house with their crimes.

Hippolytus.
Aiai. That one went to the liver. It is close to tears, this, if I
really look like a bad man, and you really think I am one.

Theseus.
The time to groan and think ahead was when you had the nerve to outrage
your father's wife.

Hippolytus.
House, if only you could find a voice and speak for me, and give your
evidence whether I am a bad man.

Theseus.
You run to witnesses that cannot talk. Clever. But the deed does not
need to talk. It informs on you, and it says you are bad.

Hippolytus.
Pheu. If only I could stand opposite myself and look at me, so that I
could weep at what I am suffering.

Theseus.
Yes, you have practised worshipping yourself far more than you have
practised being just and doing right by your parents.

Hippolytus.
My poor, unhappy mother. What a bitter birth. May no friend of mine
ever be a bastard.

Theseus.
Drag him out of here, slaves. Didn't you hear me? I proclaimed him
banished long ago.

Hippolytus.
Any one of them who touches me will be sorry for it. You do it
yourself, if you have the stomach for it. Push me out of the land.

Theseus.
I will, if you don't obey what I have said. No pity for your exile is
creeping over me.

(Theseus goes into the house.)

Hippolytus.
It is fixed, then, it seems. Oh, I am wretched. I know the truth, and I
do not know how to say it. Daughter of Leto, dearest to me of all the
gods, you who sit beside me and hunt beside me, I am to leave glorious
Athens. Goodbye, then, city and land of Erechtheus. Ground of Troezen,
you have so much happiness in you for a boy to grow up in, goodbye.
This is the last time I will look at you and speak to you. Come, young
men of this country, my own age, say goodbye to me and see me out of
the land. You will never see another man who keeps himself in hand
better than I do, even if my father does not think so.

(Hippolytus goes out. His huntsmen stay behind.)

Huntsmen.
	Truly, when the care the gods take of us comes into my mind,
	it takes a great weight of grief away.
	But I keep a hope, deep down, that I understand something,
	and then I look at what happens to mortals, and at what they do,
	and I am left behind.
	One thing gives way to another, from every side,
	and men's lives shift, forever wandering.

Chorus.
	If I could pray, and fate would grant me this from the gods:
	luck, and wealth with it, and a heart that grief has never touched.
	An opinion of things neither too exact
	nor stamped with the false coin's mark,
	but easy ways, changing with tomorrow as tomorrow comes,
	and so, always,
	a life that goes well.

Huntsmen.
	My mind is not clear any more. What I see is past all hope.
	The brightest star of Athens, of the whole Greek land,
	we have seen him, we have seen him, driven by a father's anger
	away to another country.
	Sands of the city's shore,
	oak-woods on the mountain, where he killed the wild things
	running with his quick-footed hounds,
	in the company of holy Dictynna, Artemis of the nets.

Chorus.
	No more will you mount behind your yoked Venetian colts
	and hold the track round Limna
	under the hooves of your trained horse.
	The music that never slept under the frame of the strings
	will stop in your father's house.
	No garlands now at the resting places of Leto's daughter
	in the deep green grass.
	And with your exile the contest is over,
	the girls' race for your bed.

Chorus.
	And I, for your misfortune,
	will spend a fate that is no fate in tears.
	Poor mother, you gave birth for nothing.
	Pheu. I am raging at the gods.
	Io, io.
	Graces, who go yoked together, why do you send
	this poor man out of his father's land,
	guilty of none of this ruin,
	away from this house?

Chorus.
And here comes one of Hippolytus's attendants, hurrying toward the
house with a dark look on his face.

Messenger.
Women, where would I find Theseus, the king of this land? If you know,
tell me. Is he inside the house here?

Chorus.
Here he is himself, coming out of the house.

Messenger.
Theseus, I bring news that is worth your worry, for you and for
the citizens who live in the city of Athens and within the borders
of Troezen.

Theseus.
What is it? Has some new disaster caught up with the two neighbouring
cities?

Messenger.
Hippolytus is no more. That is one way to put it. He is still looking
at the light, but the scales are tipping.

Theseus.
Who did it? Did somebody come at him out of hatred, somebody whose wife
he had disgraced by force, the way he did his father's?

Messenger.
His own chariot destroyed him. That, and the curses from your mouth,
the ones you prayed to your father, the ruler of the sea, against your
son.

Theseus.
Gods. Poseidon. So you really are my father: you heard my prayers. How
did he die? Tell me. How did the club of Justice come down on the man
who disgraced me?

Messenger.
We were near the shore where the waves come in, combing out the
horses' manes with brushes, and crying while we did it, because a
messenger had come and told us that Hippolytus would never set foot in
this land again; you had sentenced him to a miserable exile. Then he
came down to the beach to us himself, singing the same tearful song,
and behind him walked an enormous crowd of friends and young men his
own age. After a while he stopped groaning and said, "Why am I raving
like this? My father's orders have to be obeyed. Harness the horses to
the chariot, slaves. This city is not mine any more." From then on
every man was hurrying, and faster than you could say it we had the
mares harnessed and standing beside the master. He snatched the reins
off the rail and fitted his feet into the footholds, boots and all.
And first he spread out his hands and said to the gods, "Zeus, let me
stop living if I am a bad man. And let my father come to understand
how he is dishonouring me, whether I am dead by then or still looking
at the light." With that he took the goad in his hands and laid it on
all the mares together, and we, his servants, followed close to the
chariot, by the horses' heads, along the road that runs straight
to Argos and Epidaurus.

When we struck into empty country, where there is a stretch of shore
beyond the border of this land, lying toward the Saronic gulf, a sound
came out of the ground there, like the thunder of Zeus, a deep roar,
horrible to hear. The horses put their heads straight up and their
ears to the sky, and a raw panic went through us: where could that
noise be coming from? We looked toward the shore where the surf
pounds, and we saw a wave, unholy, propped against the sky, so high
that it took the cliffs of Sciron out of my sight. It hid the Isthmus
and the rock of Asclepius. Then it swelled up, foam boiling round it,
the sea's breath blowing through it, and came on toward the shore
where the four-horse chariot was standing. And with the surge itself,
with the third wave, the great one, the sea put out a bull on the
sand: a savage monster. The whole land filled with its bellowing and
roared back, a horrible sound, and to us who were watching it was a
sight too big for our eyes to hold.

At once a terrible fear fell on the mares. The master, who had lived
with horses and their ways all his life, grabbed the reins in both
hands and pulled, the way a sailor pulls an oar, throwing his body
back against the straps. But the mares bit down on the fire-forged
bits with their jaws and carried him off by force, not turning for the
helmsman's hand or the harness or the jointed car. And if he took the
steering and pointed their run toward the soft ground, the bull showed
itself in front of them, to turn them back, maddening the four-horse
team with terror. And if they rushed toward the rocks, out of their
minds, it came up close, in silence, keeping pace with the chariot
rail, until it tripped them and threw them over, driving the rim of
the wheel against a stone. Everything went together in one heap. The
hubs of the wheels jumped into the air, and the pins out of the axles.
And he himself, poor man, tangled in the reins, was dragged along,
tied in a knot he could not undo, his dear head pounded against the
rocks, his flesh breaking, and he was shouting things that were
terrible to hear: "Stop, you that I fed at my own mangers. Do not wipe
me out. Oh, my father's wretched curse. Who is here who wants to save
the best of men?" Many of us wanted to, but we were left behind, too
slow. Then somehow, I do not know how, he came loose from the cut
leather straps and fell, with a little life still breathing in him.
And the horses vanished, and that cursed monster of a bull with them,
somewhere in the rocky ground, I do not know where.

I am a slave of your house, my lord. But there is one thing I will
never be able to do, and that is believe that your son is a bad man.
Not if the whole race of women hanged itself, and somebody filled
every pine tree on Mount Ida with writing. I know he is good.

Chorus.
Aiai. The disaster has come to pass, new evils on top of the old, and
there is no escape from fate and from what has to be.

Theseus.
Out of hatred for the man this has happened to, I was glad of your
story. But now, out of respect for the gods, and for him, because he
is my son, I take no pleasure in these evils and no pain either.

Messenger.
Then what? Should we bring him here, or what do we do with the poor
man to please your mind? Think about it. If you take my advice, you
will not be cruel to your son in his misfortune.

Theseus.
Bring him here. I want to see him with my own eyes, the man who denied
he had defiled my bed, and prove him wrong with words and with what
the gods have done to him.

Chorus.
	You lead the unbending mind of gods and of mortals,
	Cypris, and with you
	goes the one with the painted wings, who wraps them round his prey
	on the quickest feather there is.
	He flies over the earth and over the loud salt sea,
	and Love casts his spell on whatever maddened heart
	he swoops down on, winged, shining gold:
	on the nature of the cubs in the mountains, and the creatures of the deep,
	and everything the earth feeds,
	and everything the blazing sun looks down on,
	and men. Over all of them the royal honour,
	Cypris, is yours alone. You rule them.

(Artemis appears above the house.)

Artemis.
	You, well-born son of Aegeus, I command you
	to listen.
	It is Leto's daughter, Artemis, who is speaking to you.
	Theseus, poor man, why do you take pleasure in this,
	when you have killed your son against everything holy,
	persuaded by your wife's lying words
	about a thing you never saw? The ruin you took on is plain enough to see.
	Why don't you hide your body in the pit under the earth
	for shame,
	or change your life for a bird's and go up into the air
	and lift your foot out of this pain?
	Because among good men there is no
	share of life you can call yours any more.
	Listen, Theseus, to how your troubles stand.
	And yet I will get nothing done by it. I will only hurt you.
	But this is what I came for: to show that your son's mind
	was just, so that he can die with a good name,
	and to show your wife's frenzy, or, in a way,
	her nobility. She was stung by the goads of the goddess
	most hateful to those of us who take our pleasure in staying virgin,
	and she fell in love with your son.
	She tried to beat Cypris with her judgement,
	and against her will she was destroyed by her nurse's scheming.
	The nurse bound your son by oaths, and told him the sickness.
	He, being the just man he is, did not go along with her words,
	and even when you abused him he would not
	tear up the pledge of his oath. He is a god-fearing man.
	And she, afraid she would be found out and put to the test,
	wrote a lying letter and destroyed your son
	by trickery. And she convinced you all the same.

Theseus.
Oh.

Artemis.
Does the story bite, Theseus? Keep still. Hear the rest, and you will
have more to groan about. You know you had three curses from your
father, with a sure result? You took one of them, you utter villain,
and used it on your own son, when you could have used it on some enemy.
Your father the sea-god meant well by you, and he gave what he had to
give, since he had promised. But you have shown yourself a bad man, in
his eyes and in mine. You did not wait for proof or for the voice of
the seers. You did not put it to the test. You did not give it a long
time's thought. Faster than you should have, you let the curse go at
your son, and you killed him.

Theseus.
Lady, let me die.

Artemis.
What you did is terrible. But even so, you can still be forgiven for
it. Cypris wanted this to happen; she was filling up her anger. And
this is the law among the gods: none of us chooses to stand in the way
of another god's will when that god is set on something. We always
stand aside. Because, you can be sure of this, if I were not afraid of
Zeus I would never have come to such shame as to let the man I loved
best of all mortals die. As for your fault: not knowing, first of all,
clears you of wickedness. And then your wife, by dying, used up every
chance of testing her words, and so convinced your mind. These evils
have broken over you most of all, but there is grief in it for me too.
The gods take no pleasure when the god-fearing die. The bad, though,
we destroy, children and house and all.

(Hippolytus is carried in by his huntsmen, broken, and barely alive.)

Chorus.
	And here he comes, the poor man,
	his young flesh and his fair head
	torn to pieces. Oh, the pain of this house.
	What a double grief has come down on these halls,
	sent by the gods, and gripping them.

Hippolytus.
	Aiai, aiai.
	I am wretched. An unjust father's
	unjust curse has torn me apart.
	I am destroyed, poor man. Oh, oh.
	Pains shoot through my head,
	a spasm jumps through my brain.
	Stop. Let me rest my body. It has given up.
	E, e.
	Hateful team of horses, that I fed
	with my own hand,
	you have wrecked me, you have killed me.
	Pheu, pheu. By the gods, slaves, gently.
	Touch my torn skin gently with your hands.
	Who is standing there on my right side?
	Lift me carefully. Pull me along evenly,
	the man the god hates, cursed
	by his father's mistakes. Zeus, Zeus, do you see this?
	This is me, the holy one, the worshipper of gods.
	This is me, who outdid everyone at keeping himself in hand,
	going down to Hades with my eyes open, my life
	destroyed from the top down. All my labours of piety
	toward mankind
	were for nothing.

Hippolytus.
	Aiai, aiai.
	And now the pain, the pain comes over me again.
	Let go of me, you poor men.
	Let death come to me now, death the Healer.
	Finish me, finish off the man
	the god hates. I long for a two-edged spear
	to cut me in pieces
	and lay my life down to sleep.
	Oh, my father's wretched curse.
	The evil of old blood-stained kinsmen,
	of ancestors long ago, is coming out past its boundary
	and does not wait,
	and it has come for me. Why, when I am
	guilty of none of it?
	Io moi moi.
	What can I say? How can I get my life free
	of this pain that has no pity? If only
	the black night-necessity of Hades
	would lay me down to sleep, the man the god hates.

Artemis.
Poor man. What a disaster you have been yoked to. It was the nobility
of your mind that destroyed you.

Hippolytus.
Ah. A breath of divine fragrance. Even in this agony I felt you, and my
body grew lighter. The goddess Artemis is here in this place.

Artemis.
Poor man, she is. The dearest of the gods to you.

Hippolytus.
Do you see me, lady, the state I am in, the wretch I am?

Artemis.
I see you. But it is not lawful for tears to fall from my eyes.

Hippolytus.
You have no huntsman now, and no servant.

Artemis.
No. But you are dear to me even as you die.

Hippolytus.
No one to drive your horses, or guard your statues.

Artemis.
It was Cypris who plotted this. She stops at nothing.

Hippolytus.
Oh. Now I understand which god destroyed me.

Artemis.
She blamed you for the honour you did not pay her, and she hated you
for keeping yourself in hand.

Hippolytus.
Three of us. She destroyed three of us, Cypris. I see it now.

Artemis.
Your father, and you, and third, his wife.

Hippolytus.
Then I groan for my father's bad luck as well as mine.

Artemis.
He was deceived by a goddess's design.

Hippolytus.
Father, how wretched this disaster has made you.

Theseus.
I am finished, child. There is no joy left in life for me.

Hippolytus.
I grieve for you more than for myself, for the mistake you made.

Theseus.
If only I could be the corpse, child, instead of you.

Hippolytus.
Bitter gifts, the gifts of your father Poseidon.

Theseus.
I wish they had never come into my mouth.

Hippolytus.
Why? You would have killed me anyway, as angry as you were then.

Theseus.
Yes. The gods had knocked my judgement off its feet.

Hippolytus.
Pheu. If only the race of mortals could lay a curse on the gods.

Artemis.
Let it be. Not even under the darkness of the earth will the angry
will of the goddess Cypris come down on your body and go unavenged.
You are owed that, for your piety and your good mind. I will take my
revenge on her with my own hand: whichever mortal man she loves best
of all, I will punish him with these arrows, which nothing escapes.
And to you, poor suffering man, in return for these evils, I will give
the greatest honours in the city of Troezen. Girls not yet yoked in
marriage will cut their hair for you before their weddings, and
through the long ages you will harvest the deepest grief of their
tears. The girls will always make you their care and put you in their
songs, and Phaedra's love for you will not fall nameless and be passed
over in silence.

And you, child of old Aegeus, take your son in your arms and hold him
close. You destroyed him without meaning to. When the gods arrange it,
it is natural for human beings to go wrong. And you, Hippolytus, I
advise you not to hate your father. You had a fate, and this is how it
broke you. Goodbye. It is not lawful for me to look at the dead, or to
stain my eye with the last breaths of the dying. And I see you are
already close to that.

(Artemis is gone from above the house.)

Hippolytus.
Goodbye to you too. Go, blessed virgin. You leave a long friendship
easily. I end the quarrel with my father, since you want it. Before
now I always obeyed what you said. Aiai. The darkness is reaching my
eyes already. Take hold of me, father, and hold my body straight.

Theseus.
Oh, child, what are you doing to me, the man the god hates?

Hippolytus.
I am gone. I can see the gates of the dead.

Theseus.
And you leave my hand unclean?

Hippolytus.
No. I set you free of this killing.

Theseus.
What are you saying? You let me go free of your blood?

Hippolytus.
I call Artemis to witness it, Artemis who tames with the bow.

Theseus.
Dearest boy. How noble you show yourself to your father.

Hippolytus.
Pray for legitimate sons like this one.

Theseus.
Oh, your mind, so god-fearing and so good.

Hippolytus.
Goodbye to you too. Goodbye, father, many times over.

Theseus.
Don't desert me now, child. Hold on.

Hippolytus.
My holding on is done. I am gone, father. Cover my face with my robes,
quickly.

Theseus.
Glorious Athens, and the land that Pallas marked out for her own, what
a man you will have lost. Oh, I am wretched. How many times, Cypris, I
will remember what you have done.

Chorus.
	This grief has come to every citizen alike,
	and nobody expected it.
	There will be a beating of many tears.
	When the great fall, the news of it
	deserves grief, and takes a stronger hold.
