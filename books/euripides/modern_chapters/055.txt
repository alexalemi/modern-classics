Rhesus
(Part 1 of 2)

(The Trojan camp on the plain in front of Troy, in the middle of the
night. Hector is asleep on a bed of leaves. The sentries of the night
watch come in quietly and stand over him.)

Chorus.
	Go to Hector's bed.
	Which of the king's shield-bearers is awake,
	or of the men in armour?
	Let him take the news we bring,
	we who keep the fourth watch of the night
	on guard in front of the whole army.
	Lift your head. Prop it on your elbow.
	Open that fierce eye of yours.
	Get up off the leaves you are lying on,
	Hector. It is time to listen.

Hector.
	Who is that? A friend's voice? Who is the man?
	What is the password? Speak.
	Who is coming near our beds
	out of the night? You have to tell me.

Chorus.
	Sentries of the army.

Hector.
	What is this noise you bring?

Chorus.
	Steady.

Hector.
	I am steady.
	Is there an ambush out there in the dark?

Chorus.
	There is not.

Hector.
	Then why leave your post
	and stir the army up,
	unless you have some news out of the night?
	Do you not know that we lie down
	close to the Argive spears,
	in full armour, to sleep?

Chorus.
	Arm your hand. Hector,
	go to the beds of the allies,
	tell them to lift the spear. Wake them up.
	Send friends to your own company.
	Get the bits into the horses' mouths.
	Who will go to the son of Panthus,
	or to Europa's son, the captain of the Lycians?
	Where are the men who watch the sacrifices?
	Where are the leaders of the light-armed troops,
	and the Phrygian bowmen?
	String the horn-tipped bows.

Hector.
	Some of your news is frightening to hear
	and some of it is meant to steady me, and none of it is clear.
	Has Pan, the son of Cronus, cracked
	his whip and set you shaking? You leave the watch
	and stir the army up. What are you shouting?
	What am I to say your news is? You have said a great deal
	and shown me nothing plainly.

Chorus.
	The Argive army is burning fires,
	Hector, all through the dark,
	and the ships' moorings are lit up with torches.
	The whole army has gone to Agamemnon's tent
	in the night, in an uproar,
	wanting some new
	word. That sea-going army has never
	been frightened like this before.
	And I, suspecting what is coming,
	came to you as a messenger,
	so you can never lay any blame on me.

Hector.
You have come at the right moment, even if the news is frightening. The
men mean to slip away from this land by night, under oars, without my
seeing them. Those fires burning out there in the dark please me. You
god, whoever you are, you robbed me in the middle of my luck. You took
the meat out of a lion's mouth, before this spear had swept the whole
Argive army away in one stroke. If the sun's bright lamps had not gone
out on me, I would never have held my winning spear back until I had
burned their ships and gone from tent to tent killing Achaeans with
this hand that has killed so many. I was ready to throw the spear that
night and use the good luck a god had sent me. But my clever prophets,
the men who know the will of heaven, talked me into waiting for
daylight, and then leaving not one Achaean alive on dry land. Well, the
enemy is not waiting for my sacrificers' advice. A runaway is strong in
the dark. So the word has to go through the army at once: pick up your
weapons, stop sleeping. Then when one of them jumps aboard a ship he
will get a gash across the back and splash the boarding ladders with
blood, and others will be caught in the ropes and tied up and learn to
farm the fields of Phrygia.

Chorus.
Hector, you are moving fast, before you know what is happening. We do
not know for certain that the men are running.

Hector.
What other reason would the Argive army have to burn fires?

Chorus.
I do not know. But my mind is very suspicious of it.

Hector.
If this frightens you, then you may as well be frightened of anything.

Chorus.
The enemy never lit that much light before.

Hector.
And they were never beaten and routed as shamefully before.

Chorus.
You did that. Now look at what is left to do.

Hector.
There is one simple thing to say about an enemy: arm.

Chorus.
Look, here comes Aeneas, and in a great hurry, with something new to
tell his friends.

Aeneas.
Hector, why have the sentries come through the camp at night in a
panic, to your bed? Why the conference in the dark, and why is the army
on the move?

Hector.
Aeneas, get your body into your armour.

Aeneas.
What is it? Is there word of some hidden trick from the enemy, set up
in the night?

Hector.
The men are running. They are going aboard their ships.

Aeneas.
What solid proof of that can you give me?

Hector.
They are burning torches all night long. I do not think they will wait
for tomorrow. They have lit fires on their well-benched ships and they
mean to run for home and leave this country.

Aeneas.
And you are arming for this. To do what?

Hector.
I will catch them with my spear as they run and jump aboard, and I will
come down on them hard. It would be shameful for us, and worse than
shameful, it would be cowardice, to let our enemies get away without a
fight when a god is handing them over, after all the harm they have
done us.

Aeneas.
I wish you were as good at planning as you are with your hands. But no
one man is born knowing everything. Each of us is given his own gift:
yours is fighting, and another man's is thinking well. You heard that
the Achaeans are burning torches, you were lifted right off the ground
by it, and now you mean to take the army over the ditches in the dead
of the night. Suppose you get across that deep hollow trench and find
the enemy is not running from this land at all but standing and looking
straight at your spear. If you lose, you will never get back. How does
an army get through the stakes in a rout? How do the chariot drivers
cross the causeways without smashing the axle-boxes of their wheels?
And if you win, you still have Peleus' son sitting fresh behind them,
and he will not let you throw fire on the ships or snatch the Achaeans
away, whatever you imagine. The man burns, and his arm is a tower. No.
Let the army sleep quietly beside their shields and rest from the
killing work. My advice is to send a spy in against the enemy, whoever
is willing to go. If they really are taking to flight, we march out and
fall on the Argive army. If those fires are drawing us into a trap, the
spy will tell us what the enemy has built, and we can decide then. That
is my opinion, my lord.

Chorus.
	That is what we think. Change your mind and think it too.
	I have no love for a general's power when it slips.
	What could be better
	than a fast runner going down close to the ships
	to see what it is
	that keeps the enemy burning fires in front of their moorings?

Hector.
You win, since it pleases everyone. Go and settle the allies down. The
army may start moving when it hears there are meetings in the night. I
will send a man to spy on the enemy. If we find out they have some
scheme, you will hear the whole of it and be there to know what was
said. But if they are pulling out and starting to run, keep your ear
open for the trumpet, because I will not be waiting. I will be at the
hauling-ground where the ships lie, tonight, on top of the Argive army.

Aeneas.
Send him quickly. Your thinking is safe now. And when the moment comes,
you will see me standing beside you and holding.

Hector.
Which of the Trojans here in council is willing to go and spy on the
Argive ships? Who will do this country that service? Who says he will?
I cannot do everything myself for my father's city and for our allies.

Dolon.
I will throw myself into this danger for the country. I will go and spy
on the Argive ships, learn every plan the Achaeans have, and come back.
Those are my terms for taking the work on.

Hector.
Your name is a word for a trick, Dolon, and it fits you, and so does
your love for this city. Your father's house had a name before this.
Now you have made it twice the name it was.

Dolon.
Then a man has to work. But when he works he ought to carry off a wage
worth having. Put a profit on top of any job and it doubles the good
will in it.

Hector.
Yes. That is fair, and I do not say otherwise. Name your wage. Anything
but my kingship.

Dolon.
I do not want your kingship, with a city on its back.

Hector.
Then marry one of Priam's daughters and become one of the family.

Dolon.
I do not want to marry above myself either.

Hector.
There is gold, if that is the prize you will ask for.

Dolon.
I have gold at home. I am not short of a living.

Hector.
Then what do you want out of everything Troy has stored up?

Dolon.
Promise me a gift for when you have taken the Achaeans.

Hector.
I will give it. Ask for anyone except the commanders of the fleet.

Dolon.
Kill them. I am not asking you to keep your hand off Menelaus.

Hector.
It is not the son of Oileus you are asking me for?

Dolon.
Hands that have been brought up well are no good at farming.

Hector.
Then which of the Achaeans do you want alive, to hold for ransom?

Dolon.
I told you before. There is gold in my house.

Hector.
Well then, you will be there yourself and take your pick of the
plunder.

Dolon.
Nail the plunder up for the gods on their temples.

Hector.
Then what greater prize than any of these are you going to ask me for?

Dolon.
The horses of Achilles. A man throwing his life onto the dice a god
shakes should be working for something worth having.

Hector.
Then we are rivals, because I am in love with those horses too. They
are immortal, born from immortals, and they carry the raging son of
Peleus. Poseidon, the lord of the sea, broke them in and gave them to
Peleus, so the story goes. All the same, I urged you into this and I
will not cheat you. I will give them to you: Achilles' team, the finest
thing your house will ever hold.

Dolon.
Thank you. And taking it, I say I am taking the finest gift any
Phrygian ever had for his courage. You have no reason to be jealous.
There are ten thousand other things to please you, being the best man
in this land.

Chorus.
	The contest is great, and what you plan to win is great.
	You will be a blessed man if you get it.
	This work will bring you glory.
	It is a great thing, too, to marry into a king's house.
	Let Justice keep her eye on what comes from the gods.
	What men can give looks to be yours already.

Dolon.
I will go. First to my house and my own hearth, and I will put the
right gear on my body, and from there I will run for the Argive ships.

Chorus.
And what will you put on instead of the clothes you have?

Dolon.
Something that suits the job and a thief's footsteps.

Chorus.
A clever man is the one to learn something clever from. Tell us: what
is the harness going to be?

Dolon.
I will fasten a wolf's skin over my back and put the animal's open jaws
over my head. I will fit its front feet to my hands and its legs to my
legs, and I will copy a wolf's four-footed walk, so that the enemy
cannot make me out, as I come up to the ditches and the barricades in
front of the ships. And when I reach empty ground I will go on two
feet. That is how the trick is put together.

Chorus.
Then may Hermes, the son of Maia, send you there and back safely, since
he is the lord of thieves. You know the job. All you need now is luck.

Dolon.
I will come through safely. And I will kill Odysseus and bring you his
head. You will have a clear token, and you will say that Dolon went to
the Argive ships. Or the son of Tydeus. My hand will not be clean of
blood when I come home, and I will be home before the light reaches the
ground.

(Dolon goes out into the dark, toward the Greek camp.)

Chorus.
	Lord of Thymbra, lord of Delos,
	you who walk in your temple in Lycia,
	Apollo, holy head, come with your bow,
	come tonight,
	be the man's rescue and the guide
	who leads him, and take the side of the sons of Dardanus,
	you who can do anything, you who built
	the old walls of Troy.

Chorus.
	Let him reach the moorings, let him come
	and see the Greek army with his own eyes,
	and let him turn back again to the altars
	of his father's house in Troy.
	And one day let him ride behind the horses of Phthia,
	when our master has broken the Achaean war,
	the horses the sea god gave
	to Peleus, the son of Aeacus.

Chorus.
	He alone had the heart, for his house and his land,
	to go down and look at the moorings. I admire
	his nerve. Good men are rare
	when the sun goes out
	over the water
	and the city
	is rolling. There is a brave man among the Phrygians. There is.
	There is daring in the
	spear here. Where are the Mysians
	who think our alliance is worth nothing?

Chorus.
	Which of the Achaeans will the killer on the ground
	stab in his hut, going on four feet
	in the shape of an animal
	of the earth? Let him take Menelaus,
	and kill Agamemnon and carry
	the head
	to Helen's hands, a grief for the marriage she made,
	the man who came against my city,
	against the land of Troy,
	with an army of a thousand ships.

(A herdsman comes down from the slopes of Mount Ida.)

Shepherd.
My lord, I hope that for the rest of my life I bring my masters news
like the news I am bringing you now.

Hector.
There is a great deal of thickheadedness in country people. Here you
are, coming to men in armour with news about sheep, and this is not the
place for it. Do you not know where my house is, or my father's throne?
That is where you should have gone shouting about how well your flocks
are doing.

Messenger.
We herdsmen are thick. I will not argue about that. But I have good
news for you all the same.

Hector.
Stop telling me how things are around the sheepfold. We have fighting
in front of us and spears in our hands.

Messenger.
Those are exactly the things I came to tell you about. A man is on his
way here at the head of an army beyond counting, a friend to you and an
ally to this land.

Hector.
And what country did he leave empty behind him?

Messenger.
Thrace. His father is called Strymon.

Hector.
Are you telling me Rhesus is setting foot in Troy?

Messenger.
You have it. And you have taken half my speech off me.

Hector.
And how does he come to be up in the pastures of Ida, off the broad
wagon road across the plain?

Messenger.
I do not know exactly, though I can guess. It is no light thing to
bring an army in at night, when you have heard the plains are packed
with enemy troops. He gave us country people a fright, we who live on
the rock of Ida, where the first hearth in this land was rooted, coming
through the wood in the dark with the wild animals in it. The Thracian
army came pouring on with a huge noise, and we were knocked out of our
senses and drove the flocks up to the high ground, in case some of the
Argives were coming to lift the cattle and strip your steadings.
Then the sound that reached our ears was not Greek, and our fear went
away. I went and stopped the men who were scouting the road ahead of
their king, and asked them in Thracian: who is the general, and whose
son is he called, this man coming to the city as an ally to the sons of
Priam? And when I had heard everything I wanted to know, I stood still.
And I could see Rhesus, standing in his chariot behind Thracian horses,
like a god. There was a plate of gold holding the yoke on the necks of
the colts, and the colts were whiter than snow. On his shoulders the
light shield flashed with figures welded into it in gold. A bronze
Gorgon, like the one on the goddess's shield, was strapped to the
horses' foreheads, and it rang out fear with all its bells. As for the
size of that army, I could not put a number on it however I counted; it
was too much to take in with the eye. Many horsemen, many companies of
shield-men, many archers with their shafts, and a great crowd of
light-armed men running along with them, all of them in Thracian gear.
That is the man who has come to Troy as an ally. Peleus' son will not
be able to get away from him, either by running or by standing up to
him with the spear.

Chorus.
When the gods are standing steady on the citizens' side, the whole
thing runs downhill toward the good.

Hector.
Now that my spear is winning and Zeus is on our side, I will find
plenty of friends. But we have no need of any of them, the ones who did
not work beside us long ago, when Ares was blowing hard and tearing
this country's sails to pieces. Rhesus has shown what kind of friend he
was to Troy. He is coming to the feast, and he was not out with the
hunters who took the game, and he never wore out a spear beside us.

Chorus.
You are right to think little of them, and right to blame friends like
that. But take the ones who are willing to help the city.

Hector.
We who have kept Troy safe all this time are enough.

Chorus.
Are you so sure you have already caught the enemy?

Hector.
I am sure. The god's light tomorrow will show it.

Chorus.
Watch what is coming. A god turns a great many things over.

Hector.
I hate a man who comes running to help his friends late. Well then:
since he has come, let him come to our table not as an ally but as a
guest, one stranger among strangers. As far as he is concerned, the
gratitude of Priam's sons is gone.

Chorus.
My lord, pushing allies away earns ill will.

Messenger.
The sight of him alone would frighten the enemy.

Hector.
Your advice is good, and you see the moment. So let Rhesus in his
golden armour come and join the allies of this land, on the strength of
the messenger's report.

(The herdsman goes back up toward the hills. Trumpets sound out on the
plain as the Thracian army comes in.)

Chorus.
	Let Adrasteia, the daughter of Zeus,
	who punishes a boast, keep the ill will off my mouth.
	I am going to say all of it,
	everything my heart loves to say.
	You have come, son of the river,
	you have come, you are at the courtyard of the god of friendship,
	and you are welcome, because at last
	your mother, the Muse of Pieria, and the river
	with the beautiful bridges are bringing you here.

Chorus.
	Strymon, who whirled once
	as running water through the clean
	folds of the singing Muse
	and planted your young life in her.
	You are Zeus to me, Zeus of the daylight,
	coming here driving your dappled colts.
	Now, my country, my Phrygia,
	now with a god's help you can say the name
	of Zeus who sets us free.

Chorus.
	Will old Troy ever again
	spend a whole day
	in the drinking, in the companies
	of love, with the harps
	and the cups
	going round in wandering contests,
	while the sons of Atreus go over the sea
	to Sparta,
	away from the shore of Troy?
	Friend, I wish you would do this for me
	with your hand and your spear, and then go home.

Chorus.
	Come, show yourself, hold that shield
	heavy with gold up in the face
	of Peleus' son, tilt it sideways
	along the chariot's split rail, work
	the colts up, shake
	the double-pointed javelin. Nobody who stands
	against you will ever dance again
	on the floor of Argive Hera's ground.
	No. This land will carry him
	as the dearest weight it ever took,
	killed by a Thracian death.

Chorus.
	Io, io, great king.
	Thrace, what a fine
	cub you raised, a ruler to look at.
	Look at the strength of that body bound in gold,
	listen to the boasting of the bells
	clattering at the shield-straps.
	A god, Troy, a god. Ares himself,
	the colt of Strymon and of the singing
	Muse, has come and is breathing on you.

Rhesus.
Greetings, brave son of a brave father, king of this land, Hector. It
is a long-delayed day, and I greet you on it. I am glad to see you
winning, and camped up against the enemy's towers. I am here to help
you dig their walls down and burn their ships.

Hector.
Son of the singing mother, one of the Muses, and of Strymon, the
Thracian river: I like to say what is true, always, and I am not a
double man. You should have come long ago, long ago, and taken your
share of this country's work, and not left Troy, as far as you were
concerned, to fall to the enemy spear under the Argives. You cannot say
that nobody called you, and that is why you never came to your friends,
never defended them, never even turned your head. What herald, what
council of Phrygian elders did not go to you and beg you to defend the
city? What set of gifts did we not send you? And you, a man of our own
blood, a barbarian yourself, drank us away to the Greeks: your own
barbarians, toasted off for your share of the wine. And it was this arm
that took you out of a small kingship and made you the great lord of
Thrace, when I went at the best men of Thrace face to face around
Pangaeum and the country of the Paeonians and broke their shields, and
enslaved their people and handed them to you. You kicked that great
favour aside, and now you come running to help your friends late, when
they are sick. And men with no blood tie to us at all have been here a
long time. Some of them lie fallen in heaped-up graves, which is no
small proof of loyalty to a city. Others stand it out in their armour
beside their horses and chariots, holding on through the cold blast and
the god's thirsty fire, and not lying on couches draining cup after cup
the way you do. That is my charge against you, and I am saying it to
your face, so you know Hector is a free man.

Rhesus.
I am the same kind of man myself. I cut a straight road through what I
say, and I am not a double man either. Being away from this country
hurt me more than it hurt you. It wore at my liver and I could hardly
carry it. But the land on my border, the Scythian people, made war on
me just as I was setting out to cross over to Troy. I had already
reached the shores of the Friendly Sea, to ferry the Thracian army
across. That was where Scythian blood was poured out on the ground by
my spear, with Thracian blood mixed into it. That was the accident that
kept me from reaching the plain of Troy and coming to you as an ally.
And when I had destroyed them, and taken their children as hostages,
and set the yearly tribute they must bring to my house, then I came. I
crossed the mouth of the sea in ships and did the rest of it on foot,
across the boundaries of countries. Not the way you jeer at me, tipping
back my cupfuls, not sleeping in houses stuffed with gold, but out in
the frozen squalls that batter the Thracian sea and press down on the
Paeonians, and I know what it is to lie awake in nothing but this
cloak. I came late, but I came at the right moment. You have been
fighting for ten years now and finishing nothing, throwing the dice
against the Argives day after day and losing. One day's light will be
enough for me. I will break their towers, fall on their moorings, and
kill the Achaeans. The next day I will go home from Troy, having cut
your labour short. Let none of you lift a shield. I will take the
Achaeans who boast so loudly and break them with my spear, late as I
have come.

Chorus.
	Io, io.
	What you say is dear to us, and you are dear, and you come from Zeus.
	Only let Zeus on high be willing
	to keep the unbeatable ill will away from your words.
	The seafaring spear out of Argos
	never brought,
	not before and not now, a man greater than you. How could
	Achilles stand up to your spear,
	how could Ajax?
	If only I could see the day, my lord,
	when you make them pay
	with that hand of yours that kills so many, and its lance.

Rhesus.
That is the sort of thing I am ready to do for you, to make up for the
long time I was away. I say it with Adrasteia's leave. Once we have set
this city free of its enemies, and you have picked out the first fruits
for the gods, I want to march with you against the country of the
Argives and go through the whole of Greece and lay it waste with the
spear, so they can learn in their turn what it is to be treated badly.

Hector.
If I could be rid of the trouble in front of me and run this city the
way it was, safe as it used to be, I would owe the gods a great deal of
thanks. But Argos, and the pasture lands of Greece: those are not so
easy to sack with the spear as you say.

Rhesus.
Do they not say the best of the Greeks came here?

Hector.
And we do not think little of them. We have as much as we can drive.

Rhesus.
Then when we have killed these men, is the whole job not done?

Hector.
Do not let go of what is close and stare at what is far away.

Rhesus.
It seems to be enough for you to be hurt and never hurt back.

Hector.
I rule a great kingdom, even standing here. Now: the left wing, or the
right, or in the middle among the allies. You can plant your shield
wherever you like and set your army up.

Rhesus.
I want to fight the enemy alone, Hector. But if you think it is
shameful, after all your long work before this, to have no hand in
burning the ships' sterns, then post me facing Achilles and his troops.

Hector.
You cannot set that raging spear of yours against that man.

Rhesus.
But the word was that he sailed to Troy.

Hector.
He sailed, and he is here. But he is in a rage with the commanders and
does not lift a spear with them.

Rhesus.
Then who has the next name after him in their army?

Hector.
Ajax seems to me to fall short of him in nothing, and neither does the
son of Tydeus. And there is Odysseus, the smoothest piece of work
alive, bold enough in spirit, and the man who has done more outrage to
this country than anyone. He came into Athena's precinct by night,
stole her image and carried it off to the Argive ships. Another time he
got inside our walls dressed as a beggar collecting for a god, cursing
the Argives out loud, and he had been sent to Troy as a spy. Then he
killed the guards and the men beside the gates and got out again. He is
always turning up in an ambush around the altar of Apollo of Thymbra,
close to the town. He is a cunning piece of trouble to wrestle with.

Rhesus.
No brave man thinks it is right to kill an enemy in secret. He goes
straight at him. This man you say sits in a thief's hiding place
building schemes: I will take him alive, drive a spike up his backbone
at the way out of the gates, and stand him there as a feast for the
vultures on the wing. He is a pirate and a robber of the gods' shrines,
and that is the death he ought to die.

Hector.
Now make your camp, since it is night. I will show you myself where
your army has to sleep, apart from the line we have drawn up. Our
password is Apollo, if we should need one. Hear it and remember it, and
pass it on to the Thracian army. And you sentries must go out ahead of
the ranks and keep an alert watch and receive Dolon, our spy on the
ships. If he is safe, he must be getting close to the Trojan camp by
now.
