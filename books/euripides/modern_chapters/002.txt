Alcestis
(Part 3 of 3)

Servant.
Alive? Do you not know what has happened in this house?

Heracles.
I do, unless your master lied to me about something.

Servant.
He is too kind to his guests. Far too kind.

Heracles.
Was I supposed to go without my dinner because some outsider died?

Servant.
An outsider. Oh yes. She was a complete outsider, she was.

Heracles.
Is there some trouble here that he did not tell me about?

Servant.
Go and enjoy yourself. Our masters' troubles are our business.

Heracles.
That does not sound like the start of a stranger's grief.

Servant.
No. If it were, watching you carouse would not have hurt me the way
it did.

Heracles.
Have my hosts done something terrible to me, then?

Servant.
You did not come at the right time for this house to take you in. We are
in mourning. You can see the clothes, black, and the shorn hair.

Heracles.
Who is it that is dead? One of the children gone? Or his old father?

Servant.
It is the wife of Admetus who is dead, stranger.

Heracles.
What did you say? And after that you still sat me down to dinner?

Servant.
He was ashamed to turn you away from his door.

Heracles.
Poor man. What a wife he has lost.

Servant.
We are all lost, not just her.

Heracles.
But I noticed. I saw it: his eyes streaming, his hair cut short, his
face. But he talked me round. He said it was an outsider's funeral he
was taking to the grave. So I forced myself through these gates, and
I sat drinking in the house of a man who loves his guests, while he
was in that state. And then I went on carousing, with garlands piled
on my head? But you. To say nothing, with a disaster like this lying
on the house. Where is he burying her? Where do I go to find her?

Servant.
Straight along the road that leads to Larissa. You will see the tomb
of cut stone, just outside the town.

Heracles.
Heart of mine, you have put up with so much, and you, my hand: now
show what kind of son Alcmene of Tiryns, the daughter of Electryon,
bore to Zeus. I have to save this woman who has just died. I have to
set Alcestis back in this house and pay Admetus back for what he did
for me. I will go and watch for Death, the black-robed lord of the
dead, and I think I will find him near the tomb, drinking the blood
of the offerings. If I rush him out of hiding and get hold of him and
lock my arms round him in a ring, there is nobody who will get him
out of it, however his ribs ache, until he lets the woman go to me.
And if I miss my quarry, and he does not come to the clotted blood,
I will go down to the sunless house of Persephone and her Lord below,
and I will ask them for her. I am confident I will bring Alcestis up,
and put her into the hands of my host, who took me into his house and
did not drive me away, though he had been struck a heavy blow. He hid
it, because he is a noble man, out of respect for me. Who in Thessaly
loves his guests more than this man does? Who in all Greece? He will
never say that he did a good turn to a bad man, noble as he is.

(Heracles goes off toward the tomb, and the Servant goes into the
house. Admetus comes back from the burial, with the Chorus.)

Admetus.
	Io. Hateful,
	the way in. Hateful, the sight of a house
	with the wife gone. Io moi moi. Ai ai.
	Where can I go? Where can I stand? What can I say? What not say?
	How can I die?
	The mother who bore me bore me under a heavy fate.
	I envy the dead. I want to be them.
	I long to live in those houses of theirs.
	I take no joy in looking at the light,
	or in setting my foot on the earth.
	That was the hostage Death took from me
	and handed to Hades.

Chorus.
	Go forward, go on. Go into the shelter of the house.

Admetus.
	Aiai.

Chorus.
	You have suffered things worth crying over.

Admetus.
	E, e.

Chorus.
	You have walked through pain, I know it well . . .

Admetus.
	Pheu, pheu.

Chorus.
	but you do nothing for the woman below.

Admetus.
	Io moi moi.

Chorus.
	Never to see your dear wife's face
	in front of you again. That is hard.

Admetus.
	You have named the thing that scars my heart.
	What worse can happen to a man than losing
	a faithful wife? I wish I had never married,
	never lived with her in this house.
	I envy the unmarried and the childless.
	They have one life. To ache for that
	is a burden a man can carry.
	But children falling sick, and marriage beds
	torn apart by death,
	no one can bear to watch it, when he could have been
	childless and unmarried all his life.

Chorus.
	Fate, fate has come, and there is no wrestling with it.

Admetus.
	Aiai.

Chorus.
	But you set no limit to your grief.

Admetus.
	E, e.

Chorus.
	Heavy to carry. And yet . . .

Admetus.
	Pheu, pheu.

Chorus.
	bear it. You are not the first man to lose . . .

Admetus.
	Io moi moi.

Chorus.
	a wife. Disaster shows itself
	to one mortal after another, and presses down.

Admetus.
	Long grief, long sorrow for the people we love
	under the earth.
	Why did you stop me throwing myself
	into the open trench of the tomb, to lie there dead
	with her, the best woman by far?
	Hades would have had two souls for one,
	the two most faithful, crossing
	the lake under the earth together.

Chorus.
	I had a kinsman
	whose son died in his house,
	a boy worth mourning,
	his only child. And yet
	he carried the evil, and carried it well enough, childless as he was,
	his hair already grey,
	already stooping forward,
	far along the road of his life.

Admetus.
	Shape of my house, how can I go in?
	How can I live here, when my luck
	has turned over? Oh. The distance is so wide.
	Then, with pine torches from Pelion
	and wedding songs, I walked in,
	holding my dear wife's hand,
	and a loud crowd of revellers came behind,
	calling the dead woman and me happy:
	two people well born, from noble stock
	on both sides, yoked together.
	Now, in place of the wedding songs, a wail,
	and black clothes in place of white,
	escort me in
	to an empty bed.

Chorus.
	On the heels of good fortune
	this pain came to you, who had never known evil.
	But you saved
	your life and your breath.
	Your wife died. She left her love behind her.
	What is new in that? Death has already
	unyoked many men
	from their wives.

Admetus.
Friends, I think my wife's fate is luckier than mine, though it does
not look it. No pain will ever touch her again. She has finished with
all her troubles, and with glory. But I, who should not be alive, who
slipped past my fated hour, I will live out a life of misery. I am
only now learning it. How am I going to bear walking in through these
doors? Who will I speak to, and who will speak to me, so that I get
any pleasure from coming in? Where will I turn? The emptiness inside
will drive me out, when I see my wife's bed empty, and the chairs she
used to sit in, and the floor unswept under the roof, and the children
falling at my knees and crying for their mother, and the slaves
groaning for the mistress they have lost from the house.

That is how it will be at home. And outside, the weddings of Thessaly
and the gatherings full of women will drive me away, because I will
not be able to stand looking at women my wife's age. And whoever
happens to be my enemy will say this about me: "Look at him, living
in disgrace. He did not have the nerve to die. He gave the woman he
married in his place, out of cowardice, and escaped Hades. And he
thinks he is a man? He hates his own parents, when he was not willing
to die himself." That is the name I will have, on top of my troubles.
So what is the profit in living, friends, with a bad name and a bad
life?

Chorus.
	I have gone up through the Muses' country
	and high into the air,
	I have taken hold of many arguments,
	and I have found nothing stronger
	than Necessity. There is no drug
	on the Thracian tablets
	that the voice of Orpheus wrote out,
	none among all the medicines Apollo gave the sons of Asclepius,
	cut from the roots to heal the pains of mortal men.

Chorus.
	She is the only goddess with no altar
	you can go to, no image,
	and she does not listen to sacrifice.
	Lady, do not come to me
	any harder than you have in my life before.
	Whatever Zeus nods his head to,
	he brings to its end with you.
	Even the iron of the Chalybes you break by force,
	and there is no mercy in you, no relenting in that sheer will.

Chorus.
	And you, Admetus: the goddess has caught you in the grip of her hands, and you cannot escape.
	Bear it. You will never bring the dead
	up from below by crying.
	Even the sons of gods fade
	into the dark of death.
	She was dear when she was with us,
	and dead, she will be dear still.
	The noblest wife of all women was the one you yoked to your bed.

Chorus.
	Do not let your wife's tomb be counted
	a mound of the dead, like the other dead.
	Let it be honoured like the gods,
	something travellers bow to.
	Someone stepping off the road
	onto the side path will say:
	"This woman died for her husband, once.
	Now she is a blessed spirit.
	Hail, Lady. Be good to us." That is how
	they will speak to her.

(Heracles comes back, leading a woman whose face is veiled.)

Chorus.
And here, it seems, is the son of Alcmene, Admetus, on his way to your
hearth.

Heracles.
Admetus, a man should speak freely to a friend, and not keep his
complaints down under his ribs and say nothing. I came to you when you
were in trouble, and I thought I had earned the right to stand close
and be counted as a friend. But you did not tell me your wife's body
was laid out in the house. You gave me a guest's welcome instead, as
if you were taken up with some outsider's grief. And I put a garland
on my head and poured out wine to the gods, in your house, with that
disaster in it. I blame you for that. I do blame you, after what I
went through. But I do not want to hurt you when you are down.

What I have come back here for, I will tell you. Take this woman and
keep her safe for me until I come back with the horses from Thrace,
once I have killed the king of the Bistones. And if something happens
to me that I hope does not happen, because I do hope to come home, I
give her to you to serve in your house. She came into my hands with a
lot of hard work. I found some people setting up a public contest, a
real job for athletes, and that is where I got her: I took her as the
prize. The men who won the light events led away horses, and the ones
who won the bigger events, the boxing and the wrestling, got herds of
cattle, and a woman went with the cattle. I happened to be there, and
it would have been a disgrace to pass up a prize with glory in it. So,
as I said, you must look after the woman. I did not steal her. I won
her with hard work, and I have brought her here. And in time, maybe
you will thank me for it too.

Admetus.
It was not from any disrespect to you, or because I count you among
people I am ashamed of, that I hid my wife's miserable fate from you.
It would have been pain piled on pain if you had gone off to some
other host's house. It was enough for me to cry over my own trouble.
But the woman, my lord, if there is any way at all, I beg you, tell
some other Thessalian to keep her, someone who has not suffered what
I have. You have plenty of friends in Pherae. Do not remind me of my
misery. I could not look at her in the house and keep my eyes dry. Do
not put a sickness on top of the sickness I have. I am weighed down
enough with what has happened to me.

And where in the house would a young woman live? She is young: her
dress and her jewellery show it. Is she to live in the men's quarters?
And how will she stay untouched, moving about among young men? A young
man in his prime, Heracles, is not easy to hold back. I am thinking
of your interests. Or do I take her into the dead woman's bedroom and
keep her there? And how do I bring her into that woman's bed? I am
afraid of blame from two sides. From the townspeople, in case someone
accuses me of betraying the woman who saved me and falling into
another young woman's bed. And from the dead woman herself. She
deserves my respect. I have to be very careful.

And you, woman, whoever you are, know this: you have the same measure
of body as Alcestis. You are her shape. Oh. By the gods, take this
woman out of my sight. Do not take me when I am already taken. When I
look at her I think I am looking at my wife. It churns my heart, and
the springs have broken open in my eyes. Oh, I am a wretched man. Only
now am I tasting how bitter this grief is.

Chorus.
I could not call your luck good. But whoever you are, you have to bear
what a god gives.

Heracles.
If only I had the power to bring your wife up into the light from the
houses below and do you that favour.

Admetus.
I know you would want to. But how? The dead do not come back to the
light.

Heracles.
Then do not go too far. Carry it the way you should.

Admetus.
It is easier to give advice than to suffer and hold out.

Heracles.
What good will it do you if you want to groan for ever?

Admetus.
I know that myself. But some longing drags me on.

Heracles.
Yes. Loving the dead brings tears.

Admetus.
She has destroyed me, more than I can say.

Heracles.
You have lost a good wife. Who will deny it?

Admetus.
So that this man takes no more pleasure in life.

Heracles.
Time will soften it. The evil is still young.

Admetus.
Time. Yes, you can say time, if time means dying.

Heracles.
A woman will cure you. The longing for a new marriage.

Admetus.
Be quiet. What a thing to say. I would not have believed it of you.

Heracles.
What? You will not marry again? You will keep an empty bed?

Admetus.
There is no woman who will lie in it.

Heracles.
Do you expect that to do the dead woman any good?

Admetus.
She has to be honoured, wherever she is.

Heracles.
I approve. I do approve. But you are convicting yourself of foolishness.

Admetus.
As long as you never call this man a bridegroom.

Heracles.
I praised you because you are a faithful friend to your wife.

Admetus.
May I die if I ever betray her, even though she is gone.

Heracles.
Then take this woman into your noble house.

Admetus.
No. I beg you, by Zeus who fathered you.

Heracles.
And yet you will be making a mistake if you do not.

Admetus.
And if I do, grief will gnaw at my heart.

Heracles.
Do it. The favour may turn out to land just where it is needed.

Admetus.
Pheu. If only you had never won her in that contest.

Heracles.
But when I win, you win with me.

Admetus.
Well said. But the woman must go.

Heracles.
She will go, if she has to. But first, look at whether she has to.

Admetus.
She has to, unless you mean to lose your temper with me.

Heracles.
I know something, and that is why I am pressing it.

Admetus.
Win, then. But what you are doing gives me no pleasure.

Heracles.
There will come a time when you praise me. Just do it.

Admetus.
Take her in, servants, if this woman has to be received in the house.

Heracles.
I will not hand the woman over to servants.

Admetus.
Then take her into the house yourself, if you like.

Heracles.
No. I am going to put her into your hands.

Admetus.
I will not touch her. She is free to go inside.

Heracles.
I trust your right hand, and only yours.

Admetus.
My lord, you are forcing me to do this against my will.

Heracles.
Have the nerve. Stretch out your hand and touch the stranger.

Admetus.
There. I am holding it out, as if I were cutting off the Gorgon's
head.

Heracles.
Have you got her?

Admetus.
Got her? Yes.

Heracles.
Then keep her safe. And one day you will say the son of Zeus was a
noble guest.

(He lifts the veil from the woman's face.)

Look at her. See if she seems at all like your wife. You are a lucky
man now; step out of your grief.

Admetus.
Gods, what am I going to say? This is a wonder I never hoped for. Is
this really my wife I am looking at? Or is some god mocking me,
knocking me out of my senses with joy?

Heracles.
No. It is your own wife you are looking at.

Admetus.
Make sure it is not some ghost from below.

Heracles.
The guest you took in was no conjuror of spirits.

Admetus.
But am I looking at my wife, the woman I buried?

Heracles.
Be sure of it. I am not surprised you cannot believe your luck.

Admetus.
Can I touch her? Can I speak to her as my living wife?

Heracles.
Speak to her. You have everything you wanted.

Admetus.
Face and body of my dearest wife. I have you, past all hope, when I
thought I would never see you again.

Heracles.
You have her. Only let no god be jealous of it.

Admetus.
Noble son of almighty Zeus, be happy, and may the father who begot you
keep you safe. You alone have set my house back on its feet. How did
you send her up from below into this light?

Heracles.
I fought the god who had her in his power.

Admetus.
Where did you fight this fight with Death, you say?

Heracles.
Right beside the tomb. I sprang out of hiding and got my hands on him.

Admetus.
But why is she standing there without a word?

Heracles.
You are not allowed to hear her speak to you yet. Not until she has
been purified from her consecration to the gods below, and the third
day comes. Take her inside now. And in future, Admetus, be a just man
and treat your guests with reverence. Goodbye. I have a job waiting,
and I am going to do it for Eurystheus, the king, Sthenelus's son.

Admetus.
Stay with us. Share our hearth.

Heracles.
Another time. Now I have to hurry.

Admetus.
Good luck to you, and may you come home again. And to the citizens,
and to all four districts of my Thessaly, I give this order: set up
dances for this good fortune, and make the altars smoke with the
sacrifice of cattle and with prayers. Because now we have changed our
life for a better one than the life before. I am a lucky man, and I
will not deny it.

(Heracles goes. Admetus leads Alcestis into the house.)

Chorus.
	Many are the shapes of what the gods do,
	and many the things they bring about that no one expected.
	What we counted on did not happen,
	and for what no one counted on, a god found a way.
	That is how this business ended.
