The Trojan Women
(Part 1 of 3)

(The Greek camp on the shore at Troy, the morning after the city fell.
Smoke rises from the ruins behind. In front of the tents where the
captured women are kept, Hecuba lies on the ground. Poseidon appears.)

Poseidon.
I am Poseidon. I have come up from the salt depths of the Aegean, where
the Nereids dance in their choruses and their feet trace the loveliest
of patterns. Ever since the day Apollo and I set the stone towers round
this land of Troy, straight and true to the line, my heart has never
once lost its goodwill towards the city of my Phrygians. And now it is
smoking. It has been sacked and destroyed by the Argive spear. Epeius
of Phocis, from under Parnassus, built a horse by Athena's contrivance,
pregnant with weapons, and sent the deadly image in through the towers;
and men in later days will call it the Wooden Horse, for the spears it
hid inside it.

The groves are deserted, and the temples of the gods are running with
blood. Priam has fallen dead on the steps of the altar of Zeus who
guards the courtyard. Gold in heaps and Phrygian plunder are being
carried down to the Achaean ships, and the Greeks who came against this
city are waiting for a wind at their sterns, so that after ten seasons
of sowing they can see their wives and children again and be glad. And
I am leaving famous Troy and my altars, because I have been beaten, by
Hera, the Argive goddess, and by Athena, who destroyed the Phrygians
between them. When a cruel emptiness takes hold of a city, the worship
of the gods sickens, and nobody wants to honour them any more.

The Scamander is loud with the wailing of the captive women as their
masters are allotted. Some have gone to the Arcadians, some to the men
of Thessaly, some to the sons of Theseus, the leaders of the Athenians.
The Trojan women not yet allotted are under these roofs here, set aside
for the chief men of the army, and with them Helen, the Spartan,
Tyndareus's daughter, quite rightly counted as a captive. And if anyone
wants to look at misery, here it is. Hecuba, lying in front of the
gates, crying many tears for many reasons. Her daughter Polyxena has
been killed at the mound of Achilles's tomb, a wretched death, and in
secret; Hecuba does not know it. Priam is gone, and her children. And
the girl the lord Apollo let run wild, Cassandra: Agamemnon is going to
force her into his bed in the dark, and never mind the god, or what
piety requires.

Goodbye, city that was happy once. Goodbye, towers of smooth-cut stone.
If Athena, Zeus's daughter, had not destroyed you, you would be standing
on your foundations still.

(Athena appears.)

Athena.
Am I allowed to speak to my father's nearest kinsman, a great god,
honoured among the gods, if I set aside the hatred between us?

Poseidon.
You are. Talk between relatives, Lady Athena, is no small charm on the
heart.

Athena.
I am glad you are in a gentle mood. I bring proposals, my lord, that
concern us both, and I want to put them between us.

Poseidon.
Is it a new word from some god? From Zeus, or from one of the other
powers?

Athena.
No. It is about Troy, whose ground we are standing on. I have come to
your power, to make it one with mine.

Poseidon.
Don't tell me you have thrown away the old hatred and come round to
pitying the city, now that it is burned to ashes?

Athena.
Go back to the first point. Will you share in my plans, and help me do
what I want done?

Poseidon.
Certainly. But I want to know your side of it too. Have you come here
for the Achaeans, or for the Phrygians?

Athena.
I want to give my old enemies the Trojans some joy, and to give the
Achaean army a bitter journey home.

Poseidon.
Why do you leap about like this, from one mood to another? You hate too
hard and you love too hard, whoever it happens to be.

Athena.
Do you not know the outrage that was done to me, and to my temples?

Poseidon.
I know. When Ajax dragged Cassandra away by force.

Athena.
And the Achaeans did nothing to him. Said nothing to him.

Poseidon.
And yet it was your strength that sacked Troy for them.

Athena.
Which is exactly why I want to hurt them, with your help.

Poseidon.
Whatever you want from me is ready. What will you do?

Athena.
I want to give them a homecoming that is no homecoming.

Poseidon.
While they are still on land, or out on the salt sea?

Athena.
When they have sailed from Troy for home. Zeus will send rain, and hail
past counting, and black blasts of wind out of the sky. And he says he
will give me his lightning-fire, to strike the Achaeans and burn their
ships. You do your part. Make the Aegean crossing roar with towering
waves and whirlpools of brine, and fill the hollow bay of Euboea with
corpses, so that from now on the Achaeans learn to respect my temples,
and to honour the other gods as well.

Poseidon.
It will be done. A favour like this does not need long speeches. I will
stir up the open Aegean. The beaches of Myconos, the reefs of Delos,
Scyros and Lemnos and the headlands of Caphereus will hold the bodies
of many dead men. Go to Olympus, take the lightning bolts from your
father's hands, and watch for the moment when the Argive army lets out
its cables.

He is a fool, whoever among mortals sacks cities, and temples, and
tombs, the holy places of the dead. He gives them over to emptiness,
and later he is destroyed himself.

(The gods go. Hecuba stirs on the ground and lifts her head.)

Hecuba.
	Up, unlucky woman. Lift your head off the ground.
	Lift your neck. This is not Troy any more,
	and we are not the royal family of Troy.
	The god's luck has turned. Bear it.
	Sail with the current, sail with the luck you have,
	do not set the prow of your life
	against the wave. Sail as chance takes you.
	Aiai. Aiai.
	What is there for me not to groan about, in my misery,
	when my country is gone, and my children, and my husband?
	The great swollen pride of my ancestors,
	shrunk down to this. So you were nothing all along.
	What should I keep quiet about? What should I not keep quiet about?
	What should I sing a dirge for?
	Wretched woman that I am, my heavy-fated limbs
	lie the way they lie,
	my back stretched out on a hard bed.
	Oh, my head. Oh, my temples,
	and my ribs. How I long to roll over
	and shift my back and my spine
	from one side to the other, always
	to the sound of my own weeping.
	But even this is music to the wretched,
	to sing of ruin without a dance.

Hecuba.
	Prows of the ships, that came with your quick oars
	to holy Troy over the purple sea,
	past the good harbours of Greece,
	with the hateful war-song of the pipes
	and the sweet voice of the flutes,
	and hung out your hawsers of woven Egyptian rope,
	aiai, in the bays of Troy.
	You came after the hated wife of Menelaus,
	the shame of Castor, the disgrace of the river Eurotas,
	the woman who cut the throat
	of Priam, the father of fifty children,
	and ran me, wretched Hecuba, aground
	on this ruin.
	Oh. What a seat I sit on,
	camped here beside the tents of Agamemnon.
	I am being led away as a slave,
	an old woman out of her house, my head
	shorn pitifully in mourning.
	Wretched wives of the bronze-speared Trojans,
	and girls, girls, unlucky brides,
	Troy is smouldering. Let us cry aiai.
	Like a mother bird over her winged
	nestlings, I will lead the cry,
	the song, and not the same song
	as once, when I leaned on Priam's sceptre
	and led the Phrygian gods their proud music,
	my foot beating the time, the first in the dance.

(Some of the captive women come out of the tents.)

First Half-Chorus.
	Hecuba, why are you crying out? Why are you shouting?
	What does it mean? Through the walls
	I heard the pitiful sounds you were making,
	and fear shot through the breasts
	of the Trojan women who sit inside these tents
	and cry aiai for their slavery.

Hecuba.
	My children, already, at the ships of the Argives...

First Half-Chorus.
	The rowers' hands are moving?
	Oh, no. What do they want? Are they really going to
	ship me away from my own country now?

Hecuba.
	I do not know. But I can guess. Ruin.

First Half-Chorus.
	Io. Io.
	Wretched Trojan women, come out of the tents
	and hear what is going to happen to you.
	The Argives are making ready to sail home.

Hecuba.
	Ai, ai.
	Not her. Do not send
	Cassandra out to me,
	raving, out of her mind,
	a shame in front of the Argives,
	a madwoman. Do not add pain to my pain.
	Io.
	Troy, Troy, unlucky Troy, you are gone,
	and unlucky the people who are leaving you,
	the living and the dead alike.

(The rest of the women come out.)

Second Half-Chorus.
	Oh. I have left the tents of Agamemnon,
	trembling, to hear from you,
	my queen. Have the Argives decided
	to kill me, wretched as I am?
	Or are the sailors at the sterns already
	getting ready to move the oars?

Hecuba.
	My child, you woke before dawn and your heart woke with you.

Second Half-Chorus.
	I came out because I was shaking with terror.
	Has a herald come already from the Greeks?
	Whose slave am I to be, poor wretch?

Hecuba.
	You are not far, I think, from being allotted.

Second Half-Chorus.
	Io. Io.
	Which of the Argives, or the men of Phthia,
	or some island country, is going to take me,
	unlucky as I am, far from Troy?

Hecuba.
	Ah. Ah.
	And I, poor wretch,
	whose slave will I be, and where, in what country,
	an old woman, useless as a drone,
	the shape of a corpse,
	the feeble image of the dead,
	aiai,
	set to keep the watch at somebody's front door,
	or to nurse their children, I who held
	the highest honours in Troy?

Chorus.
	Aiai, aiai. What laments can you find
	to cry aiai for the ruin they have made of you?
	No more will I twirl the shuttle
	back and forth on the looms of Ida.
	I look my last on the bodies of my children,
	my last.
	And there is worse work coming.
	Either I am forced into some Greek's bed,
	and may that night be damned, and the luck that brings it,
	or I fetch the water from Peirene,
	a pitiful servant of the holy spring.
	If only we could come to the famous
	blessed country of Theseus.
	But never, never the swirl of the Eurotas,
	the hateful home of Helen,
	where I would meet Menelaus as his slave,
	the man who sacked Troy.

Chorus.
	The holy country of the Peneus,
	the loveliest footstool of Olympus,
	I have heard is heavy with wealth
	and with fine harvests.
	That would be my second choice, after the holy,
	sacred country of Theseus.
	And the land of Hephaestus under Etna,
	across the water from Phoenicia,
	the mother of the Sicilian mountains, I hear
	is proclaimed for its crowns of courage.
	And the land that sailors find nearest
	on the Ionian sea,
	watered by the Crathis, that loveliest river,
	that turns the hair red-gold
	and feeds a country of good men
	with its holy springs, and makes it rich.

Chorus.
	And here, from the army of the Greeks,
	comes a herald, carrying fresh news,
	hurrying, making his quick feet fly.
	What is he bringing? What is he going to say?
	We are slaves of the Dorian land already.

(Talthybius comes in from the camp, with soldiers.)

Talthybius.
Hecuba, you know me. I have come many times to Troy as a herald from
the Achaean army, and you knew me before this, lady. I am Talthybius,
and I have come with fresh news.

Hecuba.
	Aiai. It has come,
	friends, Trojan women. It has come, the thing I have been afraid of
	for so long.

Talthybius.
	You have been allotted already, if that was what you were afraid of.

Hecuba.
	Aiai. Which city
	did you say? Somewhere in Thessaly,
	or Phthia, or the land of Cadmus?

Talthybius.
	You have gone to your masters one by one, not all together.

Hecuba.
	Then who has drawn whom? Which of the women of Troy
	has a lucky fate waiting for her?

Talthybius.
	I know. But ask me one at a time, not all at once.

Hecuba.
	Then my child. Who
	drew her? Tell me. Poor Cassandra.

Talthybius.
	King Agamemnon took her, as a prize set apart for himself.

Hecuba.
	As a slave for his Spartan
	wife? Io moi moi.

Talthybius.
	No. For his bed, in the dark, as a bride nobody will speak of.

Hecuba.
	Apollo's virgin? The girl the god with the golden hair
	gave the gift of a life with no man in her bed?

Talthybius.
	Desire shot him. Desire for the god-possessed girl.

Hecuba.
	Throw them away, child, the holy keys,
	tear off your body the sacred wreaths
	you wear, the garlands you are dressed in.

Talthybius.
	What, is it nothing to her, to win a king's bed?

Hecuba.
	And the child you took from me the other day, the youngest? Where is she?

Talthybius.
	You mean Polyxena? Or who is it you are asking about?

Hecuba.
	Her. Who did the lot yoke her to?

Talthybius.
	She has been appointed to serve at Achilles's tomb.

Hecuba.
	Oh. I gave birth to a servant for a grave.
	But what law is this, what custom
	of the Greeks is this, my friend?

Talthybius.
Count your daughter lucky. She is well off.

Hecuba.
What is that you said? Tell me, does she still see the sun?

Talthybius.
Her fate has her now. She is free of all her troubles.

Hecuba.
And the wife of Hector, the master of bronze? Poor Andromache. What
fortune has she got?

Talthybius.
Achilles's son took her too, as a prize set apart.

Hecuba.
	And I, whose servant am I,
	I who need a stick in my hand
	for a third foot, with my old grey head?

Talthybius.
	Odysseus, the king of Ithaca, drew you, to have as his slave.

Hecuba.
	E, e.
	Beat the shorn head,
	tear both cheeks with your nails.
	Io moi moi.
	I have drawn a slave's lot under a filthy, treacherous man,
	the enemy of justice, a beast with no law in him,
	who turns everything from there to here, and then
	back from here to there again, with his two-faced tongue,
	and makes every friend he had before into an enemy.
	Weep for me, women of Troy.
	I am gone, and my luck has gone. I am finished,
	poor wretch. The lot I have fallen on
	is the worst of all.

Chorus.
You know your own fate, my queen. But mine? Which of the Achaeans,
which of the Greeks, holds my fortune in his hands?

Talthybius.
Go, slaves. Bring Cassandra out here to me as fast as you can, so that
I can put her into the general's hands, and then take the rest of the
captive women to the other chiefs, whoever drew them.

Hold on. What is that light of pine torches blazing inside there? Are
they setting fire to the tents? What are the Trojan women doing? Are
they burning themselves alive because they are about to be taken away
from this country to Argos? Do they want to die? People who have been
free take very badly to trouble like this. It is a stiff neck that
cannot bend. Open up. Open up. What suits them may not suit the
Achaeans, and I do not want the blame for it landing on me.

Hecuba.
No. Nobody is setting fires. It is my daughter Cassandra. She is
running this way, out of her mind.

(Cassandra bursts out of the tents, carrying a lighted torch and
dressed as a priestess of Apollo, with garlands in her hair.)

Cassandra.
	Lift it high. Bring it here.
	Bring the light. Oh. I worship. I set it blazing, look, look,
	this holy place, with torches.
	Lord Hymen.
	Blessed is the bridegroom,
	and blessed am I, to be married
	into a royal bed in Argos.
	Hymen, oh Lord Hymen.
	Since you, mother, with your tears
	and your groans keep mourning
	my dead father and the country we loved,
	I light the fire for my own wedding,
	and set its light blazing
	into brightness, into glory,
	giving it, Hymen, to you,
	giving it, Hecate, a light
	for a virgin's marriage bed,
	as the custom is.
	Lift your foot. Dance.
	Lead the dance up to the sky. Euan, euoi.
	As if it were for my father's
	happiest of fortunes.
	The dance is holy.
	Lead it, Apollo, now. In your temple
	among the laurel trees I serve as your priestess.
	Hymen, oh Hymen, Hymen.
	Dance, mother, laugh out loud,
	twine your feet with mine, this way, that way,
	carrying the step I love best.
	Shout Hymen, oh,
	with blessed songs
	and cries for the bride.
	Come, girls of Phrygia in your fine dresses,
	sing of my marriage, sing
	the husband fate has laid down
	for my bed.

Chorus.
My queen, will you not take hold of the girl? She is possessed. She
will go running with those light feet of hers straight into the Argive
army.

Hecuba.
Hephaestus, you carry the torch at the weddings of mortals, but this is
a miserable flame you are fanning up here, and nothing like the great
hopes we had. Oh, my child. I never dreamed you would be married like
this, at the point of a spear, by an Argive's sword. Give me the light.
You are not carrying the fire straight, running about in your madness,
and your troubles have not brought you to your senses, child. You are
just as you were. Take the torches inside, women of Troy, and answer
her wedding songs with your tears.

Cassandra.
Mother, crown my head. I have won. Be glad for me, for my royal
wedding. Send me off, and if you find me at all unwilling, push me
there by force. Because if Apollo is any prophet at all, Agamemnon, the
famous king of the Achaeans, is marrying a wife who will cost him more
than Helen did. I am going to kill him. I am going to wreck his house
in return for mine, and take the price of my brothers and my father.

But I will let that go. I will not sing about the axe that is going to
come down on my neck, and on other necks; or the killing of a mother
that my marriage will set going; or the overturning of the house of
Atreus. What I will do is show you that this city is luckier than the
Achaeans. I am possessed by the god, yes, but for this much I will step
outside my raving.

They came hunting Helen, and for one woman, for one bout of desire,
they lost men by the tens of thousands. Their general, the clever one,
destroyed what he loved most for the sake of what he hated most: for a
woman, he gave up to his brother the joy of his own children at home.
And she went of her own free will. Nobody carried her off by force.
Then, when they reached the banks of the Scamander, they started dying,
and not because anyone was robbing them of their frontiers or their
high-towered homeland. The men Ares took never saw their children
again. No wife's hands wrapped them in their shrouds. They lie in a
foreign country. And at home it was the same story. Women died widows;
fathers died childless in their houses, having brought up sons for
other people; and there is nobody at their graves to pour blood on the
ground for them. That is the praise their army has earned. Better to
say nothing about the shameful part. I would not want my Muse to be a
singer of evil things.

The Trojans, now. First, they were dying for their country, and there
is no finer glory than that. The ones the spear took were carried home
dead by the people who loved them, and lay wrapped in the earth of
their fathers' land, laid out by the hands that should have laid them
out. And the Phrygians who did not die in battle lived every day with
their wives and children beside them, which is a joy the Achaeans went
without. As for Hector, and the grief you feel about him, listen to
how it really stands. He is dead and gone, and he is known as the best
man there was, and it was the Achaeans' coming that did that. If they
had stayed at home, nobody would ever have found out how good he was.
And Paris married the daughter of Zeus. If he had not, nobody would
ever have talked about the marriage he made.

So anyone with any sense should keep clear of war. But if it comes to
war, it is no shameful crown for a city to die well, and dying badly is
the disgrace. So do not pity your country, mother, and do not pity my
bed. With this marriage of mine, I am going to destroy the people you
and I hate most.

Chorus.
How sweetly you laugh at the disasters of your own house. And how
sweetly you sing of things that you may not be able to show are true.

Talthybius.
If Apollo had not driven your wits wild, you would not be sending my
commanders out of this country with prophecies like that and getting
away with it free of charge. But there it is. The grand people, the
ones with the reputation for cleverness, turn out to be no better than
the nobodies. The greatest king of all the Greeks, the dear son of
Atreus, has fallen for a passion he set apart for himself: for this
madwoman. And I am a poor man, but I would not have taken her into my
bed. As for you, since your wits are not all there, your insults to
Argos and your praises of the Phrygians I hand over to the winds to
carry off. Follow me to the ships, a fine bride for the general.

And you, when the son of Laertes wants to take you, go with him. You
will be the servant of a woman who keeps herself in hand, or so they
say, the ones who came to Troy.

Cassandra.
A clever servant, this one. Why do heralds have the name they have,
these errand boys of kings and cities, the one thing the whole human
race agrees on hating? You say my mother is going to end up in the
halls of Odysseus? Then where are Apollo's words, the ones he made
clear to me, that she will die here? I will not throw the rest of it in
her face.

Poor man. He does not know what suffering is waiting for him. The day
will come when my troubles and the Phrygians' look like gold to him.
On top of his ten years here, he will fill out another ten before he
reaches his own country, and he will reach it alone. He will come to
the narrow passage between the rocks where the terrible Charybdis
lives; and the Cyclops, who walks the mountains and eats men raw; and
Circe of Liguria, who turns men into pigs; and wrecks on the salt sea;
and the lotus, and the longing to eat it; and the holy cattle of the
Sun, whose flesh will one day find a voice and speak, and Odysseus will
not like what it says. To cut it short: he will go down alive into the
house of Hades, and when he has escaped the water of the lake he will
come home and find ten thousand troubles waiting in his house.

Cassandra.
But why am I shooting off Odysseus's troubles like arrows? Go, as fast
as you can. Let me marry my bridegroom in the house of Hades. You are a
bad man and you will get a bad burial, at night, not in the daylight,
you commander of the Greeks who thinks he is doing something grand. And
me, the gullies will throw out my corpse naked, running with the winter
rains, near my bridegroom's grave, for the wild beasts to tear at.
Apollo's servant.

Garlands of the god I loved best, holy ornaments of my ecstasy,
goodbye. I have left your festivals behind, where I used to be so glad.
Off my body, go, I tear you off, so that while my flesh is still pure I
can give them to the racing winds to carry to you, lord of prophecy.
Where is the general's ship? Where must I go to board her? You cannot
be too quick now in watching for a wind for your sails, since when you
carry me out of this country you carry one of the three Furies with
you. Goodbye, mother. Do not cry. Oh, my dear country, and my brothers
under the earth, and my father who begot us, it will not be long before
you welcome me. I will come down to the dead in victory, when I have
destroyed the house of the sons of Atreus, who destroyed us.

(Talthybius leads Cassandra away towards the ships. Hecuba sinks to
the ground.)

Chorus.
You women who attend old Hecuba, do you not see your mistress has
fallen full length, without a word? Will you not take hold of her? Are
you going to let an old woman lie where she fell, you useless things?
Lift her up.

Hecuba.
Let me lie where I fell, girls. Help I do not want is no help. What I
am suffering, and have suffered, and am still going to suffer, has
earned me the right to lie here. Gods. They are poor allies, the ones I
am calling on. But there is a certain form to it, calling on the gods,
when one of us falls into bad luck. So first I want to sing of the good
things. That way I will stir up more pity for the bad ones.

I was royal, and I married into royalty, and there I had children, the
best of men, not just a number to be counted, but the finest in all
Phrygia. No woman in Troy or in Greece, no barbarian woman either, could
ever boast of giving birth to sons like them. And I saw them fall to
the Greek spear, and I cut off this hair of mine at their graves. And
Priam, who fathered them, I did not weep for him because somebody
brought me the news. I saw him with these eyes, myself, his throat cut
at the altar of the courtyard, and I saw the city taken. And the girls
I brought up to be prizes worth having for bridegrooms of the highest
rank, I brought them up for other men, and they were torn out of my
hands. There is no hope that they will ever see me, and I will never
see them again.

And the last thing, the coping stone on all my wretched troubles: I am
going to Greece, a slave, an old woman. And they will set me to the
work that suits old age worst of all: to keep the door and mind the
keys, I, the mother of Hector, or to bake the bread, and to sleep on
the floor with my wrinkled back, after a royal bed, in torn rags
wrapped round a torn body, in scraps of a dress that the fortunate
would not think fit to wear. Oh, poor wretch that I am. All this I have
had, and all this I have coming, because one woman got married once.

Oh, my child. Oh, Cassandra, who danced with the gods in their ecstasy,
what a disaster has undone your purity. And you, poor girl, where are
you, Polyxena? Of all the children I bore, the sons and the daughters,
so many of them, not one is any help to their wretched mother now. So
why lift me up? What is there to hope for? Take me, the feet that once
stepped so delicately in Troy and are a slave's feet now, to a bed of
straw on the ground and a pillow of stone, so that I can fall on it and
be destroyed, worn away with crying. Of all the people who are
fortunate, count none of them lucky until he is dead.
