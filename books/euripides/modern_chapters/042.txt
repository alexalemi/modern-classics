The Phoenician Women
(Part 4 of 4)

Chorus.
Your sister has left the house, Creon, and her daughter Antigone went
out with her, the two of them together.

Creon.
Where to? What has happened? Tell me.

Chorus.
She heard that her sons were about to meet shield to shield, in single
combat, for the royal house.

Creon.
What are you saying? I was cradling the body of my own boy. I never got
as far as hearing this too.

Chorus.
Your sister has been gone a long while, Creon. I think the fight for
life and death between the sons of Oedipus has already been fought.

Creon.
Oh. And there is the sign of it, I can see it: the dark look on the face
of a messenger, coming this way. He will tell us everything that has
been done.

(A Messenger comes in from the battlefield.)

Messenger.
God help me, what story do I tell? What grief do I cry out first?

Creon.
We are finished. That is no fair face to put on the start of a speech.

Messenger.
God help me, I say it twice. What I am carrying is a great evil.

Creon.
On top of the evils already done. What is it? Say it.

Messenger.
Your sister's sons are no longer in the light, Creon.

Creon.
Aiai. That is a great grief you are crying out, for me and for the city.
House of Oedipus, did you hear this? Both sons dead, and dead the same
way?

Chorus.
Enough to make it weep, if it happened to have feelings.

Creon.
Oh, the heaviest blow fortune ever struck. Oh, the evils. I am a wretched
man. God help me.

Messenger.
You would say so, if you knew the evils on top of these.

Creon.
How could anything be worse fortune than this?

Messenger.
Your sister is dead. She died with her two sons.

Chorus.
	Raise it, raise the cry of mourning,
	and beat your heads with your white arms.

Creon.
Jocasta, poor woman, what an end you came to. Your life and your marriage
together, the whole of what you had to bear from the Sphinx and her
riddles, over. And how was it done, the killing of the two boys, the
curse of Oedipus fighting itself out? Tell me.

Messenger.
You know how the fighting in front of the towers went for us. The circuit
of the walls is not far off, so none of that was hidden from you. But
once the young sons of old Oedipus had fitted their bodies with bronze
armour, they came out and took their stand in the middle ground between
the two armies, two captains, two generals, ready for the contest, ready
to settle it with the strength of one spear each.

Polyneices looked towards Argos and made his prayer. "Lady Hera. I am
your man, since I married the daughter of Adrastus and live in your
country. Grant me this: let me kill my brother, and let my right hand,
raised against his, come home red with his blood and victorious." He was
asking for the most shameful crown there is, to kill his own blood. Tears
came to many eyes at the size of the misfortune, and men looked at one
another, passing the glance from face to face.

Eteocles looked towards the temple of Athena with the golden shield and
prayed. "Daughter of Zeus, grant that this arm of mine throws the
winning spear from my hand into my brother's chest, and kills the man
who came to sack my country."

Then the call of the Tuscan trumpet was let loose like a torch, the
signal for the bloody fight, and they went at each other at a terrible
run. Like two boars whetting their savage jaws they closed, beards
soaked with foam. They lunged with their spears, but each crouched down
under the circle of his shield, so the iron would slide off and find
nothing. If either saw the other's eye showing above the rim, he worked
his lance towards it, hoping to get in first with the point. But they
both kept their eyes so close to the spy-holes of their shields that the
spears had nothing to do. More sweat ran off the men watching than off
the men fighting, out of fear for the friends they loved.

Then Eteocles kicked aside a stone that had rolled under his foot, and
in doing it he put his leg out beyond his shield. Polyneices saw a blow
offered to his iron and met it with his spear, and the Argive point went
through the shin. The whole army of the Danaids roared. But in the same
effort the man who had been wounded first saw a shoulder left bare, and
drove his lance with all his weight into Polyneices' chest, and gave the
citizens of Cadmus their joy back, and snapped the head off his spear
doing it. Left without a weapon he backed away step by step, then
snatched up a lump of marble and threw it and broke the other's javelin
in the middle. Now Ares had made it even. Both of them had lost the
shaft out of their hand.

So they grabbed the hilts of their swords and came together, and their
shields clashed, and they set up a great turmoil of fighting, round and
round each other. Then Eteocles somehow thought of the Thessalian trick,
and brought it in; he knew it from time spent in that country. He broke
off from the struggle in front of him and drew his left foot back,
watching the hollow of the other man's belly from a distance, then
stepped in on his right leg and drove the sword through the navel and
lodged it in the spine. Ribs and belly folded together, and Polyneices
went down, poor man, in a spray of blood.

And the other, as if he had won now, as if the fight were over, threw
his sword down on the ground and started stripping him, with his whole
mind on that and none of it on himself. And that was what brought him
down. Polyneices was still breathing, a little, and he had kept hold of
his iron through his miserable fall, and with difficulty, but he did it,
he pushed the sword up into Eteocles' liver: Polyneices, the one who had
fallen first. And they lie there, both, biting the earth, close beside
each other, and they have not decided who was the stronger.

Chorus.
Ah. Ah. Oedipus, how I groan for your evils. It looks as if a god has
filled your curses to the brim.

Messenger.
Now hear the evils that came on top of these. When the two sons had
fallen and were letting go of life, at that moment their wretched
mother came rushing up, with the girl beside her, as fast as her feet
would take her. She saw them, wounded, the wounds that kill, and she
wailed: "Children, I have come to help you and I have come too late."
She threw herself on each of her children in turn and wept and keened,
groaning over all the long labour of the breasts that fed them, and
their sister, standing at her side like a shield-bearer, cried with her: "You two
were meant to keep our mother in her old age. My dearest brothers, you
have thrown my marriage away."

Then lord Eteocles let a hard dying breath out of his chest, and heard
his mother, and laid a wet hand on her. He sent no voice out, but he
spoke to her with tears, from his eyes, so as to show that he loved her.
And the other was still breathing, and Polyneices looked at his sister
and his old mother and said this: "We are finished, mother. I pity you,
and this sister of mine, and my brother lying dead. The one I loved
became my enemy, but I loved him all the same. Bury me, mother, mother
who bore me, and you too, sister, in the land of my fathers, and talk
the city out of its anger, so that I get at least this much of my
father's country, even if I have lost the house. Close my eyelids with
your hand, mother" (and he put her hand on his eyes himself) "and
goodbye. The dark is closing round me already." Then both of them, at
the same moment, breathed out their wretched lives.

And the mother, when she saw what had happened, was past bearing it. She
snatched a sword from the dead and did a terrible thing. She drove the
iron straight through the middle of her throat, and she lies there dead
among the people she loved most, with an arm thrown round each of them.

Then the whole crowd of soldiers leapt to their feet and fell to arguing:
we said my master had won, they said theirs had. And the generals
quarrelled too, some saying Polyneices had struck first with the spear,
others that since both were dead there was no victory anywhere. In the
middle of it Antigone slipped away from the army. Then they ran for their
weapons. But by some good forethought the people of Cadmus had stayed
sitting under their shields, and we got in first, and fell on the Argive
army before it had got itself fenced in armour. Not a man stood his
ground. They filled the plain, running, and the blood ran from the
countless dead going down under our spears. And when we had won the
battle, some set up an image of Zeus as a trophy, and some stripped the
shields off the Argive dead and sent the spoils inside the walls. And
others are coming here now with Antigone, carrying the dead, for the
people who loved them to mourn over. So for the city these contests came
out two ways: the luckiest that could be, and the unluckiest.

(The Messenger goes. The bodies of Eteocles, Polyneices and Jocasta are
carried in, with Antigone walking beside them.)

Chorus.
	The bad luck of this house
	no longer reaches us as a rumour. Look, you can see it:
	three bodies of the dead, here already,
	at the palace door, who drew one death between them
	and a lifetime in the dark.

Antigone.
	I am not veiling the soft cheek
	with its curls. I am not hiding, like a modest girl,
	the crimson under my eyes, the blush on my face.
	I am carried along, a bacchant of the dead,
	throwing the veil off my hair,
	letting the fine saffron of my dress fall loose,
	an escort for the dead, full of groaning. Aiai. Io moi.
	Polyneices, Man of Many Quarrels, your name came true. Omoi moi, Thebes.
	Your quarrel, no, not a quarrel, murder on top of murder,
	has destroyed the house of Oedipus,
	finished it in terrible blood, in grievous blood.
	What singer,
	what music-maker's groan over tears
	and more tears, house, oh house,
	shall I call in,
	carrying these three bodies of my own blood,
	a mother and her children, the Fury's delight?
	She wrecked the house of Oedipus from top to bottom,
	the day he understood
	the song nobody understood, the song of the wild Sphinx,
	and killed the singer where she sat.
	Io moi moi, father.
	What Greek, what barbarian,
	what nobleman of the old days
	ever bore such evils
	of a single day's blood,
	such griefs laid out in plain sight?
	Poor girl, how I cry.
	What bird, sitting up in the high-leaved
	branches of an oak or a fir,
	will sing along with my grief,
	a girl mourning a mother who is gone?
	Ailinon, with these wails
	I cry out ahead of time for the solitary life
	I will lead for the rest of time,
	in tears that never stop dripping.
	On whom shall I throw the first
	offerings torn out of my hair?
	On the two breasts of my mother
	that gave me milk,
	or on my brothers'
	bodies, on the ruin of their wounds?
	Ototototoi. Come out of your
	house, carrying your blind eyes,
	old father, show yourself,
	Oedipus, show your wretched life, you who threw
	a misty darkness over your own eyes inside that house
	and go on dragging out a long-drawn breath of living.
	Do you hear, you wandering the courtyard
	on your old feet,
	or lying wretched
	in your bed?

(Oedipus comes out of the palace, feeling his way with a staff.)

Oedipus.
	Girl, why have you brought me out into the light,
	me, a blind man and his stick,
	out of my bed in the dark rooms,
	with these most pitiful tears of yours,
	a grey unseen phantom of air, or
	a corpse from under the earth, or
	a dream with wings?

Antigone.
	It is bad news you are going to hear, father.
	Your children no longer look at the light,
	and neither does your wife, who worked
	for ever at your side like a second staff, tending your blind feet.
	Father. Omoi.

Oedipus.
	Omoi for what I suffer. I have cause to groan at this, to cry out.
	Three lives. By what fate,
	how, did they leave the light? Child, tell me.

Antigone.
	I do not say it to reproach you or to mock you,
	I say it in pain: your own avenging spirit,
	heavy with swords
	and fire and pitiless fighting, came down on your sons.
	Father. O moi.

Oedipus.
	Aiai.

Antigone.
	Why do you groan at that,

Oedipus.
	My children.

Antigone.
	You have gone through pain.
	But if you could still see the sun's four-horse chariot
	and turn the light of your eyes
	onto these dead bodies here...

Oedipus.
	What happened to my children is plain enough.
	But my poor wife, child, by what fate did she die on me?

Antigone.
	Weeping, wailing,
	where everyone could see,
	she carried her breast to her children, carried it,
	a suppliant running to beg them.
	She found her children at the Electran gate,
	in the meadow where the lotus grows, at spear-point,
	at war with each other,
	the mother did, like lions in a den,
	fighting on over their wounds, their blood
	already a cold murderous offering
	that Hades took and Ares poured.
	And she took a beaten-bronze sword from the dead men and dipped it
	into her flesh, and in grief for her children she fell across her children.
	All of it, father, in this one day,
	the god who is finishing this has heaped up
	the griefs on our house.

Chorus.
This day has been the beginning of many evils for the house of Oedipus.
May the life that is left be luckier.

Creon.
Stop the mourning now. It is time to think about burial. And you,
Oedipus, hear this. Your son Eteocles gave me the rule of this land when
he gave it as a dowry to Haemon, along with your daughter Antigone's
hand in marriage. So I will not let you live in this country any longer.
Teiresias said it in plain words: this city will never do well while you
are living in it. So get out. And I say this without any outrage, and
not as your enemy. It is those avenging spirits of yours. I am afraid
the land will suffer some evil from them.

Oedipus.
Fate, how you made me from the very start, wretched and miserable, if
ever a man was born so. Before I had even come out of my mother's womb
into the light, when I was not yet born, Apollo told Laius that I would
be my father's killer. God help me. Then, once I was born, the father
who begot me tried to kill me, in his turn, because he took me for his
enemy, since he was fated to die at my hands. So he sent me out, still
crying for the breast, to be a wretched meal for wild animals. And I
was saved from that. I wish Cithaeron had sunk into the bottomless gulf
of Tartarus for not finishing me off there, and instead my fate handed
me over to a life in another man's house, with Polybus for a master.

And I killed my own father, I, the man the god hates, and went into the
bed of my poor mother, and fathered sons who were my brothers, and I
have destroyed them, by taking over the curses of Laius and handing them
on to my children. I was not born such a fool that I could have devised
all this against my own eyes and my own children's lives without some
god being in it.

Well, then. What am I to do now, the man the god hates? Who will walk
beside me and guide my blind feet? This woman lying dead? If she were
alive, I know very well she would. My fine pair of sons? I have none. Am
I still young enough to find my own living? Where would I find it? Why
are you killing me outright like this, Creon? You will be killing me, if
you throw me out of the country. But I will not wrap my arms round your
knees and show myself a coward. I would not betray what I once was, a
man of good birth, even now that I am doing badly.

Creon.
Well said, on your side, that you will not touch my knees. And on mine,
I could not let you go on living in this land. As for these dead, one of
them must be taken into the house now. The other, the one who came with
strangers to sack the city of his fathers, Polyneices: throw his body
out unburied, beyond the borders of this country. And this will be
proclaimed to every man of Thebes: whoever is caught laying wreaths on
this corpse, or covering it with earth, will pay for it with his death.
Leave him unwept, unburied, food for birds. And you, Antigone, leave off
your keening for these three dead and take yourself indoors, and live as
a girl should until the day that is coming, the day the bed of Haemon
waits for you.

Antigone.
Father, what evils we lie in, the two of us, wretched as we are. How I
groan for you, more than for the dead. It is not as if one of your evils
weighs heavy and another does not. You were born unlucky in everything,
father. But I have a question for you, our brand-new ruler. Why are you
outraging my father here by sending him out of the country? Why are you
making laws over a poor dead body?

Creon.
This was Eteocles' decision. Not ours.

Antigone.
A senseless one, and you are a fool to obey it.

Creon.
How so? Is it not right to carry out what you have been ordered to do?

Antigone.
Not if the orders are wicked, and wickedly given.

Creon.
What? Is it not justice for this man to be given to the dogs?

Antigone.
No. The justice you are taking from him is against the law.

Creon.
Not if he was an enemy of his city, when he was not born one.

Antigone.
And so he has paid his fate over to fortune. He is dead.

Creon.
Then let him pay the penalty in his burial too.

Antigone.
What did he do wrong, if he came for his share of the land?

Creon.
This man will go unburied. You may as well learn that now.

Antigone.
I will bury him, even if the city forbids it.

Creon.
Then you will be burying yourself beside him.

Antigone.
It is an honour, for two people who love each other to lie side by side.

Creon.
Take hold of her and bring her into the house.

Antigone.
No. I will not let go of this body.

Creon.
The god has decided this, girl. Not you.

Antigone.
And this has been decided too: that the dead are not to be outraged.

Creon.
Nobody is going to put damp earth round this man.

Antigone.
Yes, they are. I beg you, Creon, by my mother Jocasta lying here.

Creon.
You are wasting your effort. You will not get it.

Antigone.
Then at least let me wash the body.

Creon.
That would be one of the things forbidden to the city.

Antigone.
Then let me put bandages round his terrible wounds.

Creon.
There is no way you are going to pay this corpse any honour.

Antigone.
My dearest. Then I will kiss your mouth, at least.

Creon.
Do not go buying yourself a disaster for your wedding with all this
grieving.

Antigone.
Do you really think I will marry your son while I am alive?

Creon.
You will have to. Where would you run away from the marriage bed?

Antigone.
Then that wedding night will make me one of the daughters of Danaus,
who killed their husbands in the dark.

Creon.
Did you hear the nerve of that? Did you hear the insult?

Antigone.
Let the iron witness it. Let the sword be my oath.

Creon.
Why are you so anxious to be out of this marriage?

Antigone.
I am going into exile with him. With my father, the most wretched man
alive.

Creon.
There is nobility in you. And there is some foolishness too.

Antigone.
And I will die with him, so that you learn the rest of it.

Creon.
Go, then. You will not murder my son. Leave the country.

(Creon goes into the palace.)

Oedipus.
Daughter, I honour you for your loyalty.

Antigone.
And what should I do, father, get married while you go into exile
alone?

Oedipus.
Stay, and be happy. I will put up with my own evils.

Antigone.
And who will look after you, father, blind as you are?

Oedipus.
I will fall where my fate decides, and lie on the ground.

Antigone.
And where is Oedipus then, and the famous riddle?

Oedipus.
Gone. One day made me rich, and one day destroyed me.

Antigone.
Then should I not share in your evils too?

Oedipus.
It is a shameful thing, a daughter in exile with a blind father.

Antigone.
Not if she keeps herself in hand. Then it is a noble thing, father.

Oedipus.
Lead me over, then, so I can touch your mother.

Antigone.
There. Put your hand on her. The old woman you loved most.

Oedipus.
Mother. Wife. The most wretched woman alive.

Antigone.
She lies there pitifully, with every evil on her at once.

Oedipus.
And where are they, the fallen bodies of Eteocles and Polyneices?

Antigone.
The two of them lie stretched out here, close together.

Oedipus.
Put my blind hand on their unlucky faces.

Antigone.
There. Touch your dead children with your hand.

Oedipus.
Dear fallen bodies. Wretched sons of a wretched father.

Antigone.
And you, Polyneices, the dearest name of all to me.

Oedipus.
Now Apollo's prophecy is coming true, child.

Antigone.
Which one? Do you have more evils to tell me, on top of these?

Oedipus.
That I would die in Athens, a wanderer.

Antigone.
Where? What walled town in Attica will take you in?

Oedipus.
Holy Colonus, the home of the god of horses. But come, be a servant to
your blind father here, since you are so eager to share this exile.

Antigone.
	Go, then, into wretched exile. Stretch out your dear hand,
	old father. I will carry you along
	the way a wind carries a ship.

Oedipus.
	Look, look, I am walking.
	Child, be the guide for my feet, poor girl.

Antigone.
	I am, I am. Poor girl, yes,
	the poorest of all the girls in Thebes.

Oedipus.
	Where do I put my old feet down?
	Hand me my staff, child.

Antigone.
	This way, this way, step to me,
	this way, this way, put your foot here,
	with a strength like a dream's.

Oedipus.
	Io, io. Driving an old man out of his country
	into the most miserable exile there is.
	Io, io. Terrible, terrible, what I have endured.

Antigone.
	Endured? Endured? Justice does not see the wicked,
	and does not pay mortals back for their folly.

Oedipus.
	I am the man who rose up to the song
	of victory that reached the sky,
	when I solved the riddle nobody could solve,
	the riddle of the girl who was half a girl.

Antigone.
	You are bringing up the Sphinx again, that old reproach.
	Enough talk of the good luck you used to have.
	This was what was waiting for you all along, this misery:
	to become an exile from your own country,
	father, and die somewhere or other.
	I leave my dear friends, the girls I grew up with, tears and longing,
	and go away, far from the land of my fathers,
	to wander as no girl should.
	Ah, this dutiful heart of mine,
	in my father's misfortunes,
	will make me famous.
	Poor me, for the outrages done to you and to my brother,
	who goes out from the house a corpse, unburied,
	poor boy. And him, even if I have to die for it, father,
	I will cover with earth in the dark.

Oedipus.
	Go and show yourself to the girls your own age.

Antigone.
	I have had enough of my own laments.

Oedipus.
	Then the prayers at the altars...

Antigone.
	They have had their fill of my evils.

Oedipus.
	Then go at least to Dionysus, and his sanctuary
	that no foot may enter, in the mountains of the maenads.

Antigone.
	Dionysus, for whom I once tied on
	the Theban fawnskin and danced, up in the mountains,
	in the holy company of Semele's son?
	And give the gods a thanks that is no thanks?

Oedipus.
Citizens of a famous country, look at me. This is Oedipus, who solved
the famous riddles and was the greatest of men, who alone put down the
power of the murdering Sphinx. And now I am driven from the land, myself,
stripped of honour, a pitiful thing. But why am I keening over this, and
mourning for nothing? A man is mortal, and what the gods make necessary
he has to bear.

(Oedipus and Antigone go out together, towards the road out of Thebes.)

Chorus.
	Great Victory, holy one,
	stay with me all my life,
	and never stop crowning me.
