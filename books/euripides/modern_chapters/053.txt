Cyclops
(Part 1 of 2)

(Sicily, at the foot of Mount Etna, in front of a great cave. It is the
home of the Cyclops Polyphemus. Old Silenus comes out of it, bald and
pot-bellied, dragging an iron rake behind him.)

Silenus.
Dionysus. The troubles I have had because of you. Thousands of them,
now, and back when I was young and my body was still good for
something. First there was the time Hera drove you mad and you ran off
from the mountain nymphs who were bringing you up. Then there was the
war with the Giants, the sons of Earth: I stood at your right hand
with my shield, and I killed Enceladus, put my spear straight through
the middle of his shield and killed him. Hold on. Let me think. Am I
telling you something I saw in a dream? No, by Zeus, because I showed
you the trophies afterwards, so it must have happened.

And now I am bailing out a worse trouble than any of those. When Hera
set that gang of Tyrrhenian pirates on you, to have you carried off
and sold somewhere a long way away, I heard about it, and I put to sea
with my boys to look for you. I stood right up at the stern and took
the two steering oars myself and held her straight, and my sons sat at
the benches and whipped the grey sea white with their rowing, hunting
for you, my lord. We had got as far as Cape Malea when an east wind
blew into the ship and threw us up here, on this rock of Etna, where
the one-eyed sons of the sea god live, the Cyclopes, in their lonely
caves, killing men.

One of them caught us, and now we are slaves in his house. The one we
work for is called Polyphemus. Instead of dancing and shouting for
Dionysus, we herd the sheep of a Cyclops who has no use for the gods.
My boys, being young, graze the young sheep out on the far edges of the
hills. My orders are to stay here, fill the water troughs and sweep
out the house: the waiter to this godless Cyclops at his godless
dinners. And right now orders are orders, and I have to sweep the
place out with this iron rake, so that when my master the Cyclops gets
back we can welcome him and his sheep into a clean cave.

And here come my boys already, driving the flocks in. What is this
now? Is that the stamp of the satyr dance I hear? The same as it was
when you were the god's escort at his parties, swaggering up to
Althaea's front door to the sound of lyres?

(The Chorus of satyrs come in, driving sheep and goats in front of
them, and dancing as they come.)

Chorus.
	Son of a fine father
	and a fine mother too,
	where are you off to, up the crags?
	Isn't the breeze soft here, out of the wind,
	and isn't the grass thick?
	Isn't there river water, fresh from the eddies,
	lying in the troughs beside the cave?
	Can't you hear your lambs crying for you?

Chorus.
	Psst. Not here? No? Won't you graze
	here on the wet slope?
	Hey. I'll throw a stone at you in a minute.
	Get along, get along, horn-head,
	back to the pens, where the herdsman
	of the wild Cyclops keeps his watch.

Chorus.
	Let down those swollen udders.
	Take to your teats the babies
	you left behind in the lambing pens.
	They miss you, the little ones,
	asleep all day and bleating.
	When are you going to come inside the yard,
	leave the grassy pastures,
	and get in among the rocks of Etna?

Chorus.
	Psst. Not here? No? Won't you graze
	here on the wet slope?
	Hey. I'll throw a stone at you in a minute.
	Get along, get along, horn-head,
	back to the pens, where the herdsman
	of the wild Cyclops keeps his watch.

Chorus.
	There is no Dionysus here. No dancing here,
	no women of the god with their ivy wands,
	no rattle of drums
	beside the springs pouring out their water,
	no fresh bright drops of wine.
	I am not on Nysa with the nymphs
	singing Iacchus, Iacchus,
	the song for Aphrodite,
	the goddess I used to fly after
	with the white-footed women of the god.
	Oh, my friend.
	Dear Dionysus, where are you wandering, all alone?
	Where are you tossing that yellow hair?
	And here am I, your own attendant,
	working for the Cyclops,
	the one-eyed thing, a slave, a stray,
	in this miserable goat-skin coat,
	without your love.

Silenus.
Quiet, boys. Tell the men to round up the flocks and get them into the
cave under the rock.

Chorus.
Go on, get them in. But what's the hurry, father?

Silenus.
I can see the hull of a Greek ship down at the shore, and the lords of
the oar coming up to this cave with some captain or other. They have
empty pots slung round their necks, and water jars. They want food.
Poor strangers. Who can they be? They don't know what sort of a man
our master Polyphemus is. They have walked into a house where no guest
is welcome, and they have come, worse luck for them, straight to the
Cyclops's jaw, the jaw that eats men. Hush, though. Let's find out
where they have come from, to the Sicilian rock of Etna.

(Odysseus comes up from the shore with his men.)

Odysseus.
Strangers, could you tell us where there's a running stream we could
drink from and take the edge off our thirst? And is there anyone who
would sell food to sailors who need it? What's this? We seem to have
landed in a town that belongs to Dionysus. That's a crowd of satyrs I
can see by the caves. I'll greet the oldest first. Good day to you.

Silenus.
Good day to you, stranger. Who are you, and where are you from?

Odysseus.
Odysseus of Ithaca, king of the land of the Cephallenians.

Silenus.
I know the man. Sharp as a rattle and twice as noisy. Sisyphus's boy.

Odysseus.
That's who I am. And you can stop insulting me.

Silenus.
Where have you sailed from, to get here to Sicily?

Odysseus.
From Ilium. From the war at Troy.

Silenus.
How? Didn't you know the way home to your own country?

Odysseus.
Storm winds snatched me and carried me here by force.

Silenus.
Papai. You're bailing out the same bad luck I am.

Odysseus.
You were driven here by force too?

Silenus.
Chasing the pirates who carried off Dionysus.

Odysseus.
What country is this, and who lives here?

Silenus.
This is Etna, the highest hill in Sicily.

Odysseus.
And where are the walls? Where are the city towers?

Silenus.
There aren't any. These headlands are empty of men, stranger.

Odysseus.
Then who holds the land? Wild animals?

Silenus.
The Cyclopes. They live in caves, not houses with roofs.

Odysseus.
Who do they obey? Or does the power belong to the people?

Silenus.
They're nomads. Nobody listens to anybody about anything.

Odysseus.
Do they sow grain, Demeter's crop? Or what do they live on?

Silenus.
Milk and cheese, and the meat of sheep.

Odysseus.
Do they have the drink of Dionysus, what flows from the vine?

Silenus.
Not a drop. Which is why there's no dancing in the country they live
in.

Odysseus.
Are they kind to strangers? Do they respect the rights of a guest?

Silenus.
They say strangers make the sweetest meat.

Odysseus.
What did you say? They enjoy eating human beings?

Silenus.
Nobody has ever come here without getting his throat cut.

Odysseus.
And the Cyclops himself, where is he? Inside?

Silenus.
Gone off on Etna, hunting animals with his dogs.

Odysseus.
Then do you know what to do, so we can get away from this land?

Silenus.
I don't, Odysseus. But we'd do anything for you.

Odysseus.
Sell us bread. We've run out.

Silenus.
There isn't any, as I said. There's nothing but meat.

Odysseus.
That's sweet enough. It stops the hunger.

Silenus.
And there's cheese set with fig juice, and cow's milk.

Odysseus.
Bring them out. A man should see what he's buying, in daylight.

Silenus.
And what will you give me for them? How much gold, tell me?

Odysseus.
No gold. What I have is the drink of Dionysus.

Silenus.
Oh, the sweetest words. That's what we've been short of for so long.

Odysseus.
And it was Maron who gave it to me. The god's own son.

Silenus.
Maron? The one I brought up in these arms?

Odysseus.
The son of Dionysus, to make it perfectly clear.

Silenus.
Is it on board the ship, or have you brought it with you?

Odysseus.
This skin here is what holds it. As you can see, old man.

Silenus.
That? That wouldn't so much as fill my cheek.

Odysseus.
Oh yes it would. For every drop that runs out of the skin, it gives
twice as much again.

Silenus.
That's a lovely spring you're describing. Music to my ears.

Odysseus.
Would you like a taste first? Neat, no water?

Silenus.
Only fair. A taste is what brings on the sale.

Odysseus.
And look, I carry a cup along with the skin.

Silenus.
Go on, then. Knock it out into the cup, so I can drink and remember
what it was like.

Odysseus.
There.

Silenus.
Papaiax. What a beautiful smell it has.

Odysseus.
You can see the smell, can you?

Silenus.
No, by Zeus. But I can sniff it.

Odysseus.
Taste it, then, so you're not praising it in words alone.

Silenus.
Babai. Dionysus is asking me to dance. Ah, ah, ah.

Odysseus.
Did it gurgle down your throat nicely?

Silenus.
Right down to the ends of my toenails.

Odysseus.
And we'll give you money as well, on top.

Silenus.
Just loosen the skin. Never mind the gold.

Odysseus.
Bring out the cheeses, then, or a lamb from the flock.

Silenus.
I will. And I'll spare very little thought for my masters while I do
it. For one cup of that I'd go out of my mind. I'd hand over the
flocks of every Cyclops there is for it, and throw myself into the sea
off the white cliff of Leucas, once I'd got well drunk and smoothed
the frown off my face. The man who doesn't enjoy his drinking is mad.
Drinking is what makes this thing here stand up straight, and gets you
a handful of breast, and puts a meadow all ready for you under both
your hands, and there's dancing, and forgetting your troubles. And I'm
not going to kiss a drink like that? Not tell the Cyclops and his
stupidity to go and cry about it, and his eye, the one in the middle,
along with him?

(Silenus goes into the cave.)

Chorus.
Listen, Odysseus. We'd like a little chat with you.

Odysseus.
Go ahead. Friends talking to a friend.

Chorus.
Did you take Troy? Did you get Helen into your hands?

Odysseus.
Yes. And we sacked the whole house of Priam, every one of them.

Chorus.
Well then, once you'd caught the young lady, did you all take turns
with her, one after another? Since she does like being married to a
lot of men. The traitor. She took one look at a man in fancy
embroidered trousers, with a gold collar round the middle of his neck,
and her heart went flutter, and off she went and left Menelaus, the
best little man there is. I wish the whole race of women had never
been born anywhere at all. Except for me. I'd keep them for me.

(Silenus comes back out of the cave, carrying cheeses and leading
lambs.)

Silenus.
Here you are. The shepherds' stock, King Odysseus: the young of the
bleating sheep, and cheeses of pressed milk, and plenty of them. Take
them, and get away from the cave as fast as you can, and give me the
drink of the joyful grape in exchange. Oh. Oh no. Here comes the
Cyclops. What do we do?

Odysseus.
Then we're finished, old man. Where can we run?

Silenus.
Inside this rock. You could hide in there, at least.

Odysseus.
That is a dreadful thing to suggest. Walk into the net?

Silenus.
Nothing dreadful about it. There are plenty of hiding places in the
rock.

Odysseus.
No. I will not. Troy would groan aloud if I ran from one man, when I
stood up to ten thousand Phrygians at a time, with my shield, over and
over again. If we have to die, we'll die like men of good birth. Or we
live, and keep the good name we had before.

(The Cyclops comes in from the hills, carrying a club.)

Cyclops.
Out of the way. Stand back. What's all this? What's this idling? Why
are you carrying on like Bacchants? There's no Dionysus here. No
bronze rattles, no banging on drums. How are my new-born lambs doing
in the cave? Are they at the teat, running along beside their mothers?
Are the rush baskets full of milk, squeezed out ready for the cheese?
What do you say? What are you saying? One of you is going to be
weeping soon, from this club. Look up, not down.

Chorus.
There. We've tipped our heads back till we can see Zeus himself. I can
see the stars. I can see Orion.

Cyclops.
Is my breakfast ready? Properly done?

Chorus.
It's ready. Just make sure your throat is.

Cyclops.
And are the bowls full of milk as well?

Chorus.
Enough to drink off a whole cask, if you want to.

Cyclops.
Sheep's milk, or cow's, or mixed?

Chorus.
Whichever you like. Just don't swallow me.

Cyclops.
Not likely. You'd start jumping about in the middle of my stomach and
kill me with your dance steps. Ah. What's this crowd I see by the
pens? Have pirates put in here, or thieves? I can see lambs from my
caves with their bodies tied up in twisted willow, and cheese baskets
jumbled about, and an old man with his bald face all swollen up from a
beating.

Silenus.
Oh. Oh. I'm running a fever, poor me. Beaten to a pulp.

Cyclops.
By who? Who's been punching your head, old man?

Silenus.
These men, Cyclops. Because I wouldn't let them carry off what's
yours.

Cyclops.
Didn't they know that I'm a god, and come from gods?

Silenus.
I told them. But they carried off your things anyway, and they ate the
cheese, when I told them not to, and they were carrying out the lambs.
And they said they'd tie you up in a collar three cubits long, and
rip your guts out through your eye, the one in the middle, by force,
and flog your back raw with a whip, and then tie you up hand and foot
and throw you down among the rowing benches of their ship, and sell
you to somebody for levering rocks, or throw you into a mill to grind.

Cyclops.
Is that so? Then off you go and sharpen the cleavers and the knives,
quick as you can, and pile a great bundle of wood on the fire and get
it lit. They'll be slaughtered straight away, and fill this belly of
mine: a hot dinner straight off the coals for the carver to eat, and
the rest boiled soft and tender from the cauldron. I've had my fill of
mountain food. I've feasted on lions and deer enough. It's a long time
since I ate a man.

Silenus.
A change from the usual is all the sweeter, master. It's not as if
you've had any other strangers turning up at your cave lately.

Odysseus.
Cyclops, hear the strangers' side too, in turn. We needed food, and
we came up from our ship to your cave to buy some. This man sold us
the lambs for a cup of wine. He handed them over, and he took a drink
for them: a willing seller and willing buyers, and there was no force
in any of it. There isn't a sound word in anything he says. He's been
caught selling your property behind your back, that's all.

Silenus.
Me? I hope you die a horrible death.

Odysseus.
If I'm lying, yes.

Silenus.
By Poseidon who fathered you, Cyclops, by great Triton and by Nereus,
by Calypso and the daughters of Nereus, by the holy waves and every
kind of fish in the sea, I swear, my beautiful one, my dear little
Cyclops, my own dear little master, that I did not sell your property
to the strangers. Or may these bad boys of mine come to a bad end.
The boys I love best in the world.

Chorus.
Keep that for yourself. I saw you selling the goods to the strangers.
I saw you with my own eyes. And if I'm lying, may my father drop dead.
Don't do the strangers any wrong.

Cyclops.
You're lying. I'd trust this man before I trusted Rhadamanthus, the
judge of the dead himself, and I say he's the more honest. But I have
some questions. Where did you sail from, strangers? Where are you
from? What city brought you up?

Odysseus.
Ithacans by birth. We're from Ilium, where we sacked the city, and the
sea winds drove us off course onto your land, Cyclops.

Cyclops.
You're the ones who went to Ilium, the city by the Scamander, to get
back that worst of women, Helen, after she was carried off?

Odysseus.
That's us. We drained that dreadful labour to the bottom.

Cyclops.
A shameful expedition. Sailing to the land of the Phrygians for the
sake of one woman.

Odysseus.
A god did that. Don't blame any mortal for it. But we are begging you,
noble son of the sea god, and we are speaking to you as free men. Do
not have the heart to kill friends who have come to your cave and make
a godless meal of them for your jaws. It was we who kept your father's
temple seats safe for him in the corners of Greece, my lord. The holy
harbour at Taenarus stands unbroken, and the high coves of Malea, and
Sunium's rock with the silver in it, sacred to Athena, daughter of
Zeus, is safe, and so are the harbours of refuge at Geraestus. We did
not let the Phrygians put their heavy shame on Greece. And you have a
share in that, because you live in a corner of the Greek world, under
Etna, the rock that drips fire.

And if you turn your back on arguments, there is still a law among
mortals: to take in shipwrecked men who come begging, and give them
the gifts a guest is owed, and help them to clothes. Not to spit their
limbs on the skewers you use for oxen and fill your belly and your jaw
with them. The land of Priam has widowed Greece enough. It has drunk
the blood of many dead men, fallen to the spear, and it has destroyed
wives with no husbands, old women with no children, grey-haired
fathers. If you roast the ones who are left and use them up in one
bitter meal, where is anyone to turn? Listen to me, Cyclops. Let go of
the greed in your jaw. Choose reverence over its opposite. For a great
many men, a wicked profit has turned into a punishment.

Silenus.
I'd like to offer some advice. Don't leave a scrap of this one's meat.
And if you bite into his tongue, Cyclops, you'll turn out smart, and a
great talker.

Cyclops.
Wealth, little man, is the clever man's god. The rest is boasting and
pretty words. As for the headlands by the sea where my father has his
seat, I bid them goodbye. Why did you drag them into your speech? I
don't shiver at Zeus's thunderbolt, stranger, and I don't know of any
way in which Zeus is a stronger god than me. I don't care about him,
either way. And since I don't care, listen to why.

When he pours rain down from above, I've a snug dry lodging in this
rock. I dine on roast calf, or some wild beast, and I give my belly a
good soaking, lying on my back, and drink off a jar of milk on top of
it, and then I let off a thunderclap under my cloak to compete with
the thunder of Zeus. And when the north wind blows out of Thrace and
pours down snow, I wrap my body in animal hides and build the fire up,
and the snow is nothing to me. And the earth, whether she likes it or
not, has to grow grass and fatten my flocks, and I sacrifice them to
nobody but myself, never to the gods, and to the greatest divinity of
them all, this belly here. Because to drink and eat your fill, day
after day, and never give yourself a moment's grief: that is Zeus, for
people with any sense. The men who made the laws and tangled up human
life with them can go and cry. I'm not going to stop doing my soul
good. By eating you. And you'll get your guest-gifts too, so nobody
can find fault with me: fire, and this cauldron here that was my
father's, which will boil up nicely and hold your flesh in pieces. So
crawl inside, and stand round the altar of the god of this yard, and
make him a feast. That god is me.

(He drives Odysseus's men into the cave ahead of him.)

Odysseus.
Aiai. I got out of the troubles at Troy, and the troubles of the sea,
and now I have run aground on the judgement of a godless man, a heart
with no harbour in it. Pallas, my lady, goddess born of Zeus, help me
now. Now. I have come to a worse labour than Ilium, and to the very
edge of the drop. And you who live where the bright stars have their
seats, Zeus who protects strangers, look at this. Because if you don't
see it, then you're called Zeus for nothing. A god who is nothing at
all.

(He goes into the cave after his men.)
