Heracles
(Part 1 of 3)

(Thebes, in front of the house of Heracles. Amphitryon, an old man, sits
at the altar of Zeus the Saviour with Megara and her three small sons.
Their clothes are in rags.)

Amphitryon.
Is there anyone alive who has not heard of the man who shared his wife
with Zeus? Amphitryon of Argos, the son Alcaeus fathered, and Alcaeus
was the son of Perseus. The father of Heracles. That is me. I settled
here in Thebes, where the crop of Sown Men sprang up out of the ground,
grown from the dragon's teeth. Ares spared a handful of that race, and
their children's children fill the city of Cadmus now. Creon came from
that stock, the son of Menoeceus, the king of this country, and Creon is
the father of Megara here, the woman every Theban once sang the wedding
song for, to the sound of the pipe, on the day the famous Heracles
brought her home to my house.

Then my son left Thebes, where I had settled, left Megara and her
family, and set his heart on living in Argos, inside the walls the
Cyclopes built. I am an exile from that city, because I killed
Electryon. So to lighten my troubles, and because he wanted to live in
his own country, he offered Eurystheus a great price for my return: he
would clear the earth of its monsters. Whether Hera's goad drove him to
it or whether it was simply what had to be, I cannot say. He worked his
way through all the other labours, and for the last of them he went down
through the mouth of Taenarus into the house of Hades, to bring the
three-bodied dog up into the light. And from there he has not come back.

There is an old story the Thebans tell, that once upon a time a man
called Lycus was Dirce's husband and ruled this city of seven towers,
before Amphion and Zethus, the two sons of Zeus who rode the white
horses, became kings of the land. That Lycus had a son with his father's
name. He is no Theban; he came here from Euboea. He killed Creon, and
having killed him he rules the country, because he fell on a city that
was sick with civil strife. So our tie to Creon by marriage is turning
out, it seems, to be the worst thing that could have happened to us.
With my son deep in the earth, this new master of the land, Lycus, means
to wipe out Heracles' children, and kill his wife, to put out one
killing with another, and kill me too, if a useless old man still counts
as a man at all, so that these boys never grow up and make him pay in
blood for what he did to their mother's family.

So here I am. My son left me in this house when he went down into the
black dark under the earth, to keep it and to look after his children.
And so that Heracles' children should not die, I have sat down with
their mother at this altar of Zeus the Saviour, which my noble son set
up as a monument to his victorious spear when he beat the Minyans. We
keep our place here in want of everything: food, drink, clothes. We lay
our ribs on the bare ground. The house is sealed against us, and we sit
here because there is nowhere else safe to go. As for friends, some of
them I can see now were never real friends, and the ones who are real
cannot do anything to help us. That is what bad luck does to a person. I
hope it never comes to anyone who has even half a kind thought for me.
There is no truer test of your friends.

Megara.
Old soldier, who once took the city of the Taphians, leading the Theban
army in a famous campaign: how little we humans can be sure of about
what the gods are doing. Take me. On my father's side, fortune never
shut me out. He was counted a great man once, for his wealth. He had a
throne, and a throne is the thing long spears go leaping at, aimed at
the lives of the lucky men who hold one, out of love for it. And he had
children. And he gave me to your son. He married me to Heracles, a
brilliant match. Now all that has died and flown away, and you and I are
about to die, old man, and Heracles' children with us, the ones I am
keeping safe under my wings like a bird crouched over her chicks. And
they keep coming at me, one from this side and one from that, with their
questions. "Mother, where has Father gone? What is he doing? When is he
coming back?" They are too young to understand, and they look for their
father everywhere. I put them off with words. I make up stories. But
every time the doors creak they are startled, and every one of them is
up on his feet, ready to run and throw himself at his father's knees.

So now, old man, what hope, what road to safety are you working out for
us? I am looking to you. We cannot get across the border unseen: there
are guards at every way out, and they are too strong for us. And there
is no hope of rescue from our friends any more. Whatever your judgement
is, say it, out loud, for all of us. Otherwise our death is as good as
ready.

Amphitryon.
Daughter, it is not easy to give offhand advice in a case like this, to
be serious without doing the work. But we are weak, and let us make the
time last.

Megara.
Do you need more grief? Or are you that fond of the daylight?

Amphitryon.
I enjoy the light. And I am fond of my hopes.

Megara.
So am I. But you must not expect what cannot be expected, old man.

Amphitryon.
In putting evils off there is a cure for them.

Megara.
It is the time in between that bites me. It hurts.

Amphitryon.
Daughter, there could still be a fair wind for you and me out of this
trouble. My son, your husband, could still come. So be quiet, and dry
the streams of tears running from your children's eyes, and soothe them
with words, tell them stories, steal their fear away. A poor kind of
theft, but steal it anyway. Even human misfortunes get tired, you know.
The winds do not always blow at full strength, and the people who are
doing well do not do well to the end. Everything changes places with
everything else. The best man is the one who trusts in his hopes,
always. Giving up is the mark of a bad one.

(The Chorus, old men of Thebes, come in slowly, leaning on their
sticks.)

Chorus.
	To the high roof, to the old man's bed,
	propped on my stick, I have set out,
	a singer of wailing songs,
	like a grey old bird.
	I am nothing but words, a shape
	seen at night in a night-time dream,
	trembling, yes, but willing.
	Children, children with no father,
	old man, and you, poor mother,
	groaning for your husband
	in the house of Hades.

Chorus.
	Do not let your feet give out,
	or your heavy legs, like a colt
	under the yoke, dragging the weight
	of a wheeled cart up a rocky slope.
	Take hold of a hand, take hold of a cloak,
	whoever feels his dim footsteps failing.
	Old man, help an old man along,
	the one whose young spear stood by your young spear
	long ago, in the labours of your age-mates,
	and was never a shame to a country
	whose glory was second to none.

Chorus.
	Look. The fierce flash of their eyes,
	how like their father's.
	The bad luck has not left these children,
	and neither has the grace.
	Greece, what allies,
	what allies you will be robbing yourself of
	if you lose these boys.

(Lycus comes in with armed men.)

Chorus.
But I see Lycus, the ruler of this land, coming past the house towards
us.

Lycus.
The father of Heracles, and his wife. I have a question for you, if I
may. And I may, since I have made myself your master, and I ask what I
like. How long do you mean to spin out your lives? What hope, what
defence do you see that will keep you from dying? Do you believe the
father of these children, who is lying dead in the house of Hades, is
going to come back? You make far more of dying than it deserves, both of
you, if you have to die. You, who scattered your empty boasts across
Greece, that Zeus shared your wife and had a child with you. And you,
who were called the wife of the finest man alive.

What was the great thing your husband did, after all? He killed a
water-snake in a marsh? Or the beast of Nemea, which he caught in a
snare and then said he strangled with his bare arms? Is that what you
are fighting me with? Is that why Heracles' children should not die? The
man made a name for courage he never had, fighting animals. In
everything else he was nothing. He never held a shield on his left arm,
never came near a spear. He carried a bow, the most cowardly weapon
there is, and he was always ready to run. A bow is no proof of a man's
courage. The proof is the man who stands his ground in the ranks and
looks straight at the fast furrow the spear cuts, and does not blink.

And what I am doing, old man, is not shamelessness. It is caution. I
know that I killed Creon, this woman's father, and that I hold his
throne. So I have no wish to let these boys grow up and be left behind
to take vengeance on me for what I did.

Amphitryon.
Let Zeus defend the part of his son that is Zeus's. As for my part,
Heracles, it falls to me to show, with words, on your behalf, what an
ignorant man this is. I cannot let you be spoken of like that. First,
then, the thing that should never be said at all, because calling you a
coward, Heracles, is among the things that cannot be said. I have to
clear you of that, and the gods are my witnesses. I call on the
thunderbolt of Zeus, and on the four-horse chariot he rode in when he
drove his winged arrows into the ribs of the Giants, the earth's
children, and then sang the victory song with the gods. Or go to Pholoe,
you worst of kings, and ask that four-legged outrage, the tribe of
Centaurs, whom they would pick as the bravest man alive. Would it not be
my son, the one you say only seems to be brave? Ask Dirphys, the
mountain of the Abantes that raised you, and it would not sing your
praises. There is nowhere in your own country you could point to and
say, here I did one brave thing, and call the land as a witness.

Then you sneer at the cleverest invention there is, the archer's gear.
Listen to me now and learn something. A man who fights in armour is the
slave of his weapons. If the men in line beside him are not brave, he
dies himself, through his neighbours' cowardice. And if he breaks his
spear, he has nothing to keep death off his body: one defence, and that
is all. But every man with a good aim and a bow in his hand has the one
best thing, which is this. He can let fly ten thousand arrows, and there
are still others to keep his body from death. He stands well back and
fights the enemy off, wounding men who can see with shafts they cannot
see coming, and he never gives the other side his body. He keeps it well
guarded. And that is the cleverest thing in battle: to hurt the enemy
and keep yourself alive, and not be anchored to luck. So much for that.
My argument and yours take opposite views of the matter in hand.

And now, these children. Why do you want to kill them? What have they
done to you? I will grant you are clever in one thing: you are a coward
yourself, and you are afraid of the children of the best. But it is hard
on us all the same, if we have to die for your cowardice, when you
should have suffered that at our hands, since we are the better men, if
Zeus had any fair feeling towards us. If you want the sceptre of this
land for yourself, then let us go, let us leave the country as exiles.
Do nothing by force, or you will suffer force yourself, when the god's
wind changes for you.

Pheu. Land of Cadmus, because I am coming to you too, I am handing out
my share of blame: is this how you defend Heracles and his children? The
man who went alone into battle against the whole Minyan army and made it
possible for Thebes to look out at the world with a free eye? And I
cannot praise Greece either, and I will never keep quiet about it: I
have found her at her worst towards my son. She should have come to
these chicks with fire and spears and armour, to pay him back for
clearing the sea and the land of their filth. That was the labour, and
that was the thanks he earned. But you get none of that, children, not
from the city of Thebes and not from Greece. You look to me, a feeble
friend, and I am nothing but the noise of a tongue. The strength I had
once is gone; old age has left my limbs shaking and my power dim. If I
were young, if I still had my body under me, I would have picked up a
spear and reddened that yellow hair of his with blood, so that he would
be running from my spear this minute, out past the borders of Atlas, for
cowardice.

Chorus.
Do good men not find an opening for speech, even when they are slow to
start?

Lycus.
Go on, say what you like about me with the words you have built your
tower out of. I will answer your words with something worse. You men,
go, some of you to Helicon, some to the folds of Parnassus. Tell the
woodcutters to fell oak logs and bring them into the city. Then stack
the wood round the altar on every side, and light it, and burn their
bodies, all of them, so that they learn that it is not the dead man who
rules this land now. It is me.

And you, old men, you have set yourselves against my wishes. You will
not only be mourning Heracles' children. You will be mourning what
happens to your own houses too, whenever anything happens to them, and
you will remember that you are slaves, and that I am your king.

Chorus.
You children of the Earth, you men Ares sowed, when he tore the teeth
out of the dragon's greedy jaw: will you not lift up the staffs your
right hands lean on and bloody the unholy head of this man, who is no
Theban, a foreigner, the lowest of the low, and lords it over the young
men here? You will never be my master and enjoy it.

You will not have what I sweated for with my own hands either. Go back
where you came from and do your outrages there. As long as I live you
will never kill Heracles' children. He is not buried so deep under the
ground as all that, when he left his children behind.

You hold this land, and you ruined it to get it. The man who did it good
gets nothing that he deserves.

And am I meddling, because I do good to my friends when they are dead,
when a friend is needed most?

Right hand, how you long to take hold of a spear. But you are weak, and
the longing has died. Otherwise I would have stopped you calling me a
slave, and we would have lived in this city of Thebes with honour, the
city you are enjoying now. Because a city that is sick with civil strife
and bad counsel is not in its right mind. If it were, it would never
have taken you for its master.

Megara.
Old men, thank you. It is right for friends to feel a just anger on
their friends' behalf. But do not get yourselves hurt on our account,
raging at your masters. Amphitryon, listen to my judgement, if you think
there is anything in what I say. I love my children. How could I not
love the ones I bore, the ones I laboured for? And I think dying is a
terrible thing. But a person who strains against what has to be, I count
a fool. Since we must die, we should die without being shredded by fire,
giving our enemies something to laugh at, and to me that is a worse evil
than the dying. We owe this house a great deal, and all of it fine. You
have a glorious name as a soldier, so it is not to be borne that you
should die a coward's death. And my husband needs no witness to his good
name. He would never want these children saved at the price of a bad
one. Noble men suffer when their children shame them, and I will not
push my husband's example aside.

And look at your hope, the way I reckon it. You think your son is coming
back from under the ground? Who ever came back out of Hades from among
the dead? Or do we soften this man with words? Never. An enemy who is a
brute, you run from. It is clever men, men who were raised well, that
you give way to: touch their sense of shame and you can cut a friendly
bargain more easily. It has already crossed my mind to beg him for exile
for the children. But that is misery too, to wrap their rescue in
pitiful poverty. They say a host's face is sweet to look at for exactly
one day, when the friend at his door is a fugitive. Have the nerve to
die with us. Death is waiting for you anyway. I am calling on your noble
blood, old man. Whoever struggles against what the gods have fixed is
keen, but the keenness is madness. What must be, no one will ever make
not be.

Chorus.
If my arms were strong and someone were insulting you, it would have
been easy to stop him. Now we are nothing. From here on, Amphitryon, it
is for you to see how to push this fate aside.

Amphitryon.
It is not cowardice, and it is not any love of life, that keeps me from
dying. I want to save my son's children. But it seems I am in love with
the impossible. Look. Here is my neck, ready for the sword, to stab, to
kill, to throw off a rock. One favour for the two of us, king. We beg
you. Kill me and this poor woman before you kill the children, so we do
not have to watch them, an unholy sight, gasping out their lives and
calling for their mother and their father's father. The rest, if you are
set on it, do. We have no defence against dying.

Megara.
And I beg you to add a second favour to the first, so that one man can
do both of us a double service. Let me put on my children the dress of
the dead. Open the house, because we are locked out of it now, so that
at least they have that much of their father's house.

Lycus.
It shall be done. I tell my men to undo the bolts. Go in and dress
yourselves. I do not begrudge you clothes. And when you have put your
finery on your bodies, I will come back and give you to the world below.

(Lycus goes out with his men. The doors of the house are opened.)

Megara.
Children, follow your poor mother's steps into your father's house.
Other men own what is in it. The name is still ours.

(Megara goes in with the children.)

Amphitryon.
Zeus, it was for nothing, then, that I had you as a partner in my
marriage. For nothing that we called you the joint father of my son. You
turned out to be less of a friend than you seemed. I am a mortal and you
are a great god, and I beat you in goodness, because I did not betray
Heracles' children. You knew how to sneak into a bed, to take another
man's wife with nobody's leave. But your own friends you do not know how
to save. You are a stupid god, or you were born unjust.

(Amphitryon goes in.)

Chorus.
	Ai. Linus: Apollo sings the lament for him,
	after the happy song, dead
	in his beauty, driving the strings
	with his golden pick.
	But I want to sing of the man
	who went down into the dark
	of the earth and the dead below,
	call him the son of Zeus
	or the child of Amphitryon,
	and put a crown of praise on his labours.
	The courage of noble work is the glory of the dead.

Chorus.
	First he emptied the grove of Zeus
	of its lion, and wrapped his back
	in the tawny hide, his yellow head
	hooded in the terrible
	gape of the beast's jaws.

Chorus.
	And the mountain tribe of wild Centaurs
	he laid low one day with his murderous bow,
	killing them with winged arrows.
	Peneus knows it, the river of beautiful eddies,
	and the long fields of the plain
	that bear no crop, and the farms on Pelion,
	and the glens of Homole next door,
	where they filled their hands with pine trees
	and rode down on the land of Thessaly
	and broke it
	under their horses' hooves.

Chorus.
	And the golden-horned deer
	with the dappled back, that plundered
	the farmers, he killed, and gave
	the glory to the goddess who kills wild beasts,
	Artemis of Oenoe.

Chorus.
	He mounted the four-horse chariot
	and tamed with the bit the horses of Diomedes,
	that champed at their bloody mangers
	unbridled, greedy, meat with the blood still on it
	between their jaws, an ugly table,
	glad to be eating men.
	Then over the banks of the Hebrus
	that runs with silver he crossed,
	still labouring for the king of Mycenae.

Chorus.
	And on the shore under Pelion,
	by the springs of Anaurus,
	he killed Cycnus with his arrows,
	the murderer of guests, who lived at Amphanae
	and mixed with no one.

Chorus.
	And he came to the singing girls
	in their garden in the west,
	to pick with his hand the golden fruit
	from the leaves of the apple tree,
	killing the dragon with the fiery back
	that coiled round it, guarding it,
	a thing no one could come near.
	And he went into the deep places of the salt sea
	and made it calm for the oars of men.

Chorus.
	And under the middle of the sky
	he drove his hands to hold up its seat,
	when he came to the house of Atlas,
	and with a man's strength he carried
	the starry homes of the gods.

Chorus.
	And the mounted army of the Amazons
	round the Maeotic lake,
	fed by so many rivers,
	he went to find, through the swell of the Black Sea
	that welcomes no one,
	gathering a company of friends from all Greece.
	Who did he leave out?
	After the gold-worked cloak of the war-god's daughter,
	the deadly hunt for a belt.
	Greece took the famous spoils of the barbarian girl,
	and they are kept safe in Mycenae.

Chorus.
	And the hound of Lerna with its thousand heads,
	the many-murdering water-snake,
	he burned out with fire,
	and dipped his arrows in its poison,
	and with those he killed the three-bodied
	herdsman of Erytheia.

Chorus.
	And other races he ran, and came through them
	with glory and good luck. And then he sailed
	to Hades, the place of many tears,
	the end of his labours,
	and there, poor man, he finishes
	his life, and has not come back.
	The house is empty of friends,
	and Charon's oar is waiting
	for his children, a road out of life
	with no return, godless, unjust.
	The house looks to your hands, and you are not here.

Chorus.
	If I had the strength of my youth
	and could shake a spear in the fight,
	with the Thebans who were young with me,
	I would have stood in front of these children
	and defended them. But now
	I am left behind by my lucky youth.

(Megara comes out of the house with the children, all dressed for
burial, and Amphitryon behind them.)

Chorus.
	But look. I see them coming, dressed
	in the clothes of the dead, the children
	of the man who was so great once, Heracles,
	and his dear wife, dragging
	the children along at her feet
	like a team on a rope, and Heracles' old father.
	I am wretched. I cannot hold back
	the springs of tears in my old eyes any more.

Megara.
Well, then. Who is the priest? Who is the butcher for these unlucky
children? Who is the murderer of my miserable life? The victims are
ready. Lead them to the house of Hades. Children, we are being led away,
an ugly team of corpses, old men and young and mothers all together. My
poor fate, and my children's. I am looking at you for the very last
time. I gave you birth, and I raised you for our enemies, to be their
outrage and their laughing-stock and their prey.

Pheu. How far my hopes have knocked me from what I expected, the things
I once hoped for from your father's words. To you, your father who is
dead was giving Argos. You were going to live in Eurystheus's house and
rule the rich fields of the Pelasgian land, and he used to throw the
lion's skin over your head, the beast's hide that he armed himself with.
And you were going to be king of Thebes, the city that loves its
chariots, with my land, the plains that came to me, for your
inheritance, because that is what you talked your father into. And into
your right hand he used to lower the carved club, the wood that kept him
safe, pretending to give it to you. And you: he promised he would give
you Oechalia, the city his far-shooting arrows once destroyed. Three of
you, and three kingdoms your father was building up for you, proud of
his own courage. And I was picking out the best brides, tying you by
marriage to Athens and Sparta and Thebes, so that you would ride at
anchor on stern-cables and live a happy life. All gone. Fortune has
turned round and given you Deaths for brides instead, and given me tears
for the wedding bath. I was a fool to hope. And your grandfather here is
hosting the wedding feast, with Hades for the father of the brides. A
fine match.

Oh. Which of you do I take to my breast first, which last? Which of you
do I kiss? Which do I hold? If only I were a bee with brown wings and
could gather up the grief from all of you and carry it into one place
and give it back as one flood of tears. Dearest, if any mortal voice is
heard in the house of Hades, I am saying this to you, Heracles. Your
father is dying, and your children, and I am done for, I who was once
called blessed among mortals because of you. Help us. Come. Show
yourself, even as a shadow. Your coming would be enough, even as a
dream, because the men who are killing your children are cowards.

Amphitryon.
You see to the things below, woman. And I, Zeus, throw up my hand to the
sky and call on you: if you mean to do anything for these children, help
them now, because very soon you will be no use at all. And yet you have
been called on many times. I am working for nothing. It seems we have to
die.

Well, old men. Life is a small thing. Get through it as pleasantly as
you can, and do not grieve from morning to night. Time does not know how
to keep our hopes safe. It hurries about its own business and flies
away. Look at me. Everyone looked at me once. I did things men talked
about. And in one day fortune took it all from me, like a feather blown
up into the sky. Great wealth and a great name: I do not know anyone
whose are certain. Goodbye. You are looking at your old friend, my
age-mates, for the very last time.
