Iphigenia Among the Taurians
(Part 2 of 3)

Orestes.
Who are you, anyway? You know exactly what to ask about Greece.

Iphigenia.
I am from there. I was lost when I was still a child.

Orestes.
Then you have every right to want to know what happened there, woman.

Iphigenia.
And the general? The one they say is so fortunate?

Orestes.
Which one? The one I knew was not one of the fortunate.

Iphigenia.
There was a king, they used to say. Agamemnon, the son of Atreus.

Orestes.
I do not know. Leave that subject alone, woman.

Iphigenia.
No, by the gods. Tell me, stranger. Let me have the pleasure of it.

Orestes.
He is dead. Poor man. And he took somebody else down with him.

Iphigenia.
Dead? How? What happened to him? Oh, I am wretched.

Orestes.
Why did you groan at that? Was he something to you?

Iphigenia.
I am groaning for the good fortune he used to have.

Orestes.
Yes. He died horribly. A woman cut his throat.

Iphigenia.
Oh, pitiful. Pitiful, the woman who killed him, and the man she
killed.

Orestes.
Stop now. Do not ask me any more.

Iphigenia.
Just this one thing. Is the poor man's wife alive?

Orestes.
She is not. The son she bore killed her. He did it.

Iphigenia.
Oh, a house turned upside down. What did he want with that?

Orestes.
To pay her back for his father's death.

Iphigenia.
Ah. How thoroughly he collected the debt. A just thing, and an evil
one.

Orestes.
Just he may be. The gods have given him no luck for it.

Iphigenia.
Did Agamemnon leave any other child in the house?

Orestes.
One. Electra. A girl, unmarried.

Iphigenia.
And what about the daughter who was sacrificed? Is there any word of
her?

Orestes.
None. Only that she is dead and does not see the light.

Iphigenia.
Poor girl. And the father who killed her, poor man.

Orestes.
She died for a worthless woman. A favour that earned no thanks.

Iphigenia.
And the dead man's son. Is he in Argos?

Orestes.
He is alive. Miserable. Nowhere, and everywhere.

Iphigenia.
Lying dreams. Goodbye to you. You were nothing after all.

Orestes.
And the gods, the ones people call wise, are no more truthful than
dreams with wings. There is a great deal of confusion in the affairs of
the gods, and in the affairs of men too. Only one thing really hurts: a
man who was no fool listened to what the prophets said, and it
destroyed him. How completely, the people who know can tell you.

Chorus.
Ah, ah. And what about us? And the parents who bore me? Are they alive?
Are they dead? Who could tell us?

Iphigenia.
Listen. I have come round to a plan that would help you, strangers, and
at the same time help me. A good thing is best of all when the same
business pleases everyone. Would you be willing, if I saved your life,
to go to Argos and take a message to the people I love there, and to
carry a letter? A prisoner wrote it for me, out of pity. He did not
think of my hand as the murderer. He thought the goddess's victims die
by the law, and that the goddess holds it just. Because I have had
nobody who could go back to Argos and take my message, nobody who might
be saved and send my letter on to somebody I love. But you: you are
not, it seems, my enemy, and you know Mycenae and the people I want you
to know. So be saved. And take a reward that is nothing to be ashamed
of: your life, for the sake of a little writing. But this man, since
the city demands it, must be the goddess's sacrifice, apart from you.

Orestes.
That was all well said, stranger, except one thing. For this man to be
slaughtered is a heavy weight on me. I am the captain of this voyage of
misfortunes; he only sailed with me for the sake of my troubles. So it
is not right that I should do you a favour at the price of his death
and slip out of trouble myself. No. Do it this way. Give him the
letter. He will take it to Argos, and you will have what you want. And
me, let anyone who wants to kill me kill me. It is the most shameful
thing there is, for a man to throw his friends' lives into disaster and
save his own. This man is my friend, and I want him to see the light
every bit as much as I want to see it myself.

Iphigenia.
What a spirit. What noble root you must have grown from, to be so true
a friend to your friends. I hope the one brother I have left is a man
like you. Because I am not without a brother either, strangers, except
that I do not see him. Since this is what you want, we will send this
man with the letter, and you will die. It seems you have a great
longing for it.

Orestes.
And who will sacrifice me? Who will have the nerve for something so
terrible?

Iphigenia.
I will. I hold the office of this goddess.

Orestes.
No one would envy you that, young woman. No one would call it blessed.

Iphigenia.
But I am bound to a necessity, and I have to keep it.

Orestes.
You yourself, with a sword? A woman, sacrificing men?

Iphigenia.
No. I sprinkle the holy water round your hair.

Orestes.
And who does the killing? If I may ask that.

Iphigenia.
Inside this temple there are men whose business it is.

Orestes.
What sort of grave will take me, when I am dead?

Iphigenia.
The holy fire inside, and a wide cleft in the rock.

Orestes.
Ah. If only my sister's hand could lay my body out.

Iphigenia.
That was a useless prayer, poor man, whoever you are. She lives a long
way from this barbarian land. Still, since you happen to be an Argive,
I will not leave undone any favour I can do. I will put a great deal of
ornament on your grave. I will quench your body with yellow oil, and I
will throw onto your pyre the honey of the tawny mountain bee, the
gleaming stuff that streams from flowers. Now I will go and bring the
letter out of the goddess's temple. Do not hold any of this against me.
Guard them, attendants. Without chains. Perhaps I am about to send news
nobody hoped for to someone I love in Argos, the one I love most of
all, and the letter, telling him that the people he thinks are dead are
alive, will bring him a joy he can believe.

(Iphigenia goes into the temple.)

Chorus.
	I mourn for you,
	the one the drops of holy water
	will claim, mixed with blood.

Orestes.
	This is no case for pity. Goodbye to you, strangers.

Chorus.
	And you, young man,
	we honour you for your blessed luck:
	one day you will set foot
	on your own country again.

Pylades.
	No friend would envy it, when his friend is dying.

Chorus.
	Oh, cruel journeys.
	Ah, ah. You are being destroyed.
	Aiai, aiai. Which of you is worse off?
	My mind is still torn two ways, in doubt:
	shall I groan first for you, or for you?

Orestes.
Pylades, by the gods, do you feel what I feel?

Pylades.
I do not know. You are asking a man who has no answer.

Orestes.
Who is that young woman? How Greek her questions were: the labours at
Troy, the Achaeans' journey home, Calchas the reader of birds, Achilles
by name; and how she pitied poor Agamemnon, and asked me about his wife
and children. This stranger is an Argive by birth. She is from there.
Otherwise she would never be sending a letter and finding all this out,
as if Argos doing well were her own business.

Pylades.
You got there a little ahead of me. You have said what I was going to
say, except one thing: everyone who has moved about in the world knows
what has happened to kings. But I have been going over another thought
as well.

Orestes.
What? Share it, and you will understand it better yourself.

Pylades.
It is shameful for me to go on looking at the light when you are dead.
I sailed with you. I ought to die with you. I will be called a coward
and a wretch, in Argos and in the folded hills of Phocis. Most people
will think, because most people are bad, that I betrayed you and came
home alone, saved; or even that I murdered you, while your house was
sick, and stitched up your death for the sake of your throne, since I
married your sister, the heiress. That is what I am afraid of, and it
shames me. There is no way round it. I must breathe my last with you,
be slaughtered with you, and have my body burned with yours. I am your
friend, and I am afraid of what people would say.

Orestes.
Do not talk like that. I have to carry my own troubles, and when the
grief could be single I will not carry it double. What you call painful
and shameful, that is what it would be for me, if I got you killed, you
who have shared every labour with me. As for me, it is no bad thing,
doing as badly as I am at the gods' hands, to let go of my life. But you
are fortunate. Your house is clean; it is not sick. Mine is impious and
unlucky. If you are saved, and get sons from my sister, whom I gave you
for a wife, my name would live on, and my father's house would never be
wiped out for want of a child. Go. Live. Live in my father's house. And
when you come to Greece, and to Argos, the land of horses, by this right
hand I charge you: heap up a grave mound for me and set a memorial on
it, and let my sister give her tears and her hair to the tomb. Tell them
I died at the hand of an Argive woman, at an altar, purified for the
slaughter. And never betray my sister, when you see how lonely the
house is, my father's house, that you married into.

And goodbye. I found you the dearest of all my friends, you who hunted
with me and grew up with me, you who carried so much of the weight of
my troubles. Apollo the prophet lied to me. He worked out his scheme
and drove me as far from Greece as he could, ashamed of what he had
prophesied before. I gave him everything I had, I trusted what he said,
I killed my mother, and now I am destroyed in my turn.

Pylades.
You will have your grave, and I will never betray your sister's bed,
poor friend, since I will hold you dearer dead than I did living. But
the god's prophecy has not destroyed you yet, though you are standing
very close to the slaughter. There are, there really are, great changes
that come out of trouble when the trouble is too great. When luck
allows.

Orestes.
Quiet. Apollo's words are no help to me. Here is the woman, coming out
of the temple.

(Iphigenia comes out of the temple with the letter in her hand.)

Iphigenia.
You, go inside and get things ready for the men who are in charge of
the slaughter. Here is the letter, strangers, folded, with all its
leaves. But listen to what I want as well. No man is the same when he is
in trouble and when he has fallen out of fear into confidence. I am
afraid that the one who is going to carry this letter to Argos will get
away from this country and then count my message as nothing.

Orestes.
What do you want, then? What is the difficulty?

Iphigenia.
Let him give me his oath that he will ferry this writing to Argos, to
the people I want to send it to.

Orestes.
And will you give him the same words back?

Iphigenia.
To do what, or not do what? Tell me.

Orestes.
To send him out of this barbarian land alive.

Iphigenia.
That is fair. How else could he deliver it?

Orestes.
And will the king agree to it?

Iphigenia.
Yes. I will persuade him, and I will put this man aboard the ship
myself.

Orestes.
Swear, then. And you, lady, lead him in an oath that a god would
respect.

Iphigenia.
You must say: I will give this to your friends.

Pylades.
I will give this letter to your friends.

Iphigenia.
And I will bring you safe out past the dark rocks.

Pylades.
What god do you swear by, to witness this oath?

Iphigenia.
Artemis. I hold my office in her house.

Pylades.
And I by the lord of heaven, holy Zeus.

Iphigenia.
And if you abandon your oath and wrong me?

Pylades.
May I never get home. And you, if you do not save me?

Iphigenia.
May I never set foot in Argos alive.

Pylades.
Now listen. There is one point we passed over.

Iphigenia.
Then let it be a new one. As long as it is good.

Pylades.
Grant me this one exception. If something happens to the ship, and the
letter goes down in the waves with the cargo, and I save nothing but my
own body, then let this oath no longer bind me.

Iphigenia.
Do you know what I will do? Many attempts hit many marks. I will tell
you in words everything that is in there, written in the folds of the
letter, so you can report it to my friends. That way it is safe. If you
save the writing, it will tell them what is written in it by itself,
silently. And if this writing is lost in the sea, then by saving your
body you will save my words.

Pylades.
Well said, for the gods' sake and for mine. Tell me who I am to take
this letter to, in Argos, and what I am to say, once I have heard it
from you.

Iphigenia.
Take this message to Orestes, the son of Agamemnon. "The girl who was
slaughtered at Aulis sends you this. Iphigenia, alive, though to the
people there she is alive no longer."

Orestes.
Where is she? Has she come back from the dead?

Iphigenia.
The woman you are looking at. Do not confuse me with your talk. "Bring
me to Argos, my brother, before I die. Take me away from this barbarian
land and from the goddess's sacrifices, where my office is the killing
of strangers."

Orestes.
Pylades, what am I to say? Where are we? Where have we found ourselves?

Iphigenia.
"Or I will be a curse on your house."

Pylades.
Orestes?

Iphigenia.
Yes. So that you learn the name, hearing it twice.

Pylades.
Oh, gods.

Iphigenia.
Why are you calling on the gods in my business?

Pylades.
No reason. Go on. My mind was somewhere else. If I keep asking you
questions I will probably come out at something impossible to believe.

Iphigenia.
Tell him that the goddess Artemis saved me. She put a deer in my place;
my father sacrificed it, thinking he was driving the sharp sword into
me, and she settled me in this country. That is my message. That is
what is written in the letter.

Pylades.
Oh, what an easy oath you have bound me with. And how beautifully you
swore your own. I will not take long. I will make good the oath I
swore. Here, Orestes: I bring you a letter, and I hand it over to you,
from your sister. This woman here.

Orestes.
I take it. And I will let the folds of the letter wait, and take my joy
first, not in words. Dearest sister, I am stunned, and still I am going
to put my arms round you, arms that cannot believe it, and have my
delight, now that I have learned things that astonish me.

(He goes to take her in his arms.)

Chorus.
Stranger, you are wrong to pollute the goddess's servant, putting your
arm round robes that must not be touched.

Orestes.
My own sister, born from the same father, Agamemnon, do not turn away
from me. You have your brother, and you never thought you would.

Iphigenia.
I have you? My brother? Will you stop talking like that? Argos is full
of him, and so is Nauplia.

Orestes.
Your brother is not there, poor woman.

Iphigenia.
But the Spartan woman, the daughter of Tyndareus, was your mother?

Orestes.
And my father was the grandson of Pelops. That is where I come from.

Iphigenia.
What are you saying? Do you have any proof of this to give me?

Orestes.
I do. Ask me something about our father's house.

Iphigenia.
Then you must talk, and I must learn.

Orestes.
I will say first what I heard from Electra. You know about the quarrel
between Atreus and Thyestes?

Iphigenia.
I heard of it. The dispute was over a golden ram.

Orestes.
And you know you wove it, in a fine-spun cloth?

Iphigenia.
Oh, dearest. You are steering very close to my heart.

Orestes.
And the sun, in the middle of the loom, turning back in its course?

Iphigenia.
I wove that picture too, in fine threads.

Orestes.
And you got the wedding bath from your mother, for Aulis?

Iphigenia.
I know. The marriage was not so happy that it took the memory away.

Orestes.
And this? You gave your mother a lock of your hair to take home?

Iphigenia.
As a memorial to put in a tomb, instead of my body.

Orestes.
And now what I have seen myself, I will tell you as proof. Pelops's old
spear, in our father's house, the one he shook in his hand when he won
Hippodamia, the girl from Pisa, and killed Oenomaus. It was hidden in
your rooms, in the girls' quarters.

Iphigenia.
	Oh, dearest. Nothing else: you are the dearest.
	I have you, Orestes, my darling, from far away,
	from our father's country, from Argos, my love.

Orestes.
And I have you, the one who was thought dead. Tears, and groaning mixed
with joy, are wetting your eyes, and mine the same way.

Iphigenia.
	You were still a baby
	when I left you, tiny in the nurse's arms,
	tiny in the house.
	Oh, my soul, you are luckier than words can say.
	What can I say? This has gone past wonder,
	past anything words can reach.

Orestes.
	From now on, may we be lucky, together.

Iphigenia.
	A joy I have no place for has taken hold of me, my friends.
	I am afraid he will fly out of my hands
	up into the sky and be gone.
	Io, hearth the Cyclopes built. Io, my country,
	Mycenae, my dear,
	I thank you for his life, I thank you for his rearing,
	because you raised this brother for me
	in your house, this light of the family.

Orestes.
In our birth we are lucky, sister. In what has happened to us, our life
was born unlucky.

Iphigenia.
	I know it, wretched as I am, I know it: when my father,
	with wretchedness in his heart, put the sword to my throat.

Orestes.
	Oh. I was not there, and I think I see you there.

Iphigenia.
	No wedding song, brother, when I was led
	to the tent of Achilles,
	to a marriage bed that was a trick.
	Beside the altar there were tears and groans.
	Ah, ah, for the holy water there.

Orestes.
	I groaned too, for the daring our father dared.

Iphigenia.
	Fatherless, fatherless, the lot I drew.
	One thing comes out of another,
	by the luck some god sends.

Orestes.
	Yes: if you had killed your brother, poor woman.

Iphigenia.
	Oh, wretched, for the terrible thing I dared. Terrible, what I dared,
	terrible, oh, my brother. By a hair
	you escaped an unholy death, cut down
	by my hands.
	And what is the end of all this to be?
	What luck will come my way?
	What road can I find for you,
	to send you back from this city, away from the slaughter,
	to your Argive homeland,
	before the sword comes near your blood?
	This, this is your task, poor soul,
	to find that out.
	By land, not by ship?
	But then, on the rush of your own feet,
	you will come near to death, among barbarian tribes
	and along roads that are no roads. And through the dark
	rocks of the narrow strait it is a long way
	for a ship to run.
	Wretched, wretched woman.
	Who, then, what god, or what mortal, or what
	thing that nobody expects,
	could work a way out where there is no way, and show
	the last two children of Atreus's house
	a release from evil?

Chorus.
Among wonders, beyond anything in the stories: I saw this with my own
eyes, and I will tell it not as something I heard.

Pylades.
When friends come into the sight of friends, Orestes, it is reasonable
to throw your arms round each other. But now you must leave off the
weeping and come to this: how we are going to win the glorious name of
safety and get out of this barbarian land. That is what clever men do.
They do not step outside their luck; they seize the moment when it
comes, and leave their other pleasures for later.

Orestes.
Well said. But I think luck will look after that part, along with us.
When a man is eager, the gods' strength is likely to be the greater for
it.

Iphigenia.
Nothing is going to stop me, nothing is going to keep me from speaking,
until I have first found out what lot in life Electra has drawn. You
are all dear to me.

Orestes.
She lives with this man. She has a happy life.

Iphigenia.
And where is he from, and whose son is he?

Orestes.
Strophius of Phocis is his father's name.

Iphigenia.
Then he is the son of Atreus's daughter. My own kin.

Orestes.
A cousin. And my one certain friend.

Iphigenia.
He was not born yet when my father killed me.

Orestes.
He was not. Strophius was childless for a while.

Iphigenia.
Greetings, husband of my sister.

Orestes.
And my saviour. Not only a relation.

Iphigenia.
But that terrible business with our mother. How did you have the nerve?

Orestes.
Let us say nothing about it. I was avenging my father.

Iphigenia.
And her reason? Why did she kill her husband?

Orestes.
Leave our mother's affairs alone. It is not good for you to hear them
either.

Iphigenia.
I am silent. And does Argos look to you now?

Orestes.
Menelaus rules there. I am an exile from my country.

Iphigenia.
Surely our uncle has not done our sick house an outrage?

Orestes.
No. It is fear of the Furies that drives me out of the land.

Iphigenia.
That was the madness they reported, on the shore here?

Orestes.
It is not the first time I have been seen in my misery.

Iphigenia.
I understand. The goddesses were hounding you, for our mother.

Orestes.
To force a bloody bit between my teeth.

Iphigenia.
And why did you ferry your steps to this land?

Orestes.
I came at the command of Apollo's oracles.

Iphigenia.
To do what? Can it be spoken, or must it be kept silent?

Orestes.
I will tell you. This is where my many labours began. When my mother's
evil, the evil we are keeping silent about, came into my hands, I was
driven into exile, hounded by relay after relay of Furies, until Apollo
sent my steps to Athens, to stand trial before the goddesses who are not
to be named. There is a holy court there, which Zeus once set up for
Ares when his hands were polluted with a killing.

I went there. At first not one host would take me in willingly; I was a
man the gods hated. Then some who felt ashamed set out a guest's table
for me, a table by myself, under the same roof as themselves but apart,
and by their silence they built a wall round me so that I could not
speak, so that I would be separate from them in food and in drink; and
they filled a separate cup for every man there with an equal measure of
wine, and enjoyed themselves. And I did not think it right to call my
hosts to account. I suffered in silence and pretended not to notice,
groaning deep down because I was my mother's murderer. I hear that my
misfortune has become a festival at Athens, and that the custom still
stands: the people of Pallas honour the cup of the Feast of Pitchers.

Then when I came to the hill of Ares I stood my trial. I took one
platform, and the eldest of the Furies took the other. I spoke, and I
heard the case against me, about my mother's blood, and Apollo saved me
by giving evidence, and Pallas counted out the votes with her arm,
equal on both sides, and I left the trial for murder a winner. Those
Furies who sat there and were persuaded by the verdict marked out a
holy place for themselves right beside the court. But those who were
not persuaded by the law hounded me on and on, in a chase that never
settled, until I came again to Apollo's holy ground and stretched
myself out in front of his inner shrine, fasting, and swore that I
would break off my life and die there if Apollo, who had destroyed me,
did not save me. Then Apollo cried out a voice from the golden tripod
and sent me here, to take the statue that fell from the sky and set it
up in the land of Athens.

So help me to the safety he marked out for me. If we get hold of the
goddess's image, I will be free of my madness, and I will put you
aboard my ship with its many oars and settle you back in Mycenae.
Sister, my love, my own sister, save our father's house, and save me.
Because I am finished, all of me, and the whole line of Pelops with me,
unless we take the goddess's image that came down from heaven.

Chorus.
Some terrible anger of the gods has boiled up against the seed of
Tantalus and drives them through labour after labour.

Iphigenia.
The wish was mine before you ever came here, to be in Argos and to see
you, brother. I want what you want: to release you from your labours
and set our father's sick house upright again. I hold no anger against
the man who killed me. I want it. It would take my hand out of your
slaughter, and it would save our house. But how am I to hide it from the
goddess, and from the king, when he finds the stone base standing empty
with no statue on it? That is what I am afraid of. How can I not be
killed? What excuse could I have? But if the one thing happens, if it
all happens together, and you carry off the statue and take me away on
your fine ship, then the risk is a fine one. If I am cut off from that,
then I am lost, but you might settle your own business well and get
home. Not that I am running from it. Not even if I have to die to save
you. No. When a man dies and is gone from a house, he is missed. A
woman is a weak thing.

Orestes.
I will not be your murderer as well as my mother's. Her blood is
enough. I want to be of one mind with you, and either live with you or,
dying, share the same lot. I will take you home, if I get out of here
myself, or I will die and stay here with you. But listen to my
judgement. If this were against Artemis's will, how could Apollo have
prophesied that I would bring the goddess's statue to Pallas's city,
and see your face? Putting all of that together into one, I have hope
that we will get home.
