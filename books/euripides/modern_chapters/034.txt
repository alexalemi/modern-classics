Ion
(Part 3 of 3)

Chorus.
	There is no way, no way out of death
	for me. Wretched woman that I am.
	It is plain now, all of it plain:
	the drink-offering from Dionysus,
	the grape mixed with the quick viper's
	drops of blood, for a killing,
	the offering to the dead brought out into the light.
	Disaster for my life,
	and death by stoning for my mistress.
	What flight on wings can I find,
	or what dark hollow under the earth to go down into,
	to escape the ruin of death by stones?
	Should I climb into a chariot
	behind the fastest team of four,
	or up onto the stern of a ship?

Chorus.
	There is no hiding, unless a god
	wants you hidden and steals you away.
	Poor mistress, what is left
	for your heart to suffer? And we
	who wanted to do our neighbour harm, will we
	be harmed ourselves, the way justice says we should?

Creusa.
Women. They are hunting me down to cut my throat. The vote at Delphi has
gone against me, and I am handed over to die.

Chorus.
We know, poor lady. We know what has happened to you, and where you
stand.

Creusa.
Then where do I run? I barely got a foot out of the house ahead of them,
before they could kill me. I have come this far by slipping past my
enemies unseen.

Chorus.
Where else but to the altar?

Creusa.
And what good does that do me?

Chorus.
It is forbidden to kill a suppliant.

Creusa.
And it is by the law that I am dying.

Chorus.
Only if they take you by force.

Creusa.
Well, here they come, the bitter champions of the case, hurrying this
way with their swords out.

Chorus.
Sit down on the altar, now. If you die there, you fix the guilt of your
blood on the men who kill you, and it will hunt them. Whatever comes,
you have to bear it.

(Creusa sits down on the altar in front of the temple. Ion comes in with
armed men of Delphi.)

Ion.
Cephisus, old bull-faced river, this woman's grandfather. What a viper
you bred here. What a snake, with fire in it, looking up with murder in
its flame. There is no daring she does not have. She is every bit as
deadly as the Gorgon's blood she meant to kill me with. Take hold of
her. Let the ledges of Parnassus comb out that untouched hair of hers,
when she is flung off them and goes bouncing down the rock. I have had
good luck, meeting you before I ever got to Athens and fell into a
stepmother's hands. I took the measure of your mind here, among friends:
how much harm you wished me, how much you hated me. If you had got me
inside your walls, you would have shipped me off to the house of Hades
whole, with nothing left over. But no altar is going to save you, and no
house of Apollo. That pity you are asking for belongs to me, more than
to you, and to my mother. She may not be here in body, but her name is
never far from me. Look at her, all of you. Look at the schemer, and the
trick she has woven out of the last trick. She is cowering at the god's
altar so she will not have to pay for what she did.

Creusa.
I forbid you to kill me. I say it for myself, and for the god whose
altar I am standing at.

Ion.
What do you and Apollo have in common?

Creusa.
I give my body to the god. It is sacred now. It is his to keep.

Ion.
And yet you were poisoning the god's own servant?

Creusa.
You were not Apollo's any more. You were your father's.

Ion.
But I was his, as good as born to him. That is what I really was,
whatever I was called.

Creusa.
You were, then. Now I am his, and you are not any more.

Ion.
Not with clean hands, you are not. Mine were clean, then.

Creusa.
I tried to kill you because you were the enemy of my house.

Ion.
I never marched into your country with weapons.

Creusa.
You certainly did. And you were setting fire to the house of Erechtheus.

Ion.
With what torches? What kind of flame?

Creusa.
You were going to live in what is mine, after taking it from me by
force.

Ion.
When my father was giving me land he had won for himself.

Creusa.
And what share did the sons of Aeolus ever have in the land of Pallas
Athena?

Ion.
He saved it with weapons, not words.

Creusa.
A hired ally does not get to settle in the country he was paid to
defend.

Ion.
So you tried to kill me out of fear of what I might do?

Creusa.
So that I would not die myself, when you stopped meaning to and did it.

Ion.
You are childless, and jealous, because my father found me.

Creusa.
And you? Are you going to snatch the house out of a childless woman's
hands?

Ion.
Was there no share at all of my father's for me?

Creusa.
Whatever his shield and his spear won. That is your whole inheritance.

Ion.
Leave the altar. Leave the god's seat.

Creusa.
Go and lecture your own mother, wherever she is.

Ion.
And you will pay no penalty for trying to kill me?

Creusa.
Only if you are willing to butcher me inside this sanctuary.

Ion.
What pleasure is there for you in dying among the god's sacred wreaths?

Creusa.
I will hurt somebody who has hurt me.

Ion.
Ah. It is a terrible thing, how badly the god has made the laws for
mortals, with no clever judgement behind them. Criminals should not be
allowed to sit at an altar. They should be driven off. It is not right
for a dirty hand to touch what belongs to the gods. The just, the people
who have been wronged, should have the sacred seats. Not this, where the
good man and the bad walk up to the same place and get the same
treatment from the gods.

(The Priestess of Apollo comes out of the temple carrying an old wicker
cradle.)

Priestess.
Stop, my son. I have left the tripod where the oracles are given and
stepped across this threshold. I am Apollo's Priestess, the keeper of
the tripod's ancient custom, chosen out of all the women of Delphi.

Ion.
Welcome, mother. My dear mother, though you never bore me.

Priestess.
Well, that is what I am called, and I do not mind the name.

Ion.
Did you hear how this woman schemed to kill me?

Priestess.
I heard. And you are in the wrong as well, being so savage.

Ion.
Should I not kill the people who try to kill me?

Priestess.
Wives always hate the children of an earlier marriage.

Ion.
And we hate our stepmothers, when they treat us badly.

Priestess.
Do not. You are leaving the temple and going home to your own country,
and so you should—

Ion.
What should I do, then, if I am to be advised?

Priestess.
Go to Athens with clean hands, under good omens.

Ion.
Anyone who kills his enemies has clean hands.

Priestess.
Not you. Take the words I have for you, and listen.

Ion.
Say them. You mean me well. Whatever you say will come from that.

Priestess.
Do you see this basket I am carrying in the crook of my arm?

Ion.
I see an old cradle, with the sacred wool bands still tied round it.

Priestess.
This is what I found you in, once, a new-born baby.

Ion.
What? That is a new story you are bringing in.

Priestess.
I kept it quiet. Now I am showing it.

Ion.
Why did you hide it, all those years ago, when you took me in?

Priestess.
The god wanted to keep you as his servant in his house.

Ion.
And now he does not? How am I supposed to know that?

Priestess.
He has told you who your father is. He is sending you out of this
country.

Ion.
Did he order you to keep this, or how do you come to have it?

Priestess.
Apollo put it into my mind, that day—

Ion.
To do what? Say it. Finish what you are saying.

Priestess.
To keep what I had found, until the time that is here now.

Ion.
And what does it hold for me? Gain, or harm?

Priestess.
Hidden in here are the baby-clothes you were wrapped in.

Ion.
You are bringing me clues to find my mother?

Priestess.
Yes, because now the god wants it. Before, he did not.

Ion.
This is a day of blessed things to see.

Priestess.
Take them, then, and work hard at finding the woman who bore you.

Ion.
I will search all of Asia, and to the borders of Europe.

Priestess.
You will find that out for yourself. I brought you up for the god's
sake, my child, and for his sake I am giving these back to you. He
wanted me to take them and keep them safe, without ever telling me to;
why he wanted it, I cannot say. No living person knew that we had them,
or where they were hidden. Goodbye. I kiss you as your own mother would.
Start your search for her where you have to start: first, was it some
girl of Delphi who bore you and left you in this temple; and after that,
was it any Greek girl at all. That is everything you will get from me,
and from Apollo, who has had a share in your fortune.

(The Priestess goes back into the temple, leaving the cradle with Ion.)

Ion.
Ah. Ah. The tears are running down my face. My mind has gone back to the
day when the woman who bore me, married in secret, sold me off in secret
and never gave me her breast. I had no name. I lived a servant's life in
the god's house. What came from the god was good; what came from my luck
was heavy. The time when I should have been spoilt in a mother's arms,
tasting some pleasure in life, I was robbed of the dearest thing there
is, a mother's care. And she is wretched too, the woman who bore me. She
has suffered the same thing: she lost the joy of a son.

Now I will take this cradle and carry it to the god as an offering, so
that I do not find anything in it I do not want to find. If it turns out
some slave woman bore me, finding my mother would be worse than leaving
her alone in silence. Apollo, I dedicate this to your temple. And yet,
what am I doing? I am fighting the god's own wish. He kept my mother's
tokens for me. It has to be opened. I have to have the nerve. Whatever
is fated, I could never step past it. Sacred bands, what have you been
hiding from me? And you, fastenings, that guarded what was mine and dear
to me? Look at the lid on this rounded cradle. By some miracle it has
not aged. There is no mould on the wickerwork, and yet a long time has
gone by over these treasures.

Creusa.
What am I seeing? A vision I never hoped for.

Ion.
You be quiet. You have known plenty before this that—

Creusa.
Silence is not for me. Do not tell me what to do. I can see the basket I
once left you in, my child, when you were a baby still, no bigger than
that, in the cave of Cecrops under the Long Rocks. I am leaving this
altar, even if I have to die for it.

(Creusa comes down from the altar and runs to the cradle.)

Ion.
Take hold of her. The god has driven her mad. She has leapt off the
altar and left the carved images. Tie her arms.

Creusa.
Go on and cut my throat, do not stop. I am going to hold on to this
cradle, and to you, and to the things hidden in it that are yours.

Ion.
Is this not monstrous? I am being seized and claimed with a story.

Creusa.
No. You are being found. Found by the people who love you, as one of
their own.

Ion.
I am one of yours? And you were trying to murder me behind my back?

Creusa.
You are my son, if a son is what parents hold dearest.

Ion.
Stop weaving. I will catch you out, properly.

Creusa.
Let me come to that. That is the mark I am shooting at, child.

Ion.
Is this basket empty, or does it have something in it?

Creusa.
Your clothes. The ones I left you in, once.

Ion.
And you will name them before you see them?

Creusa.
If I cannot describe them, I agree to die.

Ion.
Speak. There is something frightening about your nerve.

Creusa.
Look. A piece of weaving I made when I was a child—

Ion.
What kind? Girls weave plenty of things.

Creusa.
Not finished. A first lesson at the shuttle, more like.

Ion.
What pattern? You will not catch me that way.

Creusa.
A Gorgon, in the middle of the threads of the cloth.

Ion.
Zeus. What fate is hunting me down?

Creusa.
And the edge is fringed with snakes, like an aegis.

Ion.
Look. There it is. The weaving. It is like finding an oracle come true.

Creusa.
Oh, the work of my girlhood at the loom, after all this time.

Ion.
Is there anything besides this, or does your luck stop at one?

Creusa.
Snakes. An old thing, made of solid gold: Athena's gift. She tells us to
bring our children up wearing them, copies of the snakes she set to
guard Erichthonius, long ago.

Ion.
To do what with? What are they for, the golden things? Tell me.

Creusa.
Necklaces, for a new-born child to wear, my son.

Ion.
Here they are, inside. The third thing. I want to know the third.

Creusa.
I put a wreath of olive round you then, from the tree Athena first
brought to her rock. If it is there, it has never lost its green. It is
flourishing still, because it came from an olive that nothing can touch.

Ion.
Mother. My dearest mother. I am so glad to see you, and I am kissing
your face, and your face is glad too.

Creusa.
My child. My light, brighter to your mother than the sun — the god will
forgive me for that — I have you in my arms, found when I had stopped
hoping, when I thought you were living under the ground with the dead
and with Persephone.

Ion.
But here I am, dear mother, in your arms, the one who died and did not
die.

Creusa.
	Io, io. Wide bright folds of the sky,
	what voice can I find, what cry
	can I shout? Where did it come from,
	this pleasure I never looked for? Where
	did we get this joy?

Ion.
Anything could have happened to me, mother, sooner than this: that I
turn out to be yours.

Creusa.
I am still trembling with fear.

Ion.
That you are holding me and do not have me?

Creusa.
	I had thrown my hopes
	far away from me.
	Io, woman, where, where did you take my baby
	up into your arms?
	Whose hand carried him into the house of Apollo?

Ion.
A god did this. But for the rest of our luck, let us be as fortunate as
we were unfortunate before.

Creusa.
	Child, you were born in tears,
	and taken from your mother's arms with wailing,
	and now I breathe against your cheek
	and I have found the most blessed happiness there is.

Ion.
	When you say what you feel, you say it for both of us.

Creusa.
	I am not childless any more. No more without a son.
	The house has its hearth again, the land has its kings,
	Erechtheus is young again,
	and the house that was born from the earth no longer looks at night:
	it looks up at the sun's torches.

Ion.
Mother, my father is here too. Let him share this happiness I have given
the two of you.

Creusa.
	My child,
	what are you saying? How I am found out, how I am found out.

Ion.
	What do you mean?

Creusa.
	You come from somewhere else. Somewhere else.

Ion.
	Oh. Did you bear me as a girl, a bastard?

Creusa.
	No torches, no dancing,
	no wedding song of mine,
	child, when your head came into the world.

Ion.
	Aiai. My birth is shameful. Mother, where am I from?

Creusa.
	Let the Gorgon-killer be my witness—

Ion.
	What are you saying?

Creusa.
	She who sits on my rocks,
	on the hill where the olive grows,
	Athena, on her throne there—

Ion.
	You are speaking in riddles to me, not plainly.

Creusa.
	Beside the rock where the nightingales sing,
	with Apollo—

Ion.
	Why do you name Apollo?

Creusa.
	I lay in a bed he kept secret.

Ion.
	Go on. You are about to tell me something good, something lucky for me.

Creusa.
	And when the tenth month came round,
	I bore you, a hidden labour, to Apollo.

Ion.
	The dearest thing I have ever heard, if it is true.

Creusa.
	And as your baby-clothes I wrapped round you
	this, my girl's work, done in secret from my mother,
	the wanderings of my shuttle.
	I gave you no milk, no breast,
	none of a mother's nursing, no water from my hands to wash you.
	In an empty cave you were thrown out
	to be killed by the beaks of birds, a feast for them,
	thrown out to Hades.

Ion.
	Mother, what a terrible thing you had the heart to do.

Creusa.
	I was bound fast in fear, child,
	and I threw your life away.
	I killed you, and I did not want to.

Ion.
	And I nearly killed you. That would have been unholy.

Creusa.
	Io. Terrible, our fortunes then,
	and terrible these now. We are spun round
	from there to here, from bad luck into good luck again,
	and the winds are shifting.
	Let them hold. What went before was evil enough. Now
	let a fair wind rise out of the evil, my boy.

Chorus.
Let no one, ever, think anything is past hoping for, when they look at
what is happening now.

Ion.
Fortune. You have already changed thousands of lives, into misery and
then back into doing well. Look at the line my life came to: killing my
mother, and suffering what I never deserved. Ah. Is this the kind of
thing you can learn every day, inside the bright circle of the sun?
Mother, I have found you, and finding you is dear to me, and as far as I
am concerned there is nothing to complain of in my birth. But the rest I
want to say to you alone. Come here. I want to say it in your ear and
throw a cover of darkness over the whole business. Be careful, mother.
Did you slip, the way girls do, into a secret affair, and then pin the
blame on the god? Are you saying you bore me to Apollo to escape the
shame of my birth, when it was not a god who fathered me?

Creusa.
No. By Athena, Victory, who once stood shield to shield with Zeus in his
chariot against the giants born from the earth: no mortal man is your
father, my son. The one who brought you up is. Lord Apollo.

Ion.
Then how could he give his own child to another father, and say I was
Xuthus's son by birth?

Creusa.
He did not say born. He is giving you, his own son, as a gift. A friend
might give his own son to a friend, to be master of his house.

Ion.
Whether the god speaks the truth or gives empty oracles, that is what is
troubling my mind, mother, and it is a fair thing to be troubled by.

Creusa.
Then listen to what has just come to me, child. Apollo is doing you a
kindness. He is settling you in a noble house. If you had been called
the god's son, you would never have had a house and a whole inheritance,
or a father's name. How could you, when I was hiding the marriage
myself, and killing you in secret? He is helping you, by handing you
over to another father.

Ion.
I am not going to chase this so lightly. I will go into the temple and
ask Apollo himself whether I am a mortal man's son or his. Ah. Who is
that, above the house where the incense burns, showing a face like the
sun, a god's face? Run, mother. We should not be looking at the gods'
business. Unless it is right that we see it.

(Athena appears above the temple.)

Athena.
Do not run. I am not an enemy you are running from. I am well disposed
to you in Athens and here alike. I am Pallas Athena, and your country is
named after me. I have come at a run, sent by Apollo. He did not think
it right to come into your sight himself, in case blame for what
happened before came up between you. So he sends me to give you his
words. This woman bore you, and Apollo is your father. He gives you to
the man he gave you to, who did not beget you, so that you can be
counted as belonging to a house of the noblest blood. When the business
was uncovered and reported, he was afraid you would die by your mother's
plotting and she by your hand, and he saved you both by his devices. The
lord meant to keep all this silent and make it known in Athens: that she
is your mother, and you were born of her and of Apollo, your father. But
to finish the matter, and the god's oracles, which are why I yoked my
chariot, listen, both of you.

Creusa, take this boy and go to the land of Cecrops, and set him on the
royal throne. He is of Erechtheus's line, and he has the right to rule
my country, and he will be famous through all Greece. His sons, four of
them from one root, will give their names to the land and to the tribes
of the people who live on my rock. Geleon will be the first. Then the
second, the Hopletes, and the Argades, and the Aegicores, whose tribe
takes its name from my aegis. And their children in turn, when the fated
time comes, will settle the island cities of the Cyclades and the coasts
of the mainland, which gives my country strength. They will settle the
plains on either side of the strait, on the two continents, Asia and
Europe, and for this boy's name's sake they will be called Ionians, and
they will be famous. To you and Xuthus together a family is coming too:
Dorus, from whom the Dorian city will be sung of through the land of
Pelops; and the second, Achaeus, who will be king of the coast near
Rhium, and a people will be marked out to carry his name. Apollo has
done everything well. First, he brought you through childbirth without
sickness, so that the people around you never knew. Then, when you had
borne this child and put him out in his baby-clothes, he ordered Hermes
to snatch the baby up in his arms and ferry him here, and he brought him
up, and did not let him breathe out his life. So now, keep silent about
this boy being yours, so that Xuthus can enjoy his belief in peace, and
you, lady, can go home with your blessings in your hands. And goodbye to
you both. After this breathing-space from your troubles, I announce a
happy fate for you.

Ion.
Pallas Athena, daughter of Zeus the greatest, we accept your words
without disbelief. I am convinced that I am Apollo's son, and hers. And
even before, that was not hard to believe.

Creusa.
Now hear from me. I praise Apollo, who I never praised before, because
the child he neglected once, he is giving back to me. These gates look
beautiful to me now, and the god's oracle too, when they were hateful
before. Now I even hang gladly from the knockers on the doors, and I
greet the gates.

Athena.
I approve of your change, and of your speaking well of the god. What the
gods do is always slow, somehow. But in the end it is not weak.

Creusa.
My child, let us go home.

Athena.
Go. I will follow you.

Creusa.
A guide who is worthy of us.

Athena.
And one who loves your city.

Creusa.
Go and sit on the ancient throne.

Ion.
It is a possession worth having.

Chorus.
Apollo, son of Zeus and Leto, goodbye. Whoever's house is being driven
hard by disaster should honour the gods and keep his courage. In the end
the good get what they deserve, and the bad, being what they are, never
do well.
