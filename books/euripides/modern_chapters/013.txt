Andromache
(Part 3 of 3)

Nurse.
Why are you torturing yourself like this? The blows the gods send come
to everyone, sooner or later. Today, or some other day.

Hermione.
	You left me, you left me, father, alone on the shore,
	like a boat with no oar, stranded, by myself.
	He will kill me, he will kill me. I will never live again
	under this roof I was married in.
	Whose statue can I run to and cling to for protection?
	Or shall I fall at a slave's knees, a slave myself?
	If only I could fly out of the land of Phthia,
	a bird with dark wings,
	or be that ship of pine
	that was the first to row through the Clashing Rocks,
	the dark ones, on its first voyage.

Nurse.
My child, I did not like the excess in you before, when you went too
far against that Trojan woman, and I do not like it now, this terror
you are working yourself into. Your husband is not going to throw away
his marriage to you because a barbarian woman talked him round with
some flimsy arguments. You are not a prisoner he brought back from
Troy. You are a great man's daughter, and he took you with a rich
dowry, out of a city that has done more than well for itself. And your
father is not going to betray you and let you be thrown out of this
house, whatever you are afraid of. Now go inside, and stop making a
show of yourself in front of the doors. If people see you out here in
front of the house, child, it will be held against you.

(The Nurse goes into the house. A young man comes in quickly, worn
from the road and foreign-looking. It is Orestes.)

Chorus.
Look. Here is a stranger, from some other country by the look of him,
hurrying towards us as fast as he can walk.

Orestes.
Women of this foreign land, is this the house of Achilles's son, the
king's palace?

Chorus.
It is. But who are you, to ask?

Orestes.
The son of Agamemnon and Clytemnestra. My name is Orestes. I am on my
way to the oracle of Zeus at Dodona. And since I have reached Phthia, I
thought I would find out about a kinswoman of mine, whether she is
alive and well: Hermione, from Sparta. She lives a long way from us,
but she is dear to me all the same.

Hermione.
Son of Agamemnon, you are a harbour in a storm to a sailor. By these
knees of yours, I beg you, pity me. You were asking how I am. I am not
doing well. And I wrap my arms round your knees, and they are as good
as the wool bands a suppliant lays on an altar.

Orestes.
Wait. What is this? Am I wrong, or is this the mistress of the house I
am looking at, the daughter of Menelaus?

Hermione.
The only daughter Helen, the child of Tyndareus, ever bore my father in
his house. Have no doubt of it.

Orestes.
Apollo, Healer, grant us a release from our troubles. What is the
matter? Is it gods or men who are hurting you?

Hermione.
Some of it is my own doing. Some of it is the man who has me. Some of
it is a god's. Whichever way I look, I am destroyed.

Orestes.
What disaster can happen to a woman who has no children yet, unless it
has to do with her bed?

Hermione.
That is exactly where I am sick. You led me straight to it.

Orestes.
Your husband loves another bed instead of yours?

Hermione.
The prisoner. Hector's wife.

Orestes.
That is a bad thing you are telling me. One man with two beds.

Hermione.
That is how it is. So I fought back.

Orestes.
You plotted against the woman? The way a woman does?

Hermione.
Death. For her and for her bastard child.

Orestes.
And did you kill them, or did something happen and take them out of
your hands?

Hermione.
Old Peleus did. He took the side of the people beneath him.

Orestes.
Did anyone share this killing with you?

Hermione.
My father. He came from Sparta for exactly that.

Orestes.
And then he was beaten by an old man's arm?

Hermione.
By shame. And he is gone, and left me alone.

Orestes.
I understand. You are afraid of your husband, because of what you did.

Hermione.
You have it. He would be right to kill me. What is there to say? But I
beg you, in the name of Zeus who watches over our family, send me as
far from this country as you can, or to my father's house. Because the
very walls here seem to have found a voice and are driving me out, and
the whole land of Phthia hates me. And if my husband comes home from
Apollo's oracle before that, he will kill me on the most shameful
charge there is, or I will be a slave to a bastard bed that I used to
be mistress of.

So how, as somebody is going to ask, did you go so wrong? It was women
coming into the house that ruined me. Bad women. They puffed me up with
this kind of talk. "You are going to put up with her? That prisoner,
the lowest of the low, a slave, in your house, sharing your bed with
you? By the Queen of heaven, if it were my house she would not be alive
to look at the daylight and get the harvest of my marriage." And I
listened to what those Sirens said, the clever, scheming, twisty
chatter of them, and I filled up with folly like a sail with wind. What
did I need to watch my husband for? I had everything I could want.
Wealth, plenty of it. I was mistress of the house. Any children I had
would be legitimate, and hers would be bastards, half slaves, and mine
would own them.

Never, never, and I will not say this only once, should a man with any
sense who has a wife let women come into his house to visit her. They
are the teachers of everything bad. One of them is making something out
of it, and helps to spoil the marriage. Another has slipped herself and
wants company in her sickness. And a great many of them are simply
lewd, and that is how the houses of men get infected. So guard the
doors of your houses well, with bolts and bars. Nothing healthy ever
comes of women visiting from outside. Only a great deal of harm.

Chorus.
You have let your tongue run too freely against your own kind. We can
forgive you for it, in your position. But even so, a woman ought to
dress up the sickness of women, not expose it.

Orestes.
It was a clever man who taught people to listen to the argument from
the other side too. I knew about the turmoil in this house, and about
the quarrel between you and Hector's wife, and I kept watch and waited,
to see whether you would stay here, or whether you were frightened
enough by the killing of a captive woman to want out of this house.

I did not come because I had any great respect for the messages you
sent me. I came so that if you gave me an opening, as you are giving me
one now, I could take you away from this house. Because you were mine,
before. You are living with this man now because your father is a
coward. Before he ever crossed into Trojan territory he gave you to me
as my wife, and then afterwards he promised you to the man who has you
now, if he took the city of Troy. And when Achilles's son came home
here, I forgave your father, and I pleaded with the bridegroom to give
up his marriage to you. I told him what had happened to me, and the god
that was on me at the time: that I could marry from among my friends,
but not easily from outside, being in exile from my home the way I am.
And he was an outrage to me. He threw my mother's murder in my face,
and the goddesses with blood in their eyes who hunt me for it. And I
was low, because of what had happened to my house, and I hurt, I hurt
badly, but I bore what had happened, and I went away robbed of my
marriage to you, against my will.

So now, since your luck has turned upside down, and you have fallen
into this disaster and have no way out, I will take you away from this
house and put you in your father's hands. Blood is a strong thing. And
when things go wrong there is nothing better than one of your own.

Hermione.
As for my marriage, that is my father's business. It is not for me to
decide. But take me out of this house as fast as you can, before my
husband gets here first and comes home, or before old Peleus finds out
that I am deserting the house and comes after me with horses.

Orestes.
Never mind the old man's arm. And do not be afraid of Achilles's son,
for all the outrage he did me. There is a machine built for him, a
death trap, knotted with knots that will not slip, and this hand built
it. I will not say what it is before it is done, but when it is being
done, the rock of Delphi will know. If my allies in the Pythian country
keep their oaths, the mother-killer will teach him that nobody marries
the woman who should have been mine. He will pay bitterly for demanding
that Lord Apollo answer for his father's blood. It will do him no good
to have changed his mind, and to be making his peace with the god now.
Between Apollo and my slanders he will die a bad death, and he will
learn what my hatred is. When men hate a god, the god turns their fate
upside down and does not let them think big.

(Orestes and Hermione go out together.)

Chorus.
	Apollo, who towered up the strong walls on the hill of Troy,
	and you, god of the sea, who drive your dark horses
	over the salt water,
	why did you take the work of your own hands
	and give it, dishonoured, to Ares,
	the master of the spear,
	and let go of Troy,
	poor Troy, poor Troy?

Chorus.
	So many chariots with fine horses you yoked
	on the banks of the Simois,
	so many bloody races of men you set going
	with no wreath for the winner.
	And the kings of Troy are dead and gone,
	and the fire no longer blazes on the altars in Troy
	for the gods,
	with the sweet smoke of incense.

Chorus.
	The son of Atreus is gone, by the hands of his wife,
	and she paid for his murder with her own death.
	Her children took it from her.
	A god's, a god's command turned against her, out of the oracle,
	when Agamemnon's son travelled from Argos
	and stood in the holy of holies,
	and became his mother's killer.
	God, Apollo, how am I to believe it?

Chorus.
	And through the market squares of Greece many women
	sang their groans for children lost,
	and wives left their homes
	for another man's bed.
	Not on you alone, and not on the people you love,
	did the heavy grief come down.
	Greece bore the plague, the plague. And it crossed over
	to the rich fields of the Phrygians too,
	a thunderbolt raining down the blood that Hades loves.

(Peleus comes in, hurrying.)

Peleus.
Women of Phthia, I am asking, so tell me. I heard a report, nothing
clear, that the daughter of Menelaus has left this house and gone, and
I have come in a hurry to find out whether it is true. When friends are
away, the people at home have to look after their affairs.

Chorus.
Peleus, what you heard is right. And it would not be decent of me to
hide the trouble I find myself in. The queen has fled from this house.

Peleus.
Afraid of what? Tell me the whole thing.

Chorus.
Afraid of her husband. That he would throw her out of the house.

Peleus.
For plotting the death of his child? Surely not.

Chorus.
Yes. And she was afraid of the prisoner too.

Peleus.
Did she leave the house with her father, or with somebody else?

Chorus.
Agamemnon's son came and took her out of the country.

Peleus.
And what does he hope to get out of it? Does he mean to marry her?

Chorus.
Yes. And he is arranging the death of your son's son.

Peleus.
Lying in wait for him, or meeting him face to face in a fight?

Chorus.
In the holy sanctuary of Apollo, with the men of Delphi.

Peleus.
Oh. This is dangerous, and it is now. Will somebody go, as fast as he
can, to the Pythian hearth and tell our friends there what is
happening here, before Achilles's son is killed by his enemies?

(A Messenger comes in.)

Messenger.
Oh, oh. What news I am bringing, and I am the unlucky one who has to
bring it, to you, old man, and to everyone who loved my master.

Peleus.
Aiai. My heart knows already. It is expecting something.

Messenger.
You have no grandson, old Peleus. I am telling you plainly. That is
how he is, cut with swords, by the men of Delphi and by the stranger
from Mycenae.

Chorus.
Ah, ah. What are you doing, old man? Do not fall. Hold yourself up.

Peleus.
I am nothing. I am destroyed. My voice is gone, and the strength is
gone out of my legs.

Messenger.
Listen. If you want to avenge your own, straighten yourself up and
hear what was done.

Peleus.
Fate, how tightly you have wrapped me up, a miserable old man at the
very end of his life. How did I lose him, the only son of my only son?
Tell me. I do not want to hear it, and I want to hear it all the same.

Messenger.
When we reached the famous ground of Apollo, we spent three bright
circuits of the sun filling our eyes with the sights. And that, it
turns out, was already suspicious. The people who live round the god's
shrine began to gather in knots and circles, and Agamemnon's son went
through the town whispering poison into every ear. "Do you see this
man, walking in and out of the god's hollow chambers, the ones full of
gold, the treasuries of the whole world? He is here for the second
time, on the same errand he came on before. He wants to sack the temple
of Apollo." And from that an angry surge ran through the city. The
magistrates crowded into their council chambers, and the men who have
charge of the god's property quietly posted a guard among the
colonnades.

We knew nothing of any of this yet. We took sheep, reared on the leafy
slopes of Parnassus, and went and stood at the altars, with our Delphian
sponsors and the Pythian seers. And somebody said, "Young man, what
shall we pray to the god for, on your behalf? What have you come for?"
And he said, "I want to make amends to Apollo for a fault I committed
before. I once demanded that he pay me for my father's blood." And
right there Orestes's story showed how strong it was: that my master
was lying, that he had come for something shameful.

He goes up inside the temple, onto its stone platform, to pray to
Apollo in front of the oracle, and he is at the burnt offering. And
there was an ambush waiting for him, men with swords, in the shade of
the laurel, and Clytemnestra's son was one of them, the one who had
stitched the whole plot together. My master stood in full view of
everyone, praying to the god, and they, with their sharpened swords,
stabbed Achilles's son from behind, and he had no armour on. He stepped
back. He had not been hit anywhere that killed. He drew his sword, and
he snatched the armour that was hanging on pegs on a pillar, and he
stood on the altar, a soldier terrible to look at, and shouted at the
sons of Delphi, asking them this: "Why are you killing me? I came here
on a holy errand. What am I dying for?" And of all those thousands
standing near, not one man said a word. They threw stones at him,
instead, out of their hands.

Pounded from every side by a thick snowstorm of them, he held his
armour out in front of him and guarded against the hits, stretching his
shield out on this side and on that. It did no good. Everything was
coming at him at once: arrows, javelins with throwing straps, spits
with two points pulled loose from the fire, the butcher's knives they
kill oxen with, landing in front of his feet. You would have seen your
boy dance a terrible war dance then, dodging what they threw. And when
they had him ringed round and were giving him no room to breathe, he
left the altar hearth where the sheep are laid, and he leapt, the leap
he leapt at Troy, straight at them. And they turned their backs and
ran, like doves when they see the hawk. Many of them fell in the crush,
from wounds, or trampled by each other in the narrow doorways, and a
scream that had no holiness in it rose in that holy house and echoed
off the rocks. And in a kind of calm my master stood there, shining in
his bright armour.

Until a voice came out of the middle of the inner shrine, something
terrible, something that made your skin creep, and it roused that
crowd and turned them back to fight. Then Achilles's son fell. A
Delphian struck him in the ribs with a sharpened sword, a Delphian
killed him, with plenty of others to help. And when he fell to the
ground, who did not bring a blade to him, who did not bring a stone,
throwing, battering? That whole beautiful body was ruined with savage
wounds. And when he was dead and lying beside the altar, they threw him
out of the temple where the incense burns.

We snatched him up as fast as we could and we are bringing him to you,
old man, to wail over and weep over, and to honour with a grave in the
earth. That is what the lord who gives oracles to everyone else, the
judge of right for all mankind, did to Achilles's son, when he was
there to make amends. He remembered an old quarrel, the way a bad man
does. So how can he be wise?

(The Messenger goes. The body of Neoptolemus is carried in on a bier.)

Chorus.
	And look, here is the king already, carried in,
	come from the land of Delphi to his home.
	Wretched the man it happened to, and wretched too,
	old man, are you. This is Achilles's cub
	you are taking into the house, and not the way you wanted.
	You have met with evil yourself,
	and fallen into one fate with him.

Peleus.
	Oh, what evil is this I am looking at,
	taking it into my hands and my house.
	Io moi moi. Aiai.
	City of Thessaly, we are destroyed,
	we are finished. There is no family left me now, no children
	left in the house.
	I am cursed with what I have suffered. Who is left,
	what friend, that I can turn my eyes to and be glad?
	Dear mouth, dear cheek, dear hands,
	if only a god had killed you under the walls of Troy
	beside the bank of the Simois.

Chorus.
Then he would have died with honour, old man, and you would have been
the happier for it.

Peleus.
	Marriage, marriage, you have destroyed
	this house and my city. Aiai.
	E, e. My child,
	if only that marriage with its cursed name
	had never wrapped my family, my children and my house,
	in the death that Hermione brought
	down on you, child.
	If only the lightning had killed her first.
	And if only you, a man, had never fastened
	on a god, on Apollo, the blame for the arrow that killed
	your father, the son of a god.

Chorus.
	Ottototoi. My master is dead. I begin the lament,
	the way the custom is
	for those who are gone below.

Peleus.
	Ottototoi. And I take it up after you, wretched as I am,
	an old man
	with no luck left, and I weep.

Chorus.
	It was a god's decree. A god brought this disaster about.

Peleus.
	Dear one,
	you have left the house empty.
	Oh, oh, and left me wretched,
	an old man with no children.

Chorus.
	You should have died, old man, died before your children.

Peleus.
	Shall I not tear my hair?
	Shall I not bring down on my head
	the deadly blow of my own hand? City,
	Apollo has robbed me
	of both my children.

Chorus.
	You have suffered so much evil, and seen so much, unlucky old man.
	What life
	will you have from now on?

Peleus.
	No children. Alone. No end to the evil.
	I will drain out
	my sorrows to the last drop, down to Hades.

Chorus.
	For nothing, then, the gods blessed you on your wedding day.

Peleus.
	Flown away,
	all of it gone. It lies
	far short of the high boasts.

Chorus.
	Alone, in a lonely house, you go up and down.

Peleus.
	You are gone, city, city.
	And this sceptre, let it go, down on the ground.
	And you, daughter of Nereus, in your cave in the dark,
	you will see me
	utterly destroyed, falling to the earth.

Chorus.
	Io, io.
	What is that moving? What god am I feeling near?
	Girls, look, look up.
	Some god is being ferried through the white air
	and coming down onto the plains of Phthia,
	where the horses graze.

(Thetis appears above the house.)

Thetis.
Peleus, for the sake of the marriage we made long ago, I have come. I
am Thetis, and I have left the house of Nereus. First, I tell you not
to take the trouble you are in too hard. I too, who ought to have borne
children I would never have to weep for, have lost the son I had by
you, Achilles, the fast runner, the first man in Greece. Now I will
tell you why I have come, and you listen.

Take this dead man, Achilles's son, to the Pythian hearth and bury him
there, a reproach to Delphi, so that his grave announces the violent
murder done by Orestes's hand. As for the prisoner, Andromache I mean,
she must go and live in the land of the Molossians, old man, joined in
a lawful marriage to Helenus, and this boy with her, the last of the
line of Aeacus. From him a line of kings must come down, one after
another, and rule Molossia in prosperity. Because your family and mine,
old man, is not to be uprooted like this. Nor is Troy's. The gods care
about Troy too, even though it fell, because Pallas Athena was set on
it.

And you, so that you will know what it was worth to have shared my bed,
I, a goddess born and the daughter of a god, will set free from
everything that mortals suffer, and make you a god, immortal and
unfading. And from then on you will live with me in the house of
Nereus, a god with a goddess, for the rest of time. From there you will
walk out of the sea with dry feet and see the son you and I loved
best, Achilles, living in his island home on the White Shore, inside
the Black Sea. But now go to Delphi, the city the gods built, and take
this body with you, and when you have buried him in the earth, come
back and sit down in the hollow of the old rock of Sepias, in the cave
there, and wait, until I come out of the sea with fifty daughters of
Nereus, dancing, to escort you. What is fated must be carried through.
This is what Zeus has decided. And stop grieving for the dead. This is
the vote the gods have passed on every human being. Everyone owes a
death.

Peleus.
Lady. Noble wife who shared my bed, daughter of Nereus, I salute you.
What you are doing is worthy of you, and of the children who came from
you. I stop my grieving, goddess, since you order it, and when I have
buried this boy I will go to the glens of Pelion, where I first took
your beautiful body in my arms.

So. Should a man with any sense not marry out of a good family, and
give his daughter to good men, and have no appetite for a bad wife, not
even if she brings a fortune into the house with her? Then the gods
would never make things go badly for him.

(Thetis vanishes. Peleus follows the body out.)

Chorus.
	Many are the shapes of what the gods do,
	and many the things they bring about that no one expected.
	What we counted on did not happen,
	and for what no one counted on, a god found a way.
	That is how this business ended.
