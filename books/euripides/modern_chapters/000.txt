Alcestis
(Part 1 of 3)

(Pherae, in Thessaly, before the house of Admetus. Apollo comes out of
it, carrying his bow.)

Apollo.
House of Admetus. I put up with a servant's table here, and I was a
god when I did it. Zeus was the cause. He killed my son Asclepius,
driving the lightning into his chest, and I lost my temper and killed
the Cyclopes, who forge his fire for him, and for that my father made
me work for wages in a mortal's house. So I came to this country and
kept a stranger's cattle, and I have kept this house safe to this very
day. I am a decent god, and I had found a decent man in the son of
Pheres, and I saved him from dying. I cheated the Fates. The goddesses
agreed with me that Admetus could slip out of the death that was
waiting for him, if he gave the powers below another corpse in
exchange. He went round everyone he loved and put the question to them,
his father, the old mother who bore him, and he found nobody willing to
die in his place and stop looking at the light, except his wife. She is
in the house now, in his arms, gasping out her life. This is the day
she is fated to die and leave the living. And I am leaving too. I love
this roof, but I cannot let the pollution touch me inside it. And here
is Death himself, already, close by. The priest of the dead. He is on
his way to take her down to the house of Hades, and he has timed it
exactly. He has been watching for this day, the day she has to die.

(Death comes in.)

Death.
	Ah. Ah.
	What are you doing at this house? Why are you hanging about here,
	Apollo? Cheating again, are you, snatching away the rights
	of the gods below and stopping them short?
	Was it not enough for you to block Admetus's death
	and trip the Fates with a dirty trick?
	And now here you are again, standing guard over her,
	with your bow in your hand,
	over the daughter of Pelias, who promised
	to die for her husband and set him free.

Apollo.
Steady. I have justice on my side, and good arguments.

Death.
Then what is the bow for, if you have justice?

Apollo.
Habit. I always carry it.

Death.
And you always help this house more than is right.

Apollo.
I cannot bear it when a friend is in trouble.

Death.
So you will rob me of this second body as well?

Apollo.
I did not take the first one from you by force.

Death.
Then how is he up here on the earth and not under it?

Apollo.
He gave you his wife instead. The one you have come for now.

Death.
And I will take her down under the ground.

Apollo.
Take her, then. I doubt I could talk you out of it.

Death.
Out of killing whoever is due? That is my job.

Apollo.
No. Your job is to deal death to people who are about to die anyway.

Death.
I see your argument. And I see what you want.

Apollo.
Is there any way, then, for Alcestis to reach old age?

Death.
There is not. I enjoy my rights too, you know.

Apollo.
You cannot take more than one life either way.

Death.
When the young die, the prize I win is bigger.

Apollo.
If she dies old, she will get a rich funeral.

Death.
You are writing the law to suit the rich, Apollo.

Apollo.
What did you say? Have you been clever all this time, without my
noticing?

Death.
People with money would buy the right to die old.

Apollo.
So you will not do me this one favour.

Death.
No. You know my ways.

Apollo.
I do. Mortals hate them, and the gods loathe them.

Death.
You cannot have everything that is not yours to have.

Apollo.
Well, you will be stopped, all the same, savage as you are. There is a
man coming to the house of Pheres. Eurystheus has sent him after a team
of horses, in the freezing country of Thrace. He will be a guest here,
in Admetus's house, and he will take this woman away from you by force.
You will get no thanks from me, then, and you will do exactly what I am
asking anyway, and I will hate you for it.

Death.
Talk all you like, you will get nothing more. This woman is going down
to the house of Hades. I am going in for her now, to begin the rite
with my sword. Once this blade has cut a lock of hair from a head, that
head belongs to the gods below.

(Apollo goes. Death goes into the house. The Chorus, old men of Pherae,
come in and gather at the door.)

Chorus.
	Why is it so quiet in front of the house?
	Why has the house of Admetus gone silent?
	And there is not one of the family near,
	to tell me whether I should be mourning
	the queen dead already, or whether she still lives
	and looks at this light, the daughter of Pelias,
	Alcestis, who to me, and to everyone,
	seemed the best wife
	a husband ever had.

Chorus.
	Does anyone hear a groan,
	or the sound of hands beating, inside the house,
	or a wail, as if it were over?
	No, and there is not a servant either
	posted at the gate. Oh, Healer,
	if only you would appear
	between the waves of this ruin.

Chorus.
	If she were dead they would not be silent.
	She is a corpse already.
	She has not been carried out of the house.
	How do you know? I am not so sure. What makes you confident?
	How could Admetus give a wife as good as that
	a funeral with nobody there?

Chorus.
	And in front of the gates I see
	no spring water, as custom has it,
	standing at the door of the dead.
	No lock of hair on the doorstep,
	cut off, the way it falls
	in mourning. No young women's hands
	beating out the grief.

Chorus.
	And yet this is the appointed day.
	What are you saying?
	The day she has to go under the earth.
	You have touched my heart. You have touched my mind.
	When the good are worn down to nothing,
	anyone who has ever
	been counted decent must grieve.

Chorus.
	There is nowhere on earth
	you could send a ship,
	not to Lycia,
	not to the waterless seat
	of Ammon in the desert,
	to save that poor woman's life.
	Death is coming for her, sheer as a cliff,
	and I do not know which god's altar
	I should go to now
	with a sheep to kill.

Chorus.
	Only one man, if he were still alive
	and his eyes saw this light,
	Apollo's son: then she might have left
	the dark seats
	and the gates of Hades and come back.
	He used to raise the dead,
	until the lightning Zeus throws struck him,
	the fire from heaven.
	But now, what hope of her life
	is there left for me to hold?

Chorus.
	The king and queen have done everything already.
	On the altars of every god
	the sacrifices stand, full and dripping blood,
	and there is no cure for the evil. None.

(A Maid comes out of the house, in tears.)

Chorus.
But here comes one of the servants out of the house, crying. What am I
going to hear? If something has happened to the master and mistress,
grief is forgivable. But we would like to know whether the lady is
still alive, or whether she is gone.

Maid.
You could say she is alive, and you could say she is dead.

Chorus.
How can the same woman be dead and still see?

Maid.
She is sinking already. Her life is tearing loose.

Chorus.
Poor man. To be the man he is, and lose a wife like that.

Maid.
The master does not know it yet. He will not until it happens.

Chorus.
Is there no hope any more of saving her life?

Maid.
None. The appointed day is forcing its way in.

Chorus.
Then is everything being done for her that should be?

Maid.
The clothes are ready that her husband will bury her in.

Chorus.
Then let her know this: she dies with a great name, and she is the best
woman under the sun, by a long way.

Maid.
Best? Of course she is the best. Who would argue with that? What would
a woman have to be, to outdo her? How could anyone show more plainly
that she put her husband first than by being willing to die for him?
The whole city knows that much. But what she did inside the house, you
will be amazed to hear.

When she realised the appointed day had come, she washed her white skin
in water from the river, and took clothes and jewels out of the cedar
chests and dressed herself beautifully, and stood in front of the
hearth and prayed. "Lady, I am going under the earth, and this is the
last time I will kneel to you, so I ask you: be a mother to my orphans.
Find my boy a loving wife, and my girl a good husband. And do not let
my children die before their time, as I their mother am dying, but let
them live out a happy life in their father's country, and enjoy it."
Then she went to every altar in Admetus's house and put wreaths on them
and prayed, breaking the sprigs off the myrtle boughs, without a tear,
without a sound, and the evil that was coming did not change the
lovely colour of her skin at all.

And then she rushed into the bedroom, to the bed, and there she did
cry, and this is what she said. "Bed, where I undid my virgin belt for
the man I am dying for, goodbye. I do not hate you. But you have killed
me, and only me: it was because I could not bear to betray you, and him,
that I am dying. Some other woman will own you. Not a better one. A
luckier one, perhaps." And she fell on it and kissed it, and the whole
bed was soaked in the flood that ran out of her eyes. When she had had
enough of crying, she tore herself off the bed and walked out, head
down, and over and over as she was leaving the room she turned round
and threw herself back down on the bed again. The children were hanging
on their mother's dress, crying. She took them in her arms, first one
and then the other, and kissed them, as a woman does who is about to
die. And every slave in the house was crying, out of pity for the
mistress. And she held out her right hand to each of them, and there
was not one so low that she did not speak to him and get his answer
back. That is the trouble in Admetus's house. If he had died, he would
be gone, and that would be that. He has escaped death, and what he has
instead is a pain he will never forget.

Chorus.
Admetus must be groaning, surely, at this disaster, if he has to lose a
wife as good as that.

Maid.
He is crying, holding his dear wife in his arms, begging her not to
leave him, asking for the impossible. She is wasting away. The sickness
is withering her. She has gone limp, a pitiful weight on his arm. But
even so, with what little breath she has left, she wants to look at the
rays of the sun. She will never see them again. This is the very last
time she will look at the sunlight and its disc. But I will go in and
say you are here. Not everyone thinks well enough of their rulers to
stand by them kindly when things go wrong. You have been my master's
friend a long time.

(The Maid goes in.)

Chorus.
	Oh, Zeus, what way out of this evil can there be,
	how, from where? What release from the fate
	that has come down on the king and queen?
	Aiai. Is someone coming out? Should I cut my hair now,
	and put on the black
	clothes of mourning already?
	It is plain, friends, plain enough. But all the same
	let us pray to the gods. The gods' power
	is the greatest there is.
	Lord Healer,
	find Admetus some way out of this evil.
	Find it, find it. You found one before,
	for this man. Now
	be the one who sets her free from death.
	Stop Hades, the killer.

Chorus.
	Papai.
	Son of Pheres, what has been done to you,
	losing your wife?
	Aiai. Is this not reason enough to cut your throat,
	and more than reason enough to hang your neck
	from a rope in the air?
	For today you will watch her die,
	the woman who was not dear to you,
	but the dearest of all.
	Look. Look.
	Here she comes out of the house, and her husband with her.
	Cry out, groan, land of Pherae,
	for the best of women,
	withering away with sickness,
	going under the earth to Hades below.

(Alcestis is carried out of the house, with Admetus beside her and the
two children.)

Chorus.
	I will never say that marriage brings
	more joy than grief. I judge by what has happened
	before, and by what I see now
	happening to the king, who loses
	the best of wives, and will live
	a life that is not a life from now on.

Alcestis.
	Sun, and light of day,
	and clouds racing in the whirl of the sky.

Admetus.
The sun sees you and me, two people who have done badly, and who did
nothing to the gods that you should die for.

Alcestis.
	Earth, and the roof of the house,
	and the bed I was married in, in Iolcus, where I was born.

Admetus.
Lift yourself up, poor woman, do not leave me. Beg the gods who rule us
to have pity.

Alcestis.
	I see the boat with the two oars. I see it, on the lake.
	The ferryman of the dead,
	Charon, with his hand on the pole,
	is calling me already. "What are you waiting for?
	Hurry up. You are holding me back." That is how
	he hurries me, in his impatience.

Admetus.
Oh. That is a bitter voyage you are telling me about. Unlucky woman,
what is happening to us?

Alcestis.
	Someone is taking me, taking me, someone is taking me. Can you not see him?
	To the courtyard of the dead.
	He is glaring at me from under dark brows,
	and he has wings. Hades.
	Let me go. What are you doing? Let go. What a road
	I am walking down, the most wretched woman alive.

Admetus.
A road that is pitiful to everyone who loves you, and most of all to
me and the children, who share this grief with me.

Alcestis.
	Let me go, let me go now.
	Lay me down, there is no strength in my feet.
	Hades is close.
	Dark night is creeping over my eyes.
	Children, children, there is no
	mother for you any more, no more.
	Goodbye, children. Enjoy this light. Look at it.

Admetus.
	Oh. That is a word of misery to hear,
	and worse to me than any death.
	By the gods, do not have the heart to leave me.
	By the children you will make orphans.
	Get up. Be brave.
	If you are dead, I am nothing.
	Living or not living, we depend on you.
	It is your love we worship.

Alcestis.
Admetus, you can see how it is with me. I want to tell you what I want,
before I die. I put you first. I set you up to go on looking at this
light at the cost of my own life, and I am dying, when I did not have to
die for you. I could have married any man in Thessaly I liked, and
lived in a house that was rich with a throne in it. I did not want to
live, torn away from you, with orphaned children. And I did not hold
back my youth, though I had gifts in it I enjoyed. And yet your father
and the mother who bore you betrayed you, when they had come to the
time of life where dying is fine, where it would have been fine to save
their son and die with honour. You were their only son. There was no
hope, if you died, of their having other children. And you and I would
have gone on living the rest of our time, and you would not be groaning
over a wife you have lost, and bringing up children without a mother.
But some god arranged it so that this is how things are.

Well. Now remember what you owe me for it. I am going to ask you for
something. Never the equivalent, because nothing is worth more than a
life. But something fair, as you will say yourself, because you love
these children no less than I do, if you have any sense. Let them be the
masters of my house, and do not marry again, and put a stepmother over
these children. She would be a worse woman than me, and out of jealousy
she would lift her hand against your children and mine. Do not do it. I
am begging you. A stepmother who comes in after the first wife hates
her children, and a viper is no gentler than she is. A boy has his
father, a great tower to him, someone to talk to and to answer him
back. But you, my daughter, how will you grow up decently? What kind of
wife will you find at your father's side? She could fasten some filthy
rumour on you, in the flower of your youth, and wreck your marriage.
Your mother will never see you married, never be there at your side to
give you courage when you are having your children, my child, when
there is nothing kinder than a mother. Because I have to die. And this
evil is not coming to me tomorrow, or the day after; in a moment I
will be counted among the people who no longer exist. Goodbye. Be
happy. And you, husband, can boast that you had the best of wives, and
you, children, that you were born from the best of mothers.

Chorus.
Do not be afraid. I am not ashamed to answer for him. He will do it,
unless he has lost his mind.

Admetus.
It will be so, it will be so, do not be afraid. You were the only wife
I had while you were alive, and when you are dead you alone will be
called my wife. No bride from Thessaly will ever take your place and
speak to me as her husband. There is no woman so well born, or so
outstandingly beautiful, that she could. And I have children enough. I
pray to the gods that I get some good of them, because I have had none
of you. I will not mourn you for a year. I will mourn you as long as my
life lasts, wife, and I will loathe the woman who bore me and hate my
father, because they loved me in words and not in deeds. You gave the
dearest thing you had in exchange for my life, and saved it. Do I not
have reason to groan, losing a wife like you?

I will put a stop to the drinking parties, and the company of men over
the wine, and the garlands, and the music that used to fill my house. I
will never touch the lyre again, or lift my heart to sing to the
Libyan pipe. You have taken all my pleasure in life away with you. And
craftsmen with clever hands will make a likeness of your body, and it
will be laid out in my bed, and I will fall on it and wrap my arms
round it and call your name, and think I am holding my dear wife in my
arms, when I am not. A cold pleasure, I know. But still, I would be
draining off some of the weight on my heart. And you might come to me
in dreams and make me glad. It is sweet to see the people we love even
at night, for as long as they stay.

If I had the tongue of Orpheus, and his music, so that I could charm
the daughter of Demeter or her husband with my singing and take you
back out of Hades, I would have gone down, and neither Pluto's hound
nor Charon at his oar, the ferryman of souls, would have stopped me,
until I had set your life back in the light. As it is, wait for me
there, when I die, and get a house ready for us to live in together.
I will give orders to these children to lay me in the same cedar coffin
as you, and stretch my side out next to yours. Not even in death would
I be parted from you, the only one who was faithful to me.

Chorus.
And I will share this grief with you, friend with friend. It is hard,
and she deserves it.

Alcestis.
Children, you heard it yourselves. Your father said he will never marry
another woman and set her over you, and never dishonour me.

Admetus.
I say it again now, and I will keep to it.

Alcestis.
On those terms, take the children from my hand.

Admetus.
I take them. A dear gift, from a dear hand.

Alcestis.
Now you be a mother to these children in my place.

Admetus.
I will have to be, now they are robbed of you.

Alcestis.
Children, I am going down below, when I ought to be living.

Admetus.
Oh, what am I going to do without you?

Alcestis.
Time will soften you. The dead are nothing.

Admetus.
Take me with you, by the gods. Take me down.

Alcestis.
It is enough that I am dying in your place.

Admetus.
God, what a wife you are robbing me of.

Alcestis.
My eyes are growing dark and heavy.

Admetus.
Then I am finished, if you really leave me, wife.

Alcestis.
You can speak of me as someone who no longer exists.

Admetus.
Lift your face. Do not leave your children.

Alcestis.
Not willingly, I promise you. But goodbye, children.

Admetus.
Look at them. Look.

Alcestis.
I am nothing now.

Admetus.
What are you doing? Are you leaving us?

Alcestis.
Goodbye.

Admetus.
I am lost.

Chorus.
She is gone. The wife of Admetus is no more.
