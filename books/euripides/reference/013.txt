[Sung]

Nurse.
	851 Why vex thyself thus? on all of us sooner or later heaven’s visitation comes.

Hermione.
	854 Thou hast left me, O my father,
	855 left me like a stranded bark, all alone, without an oar. My lord will surely slay me; no home is mine henceforth beneath my husband’s roof. What god is there to whose statue I can as a suppliant haste?
	860 or shall I throw myself in slavish wise at slavish knees? Would I could speed away from Phthia’s land on bird’s dark pinion, or like that pine-built ship,
	865 the first that ever sailed betwixt the rocks Cyanean!

Nurse.
	866 My child, I can as little praise thy previous sinful excesses, committed against the Trojan captive, as thy present exaggerated terror. Thy husband will never listen to
	870 a barbarian’s weak pleading and reject his marriage with thee for this. For thou wast no captive from Troy whom he wedded, but the daughter of a gallant sire, with a rich dower, from a city too of no mean prosperity. Nor will thy father forsake thee, as thou dreadest,
	875 and allow thee to be cast out from this house. Nay, enter now, nor show thyself before the palace, lest the sight of thee there bring reproach upon thee, my daughter. Exit Nurse.

Chorus.
	879 Lo! a stranger of foreign appearance from some other land
	880 comes hurrying towards us.

Orestes.
	881 Ladies of this foreign land! is this the home, the palace of Achilles’ son?

Chorus.
	883 Thou hast it; but who art thou to ask such a question?

Orestes.
	884 The son of Agamemnon and Clytemnestra,
	885 by name Orestes, on my way to the oracle of Zeus at Dodona. But now that I am come to Phthia, I am resolved to inquire about my kinswoman, Hermione of Sparta; is she alive and well? for though
	890 she dwells in a land far from my own, I love her none the less.

Hermione.
	891 Son of Agamemnon, thy appearing is as a haven from the storm to sailors; by thy knees I pray, have pity on me in my distress, on me of whose fortunes thou art inquiring. About thy knees
	895 I twine my arms with all the force of sacred fillets.

Orestes.
	896 Ha! what is this? Am I mistaken or do I really see before me the queen of this palace, the daughter of Menelaus?

Hermione.
	898 The same, that only child whom Helen, daughter of Tyndareus, bore my father in his halls; never doubt that.

Orestes.
	900 O saviour Phoebus, grant us respite from our woe! But what is the matter? art thou afflicted by gods or men?

Hermione.
	902 Partly by myself, partly by the man who wedded me, and partly by some god. On every side I see ruin.

Orestes.
	904 Why, what misfortune could happen to a woman as yet childless,
	905 unless her honour is concerned?

Hermione.
	906 My very complaint! Thou hast hit my case exactly.

Orestes.
	907 On whom has thy husband set his affections in thy stead?

Hermione.
	908 On his captive, Hector’s wife.

Orestes.
	909 An evil case indeed, for a man to have two wives!

Hermione.
	910 ’Tis even thus. So I resented it.

Orestes.
	911 Didst thou with woman’s craft devise a plot against thy rival?

Hermione.
	912 Yes, to slay her and her bastard child.

Orestes.
	913 And didst thou slay them, or did something happen to rescue them from thee?

Hermione.
	914 It was old Peleus, who showed regard to the weaker side.

Orestes.
	915 Hadst thou any accomplice in this attempted murder?

Hermione.
	916 My father came from Sparta for this very purpose.

Orestes.
	917 And was he after all defeated by that old man’s prowess ?

Hermione.
	918 Oh no! but by shame; and he hath gone and left me all alone.

Orestes.
	919 I understand; thou art afraid of thy husband for that thou hast done.

Hermione.
	920 Thou hast guessed it; for he will have a right to me. What can I say for myself? Yet I beseech thee by Zeus the god of our family, send me to a land as far as possible from this, or to my father’s house; for these very walls seem to cry out Begone!
	925 and all the land of Phthia bates me. But if my lord return ere that from the oracle of Phoebus, he will put me to death on a shameful charge, or enslave me to his mistress, whom I ruled before. Maybe some one will say, How was it thou didst go thus astray?
	930 I was ruined by mischievous women who came to me and puffed me up with words like these: What! wilt thou suffer that vile captive, a mere bondmaid, to dwell within thy house and share thy wedded rights? By Heaven’s queen! if it were my house
	935 she should not live to reap my marriage-harvest! And I listened to the words of these Sirens, the cunning, knavish, subtle praters, and was filled with silly thoughts. What need had I to care about my lord?
	940 I had all I wanted, wealth in plenty, a house in which I was mistress, and as for children, mine would be born in wedlock, while hers would be bastards, half-slaves to mine. Oh! never, never,–this truth will I repeat,–should men of sense, who have wives,
	945 allow women-folk to visit them in their homes, for they teach them mischief; one, to gain some private end, helps to corrupt their honour; another, having made a slip herself, wants a companion in misfortune, while many are wantons; and hence it is
	950 men’s houses are tainted. Wherefore keep strict guard upon the portals of your houses with bolts and bars; for these visits of strange women lead to no good result, but a world of mischief.

Chorus.
	954 Thou hast given thy tongue too free a rein regarding thy own sex.
	955 I can pardon thee in this case, but still women ought to smooth over their sisters’ weaknesses.

Orestes.
	957 ’Twas sage counsel he gave who taught men to hear the arguments on both sides. I, for instance, though aware of the confusion in this house,
	960 the quarrel between thee and Hector’s wife, waited awhile and watched to see whether thou wouldst stay here or from fear of that captive art minded to quit these halls. Now it was not so much regard for thy message that brought me hither,
	965 as the intention of carrying thee away from this house, if, as now, thou shouldst grant me a chance of saying so. For thou wert mine formerly, but art now living with thy present husband through thy father’s baseness; since he, before invading Troy’s domains, betrothed thee to me, and then afterwards promised thee
	970 to thy present lord, provided he captured the city of Troy.
	971 So, as soon as Achilles’ son returned hither, I forgave thy father, but entreated the bridegroom to forego his marriage with thee, telling him all I had gone through and my present misfortune; I might get
	975 a wife, I said, from amongst friends, but outside their circle ’twas no easy task for one exiled like myself from home. Thereat he grew abusive, taunting me with my mother’s murder and those blood-boltered fiends. And I was humbled by the fortunes of my house,
	980 and though ’tis true, I grieved, yet did I endure my sorrow, and reluctantly departed, robbed of thy promised hand. Now therefore, since thou findest thy fortune so abruptly changed and art fallen thus on evil days and hast no help, I will take thee hence and place thee in thy father’s hands.
	985 For kinship hath strong claims, and in adversity there is naught better than a kinsman’s kindly aid.

Hermione.
	987 As for my marriage, my father must look to it; ’tis not for me to decide that. Yes, take me hence as soon as may be,
	990 lest my husband come back to his house before I am gone, or Peleus hear that I am deserting his son’s abode and pursue me on horse-back.

Orestes.
	993 Rest easy about the old man’s power; and, as for Achilles’ son with all his insolence to me, never fear him;
	995 such a crafty net this hand hath woven and set for his death with knots that none can loose; whereof I will not speak before the time, but, when my plot begins to work, Delphi’s rock will witness it. If but my allies
	1000 in the Pythian land abide by their oaths, this same murderer of his mother will show that no one else shall marry thee my rightful bride. To his cost will he demand satisfaction of King Phoebus for his father’s blood; nor shall his repentance avail him, though he is now submitting to the god.
	1005 No! he shall perish miserably by Apollo’s hand and my fake accusations; so shall he end out my enmity. For the deity upsets the fortune of them that hate him. and suffers them not to be high-minded. Exeunt Orestes and Hermione.

Chorus.
	1009 O Phoebus! who didst fence the hill of Ilium with a fair coronal of towers,
	1010 and thou, ocean-god! coursing o’er the main with thy dark steeds, wherefore did ye hand over in dishonour
	1015 your own handiwork to the war-god, master of the spear, abandoning Troy to wretchedness?

Chorus.
	1019 Many a well-horsed car ye yoked on the banks of Simois,
	1020 and many a bloody tournament did ye ordain with never a prize to win; and Ilium’s princes are dead and gone; no longer in Troy
	1025 is seen the blaze of fire on altars of the gods with the smoke of incense.

Chorus.
	1027 The son of Atreus is no more, slain by the hand of his wife, and she herself hath paid the debt of blood by death,
	1030 and from her children’s hands received her doom. The god’s own bidding from his oracle was levelled against her, in the day that Agamemnon’s son set forth from Argos and visited his shrine;
	1035 so he slew her, aye, spilt his own mother’s blood. O Phoebus, O thou power divine, how can I believe the story?

Chorus.
	1037 Anon wherever Hellenes gather, was heard the voice of lamentation, mothers weeping o’er their children’s fate,
	1040 as they left their homes to mate with strangers. Ah! thou art not the only one, nor thy dear ones either, on whom the cloud of grief hath fallen. Hellas had to bear the visitation, and thence the scourge crossed to Phrygia’s
	1045 fruitful fields, raining the bloody drops the; death-god loves.

[Spoken]

Peleus.
1047 Ye dames of Phthia, answer my questions. I heard a vague rumour that the daughter of Menelaus had left these halls
1050 and fled; so now am I come in hot haste to learn if this be true; for it is the duty of those who are at home to labour in the interests of their absent friends.

Chorus.
1053 Thou hast heard aright, O Peleus; ill would it become me to hide the evil case in which I now find myself;
1055 our queen has fled and left these halls.

Peleus.
1056 What was she afraid of? explain that to me.

Chorus.
1057 She was fearful her lord would cast her out

Peleus.
1058 In return for plotting his child’s death? surely not?

Chorus.
1059 Yea, and she was afraid of yon captive.

Peleus.
1060 With whom did she leave the house? with her father?

Chorus.
1061 The son of Agamemnon came and took her hence.

Peleus.
1062 What view hath he to further thereby? Will he marry her?

Chorus.
1063 Yes, and he is plotting thy grandson’s death.

Peleus.
1064 From an ambuscade, or meeting him fairly face to face?

Chorus.
1065 In the holy place of Loxias, leagued with Delphians.

Peleus.
1066 God help us! This is an immediate danger. Hasten one of you with all speed to the Pythian altar and tell our friends there what has happened here, ere Achilles’ son be slain by his enemies. Enter a Messenger.

Messenger.
1070 Woe worth the day! what evil tidings have I brought for thee, old sire, and for all who love my master! woe is me!

Peleus.
1072 Alas! my prophetic soul hath a presentiment.

Messenger.
1073 Aged Peleus, hearken! Thy grandson is no more; so grievously is he smitten
1075 by the men of Delphi and the stranger from Mycenae.

Chorus.
1076 Ah! what wilt thou do, old man? Fall not; uplift thyself.

Peleus.
1077b I am a thing of naught; death is come upon me. My voice is choked, my limbs droop beneath me.

Messenger.
1079 Hearken; if thou art eager also to avenge thy friends,
1080 lift up thyself and hear what happened.

Peleus.
1081 Ah, destiny! how tightly hast thou caught me in thy toils, a poor old man at life’s extremest verge! But tell me how he was taken from me, my one son’s only child; unwelcome as such news is, I fain would hear it.

Messenger.
1085 As soon as we reached the famous soil of Phoebus, for three whole days were we feasting our eyes with the sight. And this, it seems, caused suspicion; for the folk, who dwell near the god’s shrine, began to collect in groups,
1090 while Agamemnon’s son, going to and fro through the town, would whisper in each man’s ear malignant hints: Do ye see yon fellow, going in and out of the god’s treasure-chambers, which are full of the gold stored there by all mankind? He is come hither a second time on the same mission as before,
1095 eager to sack the temple of Phoebus. Thereon there ran an angry murmur through the city, and the magistrates flocked to their council-chamber, while those, who have charge of the god’s treasures, had a guard privately placed amongst the colonnades.
1100 But we, knowing naught as yet of this, took sheep fed in the pastures of Parnassus, and went our way and stationed ourselves at the altars with vouchers and Pythian seers. And one said: What prayer, young warrior,
1105 wouldst thou have us offer to the god? Wherefore art thou come? And he answered: I wish to make atonement to Phoebus for my past transgression; for once I claimed from him satisfaction for my father’s blood. Thereupon the rumour, spread by Orestes, proved to have great weight,
1110 suggesting that my master was lying and had come on a shameful errand. But he crosses the threshold of the temple to pray to Phoebus before his oracle, and was busy with his burnt-offering; when a body of men armed with swords set themselves in ambush against him
1115 in the cover of the bay- trees, and Clytemnestra’s son, that had contrived the whole plot was one of them. There stood the young man praying to the god in sight of all, when lo! with their sharp swords they stabbed Achilles’ unprotected son from behind.
1120 But he stepped back, for it was not a mortal wound he had received, and drew his sword, and snatching armour from the pegs where it hung on a pillar, took his stand upon the altar-steps, the picture of a warrior grim; then cried he to the sons of Delphi, and asked them:
1125 Why seek to slay me when I am come on a holy mission? What cause is there why I should die? But of all that throng of bystanders, no man answered him a word, but they set to hurling stones. Then he, though bruised and battered by the showers of missiles from all sides,
1130 covered himself behind his mail and tried to ward off the attack, holding his shield first here, then there, at arm’s length, but all of no avail; for a storm of darts, arrows and javelins, hurtling spits with double points, and butchers’ knives for slaying steers, came flying at his feet;
1135 and terrible was the war-dance thou hadst then seen thy grandson dance to avoid their marksmanship. At last, when they were hemming him in on all sides, allowing him no breathing space, he left the shelter of the altar, the hearth where victims are placed, and with one bound was on them as on the Trojans of yore;
1140 and they turned and fled like doves when they see the hawk. Many fell in the confusion; some wounded, and others trodden down by one another along the narrow passages; and in that hushed holy house uprose unholy din
1145 and echoed back from the rocks. Calm and still my master stood there in his gleaming harness like a flash of light, till from the inmost shrine there came a voice of thrilling horror, stirring the crowd to make a stand. Then fell Achilles’ son,
1150 smitten through the flank by some Delphian’s biting blade, some fellow that slew him with a host to help; and as he fell, there was not one that did not stab him, or cast a rock and batter his corpse. So his whole body,
1155 once so fair, was marred with savage wounds. At last they cast the lifeless clay, lying near the altar, forth from the fragrant fane. And we gathered up his remains forthwith and are bringing them to thee,
1160 old prince, to mourn and weep and honour with a deep-dug tomb.
1161 This is how that prince who vouchsafeth oracles to others, that judge of what is right for all the world, hath revenged himself on Achilles’ son, remembering his ancient quarrel as a wicked man would.
1165 How then can he be wise? Exit Messenger. The body of Neoptolemus is carried in on a bier.

[Sung]

Chorus.
	1166 Lo! E’en now our prince is being carried on a bier from Delphi’s land unto his home. Woe for him and his sad fate, and woe for thee, old sire! for this is not the welcome thou wouldst give Achilles’ son,
	1170 the lion’s whelp; thyself too by this sad mischance dost share his evil lot.

Peleus.
	1173 Ah! woe is me! here is a sad sight for me to see and take unto my halls!
	1175 Ah me! ah me! I am undone, thou city of Thessaly! My line now ends; I have no children left me in my home. Oh! the sorrows I seem born to endure! What
	1180 friend can I look to for relief? Ah, dear lips, and cheeks, and hands! Would thy destiny had slain thee ’neath Ilium’s walls beside the banks of Simois!

Chorus.
	1184 Had he so died, my aged lord, he had won him honour thereby,
	1185 and thine had been the happier lot.

Peleus.
	1186 O marriage, marriage, woe to thee! thou bane of my home, thou destroyer of my city! Ah my child, my boy! would that the honour of wedding thee,
	1190 fraught with evil as it was to my children and house, had not thrown oer thee, my son, Hermiones deadly net! O that the thunderbolt had slain her sooner! and that thou, rash mortal, hadst never charged
	1195 the great god Phoebus with aiming that murderous shaft that spilt thy hero-fathers blood!

Chorus.
	1197 Woe! woe! alas! With due observance of funeral rites will I begin the mourning for my dead master.

Peleus.
	1200 Alack and well-a-day! I take up the tearful dirge, ah me! old and wretched as I am.

Chorus.
	1203 ’Tis Heaven’s decree; God willed this heavy stroke.

Peleus.
	1204 O darling child,
	1205 thou hast left me all alone in my halls, old and childless by thy loss.

Chorus.
	1208 Thou shouldst have died, old sire, before thy children.

Peleus.
	1209 Shall I not tear my hair,
	1210 and smite upon my head with grievous blows? O city! of both my children hath Phoebus robbed me.

Chorus.
	1214 What evils thou hast suffered, what sorrows thou hast seen, thou poor old man!
	1215 what shall be thy life hereafter?

Peleus.
	1216 Childless, desolate, with no limit to my grief, I must drain the cup of woe, until I die.

Chorus.
	1218 ’Twas all in vain the gods wished thee joy on thy wedding day.

Peleus.
	1219 All my hopes have flown away,
	1220 fallen short of my high boasts.

Chorus.
	1221 A lonely dweller in a lonely home art thou.

Peleus.
	1222 I have no city any longer; there! on the ground my sceptre do I cast; and thou, daughter of Nereus, neath thy dim grotto,
	1225 shalt see me grovelling in the dust, a ruined king.

Chorus.
	1226 Look, look! A dim form of divine appearance in seen hovering in mid air. What is that moving? what influence divine am I conscious of? Look, maidens, mark it well; see, yonder is some deity, wafted through the lustrous air and alighting on the plains
	1230 of Phthia, home of steeds.

Thetis.
	1231 O Peleus! because of my wedded days with thee now long agone, I Thetis am come from the halls of Nereus. And first I counsel thee not to grieve to excess in thy present distress,
	1235 for I too who need ne’er have born children to my sorrow, have lost the child of our love, Achilles swift of foot, foremost of the sons of Hellas. Next will I declare why I am come, and do thou give ear. Carry yonder corpse, Achilles’ son,
	1240 to the Pythian altar and there bury it, a reproach to Delphi, that his tomb may proclaim the violent death he met at the hand of Orestes. And for his captive wife Andromache,–she must dwell in the Molossian land,
	1245 united in honourable wedlock with Helenus, and with her this babe, the sole survivor as he is of all the line of Aeacus, for from him a succession of prosperous kings of Molossia is to go on unbroken; for
	1250 the race that springs from thee and me, my aged lord, must not thus be brought to naught; no! nor Troy’s line either; for her fate too is cared for by the gods, albeit her fall was due to the eager wish of Pallas. Thee too, that thou mayst know the saving grace of wedding me, will I, a goddess born and daughter of a god,
	1255 release from all the ills that flesh is heir to and make a deity to know not death nor decay. From henceforth in the halls of Nereus shalt thou dwell with me, god and goddess together; thence shalt thou rise dry-shod from out the main
	1260 and see Achilles, our dear son, settled in his island-home by the strand of Leuce, that is girdled by the Euxine sea. But get thee to Delphi’s god-built town, carrying this corpse with thee, and, after thou hast buried him,
	1265 return and settle in the cave which time hath hollowed in the Sepian rock and there abide, till from the sea I come with choir of fifty Nereids to be thy escort thence; for fate’s decree thou must fulfil; such is the pleasure of Zeus.
	1270 Cease then to mourn the dead; this is the lot which heaven assigns to all, and all must pay their debt to death.

Peleus.
	1273 Great queen, my honoured wife, from Nereus sprung, all hail! thou art
	1275 acting herein as befits thyself and thy children. So I will stay my grief at thy bidding, goddess, and, when I have buried the dead, will seek the glens of Pelion, even the place where I took thy beauteous form to my embrace. Surely after this every prudent man will seek to marry a wife of noble stock
	1280 and give his daughter to a husband good and true, never setting his heart on a worthless woman, not even though she bring a sumptuous dowry to his house. So would men ne’er suffer ill at heaven’s hand.

Chorus.
	1284 Many are the shapes of Heaven’s denizens,
	1285 and many a thing they bring to pass contrary to our expectation; that which we thought would be is not accomplished, while for the unexpected God finds out a way. E’en such hath been the issue of this matter.
