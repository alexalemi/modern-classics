Cyclops (Part 2 of 2) -- Coleridge's prose, crib only

[Sung]

Chorus.
	369 Oh! the ruthless monster! to sacrifice his guests at his own hearth, the suppliants of his halls, cleaving and tearing and serving up to his loathsome teeth a feast of human flesh, hot from the coals.

Chorus.

[Spoken]

Odysseus.
375 (reappearing with a look of horror.) O Zeus! what can I say after the hideous sights I have seen inside the cave, things past belief, resembling more the tales men tell than aught they do?

Chorus.
377 What news, Odysseus? has the Cyclops, most godless monster, been feasting on thy dear comrades?

Odysseus.
379 Aye, he singled out a pair, on whom the flesh was
380 fattest and in best condition, and took them up in his hand to weigh.

Chorus.
381 How went it with you then, poor wretch?

Odysseus.
382 When we had entered yonder rocky abode, he lighted first a fire, throwing logs of towering oak upon his spacious hearth,
385 enough for three waggons to carry as their load; next, close by the blazing flame, he placed his couch of pine-boughs laid upon the floor, and filled a bowl of some ten firkins, pouring white milk thereinto, after he had milked his kine;
390 and by his side he put a can of ivy-wood, whose breadth was three cubits and its depth four maybe; next he set his brazen pot a-boiling on the fire, spits too he set beside him, fashioned of the branches of thorn, their points hardened in the fire and the rest of them trimmed with the hatchet,
395 and the blood-bowls of Aetna for the axe’s edge. Now when that hell-cook, god-detested, had everything quite ready, he caught up a pair of my companions and proceeded deliberately to cut the throat of one of them over the yawning brazen pot;
400 but the other he clutched by the tendon of his heel, and, striking him against a sharp point of rocky stone, dashed out his brains; then, after hacking the fleshy parts with glutton cleaver, he set to grilling them, but the limbs he threw into his cauldron to seethe.
405 And I, poor wretch, drew near with streaming eyes and waited on the Cyclops; but the others kept cowering like frightened birds in crannies of the rock, and the blood forsook their skin.
409 Anon, when he had gorged himself upon my comrades’ flesh and
410 had fallen on his back, breathing heavily, there came a sudden inspiration to me. I filled a cup of this Maronian wine and offered him a draught, saying, Cyclops, son of Ocean’s god, see here what heavenly drink the grapes of Hellas yield,
415 glad gift of Dionysus. He, glutted with his shameless meal, took and drained it at one draught, and, lifting up his hand, he thanked me thus, Dearest to me of all my guests! fair the drink thou givest me to crown so fair a feast.
420 Now when I saw his delight, I gave him another cup, knowing the wine would make him rue it, and he would soon be paying the penalty. Then he set to singing; but I kept filling bumper after bumper and heating him with drink.
425 So there he is singing discordantly amid the weeping of my fellow-sailors, and the cave re-echoes; but I have made my way out quietly and would fain save thee and myself, if thou wilt. Tell me then, is it your wish, or is it not, to fly from this unsocial wretch
430 and take up your abode with Naiad nymphs in the halls of the Bacchic god? Thy father within approves this scheme; but there! he is powerless, getting all he can out of his liquor; his wings are snared by the cup as if he had flown against bird-lime, and he is fuddled; but thou art young and lusty;
435 so save thyself with my help and regain thy old friend Dionysus, so little like the Cyclops.

Chorus.
437 Best of friends, would we might see that day, escaping the godless Cyclops! for ’tis long
440 we have been without the joys of men, unable to escape him.

Odysseus.
441 Hear then how I will requite this vile monster and rescue you from thraldom.

Chorus.
443 Tell me how; no note of Asiatic lyre would sound more sweetly in our ears than news of the Cyclops’ death.

Odysseus.
445 Delighted with this liquor of the Bacchic god, he fain would go a-revelling with his brethren.

Chorus.
447 I understand; thy purpose is to seize and slay him in the thickets when alone, or push him down a precipice.

Odysseus.
449 Not at ail; my plan is fraught with subtlety.

Chorus.
450 What then? Truly we have long heard of thy cleverness.

Odysseus.
451 I mean to keep him from this revel, saying he must not give this drink to his brethren but keep it for himself alone and lead a happy life. Then when he falls asleep, o’ermastered by the Bacchic god,
455 I will put a point with this sword of mine to an olive-branch I saw lying in the cave, and will set it on fire; and when I see it well alight, I will lift the heated brand, and, thrusting it full in the Cyclops’ eye, melt out his sight with its blaze;
460 and, as when a man in fitting the timbers of a ship makes his auger spin to and fro with a double strap, so will I make the brand revolve in the eye that gives the Cyclops light and will scorch up the pupil thereof.

Chorus.
464 Ho! ho! how glad I feel! wild with joy at the contrivance!

Odysseus.
466 That done, I will embark thee and those thou lovest with old Silenus in the deep hold of my black ship, my ship with double banks of oars, and carry you away from this land.

Chorus.
469 Well, can I too lay hold of the blinding brand, as though the god’s libation had been poured? for I would fain have a share in this offering of blood.

Odysseus.
472 Indeed thou must, for the brand is large, and thou must help hold it.

Chorus.
473 How lightly would I lift the load of e’en a hundred wains, if that will help us to grub out
475 the eye of the doomed Cyclops, like a wasp’s nest.

Odysseus.
476 Hush! for now thou knowest my plot in fall, and when I bid you, obey the author of it; for I am not the man to desert my friends inside the cave and save myself alone.
480 And yet I might escape; I am clear of the cavern’s depths already; but no! to desert the friends with whom I journeyed hither and only save myself is not a righteous course. [Re-enters the cave.

First Half-Chorus.
	483 Come, who will be the first and who the next to him upon the list to grip the handle of the brand,
	485 and, thrusting it into the Cyclops’ eye, gouge out the light thereof?

Second Half-Chorus.
	487 Hush! hush! Behold the drunkard leaves his rocky home, trolling loud some hideous lay,
	490 a clumsy tuneless clown, whom tears await. Come, let us give this boor a lesson in revelry. Ere long will he be blind at any rate.

First Half-Chorus.
	495 Happy he who plays the Bacchanal amid the precious streams distilled from grapes, stretched at full length for a revel, his arm around the friend he loves,
	500 and some fair dainty damsel on his couch, his hair perfumed with nard and glossy, the while he calls, Oh! who will ope the door for me?

Cyclops.
	503 Ha! ha! full of wine and merry with the feast’s good cheer am I,
	505 my hold freighted like a merchant-ship up to my belly’s very top. This turf graciously invites me to seek my brother Cyclopes for a revel in the spring-tide.
	510 Come, stranger, bring the wine-skin hither and hand it over to me.

Second Half-Chorus.
	511 Forth from the house its fair lord comes, casting his fair glance round him. We have some one to befriend us. A hostile brand is awaiting thee,
	515 no tender bride in dewy grot. No single colour will those garlands have, that soon shall cling so close about thy brow.

[Spoken]

Odysseus.
519 (Returning with the wineskin.) Hearken, Cyclops; for I am well versed in the ways of Bacchus,
520 whom I have given thee to drink.

Cyclops.
521 And who is Bacchus? some reputed god?

Odysseus.
522 The greatest god men know to cheer their life.

Cyclops.
523 I like his after-taste at any rate.

Odysseus.
524 This is the kind of god he is; he harmeth no man.

Cyclops.
525 But how does a god like being housed in a wine-skin?

Odysseus.
526 Put him where one may, he is content there.

Cyclops.
527 It is not right that gods should be clad in leather.

Odysseus.
528 What of that, provided he please thee? does the leather hurt thee?

Cyclops.
529 I hate the wine-skin, but the liquor we have here I love.

Odysseus.
530 Stay, then, Cyclops; drink and be merry.

Cyclops.
531 Must I not give my brethren a share in this liquor?

Odysseus.
532 No, keep it thyself and thou wilt appear of more honour.

Cyclops.
533 Give it my friends and I shall appear of more use.

Odysseus.
534 Revelling is apt to end in blows, abuse, and strife.

Cyclops.
535 I may be drunk, but no man will lay hands on me for all that.

Odysseus.
536 Better stay at home, my friend, after a carouse.

Cyclops.
537 Who loves not revelling then is but a simpleton.

Odysseus.
538 But whoso stays at home, when drunk, is wise.

Cyclops.
539 What shall we do, Silenus? art minded to stay?

Silenus.
540 That I am; for what need have we of others to share our drink, Cyclops?

Cyclops.
541 Well, truly the turf is soft as down with its fresh flowering plants.

Silenus.
542 (seating himself.) Aye, and ’tis pleasant drinking in the warm sunshine.

Cyclops.

Silenus.
543 Come, let me see thee stretch thy carcase on the ground.

Cyclops.
544 (sitting dawn.) There then!
545 Why art thou putting the mixing-bowl behind me?

Silenus.
546 That no one passing by may come upon it.

Cyclops.
546b Nay, but thy purpose is to drink upon the sly; set it between us. (To Odysseus.) Now tell me, stranger, by what name to call thee.

Odysseus.
549 Noman. What boon shall I receive of thee to earn my thanks?

Cyclops.
550 I will feast on thee last, after all thy comrades.

Odysseus.
551 Fair indeed the honour thou bestowest on thy guest, sir Cyclops!

Cyclops.
552 (turning suddenly to Silenus.) Ho, sirrah! what art thou about? taking a stealthy pull at the wine?

Silenus.
553 No, but it kissed me for my good looks.

Cyclops.
554 Thou shalt smart, if thou kiss the wine when it kisses not thee.

Silenus.
555 Oh! but it did, for it says it is in love with my handsome face.

Cyclops.
556 (holding out his cup.) Pour in; only give me my cup full.

Silenus.
557 H’m! how is it mixed? just let me make sure. (Takes another pull.)

Cyclops.
558 Perdition! give it me at once.

Silenus.
558b Oh, no! I really cannot, till I see thee with a crown on, and have another taste myself.

Cyclops.
560 My cup-bearer is a cheat.

Silenus.
560b No really, but the wine is so luscious. Thou must wipe thy lips, though, to get a draught.

Cyclops.
562 There! my lips and beard are clean now.

Silenus.
563 Bend thine elbow gracefully, and then quaff thy cup, as thou seest me do, and as now thou seest me not. (Burying his face in his cup.)

Cyclops.
565 Aha! what next?

Silenus.
565b I drank it off at a draught with much pleasure.

Cyclops.
566 Stranger, take the skin thyself and be my cup-bearer.

Odysseus.
567 Well, at any rate the grape is no stranger to my hand.

Cyclops.
568 Come, pour it in.

Odysseus.
568b In it goes! keep silence, that is all.

Cyclops.
569 A difficult task when a man is deep in his cups.

Odysseus.
570 Here, take and drink it off; leave none.

Cyclops.

Odysseus.
571 Thou must be silent and only give in when the liquor does.

Cyclops.
572 God wot! it is a clever stock that bears the grape.

Odysseus.
573 Aye, and if thou but swallow plenty of it after a plentiful meal, moistening thy belly till its thirst is gone, it will throw thee into slumber;
575 but if thou leave aught behind, the Bacchic god will parch thee for it.

Cyclops.
576 Ha! ha! what a trouble it was getting out! This is pleasure unalloyed; earth and sky seem whirling round together; I see the throne of Zeus
580 and all the godhead’s majesty. Kiss thee! no! There are the Graces trying to tempt me. I shall rest well enough with my Ganymede here; yea, by the Graces, right fairly.

Silenus.
585 What! Cyclops, am I Ganymede, Zeus’s minion?

Cyclops.
586 (attempting to carry him into the cave.) To be sure, Ganymede whom I am carrying off from the halls of Dardanus.

Silenus.
587 I am undone, my children; outrageous treatment waits me.

Chorus.
588 Dost find fault with thy lover? dost scorn him in his cups?

Silenus.
589 Woe is me! most bitter shall I find the wine ere long. [Exit Silenus, dragged away by Cyclops.

Odysseus.
590 Up now, children of Dionysus, sons of a noble sire, soon will yon creature in the cave, relaxed in slumber as ye see him, spew from his shameless maw the meat. Already the brand inside his lair is vomiting a cloud of smoke; and the only reason we prepared it was to burn
595 the Cyclops’ eye; so mind thou quit thee like a man.

Chorus.
596 I will have a spirit as of rock or adamant; but go inside, before my father suffers any shameful treatment; for here thou hast things ready.

Odysseus.
599 O Hephaestus, lord of Aetna, rid thyself for once and all of a troublesome neighbour
600 by burning his bright eye out. Come, Sleep, as well, offspring of sable Night, come with all thy power on the monster god-detested; and never after Troy’s most glorious toils destroy Odysseus and his crew
605 by the hands of one who recketh naught of God or man; else must we reckon Chance a goddess, and Heaven’s wall inferior to hers. [Odysseus re-enters the cave.

Chorus.
	608 Tightly the pincers shall grip the neck
	610 of him who feasts upon his guest; for soon will he lose the light of his eye by fire; already the brand, a tree’s huge limb,
	615 lurks amid the embers charred.
	616 Oh! come ye then and work his doom, pluck out the maddened Cyclops’ eye, that he may rue his drinking.
	620 And I too fain would leave the Cyclops’ lonely land and see king Bromius, ivy-crowned, the god I sorely miss. Ah! shall I ever come to that?

Odysseus.
	624 (leaving the cave cautiously.) Silence, ye cattle! I adjure you;
	625 close your lips; make not a sound! I’ll not let a man of you so much as breathe or wink or clear his throat, that yon pest awake not, until the sight in the Cyclops’ eye has passed through the fiery ordeal.

Chorus.
	629 Silent we stand with ’bated breath.

Odysseus.
	630 In then, and mind your fingers grip the brand, for it is splendidly red-hot.

Chorus.
	632 Thyself ordain who first must seize the blazing bar and burn the Cyclops’ eye out, that we may share alike whate’er betides.

Odysseus.
	640 Sprained thy ankle, standing still?

Second Half-Chorus.
	640b Yes, and my eyes are full of dust or ashes from somewhere or other.

Odysseus.

Chorus.
	643 Because I feel for my back and spine, and express no wish to have my teeth
	645 knocked out, I am a coward, am I? Well, but I know a spell of Orpheus, a most excellent one, to make the brand enter his skull of its own accord, and set alight the one-eyed son of Earth.

Odysseus.
	649 Long since I knew thou wert by nature such an one,
	650 and now I know it better; I must employ my own friends; but, though thou bring no active aid, cheer us on at any rate, that I may find my friends emboldened by thy encouragement. [Exit Odysseus.

Chorus.
	654 That will I do; the Carian shall run the risk for us;
	655 and as far as encouragement goes, let the Cyclops smoulder.

Chorus.
	656 What ho! my gallants, thrust away, make haste and burn his eye-brow off, the monster’s guest-devouring. Oh! singe and scorch
	660 the shepherd of Aetna; twirl the brand and drag it round and be careful lest in his agony he treat thee to some wantonness.

Cyclops.
	663 (Bellowing in the cave.) Oh! oh! my once bright eye is burnt to cinders now.

Chorus.
	664 Sweet indeed the triumph-song; pray sing it to us, Cyclops.

Cyclops.
	665 (from within.) Oh! oh! once more; what outrage on me and what ruin! But never shall ye escape this rocky cave unpunished, ye worthless creatures; for I will stand in the entrance of the cleft and fit my hands into it thus. [Staggering to the entrance.

Chorus.
	669 Why dost thou cry out, Cyclops?

Cyclops.
	669b I am undone.

Chorus.
	670 Thou art indeed a sorry sight.

Cyclops.
	670b Aye, and a sad one, too.

Chorus.
	671 Didst fall among the coals in a drunken fit?

Cyclops.
	672 Noman has undone me.

Chorus.
	672b Then there is no one hurting thee after all.

Cyclops.
	673 Noman is blinding me.

Chorus.
	673b Then art thou not blind.

Cyclops.
	674 As blind as thou, forsooth.

Chorus.
	674b How, pray, could no man have made thee blind?

Cyclops.
	675 Thou mockest me; but where is this Noman.

Chorus.
	675b Nowhere, Cyclops.

Cyclops.
	676 It was the stranger, vile wretch! who proved my ruin, that thou mayst understand rightly, by swilling me with the liquor he gave me.

Chorus.
	678 Ah! wine is a terrible foe, hard to wrestle with.

Cyclops.
	679 Tell me, I adjure thee, have they escaped or are they still within?

Chorus.
	680 Here they are ranged in silence, taking the rock to screen them.

Cyclops.
	681b On which side?

Chorus.
	682 On thy right.

Cyclops.
	682b Where?

Chorus.
	682c Close against the rock. Hast caught them?

Cyclops.
	683b Trouble on trouble! I have run my skull against the rock and cracked it.

Chorus.
	684b Aye, and they are escaping thee.

Cyclops.
	685 This way, was it not? ’Twas this way thou saidst.

Chorus.
	685b No, not this way.

Cyclops.
	686 Which then?

Chorus.
	686b They are getting round thee on the left.

Cyclops.
	687 Alas! I am being mocked; ye jeer me in my evil plight.

Chorus.
	688 They are no longer there; but facing thee that stranger stands.

Cyclops.
	689 Master of villainy, where, oh! where art thou?

Odysseus.
	689b Some way from thee
	690 I am keeping careful guard over the person of Odysseus.

Cyclops.
	691 What, a new name! hast changed thine?

Odysseus.
	692 Yes, Odysseus the name my father gave me. But thou wert doomed to pay for thy unholy feast; for I should have seen Troy burned to but sorry purpose,
	695 unless I had avenged on thee the slaughter of my comrades.

Cyclops.
	696 Woe is me! ’tis an old oracle coming true; yes, it said I should have my eye put out by thee on thy way home from Troy; but it likewise foretold that thou wouldst surely pay for this,
	700 tossing on the sea for many a day.

Odysseus.
	701 Go hang! E’en as I say, so have I done. And now will I get me to the beach and start my hollow ship across the sea of Sicily to the land of my fathers.

Cyclops.
	704 Thou shalt not; I will break a boulder off this rock
	705 and crush thee, crew and all, beneath my throw. Blind though I be, I will climb the hill, mounting through yonder tunnel.

Chorus.
	708 As for us, henceforth will we be the servants of Bacchus, sharing the voyage of this hero Odysseus.
