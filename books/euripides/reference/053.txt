Cyclops (Part 1 of 2) -- Coleridge's prose, crib only

[Spoken]

(Scene. — Mount Aetna in Sicily, before the cave of the Cyclops.)

Silenus.
1 O Bromius, unnumbered are the toils I bear because of thee, no less now than when I was young and hale; first, when thou wert driven mad by Hera and didst leave the mountain nymphs, thy nurses;
5 next, when in battle with earth-born spearmen I stood beside thee on the right as squire, and slew Enceladus, smiting him full in the middle of his targe with my spear. Come, though, let me see; must I confess ’twas all a dream? No, by Zeus! since I really showed his spoils to the Bacchic god.
10 And now am I enduring to the full a toil still worse than those. For when Hera sent forth a race of Tyrrhene pirates against thee, that thou mightest be smuggled far away, I, as soon as the news reached me, sailed in quest of thee with my children; and, taking the helm
15 myself, I stood on the end of the stern and steered our trim craft; and my sons, sitting at the oars, made the grey billows froth and foam as they sought thee, my liege. But just as we had come nigh Malea in our course, an east-wind blew upon the ship
20 and drove us hither to the rock of Aetna, where in lonely caverns dwell the one-eyed children of ocean’s god, the murdering Cyclopes. Captured by one of them we are slaves in his house; Polyphemus they call him whom we serve;
25 and instead of Bacchic revelry we are herding a godless Cyclops’s flocks; and so it is my children, striplings as they are, tend the young thereof on the edge of the downs; while my appointed task is to stay here and fill the troughs and sweep out the cave,
30 or wait upon the ungodly Cyclops at his impious feasts. His orders now compel obedience; I have to scrape out his house with the rake you see, so as to receive the Cyclops, my absent master,
35 and his sheep in clean caverns.
36 But already I see my children driving their browsing flocks towards me.
37 What means this? is the beat of feet in the Sicinnis dance the same to you now as when ye attended the Bacchic god in his revelries
40 and made your way with dainty steps to the mask of lyres to the halls of Althaea?

[Sung]

Chorus.
	41 Offspring of well-bred sires and dams, pray whither wilt thou be gone from me to the rocks? Hast thou not here a gentle breeze,
	45 and grass to browse, and water from the eddying stream set near the cave in troughs? and are not thy young ones bleating for thee?

Chorus.
	49 Pst! pst! wilt thou not browse here,
	50 here on the dewy slope? Ho? ho! ere long will I cast a stone at thee. Away, away! O horned one, to the fold-keeper of the Cyclops, the country-ranging shepherd.

Chorus.
	55 Loosen thy bursting udder; welcome to thy teats the kids, whom thou leavest in the lambkins’ pens. Those little bleating kids, asleep the livelong day, miss thee;
	60 wilt then leave at last the rich grass pastures on the peaks of Aetna and enter the fold?

Chorus.

Chorus.
	63 Here we have no Bromian god; no dances here, or Bacchantes thyrsus-bearing;
	65 no roll of drums, or drops of sparkling wine by gurgling founts; nor is it now with Nymphs in Nysa I sing a song of Bacchus, Bacchus!
	70 to the queen of love, in quest of whom I once sped on with Bacchantes, white of foot.
	73 Dear friend, dear Bacchic god, whither art roaming alone,
	75 waving thy auburn locks, while I, thy minister, do service to the one-eyed Cyclops, a slave and wanderer I,
	80 clad in this wretched goat-skin dress, severed from thy love?

[Spoken]

Silenus.
82 Hush, children! and bid our servants fold the flocks in the rock-roofed cavern.

Chorus.
84 (To Servants.) Away! (To Silenus.) But prithee, why such haste, father?

Silenus.
85 I see the hull of a ship from Hellas at the shore, and men, that wield the oar, on their way to this cave with some chieftain. About their necks they carry empty vessels and pitchers for water; they are in want of food. Luckless strangers!
90 who can they be? They know not what manner of man our master Polyphemus is, to have set foot here in his cheerless abode and come to the jaws of the cannibal Cyclops in an evil hour. But hold ye your peace, that we may inquire
95 whence they come to the peak of Sicilian Aetna.

Odysseus.
96 Pray tell us, sirs, of some river-spring whence we might draw a draught to slake our thirst, or of someone willing to sell victuals to mariners in need.
99 Why, what is this? We seem to have chanced upon a city of the Bromian god;
100 here by the caves I see a group of Satyrs. To the eldest first I bid All hail!

Silenus.
102 All hail, sir! tell me who thou art, and name thy country.

Odysseus.
103 Odysseus of Ithaca, king of the Cephallenians’ land.

Silenus.
104 I know him for a prating knave, one of Sisyphus’ shrewd offspring.

Odysseus.
105 I am the man; abuse me not.

Silenus.
106 Whence hast thou sailed hither to Sicily?

Odysseus.
107 From Ilium and the toils of Troy.

Silenus.
108 How was that? didst thou not know the passage to thy native land?

Odysseus.
109 Tempestuous winds drove me hither against my will.

Silenus.
110 God wot! thou art in the same plight as I am.

Odysseus.
111 Why, wert thou too drifted hither against thy will?

Silenus.
112 I was, as I pursued the pirates who carried Bromius off.

Odysseus.
113 What land is this and who are its inhabitants?

Silenus.
114 This is mount Aetna, the highest point in Sicily.

Odysseus.
115 But where are the city-walls and ramparts?

Silenus.
116 There are none; the headlands, sir, are void of men.

Odysseus.
117 Who then possess the land? the race of wild creatures?

Silenus.
118 The Cyclopes, who have caves, not roofed houses.

Odysseus.
119 Obedient unto whom? or is the power in the people’s hands?

Silenus.
120 They are rovers; no man obeys another in anything.

Odysseus.
121 Do they sow Demeter’s grain, or on what do they live?

Silenus.
122 On milk and cheese and flesh of sheep.

Odysseus.
123 Have they the drink of Bromius, the juice of the vine?

Silenus.
124 No indeed! and thus it is a joyless land they dwell in.

Odysseus.
125 Are they hospitable and reverent towards strangers?

Silenus.
126 Strangers, they say, supply the daintiest meat.

Odysseus.
127 What, do they delight in killing men and eating them?

Silenus.
128 No one has ever arrived here without being butchered.

Odysseus.
129 Where is the Cyclops himself? inside his dwelling?

Silenus.
130 He is gone hunting wild beasts with hounds on Aetna.

Odysseus.
131 Dost know then what to do, that we may be gone from the land?

Silenus.
132 Not I, Odysseus; but I would do anything for thee.

Odysseus.
133 Sell us food, of which we are in need.

Silenus.
134 There is nothing but flesh, as I said.

Odysseus.
135 Well, even that is a pleasant preventive of hunger.

Silenus.
136 And there is cheese curdled with fig-juice, and the milk of kine.

Odysseus.
137 Bring them out; a man should see his purchases.

Silenus.
138 But tell me, how much gold wilt thou give me in exchange?

Odysseus.
139 No gold bring I, but Dionysus’ drink.

Silenus.
140 Most welcome words! I have long been wanting that.

Odysseus.
141 Yes, it was Maron, the god’s son, who gave me a draught.

Silenus.
142 What! Maron whom once I dandled in these arms?

Odysseus.
143 The son of the Bacchic god, that thou mayst learn more certainly.

Silenus.
144 Is it inside the ship, or hast thou it with thee?

Odysseus.
145 This, as thou seest, is the skin that holds it, old sir.

Silenus.
146 Why, that would not give me so much as a mouthful.

Odysseus.
147 This, and twice as much again as will run from the skin.

Silenus.
148 Fair the rill thou speakest of, delicious to me.

Odysseus.
149 Shall I let thee taste the wine unmixed, to start with?

Silenus.
150 A reasonable offer; for of a truth a taste invites the purchase.

Odysseus.
151 Well, I haul about a cup as well as the skin.

Silenus.
152 Come, let it gurgle in, that I may revive my memory by a pull at it.

Odysseus.
153 There then!

Silenus.
153b Ye gods! what a delicious scent it has!

Odysseus.
154 What! didst thou see it?

Silenus.
154b No, i’ faith, but I smell it.

Odysseus.
155 Taste it then, that thy approval may not stop at words.

Silenus.
156 Zounds! Bacchus is inviting me to dance; ha! ha!

Odysseus.
158 Did it not gurgle finely down thy throttle?

Silenus.
159 Aye that it did, to the ends of my fingers.

Odysseus.
160 Well, we will give thee money besides.

Silenus.
161 Only undo the skin, and never mind the money.

Odysseus.
162 Bring out the cheeses then and lambs.

Silenus.
163 I will do so, with small thought of any master. For let me have a single cup of that and I would turn madman,
165 giving in exchange for it the flocks of every Cyclops and then throwing myself into the sea from the Leucadian rock, once I have been well drunk and smoothed out my wrinkled brow. For if a man rejoice not in his drinking, he is mad; for in drinking there is love
170 with all its frolic, and dancing withal, and oblivion of woe. Shall not I then purchase so rare a drink, bidding the senseless Cyclops and his central eye go hang? Exit Silenus.

Chorus.
175 Hearken, Odysseus, let us hold some converse with thee.

Odysseus.
176 Well, do so; ours is a meeting of friends.

Chorus.
177 Did you take Troy and capture the famous Helen?

Odysseus.
178 Aye, and we destroyed the whole family of Priam.

Chorus.
179 After capturing your blooming prize,
180 were all of you in turn her lovers? for she likes variety in husbands; the traitress! the sight of a man with embroidered breeches on his legs and a golden chain about his neck so fluttered her,
185 that she left Menelaus, her excellent little husband. Would there had never been a race of women born into the world at all, unless it were for me alone!

Silenus.
188 (reappearing with food.) Lo! I bring you fat food from the flocks, king Odysseus, the young of bleating sheep
190 and cheeses of curdled milk without stint. Carry them away with you and be gone from the cave at once, after giving me a drink of merry grape-juice in exchange.

Chorus.
193 Alack! yonder comes the Cyclops; what shall we do?

Odysseus.
194 Then truly are we lost, old sir! whither must we fly?

Silenus.
195 Inside this rock, for there ye may conceal yourselves.

Odysseus.
196 Dangerous advice of thine, to run into the net!

Silenus.
197 No danger; there are ways of escape in plenty in the rock.

Odysseus.
198 No, never that; for surely Troy will groan and loudly too, if we flee from a single man, when I have oft withstood
200 with my shield a countless host of Phrygians. Nay, if die we must, we will die a noble death; or, if we live, we will maintain our old renown at least with credit.

Cyclops.
203 A light here! hold it up! what is this? what means this idleness, your Bacchic revelry? Here have we no Dionysus,
205 nor clash of brass, nor roll of drums. Pray, how is it with my newly-born lambs in the caves? are they at the teat, running close to the side of their dams? Is the full amount of milk for cheeses milked out in baskets of rushes?
210 How now? what say you? One of ye will soon be shedding tears from the weight of my club; look up, not down.

Chorus.
212 There! my head is bent back till I see Zeus himself; I behold both the stars and Orion.

Cyclops.
214 Is my breakfast quite ready?

Chorus.
215 ’Tis laid; be thy throat only ready.

Cyclops.
216 Are the bowls too full of milk?

Chorus.
217 Aye, so that thou canst swill off a whole hogshead, so it please thee.

Cyclops.
218 Sheeps’ milk or cows’ milk or a mixture of both?

Chorus.
219 Whichever thou wilt; don’t swallow me, that’s all.

Cyclops.
220 Not I; for you would start kicking in the pit of my stomach and kill me by your antics. (Catching sight of Odysseus and his followers.) Ha! what is this crowd I see near the folds? Some pirates or robbers have put in here. Yes, I really see the lambs from my caves
225 tied up there with twisted osiers, cheese-presses scattered about, and old Silenus with his bald pate all swollen with blows.

Silenus.
228 Oh! oh! poor wretch that I am, pounded to a fever.

Cyclops.
229 By whom? who has been pounding thy head, old sirrah?

Silenus.
230 These are the culprits, Cyclops, all because I refused to let them plunder thee.

Cyclops.
231 Did they not know I was a god and sprung from gods?

Silenus.
232 That was what I told them, but they persisted in plundering thy goods, and, in spite of my efforts, they actually began to eat the cheese and carry off the lambs; and they said they would
235 tie thee in a three-cubit pillory and tear out thy bowels by force at thy navel, and flay thy back thoroughly with the scourge; and then, after binding thee, fling thy carcase down among the benches of their ship to sell to some one
240 for heaving up stones, or else throw thee into a mill.

Cyclops.
241 Oh, indeed! Be off then and sharpen my cleavers at once; heap high the faggots and light them; for they shall be slain forthwith and fill this maw of mine,
245 what time I pick my feast hot from the coals, waiting not for carvers, and fish up the rest from the cauldron boiled and sodden; for I have had my fill of mountain-fare and sated myself with banquets of lions and stags, but ’tis long I have been without human flesh.

Silenus.
250 Truly, master, a change like this is all the sweeter after everyday fare; for just of late there have been no fresh arrivals of strangers at these caves.

Odysseus.
253 Hear the strangers too in turn, Cyclops. We had come near the cave from our ship,
255 wishing to procure provisions by purchase, when this fellow sold us the lambs and handed them over for a stoup of wine to drink himself,—a voluntary act on both sides,—there was no violence employed at all. No, there is not a particle of truth in the story he tells,
260 now that he has been caught selling thy property behind thy back.

Silenus.
261 I? Perdition catch thee!

Odysseus.
261b If I am lying, yes.

Silenus.
262 O Cyclops, by thy sire Poseidon, by mighty Triton and Nereus, by Calypso and the daughters of Nereus,
265 by the sacred billows and all the race of fishes! I swear to thee, most noble sir, dear little Cyclops, master mine, it is not I who sell thy goods to strangers, else may these children, dearly as I love them, come to an evil end.

Chorus.
270 Keep that for thyself; with my own eyes I saw thee sell the goods to the strangers; and if I lie, perdition catch my sire! but injure not the strangers.

Cyclops.
273 Ye lie; for my part I put more faith in him than Rhadamanthus, declaring him more just.
275 But I have some questions to ask. Whence sailed ye, strangers? of what country are you? what city was it nursed your childhood?

Odysseus.
277 We are Ithacans by birth, and have been driven from our course by the winds of the sea on our way from Ilium, after sacking its citadel.

Cyclops.
280 Are ye the men who visited on Ilium, that bordereth on Scamander’s wave, the rape of Helen, worst of women?

Odysseus.
282 We are; that was the fearful labour we endured.

Cyclops.
283 A sorry expedition yours, to have sailed to the land of Phrygia for the sake of one woman!

Odysseus.
285 It was a god’s doing; blame not any son of man. But thee do we implore, most noble son of Ocean’s god, speaking as free-born men; be not so cruel as to slay thy friends on their coming to thy cave, nor regard us as food for thy jaws, an impious meal;
290 for we preserved thy sire, O king, in possession of his temple-seats deep in the nooks of Hellas; and the sacred port of Taenarus and Malea’s furthest coves remain unharmed; and Sunium’s rock, the silver-veined, sacred to Zeus-born Athena, still is safe,
295 and Geraestus, the harbour of refuge; and we did not permit Phrygians to put such an intolerable reproach on Hellas. Now in these things thou too hast a share, for thou dwellest in a corner of the land of Hellas beneath Aetna’s fire-streaming rock; and although thou turn from arguments,
300 still it is a custom amongst mortal men to receive shipwrecked sailors as their suppliants and show them hospitality and help them with raiment; not that these should fill thy jaws and belly, their limbs transfixed with spits for piercing ox-flesh. The land of Priam hath emptied Hellas quite enough,
305 drinking the blood of many whom the spear laid low, with the ruin it has brought on widowed wives, on aged childless dames, and hoary-headed sires; and if thou roast and consume the remnant,—a meal thou wilt rue,—why, where shall one turn? Nay, be persuaded by me, Cyclops;
310 forego thy ravenous greed and choose piety rather than wickedness; for on many a man ere now unrighteous gains have brought down retribution.

Silenus.
313 . . . . . I will give thee a word of advice! as for his flesh, leave not a morsel of it, and if thou eat his tongue,
315 Cyclops, thou wilt become a monstrous clever talker.

Cyclops.
316 Wealth, manikin, is the god for the wise; all else is mere vaunting and fine words. Plague take the headlands by the sea, on which my father seats himself! Why hast thou put forward these arguments?
320 I shudder not at Zeus’s thunder, nor know I wherein Zeus is a mightier god than I, sir stranger; what is more, I reck not of him; my reasons hear. When he pours down the rain from above, here in this rock in quarters snug,
325 feasting on roast calf’s flesh or some wild game and moistening well my upturned paunch with deep draughts from a tub of milk, I rival the thunderclaps of Zeus with my artillery; and when the north-wind blows from Thrace and sheddeth snow,
330 I wrap my carcase in the hides of beasts and light a fire, and what care I for snow? The earth perforce, whether she like it or not, produces grass and fattens my flocks, which I sacrifice to no one save myself
335 and this belly, the greatest of deities; but to the gods, not I! For surely to eat and drink one’s fill from day to day and give oneself no grief at all, this is the king of gods for your wise man, but lawgivers go hang, chequering, as they do, the life of man!
340 And so I will not cease from indulging myself by devouring thee; and thou shalt receive this stranger’s gift, that I may be free of blame,—fire and my father’s element yonder, and a cauldron to hold thy flesh and boil it nicely in collops.
345 So in with you, that ye may feast me well, standing round the altar to honour the cavern’s god. [Enters his cave.

Odysseus.
347 Alas! escaped from the troubles of Troy and the sea, my barque now strands upon the whim and forbidding heart of this savage.
350 O Pallas, mistress mine, goddess-daughter of Zeus, help me, help me now; for I am come to toils and depths of peril worse than all at Ilium; and thou, O Zeus, the stranger’s god, who hast thy dwelling ’mid the radiant stars, behold these things;
355 for, if thou regard them not, in vain art thou esteemed the great god Zeus, though but a thing of naught. [Follows the Cyclops reluctantly.

[Sung]

Chorus.
	356 Ope wide the portal of thy gaping throat, Cyclops; for strangers’ limbs, both boiled and grilled, are ready from off the coals for thee to gnaw and tear and mince up small,
	360 reclining in thy shaggy goatskin coat.

Chorus.
	361 Relinquish not thy meal for me; keep that boat for thyself alone. Avaunt this cave! avaunt the burnt-offerings,
	365 which the godless Cyclops offers on Aetna’s altars, exulting in meals on strangers’ flesh!
