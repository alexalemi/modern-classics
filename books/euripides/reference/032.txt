(Scene.—Before Apollo’s temple at Delphi.)

Hermes.
1 Atlas, who bears upon his brazen back the pressure of the sky, ancient dwelling of the gods, begat Maia from a daughter of one of those gods, and she bare me Hermes to mighty Zeus, to be the servant of the powers divine.
5 Lo! I am come to this land of Delphi where sits Phoebus on the centre of the world and giveth oracles to men, ever chanting lays prophetic of things that are to be. Now there is a city in Hellas of no small note, called after Pallas, goddess of the golden lance;
10 there did Phoebus force his love on Creusa, daughter of Erechtheus, beneath the rock of Pallas, northward of Athens’ steep realm, called Macrae by the kings of Attica. And she without her father’s knowledge—for such was the god’s good pleasure,—
15 bore the burden in her womb unto the end, and when her time came, she brought forth a child in the house and carried him away to the selfsame cave wherein the god declared his love to her, and she cradled him in the hollow of a rounded ark and cast him forth to die,
20 observant of the custom of her ancestors and of earth-born Erichthonius, whom the daughter of Zeus gave into the charge of the daughters of Agraulus, after setting on either side, to keep him safe, a guard of serpents twain. Hence in that land among the Erechthidae ’tis a
25 custom to protect their babes with charms of golden snakes. But ere she left the babe to die, the young mother tied about him her own broidered robe. And this is the request that Phoebus craves of me, for he is my brother, Go, brother, to those children of the soil
30 that dwell in glorious Athens, for well thou knowest Athena’s city, and take a new-born babe from out the hollow rock, his cradle and his swaddling-clothes as well, and bear him to my prophetic shrine at Delphi, and set him at the entering-in of my temple.
35 What else remains shall be my care, for that child is mine, that thou mayst know it. So I, to do my brother Loxias a service, took up the woven ark and bore it off, and at the threshold of the shrine I have laid the babe,
40 after opening the lid of the wicker cradle that the child might be seen.
41 But just as the sun-god was starting forth to run his course, a priestess chanced to enter the god’s shrine; and when her eyes lit upon the tender babe she thought it strange that any Delphian maid
45 should dare to cast her child of shame down at the temple of the god; wherefore her purpose was to remove him beyond the altar, but from pity she renounced her cruel thought, and the god to help his child did second her pity to save the babe from being cast out. So she took and brought him up,
50 but she knew not that Phoebus was his sire nor of the mother that bare him, nor yet did the child know his parents. While yet he was a child, around the altar that fed him he would ramble at his play, but when he came to man’s estate, the Delphians made him treasurer of the god
55 and steward of all his store, and found him true, and so until the present day he leads a holy life in the god’s temple. Meantime Creusa, mother of this youth, is wedded to Xuthus; and thus it came to pass; a war broke out ’twixt Athens and the folk of Chalcodon
60 who dwell in the land of Euboea; and Xuthus took part therein and helped to end it, for which he received the hand of Creusa as his guerdon, albeit he was no native, but an Achaean, sprung from Aeolus, the son of Zeus; and after many years of wedded life
65 he and Creusa still are childless; wherefore they are come to this oracle of Apollo in their desire for offspring. To this end is Loxias guiding their destiny nor hath it escaped his ken, as some suppose. For when Xuthus enters this shrine,
70 the god will give him his own son and declare that Xuthus is the sire, that so the boy may come to his mother’s home and be acknowledged by Creusa, while the marriage of Loxias remains a secret and the child obtains his rights; and he shall cause him to be called Ion, founder of a realm in Asia,
75 through all the breadth of Hellas. But now will I get me to yon grotto ’neath the laurel’s shade that I may learn what is decreed about the child. For I see the son of Loxias now coming forth to cleanse the gateway in front of the temple
80 with boughs of laurel. I greet him first of all the gods by his name Ion which he soon shall bear.

Ion.
	82 Lo! the sun-god is e’en now turning towards the earth his chariot-car resplendent; before yon fire the stars retire
	85 to night’s mysterious gloom from forth the firmament; the peaks of Parnassus, where no man may set foot, are all ablaze and hail the car of day for mortal’s service. Up mounts the smoke of myrrh, offering of the desert;
	90 to Phoebus’ roof; there on the holy tripod sits the Delphian priestess, chanting to the ears of Hellas in numbers loud, whate’er Apollo doth proclaim. Ye Delphians, votaries of Phoebus, away!
	95 to Castalia’s gushing fount as silver clear, and, when ye have bathed you in its waters pure, enter the shrine; and keep your lips in holy silence that it may be well, careful to utter words of good omen amongst yourselves
	100 to those who wish to consult the oracle; while I with laurel-sprays and sacred wreaths and drops of water sprinkled o’er the floor will purify the entrance to the shrine of Phoebus,
	105 my task each day from childhood’s hour; and with my bow will I put to flight the flocks of feathered fowls that harm his sacred offerings;
	110 for here in Phoebus’ shrine, which nurtured me, I minister, an orphan, fatherless and motherless.

Ion.
	112 Come, thou tender laurel-shoot, gathered from gardens divine to wait upon the glorious god,
	115 thou that sweepest clean the altar of Phoebus hard by his shrine, where holy founts, that ever gush with ceaseless flow,
	120 bedew the myrtle’s hallowed spray wherewith I cleanse the temple-floor the livelong day, so soon as the swift sun-god wings his flight on high, in my daily ministration.
	125 Hail Paean, prince of healing! blest, ah! doubly blest be thou, child of Latona!

Ion.
	128 Fair the service that I render to thee, Phoebus, before thy house,
	130 honouring thy seat of prophecy; a glorious task I count it, to serve not mortal man but deathless gods; wherefore I never weary
	135 of performing holy services. Phoebus is to me as the father that begot me, for as such I praise the god that gives me food. Whom I call by that helpful name of father,
	140 Tis Phoebus, who dwelleth in the temple, Hail Paean, healing god, good luck to thee and blessing, child of Latona!

Ion.
	144 My task is nearly done
	145 of sweeping with the laurel broom, so now from a golden ewer will I sprinkle o’er the ground water from Castalia’s gushing spring, scattering the liquid dew
	150 with hands from all defilement free. Oh may I never cease thus to serve Phoebus, or, if I do, may fortune smile upon me!
	154 Ha! they come, the feathered tribes,
	155 leaving their nests on Parnassus. I forbid ye to settle on the coping or enter the gilded dome. Thou herald of Zeus, that masterest the might of other birds with those talons of thine,
	160 once more shall my arrow o’ertake thee.
	161 Lo! another comes sailing towards the altar, a swan this time; take thy bright plumes elsewhere; the lyre that Phoebus tuneth to thy song shall never
	165 save thee from the bow; so fly away, and settle at the Delian mere, for if thou wilt not hearken, thy blood shall choke the utterance of thy fair melody.
	170 Ha! what new bird comes now? Does it mean to lodge a nest of dry straw for its brood beneath the gables? Soon shall my twanging bow drive thee away. Dost not hear me?
	175 Away and rear thy young amid the streams of swirling Alpheus, or get thee to the woody Isthmian glen, that Phoebus’ offerings and his shrine may take no hurt. I am loth to slay ye,
	180 ye messengers to mortal man of messages from heaven; still must I serve Phoebus, to whose tasks I am devoted, nor will I cease to minister to those that give me food.

Ion.
237 Lady, whosoe’er thou art, I see thou art of noble birth, and thy bearing proves thy gentle breeding. For from his bearing one may mostly judge
240 whether a man is nobly born. Yet am I much amazed to see thee close thine eyes in grief and with tears bedew thy noble face, when thou standest face to face with the holy oracle of Loxias. Why, lady, art thou thus disquieted?
245 Here, where all others show their joy at sight of Phoebus’ sanctuary, thine eye is wet with tears.

Creusa.
247 Most courteously, sir stranger, dost thou express surprise at these my tears; the sight of this temple of Apollo
250 recalled to me a memory of long ago, and somehow my thoughts went wandering home, though I am here myself. Ah, hapless race of women! ah, ye reckless gods! What shall I say? to what standard shall we refer justice if through the injustice of our lords and masters we are brought to ruin?

Ion.
255 Why, lady, art thou thus cast down, past all finding out?

Creusa.
256 ’Tis naught; I have shot my bolt; for what remains, I say no more, nor seek thou further to inquire.

Ion.
258 Who art thou and whence? who is the father that begat thee? by what name are we to call thee?

Creusa.
260 Creusa is my name, the daughter of Erechtheus I; my native land is Athens.

Ion.
262 A glorious city thine, lady, a noble line of ancestry! with what reverence I behold thee!

Creusa.
264 Thus far, no further goes my luck, good sir.

Ion.
265 Pray, is the current legend true—

Creusa.
266 What is thy question? I fain would learn.

Ion.
267 Was thy father’s grandsire really sprung from Earth?

Creusa.
268 Yes, Erichthonius was; but my high birth avails me not

Ion.
269 Is it true Athena reared him from the ground?

Creusa.
270 Aye, and into maidens’ hands, though not his mother’s—

Ion.
271 Consigned him, did she? as ’tis wont to be set forth in painting.

Creusa.
272 Yes, to the daughters of Cecrops, to keep him safe unseen.

Ion.
273 I have heard the maidens opened the ark wherein the goddess laid him.

Creusa.
274 And so they died, dabbling with their blood the rocky cliff.

Ion.
275 Even so. But what of this next story? Is it true or groundless?

Creusa.
276 What is thy question? Ask on, I have no calls upon my leisure.

Ion.
277 Did thy sire Erechtheus offer thy sisters as a sacrifice?

Creusa.
278 For his country’s sake he did endure to slay the maids as victims.

Ion.
279 And how didst thou, alone of al thy sisters, escape?

Creusa.
280 I was still a tender babe in my mother’s arms.

Ion.
281 Did the earth really open its mouth and swallow thy father?

Creusa.
282 The sea-god smote and slew him with his trident.

Ion.
283 Is there a spot there called Macrae?

Creusa.
284 Why ask that? what memories thou recallest!

Ion.
285 Doth the Pythian god with his flashing fire do honour to the place?

Creusa.
286 Honour, yes! Honour, indeed! would I had never seen the spot!

Ion.
287 How now? dost thou abhor that which the god holds dear?

Creusa.
288 No, no; but I and that cave are witnesses of a deed of shame.

Ion.
289 Lady, who is the Athenian lord that calls thee wife?

Creusa.
290 No citizen of Athens, but a stranger from another land.

Ion.
291 Who is he? he must have been one of noble birth.

Creusa.
292 Xuthus, son of Aeolus, sprung from Zeus.

Ion.
293 And how did he, a stranger, win thee a native born?

Creusa.
294 Hard by Athens lies a neighbouring township, Euboea.

Ion.
295 With a bounding line of waters in between, so I have heard.

Creusa.
296 This did he sack, making common cause with Cecrops’ sons.

Ion.
297 Coming as an ally, maybe; he won thy hand for this?

Creusa.
298 Yes, this was his dower of battle, the prize of his prowess.

Ion.
299 Art thou come to the oracle alone, or with thy lord?

Creusa.
300 With him. But he is now visiting the cavern of Trophonius.

Ion.
301 As a spectator merely, or to consult the oracle?

Creusa.
302 ’Tis his wish to hear the self-same answer from Trophonius and Phoebus too.

Ion.
303 Is it to seek earth’s produce or fruit of offspring that ye come?

Creusa.
304 We are childless, though wedded these many years.

Ion.
305 Hast thou never been a mother? art thou wholly childless?

Creusa.
306 Phoebus knows whether I am childless.

Ion.
307 Unhappy wife! how this doth mar thy fortune else so happy!

Creusa.
308 But who art thou? how blest I count thy mother!

Ion.
309 Lady, I am called the servant of Apollo, and so I am.

Creusa.
310 An offering of thy city, or sold to him by some master?

Ion.
311 Naught know I but this, that I am called the slave of Loxias.

Creusa.
312 Then do i in my turn pity thee, sir stranger.

Ion.
313 Because I know not her that bare me, or him that begat me.

Creusa.
314 Is thy home here in the temple, or hast thou a house to dwell in?

Ion.
315 The god’s whole temple is my house, wherever sleep o’ertakes me.

Creusa.
316 Was it as a child or young man that thou earnest to the temple?

Ion.
317 Those who seem to know the truth, say I was but a babe.

Creusa.
318 What Delphian maid, then, weaned thee?

Ion.
319 I never knew a mother’s breast. But she who brought me up—

Creusa.
320 Who was she, unhappy youth? I see thy sufferings in my own.

Ion.
321 The priestess of Phoebus; I look oh her as my mother.

Creusa.
322 Until thou earnest unto man’s estate, what nurture hadst thou?

Ion.
323 The altar fed me, and the bounty of each casual guest.

Creusa.
324 Woe is thy mother, then, whoe’er she was!

Ion.
325 Maybe my birth was some poor woman’s wrong.

Creusa.
326 Hast thou any store, for thy dress is costly enough?

Ion.
327 The god I serve gives me these robes to wear.

Creusa.
328 Wert thou never eager to inquire into thy birth?

Ion.
329 Ah! yes, lady! but I have no clue at all to guide me.

Creusa.
330 Alas! I know another woman who hath suffered as thy mother did.

Ion.
331 Who is she? If she would but help me in the task, how happy should I be!

Creusa.
332 ’Tis she on whose account I have preceded my husband hither.

Ion.
333 What are thy wishes? be sure I will serve thee, lady.

Creusa.
334 I would fain obtain a secret answer from Apollo’s oracle.

Ion.
335 Name it, then; the rest will I undertake for thee.

Creusa.
336 Hear, then, this story. Yet am I ashamed.

Ion.
337 Thus wilt thou accomplish naught, for shame is a goddess slow to act.

Creusa.
338 A friend of mine asserts that Phoebus lay with her.

Ion.
339 Phoebus with a mortal woman? Stranger lady, say not so.

Creusa.
340 Yea, and she bare the god a child without her father’s knowledge.

Ion.
341 It cannot be; some man did wrong her, and she is ashamed of it.

Creusa.
342 This she denieis herself; and she hath suffered further woe.

Ion.
343 How so, if she was wedded to a god?

Creusa.
344 The babe she bare she did expose.

Ion.
345 Where is the child who was thus cast forth? is he yet alive?

Creusa.
346 No man knoweth. That is the very thing I would ask the oracle.

Ion.
347 But if he be no more, how did he perish?

Creusa.
348 She supposes that beasts devoured the hapless babe.

Ion.
349 What proof led her to form this opinion?

Creusa.
350 She came to the place where she exposed him, but found him no longer there.

Ion.
351 Were any drops of blood upon the path?

Creusa.
352 None, she says; and yet she ranged the ground to and fro.

Ion.
353 How long is it since the babe was destroyed?

Creusa.
354 Thy age and his would measure out the self-same span, were he alive.

Ion.
355 Hath she given birth to no other child since then?

Creusa.
356 The god doth wrong her, and wretched is she in having no child.

Ion.
357 But what if Phoebus privily removed her child, and is rearing it?

Creusa.
358 Then is he acting unfairly in keeping to himself alone a joy he ought to share.

Ion.
359 Ah me! this misfortune sounds so like my own.

Creusa.
360 Thee too, fair sir, thy poor mother misses, I am sure.

Ion.
361 Oh! call me not back to piteous thoughts I had forgotten.

Creusa.
362 I am dumb; proceed with that which touches my inquiry.

Ion.
363 Dost know the one weak point in this thy story?

Creusa.
364 ’Tis all weak in that poor lady’s case.

Ion.
365 How should the god declare that which he wishes hidden?

Creusa.
366 He must, if here upon the tripod he sits for all Hellas to seek to.

Ion.
367 He is ashamed of the deed; do not question him.

Creusa.
368 Aye, but his victim has her sorrows too.

Ion.
369 There is none who will act as thy medium in this.
370 For were Phoebus in his own temple proved a villain, he would justly wreak his vengeance on the man who expounded to thee his oracles; desist then, lady; we must not prophesy against the god’s will,
375 for it would be the height of folly in us, were we to try and make the gods against their will declare reluctant truths either by sacrifice of sheep at their altars, or by omens from birds. For those answers we strive to extort from heaven, lady, are goods that bring no blessing on our getting;
380 but what they freely offer, thereby we profit.

Chorus.
381 Many are the chances that befall the many tribes of men, and diverse are their forms. But scarce one happy scene canst thou find in all the life of man.

Creusa.
384 Ah! Phoebus, here as there, art thou unjust
385 to that absent sufferer, whose cause I now am pleading. Thou didst not preserve thy child, as in duty bound, nor wilt thou, for all thy prophetic skill, answer his mother’s questioning, that, if he be no more, a mound may be raised o’er him, or, if he live, he may some day be restored to his mother’s eyes.
390 In vain is this the home of oracles if the god prevents me from learning what I wish to ask. But lo! I see my noble lord, Xuthus, nigh at hand, returning 278 from the lair of Trophonius; say nothing,
395 sir, to my husband of what I have told thee, lest I incur reproach for troubling about secrets, and the matter take a different turn to that which I sought to give it. For women stand towards men in a difficult position, and the virtuous from being mingled with the wicked amongst us are hated;
400 such is our unhappy destiny.

Xuthus.
401 First to the god all hail! for he must receive the first-fruits of my salutation, and next all hail to thee, my wife! Has my delay in arriving caused thee alarm?

Creusa.
404 By no means; but thou art come at an anxious time.
405 Tell me what response thou bringest from Trophonius, touching our future hopes of mutual offspring.

Xuthus.
407 He deigned not to forestal the prophecies of Phoebus. This only did he say, that neither thou nor I should return unto our house childless from the shrine.

Creusa.
410 Majestic mother of Phoebus, to our journey grant success, and may our previous dealings with thy son now find a better issue!

Xuthus.
413 It will be so; but who acts as the god’s spokesman here?

Ion.
414 I serve outside the shrine, others within,
415 who stand near the tripod, even the noblest of the Delphians chosen by lot, sir stranger.

Xuthus.
417 ’Tis well; I have attained the utmost of my wishes. I will go within; for I am told that a victim has been slain in public before the temple for strangers,
420 and to-day,—for it is a lucky day,—I would fain receive the god’s oracle. Do thou, my wife, take branches of laurel, and seated at the altars pray to the gods that I may carry home from Apollo’s shrine an answer that bodeth well for offspring.

Creusa.
425 All this shall be. Now, at any rate, if Loxias would retrieve his former sins, e’en though he cannot be my friend entirely, yet will I accept whate’er he deigns to give, because he is a god.

Ion.
429 Why doth this stranger lady hint dark reproaches against the god
430 unceasingly, either out of affection for her on whose behalf she seeks the oracle, or maybe because she is hiding something needing secrecy? Yet what have I to do with the daughter of Erectheus? She is naught to me.
435 No, I will go to the laver, and from golden ewers sprinkle the holy water. Yet must I warn Phoebus of what is happening to him; he ravishes a maid and proves unfaithful to her, and after secretly begetting a son leaves him to die. O! Phoebus, do not so, but as thou art supreme,
440 follow in virtue’s track; for whosoever of mortal men transgresses, him the gods punish. How, then, can it be just that you should enact your laws for men, and yourselves incur the charge of breaking them? Now I will put this case, though it will never happen.
445 Wert thou, wert Poseidon, and Zeus, the lord of heaven, to make atonement to mankind for every act of lawless love, ye would empty your temples in paying the fines for your misdeeds. For when ye pursue pleasure in preference to the claims of prudence, ye act unjustly; no longer is it fair
450 to call men wicked, if we are imitating the evil deeds of gods, but rather those who give us such examples. [Exit Ion

Ion.
510 Attendant maids, that watch and wait your mistress here at the steps of the temple fragrant with incense, say, hath Xuthus already left the holy tripod and the sanctuary, or doth he still abide within to ask yet further of his childlessness?

Chorus.
514 He is still in the temple, sir, nor hath he passed this threshold yet.
515 But hark! I hear a footstep at the outlet of the door, and lo! thou mayst see my master this moment coming out.

Xuthus.
517 All hail! my son; that word suits well as my first greeting to thee.

Ion.
518 ’Tis well with me; do but restrain thyself, and then both of us will be happy.

Xuthus.
519 Give me thy hand to grasp, thy body to embrace.

Ion.
520 Art thou in thy senses, sir, or hath some spiteful god reft thee of them?

Xuthus.
521 I am in my senses, for I have found what I hold most dear, and am eager to show my love.

Ion.
522 Cease! touch me not, nor tear these garlands of the god!

Xuthus.
523 I will embrace thee, for I am not seizing what is not my own, but only finding my own that I love full well.

Ion.
524 Hands off! or thou shalt feel an arrow pierce thy ribs.

Xuthus.
525 Why dost thou shun me, now that thou findest in me thy nearest and dearest?

Ion.
526 I am not fond of schooling boors and crazy strangers.

Xuthus.
527 Kill me, burn me, if thou wilt; for, if thou dost, thou wilt be thy father’s murderer.

Ion.
528 Thou my father, indeed i Oh! is not news like this enough to make me laugh?

Xuthus.
529 Not so; my tale, as it proceeds, will prove to thee what I assert.

Ion.
530 Pray, what hast thou to tell me?

Xuthus.
530b That I am thy own father, and thou my very child.

Ion.
531 Who says so?

Xuthus.
531b Loxias, who gave thee nurture, though thou wert my son.

Ion.
532 Thou art thy own witness.

Xuthus.
532b Nay, I have learnt the answer of the god.

Ion.
533 Thou art mistaken in the dark riddle thou hast heard.

Xuthus.
533b It seems then I do not hear aright.

Ion.
534 What said Phoebus?

Xuthus.
534b That the man who met me—

Ion.
535 When and where?

Xuthus.
535b As I came forth from the god’s temple—

Ion.
536 Well! what should happen to him?

Xuthus.
536b Should be my own true son.

Ion.
537 Thy own true son, or a gift from others?

Xuthus.
537b A gift, but mine for all that.

Ion.
538 Am I the first that thou didst meet?

Xuthus.
538b I have met no other, my son.

Ion.
539 Whence came this piece of luck?

Xuthus.
539b To both of us alike it causes surprise.

Ion.
540 Ah I but who was my mother?

Xuthus.
540b I cannot tell.

Ion.
541 Did not Phoebus tell thee that?

Xuthus.
541b I was so pleased with this, I did not ask him that.

Ion.
542 I must have sprung from mother earth.

Xuthus.
542b The ground brings forth no children.

Ion.
543 How can I be thine?

Xuthus.
543b I know not; I refer it to the god.

Ion.
544 Come, let us try another theme.

Xuthus.
544b Better hold to this, my son.

Ion.
545 Didst thou e’er indulge in illicit amours?

Xuthus.
545b Yes, in the folly of youth.

Ion.
546 Ere thou didst win Erechtheus’ daughter?

Xuthus.
546b Never since.

Ion.
547 Could it be, then, thou didst beget me?

Xuthus.
547b The time coincides therewith.

Ion.
548 In that case, how came I hither?

Xuthus.
548b That puzzles me.

Ion.
549 After that long journey too?

Xuthus.
549b That, too, perplexes me.

Ion.
550 Didst thou in days gone by come to the Pythian rock?

Xuthus.
550b Yes, to join in the mystic rites of Bacchus.

Ion.
551 Didst thou lodge with one of the public hosts?

Xuthus.
551b With one who at Delphi—

Ion.
552 Initiated thee? or what is it thou sayest?

Xuthus.
552b Among the frantic votaries of Bacchus.

Ion.
553 Wert thou sober, or in thy cups?
