Helen (Part 2 of 4) -- Coleridge's prose, crib only

[Spoken]

Old woman.
443 Go away! Stranger, my orders are to let no Hellene come near this house.

Menelaos.
445 Ah! Do not push me, or thrust me away by force.

Old woman.
446 You are to blame, for not heeding what I say.

Menelaos.
447 Announce to your master inside—

Old woman.
448 I think someone would be sorry, if I announced your words.

Menelaos.
449 I come as a shipwrecked man and a guest; such people are safe from violence.

Old woman.
450 Well, go to some other house instead of this one.

Menelaos.
451 No; I am going inside. You listen to me.

Old woman.
452 Know that you’re only causing trouble; and soon you’ll be thrown out by force.

Menelaos.
453 Alas! Where are those glorious armies of mine?

Old woman.
454 Perhaps you were grand somewhere, but not here.

Menelaos.
455 O my fortune, how we have been unworthily dishonored.

Old woman.
456 Why are your eyes wet with tears? To whom are you lamenting?

Menelaos.
457 To my fortunes, which were happy before this.

Old woman.
458 Well then, why don’t you go away and give these tears to your friends.

Menelaos.
459 What is this land? Whose palace is this?

Old woman.
460 Proteus lives here, the land is Egypt.

Menelaos.
461 Egypt? O wretched, that I have sailed here!

Old woman.
462 And why do you blame the bright gleam of the Nile?

Menelaos.
463 I do not blame it; I am sighing for my fate.

Old woman.
464 Many people are doing badly; you are not the only one.

Menelaos.
465 Is the king you name in the house?

Old woman.
466 This is his tomb; his son rules the land.

Menelaos.
467 And where might he be? Abroad, or in the house?

Old woman.
468 He is not inside; he is most bitterly opposed to the Hellenes.

Menelaos.
469 What cause does he have? I have felt the consequences of it!

Old woman.
470 Helen, the daughter of Zeus, is in this house.

Menelaos.
471 What do you mean? What did you say? Tell me again.

Old woman.
472 The daughter of Tyndareus, who once lived in Sparta.

Menelaos.
473 Where did she come from? What is the meaning of this?

Old woman.
474 She came here from the land of Lakedaimon.

Menelaos.
475 When? Surely I have not been robbed of my wife from the cave?

Old woman.
476 Before the Achaeans went to Troy, stranger. But get away from the house; for something is happening within, by which the palace is thrown into confusion. You have not come at the right time; and if my master
480 catches you, death will be your guest-gift. For I am well-disposed to Hellenes, for all that I spoke harshly to you in fear of my master.

(Exit Old woman.)

Menelaos.
483 What can I say? For after my former troubles, this present event that I hear of is an unhappy one,
485 if I have come here, bringing my wife who was taken from Troy, and she is kept safe in the cave, but some other woman who has the same name as my wife lives in this house. She said the woman was born the child of Zeus.
490 Can there be a man with the name of Zeus by the banks of Nile? For there is only one in heaven. Where in the world is there a Sparta, except by the streams of Eurotas, with its lovely reeds? The name of Tyndareus is the name of one alone.
495 Is there any land of the same name as Lakedaimon or Troy? I do not know what to say; for there are probably many things in the wide world that have the same names, both cities and women; there is nothing, then, to marvel at.
500 Besides, I will not run away from a servant’s fears; for no man is so barbaric at heart as to refuse me food when he has heard my name. The fire of Troy is famous, and I, Menelaos, who lighted it, am well known in every land.
505 I will wait for the master of the house; he gives me two things to look out for: if he is a cruel sort of person, I will keep myself hidden and go back to the shipwreck; but if he shows any softening, I will ask for help in my present state.
510 This is the worst evil for me in my misery, to beg the means of life from other kings, when I am myself a king; but it is necessary. The saying is not mine, but it is a wise word: nothing is stronger than dreadful necessity.

[Sung]

Chorus.
	515 I have heard the prophetic maiden, who gave a clear answer within the palace: Menelaos is not yet dead and buried, gone to the land of shadows where darkness takes the place of light;
	520 but he is still wearing out his life on the ocean swell and has not yet reached the haven of his country, wretched in his wandering life,
	525 bereft of every friend, approaching every land in his sea-going ship from the land of Troy.

[Spoken]

Helen.
528 Here I am, once again coming back to the sanctuary of this tomb, after learning the welcome words of Theonoe,
530 who knows all things truly; she says my husband is alive and sees the light of day; he is roaming here and there on countless voyages, not without practice in wandering, and he shall come here when he finds an end to his suffering.
535 But she left one thing unsaid: if he will escape when he has come? And I refrained from asking that question clearly; I was so glad when she told me he was safe. She said that he was near this land somewhere, cast up, shipwrecked, with a few friends.
540 Oh, when will you come? How much I long for your arrival!
541 Ah! Who is this? I am not being ambushed by the plots of Proteus’ impious son, am I? Shall I not, like a young racehorse or a worshipper of Bacchus, reach the tomb? There is something wild
545 about the looks of this man who is hunting me down.

Menelaos.
546 You there! the one trying with fearful effort to reach the base of the tomb and the pillars of burnt sacrifice, stay where you are. Why do you flee? I am amazed and speechless at the sight of your body.

Helen.
550 Women, I am being ill-treated. This man is keeping me from the tomb, and he wants to take me and give me to the king, whose wooing I was seeking to avoid.

Menelaos.
553 I am no thief, nor a servant of evil men.

Helen.
554 And yet the clothes you are wearing are unsightly enough.

Menelaos.
555 Put fear aside and stop your rapid flight.

Helen.
556 I do so, now that I have reached this spot.

Menelaos.
557 Who are you? Whom do I see in you, lady?

Helen.
558 But who are you? The same reason prompts us both.

Menelaos.
559 I never saw a closer resemblance.

Helen.
560 O gods! For the recognizing of friends is a god.

Menelaos.
561 Are you a woman from Hellas, or a native of this land?

Helen.
562 From Hellas; but I want to learn your story too.

Menelaos.
5563 You seem to me very much like Helen, lady.

Helen.
564 And you seem to me like Menelaos; I don’t know what to say.

Menelaos.
565 Well, you have correctly recognized a most unfortunate man.

Helen.
566 Oh, at last you have come to the arms of your wife!

Menelaos.
567 What do you mean by wife? Do not touch my robe.

Helen.
568 The one whom Tyndareus, my father, gave to you.

Menelaos.
569 O torch-bearing Hekate, send visions that are favorable!

Helen.
570 You see in me no specter of the night, attendant on the queen of phantoms.

Menelaos.
571 As one man, I am certainly not the husband of two women.

Helen.
572 You are the master of what other wife?

Menelaos.
573 The one hidden in the cave, whom I am bringing from Troy.

Helen.
574 You have no other wife but me.

Menelaos.
575 Can it be that I am in my right mind, but my sight is failing?

Helen.
576 Don’t you think that when you look at me you see your wife?

Menelaos.
577 Your body resembles hers, but the real truth robs me of this belief.

Helen.
578 Look; what more do you need? Who knows better than you?

Menelaos.
579 You are like her; I will not deny that at least.

Helen.
580 Who then shall teach you, if not your own eyes?

Menelaos.
581 It is there that I am ailing, because I have another wife.

Helen.
582 I did not go to Troy; that was a phantom.

Menelaos.
583 And who fashions living bodies?

Helen.
584 The air, out of which you have a wife that the gods labored over.

Menelaos.
585 What god’s handiwork? You are saying things beyond hope.

Helen.
586 Hera’s, as a substitute, so that Paris would not have me.

Menelaos.
587 How then could you be here and in Troy at the same time?

Helen.
588 The name may be in many places, though not the body.

Menelaos.
589 Let me go! I have come here with enough pain.

Helen.
590 Will you leave me, and take that phantom bride away?

Menelaos.
591 Yes, and fare well, for your likeness to Helen.

Helen.
592 I am ruined! I found you, my husband, but I will not have you.

Menelaos.
593 The greatness of my troubles over there convinces me; you do not.

Helen.
594 Ah me! Who was ever more miserable than I am?
595 Those whom I love best are leaving me, and I shall never reach the Hellenes or my own country.

Messenger.
597 (entering hurriedly.) Menelaos, I find you, after taking great trouble to look for you, wandering over the whole of this foreign land; I am sent by the comrades whom you left behind.

Menelaos.
600 What is it? Surely you are not being plundered by the foreigners?

Messenger.
601 It is a miracle; what I say is of less account than what happened.

Menelaos.
602 Tell me; for, judging by this eagerness, you are certainly bringing something new.

Messenger.
603 I say that you have suffered countless labors in vain.

Menelaos.
604 You are mourning over old sorrows; what is your message?

Messenger.
605 Your wife has disappeared, taken up into the folds of the unseen air; she is hidden in heaven, and as she left the hallowed cave where we were keeping her, she said this: Miserable Phrygians, and all the Achaeans! On my account you were dying by the banks of Skamandros,
610 through Hera’s contrivance, for you thought that Paris had Helen when he didn’t. But I, since I have stayed my appointed time, and kept the laws of fate, will now depart into the sky, my father; but the unhappy daughter of Tyndareus,
615 guilty in no way, has borne an evil name without reason.
616 Welcome, daughter of Leda, were you here after all? I was just announcing your departure up to the hidden starry realms, not knowing that you had a winged body. I will not let you mock us like this again,
620 for you gave your fill of trouble to your husband and his allies in Ilion.

Menelaos.
622 This is the meaning of that; her words have turned out to be true. O longed-for day, that has given you to my arms!

Helen.
625 O Menelaos, dearest of men, the time was long, but delight is just now ours.
627 With joy I have found my husband, friends, I have embraced my dear one, after long days of blazing light.

Menelaos.
630 And I have found you; but I have many questions about t; now I do not know what to begin with first.

[Sung]

Helen.
	632 I am so happy, the hair rises on my head and my tears run down. I fling my arms around your neck,
	635 dear husband, to have my delight.

Menelaos.
	636 O dearest sight! I have no fault to find: I have my wife, the daughter of Zeus and Leda; your brothers on their snow-white steeds
	640 blessed you, blessed you at an earlier time, while torches blazed, but the god who took you from my home is driving us on to another fortune, better than this. An evil that was good brought you together with me, your husband
	645 after a long time, but may I still benefit by my good luck.

Chorus.
	646 May you benefit indeed, and I join in the same prayer; for when there are two, it is not possible for one to be unhappy and the other not.

Helen.
	648 My dear friends, I no longer sigh or grieve over what is past.
	650 I have my husband, for whom I have been waiting to come from Troy for many years.

Menelaos.
	652 You have me, and I have you; although it was hard to live through so many days, I now understand the actions of the goddess. My joy is tearful; it has more
	655 delight than sorrow.

Helen.
	656 What can I say? What mortal could ever have hoped for this? I hold you to my heart, little as I ever thought to.

Menelaos.
	658 And I hold you, whom we thought to have gone to Ida’s city and the unhappy towers of Ilion.
	660 By the gods, how were you taken from my home?

Helen.
	661 Ah! ah! You are setting out on a bitter beginning. Ah! ah! You are asking about a bitter tale.

Menelaos.
	663 Speak; all gifts from the gods should be heard.

Helen.
	664 I detest the story I am now to introduce.

Menelaos.
	665 Tell it anyway. It is sweet to hear of troubles.

Helen.
	666 Not to the bed of the young barbarian, on the wings of oars, on the wings of desire for lawless marriage—

Menelaos.
	669 What god or fate tore you from your country?

Helen.
	670 Ah, my husband! The son of Zeus, of Zeus, brought me to the Nile.

Menelaos.
	672 Amazing! Who sent you there? O dreadful story!

Helen.
	673 I have wept bitterly, and my eyes are wet with tears; the wife of Zeus ruined me.

Menelaos.
	675 Hera? Why did she want to bring trouble to the two of us?

Helen.
	676 Alas for my terrible fate, the baths and springs, where the goddesses brightened the beauty from which the judgment came.

Menelaos.
	679 Regarding the judgment, Hera made it a cause of these troubles for you?

Helen.
	680 To take me away from Paris—

Menelaos.
	680b How? Tell me.

Helen.
	681 To whom Kypris had promised me.

Menelaos.
	681b O unhappy one!

Helen.
	682 Unhappy, unhappy; and so she brought me to Egypt.

Menelaos.
	683 Then she gave him a phantom instead, as I hear from you.

Helen.
	684 Sorrow, sorrow to your house,
	685 mother, alas.

Menelaos.
	685b What do you mean?

Helen.
	686 My mother is no more; through shame of my disgraceful marriage she tied a noose around her neck.

Menelaos.
	688 Alas! Is our daughter Hermione alive?

Helen.
	689 Ah, my husband! Unmarried, and without children, she mourns my
	690 fatal marriage.

Menelaos.
	691 O Paris, who utterly destroyed my whole house, these things ruined you also, and countless bronze-clad Danaans.

Helen.
	694 The god cast me out, ill-fated and accursed, from my country,
	695 from my city, and from you, when I left my home and bed—yet I did not leave them—for a shameful marriage.

[Spoken]

Chorus Leader.
698 If indeed you should find happiness in the future, it would be a match for the past.

Messenger.
700 Menelaos, give me as well a share of that joy which I understand, but not clearly.

Menelaos.
702 Come and take part in our talk, old man, you too.

Messenger.
703 This woman is not the arbitrator of all the trouble in Troy?

Menelaos.
704 She is not; I was tricked by the gods
705 and had in my arms the baneful image of a cloud.

Messenger.
706 What are you saying? We suffered in vain for the sake of a cloud?

Menelaos.
708 It was the work of Hera, and the rivalry of the three goddesses.

Messenger.
709 And the one who is truly your wife is this woman here?

Menelaos.
710 This is she; trust my word for that.

Messenger.
711 O daughter, how intricate and hard to trace out is the nature of the god! In some way that is good, he twists everything about, now up, now down; one man suffers, and one who has not suffered comes afterwards to a bad end,
715 having no security in his current fortune. You and your husband have had your share of trouble, you in repute, he in the heat of battle. In his eagerness, while he was eager, he got nothing; but now that he has achieved the greatest good fortune, he has it without cause.
720 You did not, after all, bring shame upon your old father or on the twin sons of Zeus, nor did you do such things as were spoken of. Now again I renew your wedding rites and remember the blazing torch I bore, running beside the four yoked horses; and you,
725 in the chariot as a bride, were leaving your happy home with this man here. Whoever pays no reverence to his master’s affairs, rejoicing with him and grieving with his troubles, is worthless. Although I was born a servant, let me still be numbered among honest
730 slaves; my mind is free, if not my name. For this is better than to suffer double misery as one man: to have a worthless heart and, being a slave, to owe obedience to any other.

Menelaos.
734 Come, old man—often by my shield
735 you have had your full share of trouble and hard work—now also have a share in my success, and go tell the friends I left behind the state of matters here, as you found them, and how my fortune stands; and bid them wait at the beach and watch eagerly for the
740 struggle which I expect awaits me; and if we should be able somehow to steal this woman away from the land, tell them to keep good watch so that we may share the luck and escape, if we can, from the barbarians.

Messenger.
744 It shall be done, lord. Now indeed I see how worthless
745 the seers’ doings are, and how full of falsehood; there was no health in the blaze of sacrifice after all, or in the cry of winged birds; even to think that birds can help mankind is certainly foolish. For Calchas gave no word or sign to the army,
750 when he saw his friends dying on behalf of a cloud, nor did Helenos; but the city was taken by storm in vain. You might say: because the god did not want them to? Then why do we consult prophets? We ought to sacrifice to the gods and ask a blessing, but leave divination alone;
755 for this was invented otherwise, as a bait for a livelihood, and no man grows rich by sacrifices if he is idle. But sound judgment and discernment are the best of seers. Exit Messenger.

Chorus Leader.
758 My views about seers coincide exactly with this old man’s; whoever has the gods as friends
760 would have the best prophecy at home.

Helen.
761 All right; so far all is well. But how you were saved, my poor husband, from Troy, there is no gain in knowing, yet friends have a desire to learn what their friends have suffered.

Menelaos.
765 Truly you have asked a great deal all at once. Why should I tell you about our losses in the Aegean, and Nauplios’ beacons on Euboia, and my visits to Crete and the cities of Libya, and the mountain-peaks of Perseus? For I would not satisfy you with the tale,
770 and by telling you these evils I would suffer still, as I did when I experienced them; and so my grief would be doubled.

Helen.
772 Your answer is better than my question. Leave out the rest, and tell me only this: how long were you a weary wanderer over the surface of the sea?

Menelaos.
775 Besides those ten years in Troy, I went through seven cycles of years on board ship.

Helen.
777 Alas, poor man, you have spoken of a long time; and, saved from there, you have come here to the slaughter.

Menelaos.
779 What do you mean? What will you say? Ah, my wife, you have ruined me.

Helen.
780 Escape from this land and flee as quickly as possible. The man who lives in this house will kill you.

Menelaos.
782 What have I done to deserve such a fate?

Helen.
783 You have come unexpectedly to hinder my marriage.

Menelaos.
784 What! Does someone plan to marry my wife?

Helen.
785 And to act in violence against me, which I have endured.

Menelaos.
786 Does he have private power, or is he the ruler of the country?

Helen.
787 He is the lord of this land, the son of Proteus.

Menelaos.
788 This is that riddle I heard from the servant.

Helen.
789 Which one of the barbarian’s gates were you standing beside?

Menelaos.
790 This one, from which I was being driven away like a beggar.

Helen.
791 You were surely not begging for food, were you? How unhappy I am!

Menelaos.
792 That was the deed, though it did not have that name.

Helen.
793 Then you know everything, it seems, about my marriage.

Menelaos.
794 I do. But if you have escaped his bed—that I do not know.

Helen.
795 Know that I have saved myself untouched for you.

Menelaos.
796 What could persuade me of this? If true, your words are sweet.

Helen.
797 Do you see my wretched sanctuary at this tomb?

Menelaos.
798 I see a miserable bed of straw, but what do you have to do with it?

Helen.
799 Here, as a suppliant, I am asking for an escape from his bed.

Menelaos.
800 For want of an altar, or because it is the barbarians’ way?

Helen.
801 This was as good a protection to me as the temples of the gods.

Menelaos.
802 Then it’s not possible for me to take you home by ship?

Helen.
803 A sword is waiting for you, rather than my bed.

Menelaos.
804 So I would be the most wretched of mortals.

Helen.
805 Do not feel shame now, but escape from this land.

Menelaos.
806 Leaving you behind? I ravaged Troy for your sake.

Helen.
807 Yes, for that is better than that our union should cause your death.

Menelaos.
808 Oh! these are coward’s words, unworthy of those days at Troy.

Helen.
809 You could not kill the tyrant, your possible intention.

Menelaos.
810 Does he then have a body that cannot be wounded by a sword?

Helen.
811 You will hear. But to undertake impossibilities is no mark of wisdom.

Menelaos.
812 And so I am to offer my hands to be bound, in silence?

Helen.
813 You are in a dilemma; we need some contrivance.

Menelaos.
814 Yes, for it is sweeter to die in action than by not acting.

Helen.
815 There is one hope, and only one, for our safety.

Menelaos.
816 Are we to buy it, or dare it, or win it with words?

Helen.
817 If the tyrant were not to learn of your arrival.

Menelaos.
818 Will any one tell him about me? He will certainly not know who I am.

Helen.
819 He has within an ally equal to the gods.

Menelaos.
820 A voice that has settled in the inmost parts of his house?

Helen.
821 No, but his sister; she is called Theonoe.

Menelaos.
822 The name is prophetic; tell me what she does.

Helen.
823 She knows everything, and she will tell her brother that you are here.

Menelaos.
824 We must die; for I cannot escape her notice.

Helen.
825 Perhaps we might persuade her by supplication—

Menelaos.
826 To do what? What hope are you leading me to?

Helen.
827 Not to tell her brother that you are here in this land.

Menelaos.
828 If we persuade her, could we get away from this country?

Helen.
829 Easily, in common with her; but secretly we could not.

Menelaos.
830 The task is yours; it is suitable for women to deal with women.

Helen.
831 Be sure that I will clasp her knees.

Menelaos.
832 Well, then, what if she rejects our proposals?

Helen.
833 You will die. And I, the unhappy one, will be married by force.

Menelaos.
834 You would be a traitor; that force of yours is all an excuse.

Helen.
835 But I have sworn a sacred oath by your life—

Menelaos.
836 What do you mean? To die? And never to take his bed in exchange for mine?

Helen.
837 Yes, by the same sword; I will lie at your side.

Menelaos.
838 Then on these conditions touch my right hand.

Helen.
839 I touch it, swearing that I will leave the light of day if you die.
