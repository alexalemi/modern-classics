Mill wrote this in 1861 and then put it away. He judged that nobody was ready for it, and he was probably right: when it finally appeared in 1869 it was mocked by people who had admired On Liberty ten years earlier. In between he had sat in the House of Commons as member for Westminster, and in 1867, during the debate on the Reform Bill, he moved that the word "man" be replaced by "person". It was the first time Parliament voted on votes for women. It was defeated, and no British woman voted in a parliamentary election for another fifty years.

One word will trip a modern reader in silence, and it is everywhere in the book. Disability does not mean an impairment. It means a legal bar — a thing the law forbids you to do because of what you were born as. Mill's subject is the disabilities of women in the sense a lawyer of 1869 would have used, and reading it the modern way turns his argument inside out.

The argument runs in four moves. First, that we do not know what women are like, because the question has never been put to the test: what is called the nature of women is an artificial thing, and you cannot learn the nature of anything by studying it only in chains. Second, and this is the hard chapter, what the law of marriage actually said in 1869, set out point by point beside what the law of slavery said. That comparison is legal rather than figurative, and both halves of it must stand: Mill says twice that wives are not in general treated as slaves are, and that is exactly what licenses him to insist that in law they are worse placed, since a slave has hours off and an acknowledged right to refuse his master the last familiarity. Third, that no occupation should be shut to anybody by an accident of birth, because the only way to find out what a person can do is to let them try. Fourth, the question sceptics actually want answered: what would anyone gain?

His answer to the last one is the surprising part, and it is the reason to read past the reputation. Not chiefly that women would gain, though they would, but that men would — that a boy raised to believe himself the superior of half the human race by birth alone is being taught something that deforms him, and that a man who picks a companion who cannot challenge him has arranged to stop learning. The real obstacle, Mill says flatly, is not any doubt about what women can do. It is that most men cannot yet bear the thought of living with an equal.

The book is complete here, with Mill's own footnotes in place. The 1869 printing heads each chapter with a bare Roman numeral, so the four chapter titles are new writing. The vocabulary of bondage, slavery and despotism is his and has not been softened. Mary Wollstonecraft's Vindication of the Rights of Woman of 1792 and Mill's On Liberty of 1859, whose dedication to Harriet Taylor points straight at this book, are both in the collection.
