Lesson 20: Logical Fallacies

To get a satisfactory knowledge of the rules of correct thinking, we need to become familiar with the most common kinds of fallacy, that is, the ways in which we often fall into bad reasoning by neglecting the rules of logic. In the earlier lessons we have been learning, so to speak, how to find the right road. Our job here is to find the turnings where we are most likely to take the wrong one.

In describing the fallacies I will follow the order and the way of classifying them that has been usual for more than 2,000 years, in fact ever since the great teacher Aristotle first explained the fallacies. Under this arrangement the fallacies are divided into two main groups: the logical fallacies and the material fallacies.

1. The logical fallacies are the ones that lie in the mere form of the statement, or, as the old Latin expressions put it, in dictione or in voce (in the wording, or in the words). So it is supposed that fallacies of this kind can be discovered without any knowledge of the subject-matter the argument is about.

2. The material fallacies, on the other hand, arise outside the mere verbal statement, or, as it is said, extra dictionem (outside the wording). So they are concerned with the subject of the argument, in re (in the matter), and they can only be detected and set right by people who know the subject.

The first group, the logical fallacies, can be divided further into the purely logical and the semi-logical. In the purely logical class we can include the plain breaches of the rules of the syllogism, which have already been described. So we can list the Purely Logical Fallacies as:

1. Fallacy of four terms (Quaternio Terminorum): a breach of Rule 1;

2. Fallacy of undistributed middle: a breach of Rule 3;

3. Fallacy of illicit process, of the major or the minor term: a breach of Rule 4;

4. Fallacy of negative premises: a breach of Rule 5; and also breaches of the 6th rule, which have been given no separate name. Breaches of the 7th and 8th rules can be resolved into the ones above (see Lesson 17), but they can also be described as they were at the end of Lesson 15.

The other part of the class of logical fallacies contains the Semi-logical fallacies. There are six of them:

	1. Fallacy of Equivocation.
	2. Fallacy of Amphiboly.
	3. Fallacy of Composition.
	4. Fallacy of Division.
	5. Fallacy of Accent.
	6. Fallacy of Figure of Speech.

I will describe and illustrate these one after another.

Equivocation is using the same term in two different senses. Any of the three terms of the syllogism can suffer from this fallacy, but usually it is the middle term, used in one sense in one premise and in another sense in the other. In that case it is often called the fallacy of ambiguous middle. When we separate the two meanings by expressing each of them in other suitable words, it becomes clear that the supposed syllogism contains four terms. So the fallacy of equivocation can be considered a disguised fallacy of four terms. Suppose someone argued that "all criminal actions ought to be punished by law; prosecutions for theft are criminal actions; therefore prosecutions for theft ought to be punished by law." In the first premise a "criminal action" is a crime. In the second it is the legal name for a criminal case brought in court. It is quite obvious that the term means totally different things in the two premises, and that there is no true middle term at all. Often, though, the ambiguity is subtle and difficult, so that people can hold different opinions about it. For example, we might argue:

"He who harms another should be punished. He who communicates an infectious disease to another person harms him. Therefore he who communicates an infectious disease to another person should be punished."

Whether this counts as a correct argument depends on what kinds of actions we think come under the term harm: whether we think carelessness, or only malice, is needed to make something harm. Many difficult legal questions are of this kind. For instance:

	Nuisances are punishable by law;
	To keep a noisy dog is a nuisance;
	To keep a noisy dog is punishable by law.

The question here would turn on how great a nuisance has to be before the law will step in to prevent it. Or again:

	Interference with another man's business is illegal;
	Underselling interferes with another man's business;
	Therefore underselling is illegal.

Here the question turns on the kind of interference, and it is obvious that underselling (selling more cheaply than a competitor) is not the kind of interference the major premise is talking about.

The Fallacy of Amphiboly, or amphibology, is an ambiguous grammatical structure in a sentence, which leads to a misunderstanding. A famous example comes in the prophecy of the spirit in Shakespeare's Henry VI: "The Duke yet lives that Henry shall depose." In plain words, "The Duke is still alive that Henry shall depose." The grammar leaves it completely uncertain whether the Duke will depose Henry or Henry will depose the Duke. This prophecy is no doubt an imitation of the ones the ancient oracle of Delphi is said to have given, and it seems that this fallacy was a great resource for oracles who were not confident in their own powers of foresight. The Latin language gives plenty of room for misreadings, because it does not require any fixed order for the words of a sentence. When there are two nouns in the accusative case with an infinitive verb, it may be hard to tell, except from the context, which of them is meant to come before the verb in sense, that is, which one is doing the action. The double meaning that can be given to "twice two and three" comes from amphiboly. It can be 7 or 10, depending on whether we add the 3 after multiplying or before. When sentences are carelessly built, it is often impossible to tell which part an adverb or a qualifying phrase belongs to. If someone says "I finished my business and returned the day after," the business may have been finished on the day after as well as the return. But it could just as well have been finished on the day before. An ambiguity of this kind can usually be avoided by simply changing the order of the words, for instance: "I finished my business, and, on the day after, returned." Amphiboly can also arise from mixing up the subjects and predicates in a compound sentence. Take "platinum and iron are very rare and useful metals." It would be amphiboly if I meant the predicate useful to go with platinum and rare with iron, which is not what the sentence intends. The word "respectively" is often used to show the reader that he is not free to apply every predicate to every subject: each predicate goes with its own subject, in order.

The Fallacy of Composition is a special case of equivocation. It comes from confusing a universal term with a collective term. In the premises of a syllogism we may say something about a class of things distributively, that is, about each one of them separately, and then in the conclusion infer the same thing about the whole put together. We can say, for example, that "all the angles of a triangle are less than two right angles," meaning that any one of the angles is less than two right angles. But we must not infer that all the angles put together are less than two right angles. We must not argue that because every member of a jury is very likely to judge wrongly, the jury as a whole is also very likely to judge wrongly. Nor must we argue that because each of the witnesses in a court case may give false or mistaken evidence, no confidence can be placed in the agreeing testimony of a number of witnesses. It is through a fallacy of Composition that protective duties (tariffs that shield a home industry from foreign competition) are still sometimes defended. Because any one trade, or any few trades, that enjoy protective duties are benefited by them, people suppose that all trades at once could be benefited in the same way. But this is impossible, because protecting one trade raises prices, and so injures all the others.

The Fallacy of Division is the reverse of the one before. It consists in using the middle term collectively in the major premise but distributively in the minor, so that the whole is divided into its parts. For example, someone might argue: "All the angles of a triangle are (together) equal to two right angles; ABC is an angle of a triangle; therefore ABC is equal to two right angles." Or again: "The inhabitants of the town consist of men, women and children of all ages; those who met in the Guildhall (the town hall) were inhabitants of the town; therefore they consisted of men, women and children of all ages." Or: "The judges of the court of appeal cannot misinterpret the law; Lord A.B. is a judge of the court of appeal; therefore he cannot misinterpret the law."

The Fallacy of Accent is any ambiguity that comes from a misplaced accent, or emphasis, on some word of a sentence. An absurd example is liable to happen when reading 1 Kings 13:27, where it is said of the prophet: "And he spake to his sons, saying, Saddle me the ass. And they saddled _him_." ("Saddle me the ass" means "saddle the donkey for me.") The word _him_ is printed in italics because the translators of the Authorized Version (the King James Bible) supplied it themselves. It is meant to refer to the ass, but read with a stress on it, it suggests a very different meaning: that the sons saddled their father. The Commandment "Thou shalt not bear false witness against thy _neighbor_" can be made, by a slight emphasis of the voice on the last word, to imply that we are free to bear false witness against other people. Mr. De Morgan, who points this out, also notes that misquoting an author, by unfairly separating a word from its context, or by italicizing words that were never meant to be italicized, produces cases of this fallacy.

It is curious to see how many different meanings the same sentence can be given, depending on which word we put the emphasis on. Take the sentence "The study of logic is not supposed to give us a knowledge of many useful facts." It can be made to imply that the study of logic does give such knowledge, even though it is not supposed to; or that it gives a knowledge of a few useful facts; or that it gives a knowledge of many useless facts. We can explain this ambiguity as follows. If you deny that a thing has the group of qualities A, B, C, D, your statement is true as long as any one of those qualities is missing, and an accent in speaking is often used to point out the one the speaker believes is missing. If you deny that a particular fruit is ripe and sweet and well-flavored, it may be unripe and sweet and well-flavored; or ripe and sour and well-flavored; or ripe and sweet and badly flavored; or any two, or even all three, of the qualities may be missing. But if you deny that it is ripe and sweet and _well-flavored_, your denial would be understood to refer to the last quality. Jeremy Bentham was so afraid of being misled by this fallacy of accent that, as I have heard, he employed a person to read to him who had a peculiarly monotonous way of reading.

The Fallacy of the Figure of Speech is the sixth and last of the semi-logical fallacies, and it is a very trivial one. It seems to consist in any grammatical mistake, or any confusion between one part of speech and another. Aristotle quite seriously gives this example: "Whatever a man walks he tramples on; a man walks the whole day; therefore he tramples on the day." Here an adverbial phrase ("the whole day," meaning for how long he walks) is turned into the object of the verb, as if the day were something he walks, the way he walks the streets.
