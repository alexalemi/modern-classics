Lesson 7: Leibnitz on Knowledge

When we study terms, we need to understand clearly what it takes to have a perfect notion of what a term means. When someone uses a name like monarch, or civilization, or autonomy, it points the mind toward some thing or some idea, and if possible we ought to have perfect knowledge of that thing or idea before we use the word. So what does this perfect knowledge consist of? What are its necessary characters? The famous mathematician and philosopher Leibniz tried to answer this question in a short treatise, or tract, first published in 1684. Several recent books on logic base what they say on the subject on this tract, and Mr. Baynes has added a complete translation of it at the end of his translation of the Port Royal Logic. Leibniz's own remarks are not always easy to understand, so I will not stick to his exact words. Instead I will try to give the simplest possible statement of his views, as Dr. Thomson and Sir William Hamilton have interpreted them.

Knowledge is either obscure or clear; either confused or distinct; either adequate or inadequate; and lastly, either symbolical or intuitive. Perfect knowledge must be clear, distinct, adequate and intuitive. If it falls short in any one of these respects, it is more or less imperfect. A notion, which simply means our knowledge of a thing, is obscure when it does not let us recognize the thing again and tell it apart from everything else. We have a clear notion of a rose, and of most common flowers, because we can recognize them with certainty and don't mix them up with each other. We also have a clear notion of our close friends, and of people we meet regularly, because we recognize them whenever we see them, with complete certainty and without hesitating. It is said that a shepherd, with practice, gets a clear notion of each sheep in his flock, so that he can pick out any one of them on its own. In the same way, a keeper of hounds learns the name and character of each hound, while other people have only an obscure idea of the hounds in general and could not tell one from another. But the geologist cannot give a clear idea of what sandstone, or conglomerate, or schist, or slate, or trap rock (a dark volcanic rock) consists of. Rocks vary endlessly in degree and character, and it is often barely possible to say whether a rock is sandstone or conglomerate, schist or slate, and so on. Among the lowest forms of life, the naturalist hardly has a clear notion of animal life as distinct from plant life: it is often hard to decide whether a protophyte (a simple one-celled organism) should be classed with the animals or with the plants.

Clear knowledge, in turn, is confused when we cannot pick out the parts and qualities of the thing we know, and can only recognize it as a whole. Anyone knows a friend instantly and could tell him apart from everyone else, yet would usually find it impossible to say how he knows him, or by what marks. He could not describe his friend's figure or features except in the very roughest way. Someone with no practice in drawing who tries to sketch even a familiar animal like a horse or a cow soon finds that he has only a confused notion of its shape, while an artist has a distinct idea of the shape of every limb. A chemist has a distinct as well as a clear notion of gold and silver. He can not only tell with certainty whether a piece of metal really is gold or silver, but can also name and describe exactly the qualities by which he knows it, and could, if necessary, list a great many other qualities as well. We have a very distinct notion of a chessboard, because we know it consists of 64 squares. All our ideas of geometrical figures, such as triangles, circles, parallelograms, squares, pentagons, hexagons and so on, are, or ought to be, perfectly distinct. But when we talk about a constitutional government, or a civilized nation, we have only the vaguest idea of what we mean. We cannot say exactly what it takes to make a government constitutional without also taking in governments we don't mean to include; and the same goes for civilized nations. These terms have meanings that are neither distinct nor clear.

Notice that no simple idea, such as the idea of the color red, can be distinct in the sense meant here, because nobody can analyze the color red or describe to someone else what it is. A person who has been blind from birth cannot be brought to imagine it, and the only way we can define what it is like is to put an actual red object in front of someone's eyes. The same is generally true of all simple sensations, whether tastes, smells, colors or sounds. So these may be known clearly, but not distinctly, in the sense Leibniz gives that word.

It is not easy to explain the difference Leibniz meant by the names adequate and inadequate. He says, "When everything which enters into a distinct notion is distinctly known, or when the last analysis is reached, the knowledge is adequate, of which I scarcely know whether a perfect example can be offered —the knowledge of numbers, however, approaches near to it."

So to have adequate knowledge of things, we must be able to pick out not only the parts that make up our notion of a thing, but also the parts that make up those parts. For instance, we might be said to have an adequate notion of a chessboard. We know it is made up of 64 squares, and we know each of those squares distinctly, because each one is made up of 4 equal straight lines joined at right angles. Even so, we cannot be said to have a distinct notion of a straight line, because we cannot really define it, or break it down into anything simpler. To be completely adequate, our knowledge would have to allow analysis after analysis, forever, and that would make adequate knowledge impossible. But, as Dr. Thomson remarks, we can count any knowledge as adequate if it carries the analysis far enough for the purpose we have in mind. An engineer, for instance, has adequate knowledge of a machine if he knows not only its various wheels and parts, but also what those parts are for, what they are made of, what shapes they have and how they act, provided that he also knows all the mechanical properties of the materials and all the geometrical properties of the shapes that could affect how the machine works. But nobody expects him to go further still and explain why iron or wood of a particular quality is strong or brittle, why oil works as a lubricant, or what axioms the principles of mechanical forces rest on.

Last of all, we must notice the very important distinction between symbolical and intuitive knowledge. Going by the original meaning of the word, intuitive would mean what we gain by seeing (from the Latin intueor, "to look at"). Any knowledge we have directly through the senses, or that comes straight into the mind, is called intuitive. So we may learn intuitively what a square or a hexagon is, but hardly what a chiliagon, a figure with 1000 sides, is.

We could not tell the difference by sight between a figure with 1000 sides and a figure with 1001 sides. Nor can we picture any such figure completely in the mind. We know it only by name, or symbolically. All large numbers, like the ones that give the speed of light (186,000 miles per second) or the distance of the sun (91,000,000 miles), are known to us only by symbols, and they are beyond the power of our imagination.

We know infinity in a similar way. So we can become acquainted, with the intellect, with something our senses could never tell us about. We also speak of nothing, of zero, of what contradicts itself, of the non-existent, and even of the unthinkable or inconceivable. These words stand for things that can never be pictured in the mind, much less perceived intuitively through the senses; they can only be handled in a purely symbolical way.

In arithmetic and algebra we deal mostly with symbolical knowledge alone. When we work through a long arithmetic question or an algebra problem, we don't need to stop at each step and picture to ourselves what the numbers and symbols mean. Algebra teaches us that if we multiply the sum of two quantities by their difference, we get the difference of their squares; in symbols, \((a + b)(a - b) = a^2 - b^2\). It is easy to see that this is true, like this:

\[\begin{array}{r} a + b \\ a - b \\ \hline a^2 + ab \\ -\,ab - b^2 \\ \hline a^2 + 0 - b^2. \end{array}\]

Here we are working in the dark, symbolically. We use the letters a and b according to certain fixed rules, without knowing or caring what they mean. And whatever meaning we later give to a and b, we can be sure the process still holds and the conclusion is true, without going over the steps again.

In geometry, though, we argue by seeing intuitively that each step is true. We actually hold a picture of the figures in our mind, and satisfy ourselves that the figures really do have the properties required. So the algebraic truth shown above in symbols can easily be proved true of lines, and of the rectangles formed by those lines, as a corollary of the 5th Proposition of Euclid's Second Book.

There is a lot to be said about the advantages of the intuitive and the symbolical methods compared with each other. The symbolical method is usually far less laborious, and gives the most widely applicable answers. But it seldom or never gives the same command and understanding of a subject as the intuitive method does. That is why the study of geometry is always indispensable in education, even though the same truths can often be proved more easily by algebra. It is Newton's special glory that he managed to explain the motions of the heavenly bodies by the geometrical, or intuitive, method, while the greatest of his successors, such as Lagrange and Laplace, have dealt with these motions with the help of symbols.

What is true of mathematics applies to every kind of reasoning. Words are symbols just as much as A, B, C or x, y, z are, and it is possible to argue with words without being aware of what they mean at all. Suppose I say that "selenium is a dyad element, and a dyad element is one capable of replacing two equivalents of hydrogen." Nobody who knows no chemistry will be able to attach any meaning to these terms, and yet anybody will be able to conclude that "selenium is capable of replacing two equivalents of hydrogen." Such a person argues in a purely symbolical way. In the same way, whenever we use words in everyday life without having their full and exact meaning in mind at that moment, we have symbolical knowledge only.

There is no worse habit for a student or a reader to fall into than accepting words in place of a knowledge of things. Reading a book of natural history about Infusoria, Foraminifera, Rotifera and the like is perhaps worse than useless if those names do not bring clear images to mind. And a student who has not watched experiments, and examined the substances with his own eyes, will not get much out of books on chemistry and physics (natural philosophy). He will meet hundreds of new terms in them, and to him they will be nothing but empty and confusing signs. For this reason we should never miss a chance to get to know, through our own senses, the forms, properties and changes of things. That way, the language we use can be used intuitively as far as possible, and we can be saved from the absurdities and fallacies we might otherwise fall into. In short, we should follow Bacon's advice, ipsis consuescere rebus: to accustom ourselves to things themselves.

Questions and Exercises

1. What are the characters of perfect knowledge?

2. Describe the kind of knowledge we have of each of the following notions or objects: A syllogism. Electricity. Motion. A triangle. Eternity. The weight of the earth (5852 trillions of tons, counting a trillion, as the British did then, as a million million million). The color of the sky.

3. Explain exactly what you mean by intuitive knowledge.
