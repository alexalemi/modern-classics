Lesson 14: The Laws of Thought

The next lessons deal with the most common form of reasoning, known as the syllogism. Before the reader goes on to them, it is worth paying careful attention to the very simple laws of thought on which all reasoning must ultimately rest. These laws describe the very simplest truths: truths on which everyone must agree, and which at the same time apply to every notion we can conceive. We cannot think correctly, or avoid plain self-contradiction, unless we obey what are called the Three Primary Laws of Thought. They can be stated like this:

1. The Law of Identity. Whatever is, is.

2. The Law of Contradiction. Nothing can both be and not be.

3. The Law of Excluded Middle. Everything must either be or not be.

Stated this way, these laws may seem absurdly obvious, and Locke and others made fun of them for exactly that reason. But I have found that students are seldom able to see their full meaning and importance at first. In Lesson 23 we will see that until recent years logicians overlooked a very simple way of explaining all arguments, once these self-evident laws are granted. It is not too much to say that the whole of logic will be plain to anyone who constantly uses these laws as the key.

The first law may be taken as the best definition we can give of identity, or sameness. If anyone did not know what the word Identity meant, it would be enough to tell him that everything is Identical with itself.

The second law needs more thought. It means that nothing can have contradictory and inconsistent qualities at the same time and in the same place. A piece of paper may be blackened in one part while it is white in other parts. Or it may be white at one time and later become black. But we cannot conceive of it being both white and black in the same place at the same time. A door that has been open may be shut, but it cannot be shut and open at once. Water may feel warm to one hand and cold to the other, but it cannot be both warm and cold to the same hand. No quality can be both present and absent at the same time, and this seems to be the simplest and most general truth we can assert about all things. It is the very nature of existence that a thing cannot be other than it is. We can safely say that all fallacy and error come from reasoning, without realizing it, in a way that is inconsistent with this law. Any statement or inference that implies a combination of contradictory qualities must be taken as impossible and false, and breaking this law is the mark of its falseness. It is easy to show that if Iron is a metal, and every metal is an element, then Iron must be an element, or else it can be nothing at all, since it would combine qualities that are inconsistent (see Lesson 23).

The Law of Excluded Middle is much less self-evident than the other two, and the reader may not see at first that it is just as important and necessary as they are. The best way to explain it is this: it is impossible to name any thing, and any quality or circumstance, without granting that the quality or circumstance either belongs to the thing or does not belong to it. The name of the law expresses the fact that there is no third, or middle, course; the answer must be Yes or No. Let the thing be rock and the quality hard; then rock must be either hard or not-hard. Gold must be either white or not white; a line must be either straight or not straight; an action must be either virtuous or not virtuous. In fact, even when we know nothing about the terms we are using, we can still make assertions about them in agreement with this law. The reader may not know, and in fact chemists may not really know for certain, whether vanadium is a metal or not a metal. But anyone knows that it must be one or the other. Some readers may not know what a cycloid is, or what an isochronous curve is, but they must know that a cycloid either is an isochronous curve or is not an isochronous curve.

Still, the law of excluded middle is not so evident that no plausible objections can be raised against it. Someone might argue that rock is not always either hard or soft, because it may be halfway between, a little hard and a little soft at the same time. This objection points to a distinction of great logical importance, and neglecting it often leads to fallacy. The law of excluded middle said nothing about hard and soft; it spoke only of hard and not-hard. If the reader chooses to put soft in place of not-hard, he falls into a serious confusion between opposite terms and contradictory terms. A thing may quite possibly be neither hard nor soft, being halfway between. But in that case it cannot fairly be called hard, so the law still holds. In the same way, water must be either warm or not-warm, but it does not follow that it must be warm or cold. The alternative not-warm plainly includes all the cases where it is cold, and also the cases where it is at a middling temperature, so that we would call it neither warm nor cold. So we must carefully separate questions of degree, or quantity, from questions of simple logical fact. Where a thing or a quality can exist to a greater or lesser extent, there are many alternatives. Warm water, for instance, may have any temperature from perhaps 70° up to 120° (Fahrenheit). Exactly the same question comes up in geometrical reasoning. Euclid, in his Elements, often argues from the self-evident truth that any line must be either greater than, equal to, or less than any other line. Another objection to the third law of thought is even more plausible. Take virtue as the thing and triangular as the quality. The Law of Excluded Middle lets us assert at once that virtue is either triangular or not-triangular. At first sight it might seem false and absurd to say that an immaterial notion like virtue is either triangular or not. Virtue has nothing in common with the material substances that occupy space, which are the only things the notion of shape belongs to. But the absurdity does not come from any falseness in the law. It comes from misreading the expression not-triangular. If, when we say that a thing is "not triangular," we are taken to imply that it has some shape, only not a triangular one, then of course the expression cannot be applied to virtue or to anything immaterial. In strict logic, however, no such implied meaning is allowed. Not-triangular includes both the things that have some shape other than triangular and the things that have no properties of shape at all; and it is in this second sense that it applies to an immaterial thing.

So these three laws are universally and necessarily true, whatever things they are applied to, and they become the foundation of reasoning. Every act of reasoning starts from certain judgments. An act of judgment consists in comparing two things or ideas and finding out whether they agree or differ, that is, whether they are identical in any of their qualities. The laws of thought tell us the very nature of this identity, which is what all thought is concerned with. But in the work of discourse, or reasoning, we need some additional laws, or axioms, or self-evident truths. They can be stated like this:

1. Two terms agreeing with one and the same third term agree with each other.

2. Two terms of which one agrees and the other does not agree with one and the same third term, do not agree with each other.

These self-evident truths are usually called the Canons, or Fundamental Principles, of Syllogism, and they are true whatever kind of agreement we are dealing with. The example we used in Lesson 1, where the terms "most useful metal" and "cheapest metal" agreed with the common third term "iron," was simply an instance of the first Canon, and there the agreement was complete identity. In the case of "the earth," "planets," and "bodies revolving in elliptical orbits," the agreement was less complete. The earth is only one of many planets, and the planets are only a small part of all the heavenly bodies that revolve in such orbits, which also include satellites, comets, meteors and double stars.

The second Canon applies to cases where there is disagreement, or difference, as in this example:

	Venus is a planet.
	Planets are not self-luminous.
	Therefore Venus is not self-luminous.

The first of these propositions states that a certain agreement exists between Venus and planet, just as in the earlier case of the earth. But the second proposition states a disagreement between planet and self-luminous bodies. From these we infer a disagreement between Venus and self-luminous body. But the reader should notice carefully that from two disagreements we can never infer anything. If the following were put forward as an argument, it would be plainly absurd:

	Sirius is not a planet.
	Planets are not self-luminous.
	Therefore Sirius is not self-luminous.

Both of the premises, or propositions given, are true, and yet the conclusion is false: all the fixed stars, Sirius among them, are self-luminous, which means they shine by their own light. In fact we may state a third Canon:

3. Two terms both disagreeing with one and the same third term may or may not agree with each other.

Self-evident rules of exactly the same kind as these three Canons are the basis of all mathematical reasoning, where they are usually called axioms. Euclid's first axiom is that "Things which are equal to the same thing are equal to one another;" and it holds true whether we apply it to the lengths of lines, the sizes of angles, areas, solids, numbers, degrees, or anything else that can be equal or unequal. So if the lines A and B are each equal to C, it is evident that each is equal to the other.

[Figure lines]

Euclid gives no axioms matching the second and third Canons, but they are really used in geometry. If A is equal to B, but D is not equal to B, it follows that A is not equal to D. In other words, things of which one is equal and the other unequal to the same third thing are unequal to each other. Lastly, A and E are two lines that are both unequal to D and unequal to each other, while A and B are two lines that are both unequal to D but equal to each other. So we can plainly see that "two things both unequal to the same thing may or may not be equal to each other."

From all this it will be clear that every piece of reasoning needs at least one agreement. If there are two agreements, we can reason to a third agreement. If there is one agreement and one difference, we can reason to a second difference. But if there are only two differences, we cannot reason to any conclusion at all. In the next lesson these self-evident principles will help explain some of the rules of the syllogism.

Logicians, however, have not kept to these Canons alone. They have often put the same truth into a different form, in an axiom called the Dictum de omni et nullo of Aristotle. This famous Latin phrase means "the statement about all and none," and the axiom, or rather pair of axioms, is usually given in these words:

Whatever is predicated of a term distributed whether affirmatively or negatively, may be predicated in like manner of everything contained under it.

Or more briefly:

What pertains to the higher class pertains also to the lower.

In plain, untechnical language this just means two things. First, whatever can be said of all the things of some sort or kind can be said of any one of them, or any part of them. Second, whatever can be denied of all the things in a class can be denied of any one of them, or any part of them. Whatever can be said of "all planets" can be said of Venus, the earth, Jupiter or any other planet. All the planets can be said to revolve in elliptical orbits, so this can be asserted of Venus, the earth, Jupiter or any other planet. In the same way, by the negative half of the Dictum, we can deny that the planets are self-luminous; and knowing that Jupiter is a planet, we can deny that Jupiter is self-luminous. A little thought will show that the affirmative Dictum is really the first Canon in a less complete and less general form, and that the negative Dictum is, in the same way, the second Canon. In fact these Dicta apply only to those cases of agreement between terms where one term is the name of a smaller class and the other is the name of a larger class that contains it. Logicians have mostly, and strangely, overlooked the important cases where one term agrees with another so far as to be identical with it. But this is not the place to discuss that subject at any length. It is treated in my little book called The Substitution of Similars.

Some logicians have held that besides the three laws called the Primary Laws of Thought, there is a fourth, called "the Principle, or Law, of Sufficient Reason." Leibniz stated it in these words:

Nothing happens without a reason why it should be so rather than otherwise. For example, suppose there is a pair of scales, exactly alike in every respect on each side, with exactly equal weights in each pan. It must stay motionless and in balance, because there is no reason why one side should go down rather than the other. It is certainly a basic assumption of the science of mechanics that if a body is pushed by two perfectly equal forces in different directions, it will move evenly between them, because there is no reason why it should move more to one side than to the other. Mr. Mansel, Sir William Hamilton and others, however, think that this law has no place in logic, even if it can be held to be self-evident at all. The question seems open to doubt, and we need not discuss it here.

I have used the word axiom so freely in this lesson that I should make its meaning as clear as I can. Philosophers do not entirely agree about where it comes from or exactly what it means, but it certainly comes from the Greek verb ἀξιόω (axioō), which is translated "to think worthy." It generally means a self-evident truth so simple that it must be assumed to be true. Since it cannot be proved from any simpler proposition, it must itself be taken as the basis of reasoning. In mathematics it is clearly used in this sense.

Questions and Exercises

1. State the three Fundamental Laws of Thought, and apply them to the following notions:

(1) Matter, organic, inorganic.

(2) Undulations, polarized, non-polarized.

(3) Figure, rectilinear, curvilinear.

2. Is it wrong to assert that an animal cannot be both vertebrate and invertebrate, given that some animals are vertebrate and some are not?

3. From the following terms, pick out those that are negatives of the others, and those that are opposites: light, plenum, gain, heat, decrease, loss, darkness, cold, increase, vacuum.

4. How does Aristotle's dictum apply to the following arguments?

(1) Silver is a good conductor of electricity; for such are all the metals.

(2) Comets cannot be without weight; for they are composed of matter, which is not without weight.
