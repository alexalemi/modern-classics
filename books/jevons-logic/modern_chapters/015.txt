Lesson 15: The Rules of the Syllogism

Syllogism is the common name for Mediate Inference: inference through a medium, or middle term. It is to be distinguished from Immediate Inference, which is inference carried out without any third, or middle, term.

We use a middle term, or medium, whenever we can't compare two things with each other directly but can compare each of them with some third thing. We can't compare the sizes of two halls by putting one inside the other. But we can measure each of them with a foot rule or some other suitable measure. That measure is common to both, and it lets us find out their relative size as accurately as we need. Or suppose we have two lots of cotton cloth and want to compare them. We don't have to carry the whole of one lot over to the other. We cut off a sample that represents exactly the quality of the first lot, and the two lots agree or differ according to whether the sample agrees with the second lot or not.

A middle term in a syllogism works very much like the medium in these cases, though not exactly the same way. Suppose, for example, that we want to find out whether or not "whales are viviparous" (that is, give birth to live young), and we have no chance to observe the fact directly. We could still show that it is so if we knew that "whales are mammalian animals" and that "all mammalian animals are viviparous." It would follow that "whales are viviparous." And as far as the inference is concerned, it makes no difference what meaning we give to the words viviparous and mammalian. In this case "mammalian animal" is the middle term.

The name Syllogism means the joining together of two propositions in thought. It comes from the Greek words σύν (syn), together, and λόγος (logos), thought. So it is exactly equivalent to the word Computation, which means thinking together (Latin con, together, and puto, to think), or reckoning. In a syllogism we unite in thought two premises, or propositions put forward, in such a way that we can draw from them, or infer, a third proposition, called the conclusion, by means of the middle term they contain. So we may define Syllogism as the act of thought by which we go from two given propositions to a third proposition, whose truth necessarily follows from the truth of the two given ones. When the argument is fully expressed in words, it is usual to call it, concretely, a syllogism.

The special rules of the syllogism are founded on the Laws of Thought and the Canons we looked at in the last lesson. They tell us exactly when one proposition can be inferred from two others. There are eight of them, as follows:

1. Every syllogism has three and only three terms.

These terms are called the major term, the minor term, and the middle term.

2. Every syllogism contains three, and only three, propositions.

These propositions are called the major premise, the minor premise, and the conclusion.

3. The middle term must be distributed at least once, and must not be ambiguous.

4. No term may be distributed in the conclusion that was not distributed in one of the premises.

5. From negative premises nothing can be inferred.

6. If one premise is negative, the conclusion must be negative; and the other way around, to prove a negative conclusion one of the premises must be negative.

From these rules we can deduce two subordinate rules, which it will be convenient to state right away all the same.

7. From two particular premises no conclusion can be drawn.

8. If one premise is particular, the conclusion must be particular.

All these rules are so extremely important that the student should not only understand perfectly what they mean and why they are true, but also learn them by heart. In the rest of this lesson we will look at what they mean and what force they have.

Since the syllogism consists in comparing two terms by means of a middle term, there obviously can't be fewer than three terms. Nor can there be more. Suppose there were four terms, say A, B, C, D, and we compared A with B and C with D. Then either we would have no common medium at all between A and D, or we would need a second syllogism: first comparing A and C with B, and then A and D with C.

You can always recognize the middle term by the fact that it does not appear in the conclusion. The major term is always the predicate of the conclusion, and the minor term is always the subject. They get these names because in the universal affirmative proposition (A) the predicate is necessarily a wider, or greater, or major term than the subject. In "all humans are mortals," for example, the predicate takes in all the other animals as well as humans, so it is obviously a major, or wider, term than humans.

Next, the syllogism necessarily consists of three parts. There is the major premise, in which the major and middle terms are compared. There is the minor premise, which in the same way compares the minor and middle terms. And there is the conclusion, which contains only the major and minor terms. In a strictly correct syllogism the major premise always comes before the minor premise, but in ordinary writing and speech this rule is seldom kept. Whichever premise contains the major term is still the major premise, wherever it stands.

The third rule is a very important one, because many fallacies come from ignoring it. When we say the middle term must be distributed at least once, we mean (see Lesson 9) that the whole of it must be referred to universally in one premise, if not in both. Take the two propositions

	All Frenchmen are Europeans,
	All Russians are Europeans.

They do not distribute the middle term at all, because both are affirmative propositions, and affirmative propositions have undistributed predicates (see Lesson 9). Plainly, Frenchmen are one part of the Europeans and Russians are another part, as Euler's method shows in Fig. 6, so there is no real middle term. These propositions would allow Russians to be Frenchmen just as well as not to be. Whether the two inner circles overlap or not, both are still inside the larger circle of Europeans. Or take the two propositions

[Figure 6]

	All Frenchmen are Europeans,
	All Parisians are Europeans.

They do not let us infer that all Parisians are Frenchmen. Of course we know that all Parisians are included among Frenchmen, but the premises would let them be placed anywhere inside the circle of Europeans. This example shows that the premises and the conclusion of an apparent argument may all be true, and the argument may still be fallacious.

The part of the third rule about an ambiguous middle term hardly needs explaining. We said in Lesson 4 that an ambiguous term is one with two different meanings, implying different connotations. It is really the same as two different terms that happen to be spelled the same way, so that they are easily mistaken for each other. Suppose we argued that "all metals are elements, and brass is metal, therefore it is an element." We would be committing a fallacy, because we would be using the middle term metal in two different senses. In one sense it means the pure, simple substances that chemists call metals. In the other it means a mixture of metals that is commonly called metal in the trades, but that chemists call an alloy. In many of the examples found in logic books, the ambiguity of the middle term is extremely obvious. But the reader should always be ready to meet cases where the ambiguity is extremely subtle and hard to see. For instance, someone might argue that "what is right should be enforced by law, and charity is right, and should therefore be enforced by the law." Here it is clear that right is used in one place for what the conscience approves, and in the other for what public opinion holds to be necessary for the good of society.

The fourth rule forbids us to distribute a term in the conclusion unless it was distributed in the premises. The only purpose of the syllogism is to prove the conclusion from the premises. So obviously we must not make a statement about anything unless that thing was mentioned in the premises in a way that justifies the statement. Suppose we argued that "because many nations are capable of self-government, and nations capable of self-government should not receive laws from a despotic government, therefore no nation should receive laws from a despotic government." We would plainly be going beyond what our premises contain. The minor term, many nations, was particular in the minor premise, and must not be made universal in the conclusion. The premises justify a statement only about the many nations that are capable of self-government. So this argument would be fallacious, and its technical name would be an illicit process of the minor term, meaning that we have treated the minor term improperly.

A breach of the fourth rule like this one is extremely easy to detect, and so it is very seldom committed.

But an illicit process, or improper treatment, of the major term is more common, because it is not so obviously false. Suppose we argued that "because all Anglo-Saxons love liberty, and Frenchmen are not Anglo-Saxons, therefore they do not love liberty." The fallacy would be fairly plain, but without knowing some logic it would not be easy to explain clearly what is wrong. The major term, loving liberty, is undistributed in the major premise, so we must take the Anglo-Saxons to be only a part of those who love liberty. So shutting Frenchmen out of the class of Anglo-Saxons does not necessarily shut them out of the class of those who love liberty (see Fig. 8). The conclusion of the false argument is negative, so it distributes its predicate, the major term. Since that term is undistributed in the major premise, we have an Illicit Major, as we may call this fallacy for short. Here is a less obvious example of the same fallacy: "Few students are capable of excelling in many branches of knowledge, and those who can excel in this way are deserving of high praise"; hence "few students are deserving of high praise." The little word "few" here has the double meaning explained earlier (see Lesson 8): it means "a few are, and so on, and the rest are not." So the conclusion is really a negative proposition, and it distributes the major term "deserving of high praise." But this major term is clearly undistributed in the major premise. That premise only says that those who can excel in many branches of knowledge are deserving of praise. It says nothing, and implies nothing, about the other students.

The fifth rule is clearly founded on the principle we noticed in the last lesson: inference can only go forward where there is agreement, and two differences, or disagreements, allow no reasoning at all. As the third Canon states, two terms may both differ from a common term and still may or may not differ from each other. Suppose we argued that Americans are not Europeans and Virginians are not Europeans. Both terms disagree with the middle term Europeans, and yet they agree with each other. In other cases the two negative premises may be plainly true while it stays quite uncertain whether the major and minor terms agree or not. It is true, for instance, that "colonists are not Europeans, and Americans are not Europeans." But this gives us no right to infer either that colonists are Americans or that they are not. Fig. 9 represents the two negative premises by keeping the circles of Colonists and Americans outside the circle of Europeans. But they can be kept outside it whether Colonists and Americans coincide partly, or wholly, or not at all. A breach of this rule of the syllogism may conveniently be called the fallacy of negative premises. We must not suppose, though, that just having a negative word (not or no) in a proposition makes it negative in the sense this rule means. Take the argument

[Figure 9]

	"What is not compound is an element.
	Gold is not compound;
	Therefore Gold is an element."

It has negatives in both premises, but it is valid all the same, because in both cases the negative belongs to the middle term, which is really the negative term not-compound.

The truth of the sixth rule depends on the truth of the axiom that if two terms agree with a common third term, they agree with each other. Remember that a negative proposition asserts disagreement. Then it is clear that a negative conclusion could not be drawn from premises that are really affirmative. The matching negative axiom prevents us from drawing an affirmative conclusion if either premise is really negative. Only practice, however, will let the student apply this rule and the ones before it with confidence, since a fallacy can be hidden and disguised by all sorts of ways of putting things. The questions at the end of Lessons 18 and 21 give many examples on which the student can become skilled at analyzing arguments.

The remaining rules of the syllogism, the 7th and the 8th, are by no means self-evident. They are in fact corollaries of the first six rules, that is, consequences that follow from them. So we will have to show in Lesson 17 that they really do follow. We may call a breach of the 7th rule a fallacy of particular premises, and a breach of the 8th rule the fallacy of a universal conclusion from a particular premise. But these fallacies can really be reduced to those of Illicit Process or Undistributed Middle.

For many details about the Aristotelian and Scholastic views of the Syllogism, and of Formal Logic in general, see the full critical notes in Mansel's edition of Aldrich's Artis Logicæ Rudimenta. 2nd Ed. Oxford. 1852.

Questions and Exercises

1. Distinguish mediate and immediate inference.

2. Define syllogism, and say what word it is synonymous with.

3. What are the six principal and two subordinate rules of the syllogism?

4. In the following syllogisms, point out in turn the conclusion, the middle term, the major term, the minor term, the major premise and the minor premise, keeping to exactly this order.

(1) All human beings are fallible; All kings are human beings; Therefore all kings are fallible.

(2) Platinum is a metal; All metals combine with oxygen; Therefore Platinum combines with oxygen.

(3) The Khoikhoi are capable of education; for the Khoikhoi are human beings, and all human beings are capable of education.

5. Explain carefully what is meant by non-distribution of the middle term.
