Lesson 33: Requisites of a Philosophical Language

Among the supporting processes that inductive reasoning needs if it is to succeed, we must count the building of a suitable language. In fact, it is impossible to overstate how much any science depends on a language that is both accurate and rich. Studying things would be almost useless if we had no names to mark those things and to record what we have observed about them.

It is easy to see that language serves three distinct and almost independent purposes:

1. As a means of communication.

2. As a mechanical aid to thought.

3. As an instrument of record and reference.

When language first arose, it was used mainly, if not only, for the first purpose. In the 1870s, many of the world's peoples were thought to build up no store of knowledge at all. We may even say that the lower animals often have some way of communicating by sounds or natural signs, and this amounts to language in the first sense, even though they cannot reason with general notions.

Some philosophers have held that reasoning is impossible without language. The thoroughgoing nominalist went so far as to say that there are no such things as general notions at all, so that general names are the only thing that is general in science and reasoning. That is no doubt false (see Lesson 2). Still, we have to admit that if our general ideas were not fixed and represented by words, we could never manage the kind of sustained thought we now enjoy. For practical purposes, the second use of language is certainly indispensable, and reasoning can almost be thought of as the same thing as the correct use of words. When language is used only to help us reason, there is no need for each word to have a fixed meaning. We could use names the way algebra uses the letters x, y, z, a, b, r and so on, to stand for whatever quantity happens to turn up in a problem. All we need is never to confuse the meaning we give a word in one argument with a different meaning we give it in another. Algebra, in fact, can be described as a very perfect kind of language built for the second purpose alone, one that can lead a person to the solution of a problem in a symbolic, mechanical way.

Language as it comes to us ready-made, grown by habit over the centuries, can serve all three purposes, though by no means perfectly. Because words have a more or less fixed customary meaning, we can not only reason with them but also communicate our thoughts or record them. It is this last use, the recording, that we now have to deal with.

Building a science takes far more facts than anyone could hold in memory with enough accuracy. So one of the indispensable aids to induction is a means of describing and recording our observations. Only in this way can knowledge pile up, so that each observer starts with the advantage of knowing what has already been recorded and proved. We need, then, to look at the way language serves to register facts, and to work out what qualities a philosophical language must have to meet the needs of science.

As an instrument of record, language clearly needs two main things:

	1. Precision or definiteness of meaning.
	2. Completeness.

A name is worse than useless if, when it is used to record a fact, it does not let us find out what kind of fact was recorded. So accuracy and precision matter more in a language than abundance. Lacking the right word will seldom lead to actual error and fallacy. It will only force us to use a roundabout phrase, or else leave the fact unrecorded. But it is obviously convenient that whenever a thing, notion or quality has to be mentioned often, there should be a name set aside for it, and only one name. Let us look in turn at what a precise and complete language must be like.

The reader may not have noticed it before, but it is certainly true: we cannot describe anything without asserting that it resembles some other thing. We can only describe a thing by giving it a name. But how are we to learn what that name means? If we explain the name with other names, we only end up with more names whose meanings we need. In the end we must learn meanings, not from names, but from the things that carry those names. If someone did not know what blue means, there would be no way to tell them except by pointing to something that gave them the sensation of blueness. If they had been blind from birth, they could never get any notion of what blueness is. It is true that many words are so familiar to us from childhood that we cannot say when or how we learned what they mean, though we must have learned it by reference to things. But as soon as we start using names more precisely, we quickly have to refer to physical objects again. So we describe the various kinds of blue as sky-blue, azure-blue, indigo-blue and cobalt-blue, and we likewise tell apart sea-green, olive-green, emerald-green, grass-green and so on. In botany, the shapes of leaves are described with words like ovate, lanceolate, linear, pinnate and peltate, which call to mind, in order, an egg, a lance, a line, a feather and a shield. When we record sizes, we equally cannot avoid comparing them with the sizes of other things. A yard or a foot means nothing unless there is a definite standard yard or foot somewhere that fixes its meaning, and the reader probably knows that once the physical standard of a length is completely lost, it can never be recovered. The word is nothing unless somewhere we have the thing it stands for.

So the first requisite of a philosophical language is plainly that "every general name must have a certain and knowable meaning." It hardly needs saying that singular or proper names, the names of particular objects, must be known too. But such names are just marks stuck on the things they name, so they do not need the same attention. General names are harder, because, as we saw in Lesson 5, they have a double meaning: their denotation, or extension, and their connotation, or intension. Of these two, it is the connotation that must be fixed. The denotation, as a general rule, cannot be limited and pinned down. Suppose the name planet had been restricted to Jupiter, Saturn, Mars, Venus and Mercury, the planets known before the telescope was invented. Then we would have needed a new name for the ones discovered later, and even so we would be making the mistake of giving different names to things that are very much alike. But if by planet we mean any round body that revolves around the sun in a slightly elliptical orbit, the name will take in every such body that is discovered from time to time. In the 1870s more than 100 were already known, counting the small planets we now call asteroids. In the same way, locomotive engine is not just the name of a certain number of engines that exist right now. If it were, we would need a new name every week, as each new engine was built or an old one scrapped. What is fixed in a general name is its connotation, the qualities implied in the things that bear the name. So as far as possible we ought to define every general name we use, not by naming the objects it denotes, but by naming the qualities it connotes. We have already dealt with definition in earlier lessons (Lessons 12 and 13), so here we only need to ask how far it is better to use words already in everyday use rather than newly invented terms.

The advantage of an old term is that it carries meaning for everyone, and to that extent it saves people from having to learn a strange technical expression. Everyone knows what heat is, and the phrase "the science of heat" means something to every person, however unlearned. But old terms have one drawback we must note: they are almost always open to ambiguity. And in fact the scientist uses the word heat differently from everyone else. In science, all things are more or less hot, whereas in everyday life we could never say that ice was hot or contained heat. Ordinarily, heat means a temperature above the usual average, and the idea only makes sense in contrast with cold. We also stretch the word by analogy to sensations of taste, as when we say pepper is hot, and even to things purely in the mind, as in a hot dispute, a hot temper and so on. If we invent a new term, Caloric, to avoid these ambiguities, we can give it as precise a meaning as we like. But we put one more obstacle in the way of studying science, because there is one more technical term to learn.

This difficulty is especially great in the science of political economy (what we now call economics). There we deal with such familiar ideas as wealth, money, value, currency, capital, labor and exchange. It is the very familiarity of these ideas that causes the greatest trouble, because different people attach different meanings to the words, and the result is endless logomachy (from the Greek λόγος (logos), word, and μάχη (machē), battle), that is, disputes that arise over merely verbal questions. Even when a writer carefully defines the sense in which he uses these terms, he cannot make other people keep his definitions in mind. The other option, inventing entirely new terms, is out of the question, since it would certainly make a book unbearable for most readers. The only advice that can be given is this: bring in a new term where it is likely to be readily accepted and to push out an old, ambiguous one; otherwise, try to remove the ambiguity of the old term by always keeping a precise definition of the intended meaning in view.

A complete philosophical language will be made up of two distinct kinds of terms, which form, respectively, the descriptive terminology and the nomenclature of the science.

A descriptive terminology, as Dr. Whewell pointed out, must include all the terms needed to describe exactly what has been observed about any object or phenomenon, so that we have a permanent record of the observation. For every quality, shape, circumstance, degree or quantity there must be a suitable name or way of expressing it. So when we record the discovery of a new mineral, we ought to be able to pin down in words its exact crystal form, its color, its degree of hardness, its specific gravity, its smell and taste if it has any, and many other qualities that may turn out to matter. Modern botany grew out of the efforts of Linnaeus to create a system of terms with which every part and feature of a plant can be described accurately. The language of botany, improved since his time, is the most complete example of a scientific terminology. Geology, in my view, suffers badly from the difficulty of finding accurate terms. Names such as trap, basalt, gneiss, granite, tuff, greenstone, trachyte, porphyry, lava and so on are very vague, and there are no precise descriptive terms to define them and tell them apart. Where a quality does not come in degrees or quantities, it needs only a single name. Otherwise we must find some way of measuring it exactly and expressing the measurement. Inventing an instrument to measure a quality that was never measured before is always an important step in science, and the making of the thermometer by Fahrenheit and of the pendulum clock by Huygens were turning points in science.

On the other hand, each science needs a nomenclature, a collection of names for the distinct objects, or classes of objects, it deals with. In mineralogy the names of the separate minerals, such as haematite, topaz, amphibole, epidote, blende and polybasite, make up the nomenclature. In chemistry we have all the names of the elements, together with a vast apparatus of names for organic and other compounds, such as ethyl, acetyl, cyanogen, naphthalin, benzol and so on. In astronomy the names of the planets, satellites, nebulas, constellations and individual stars form a nomenclature, though by no means a perfect or convenient one. Geology likewise has a nomenclature that is necessarily incomplete, in the names of its successive formations: silurian, devonian, carboniferous, permian, triassic, eocene, miocene, pliocene, post-pliocene and so on.

Clearly a nomenclature must have names at various levels of generality. It must include individual objects, if they need to be recorded separately; the infimæ species, if there are any; and wider classes above them, all the way up to the summa genera, the widest notions the science covers. In astronomy we deal mostly with the names of individual objects, and so far there is little room for classification. In natural sciences such as botany or zoology there is seldom or never any need for names of individuals. That is because, in these sciences, a countless multitude of individuals generally resemble one another very closely in a great many properties, so that they make up what has been called a natural kind. Mr. J. S. Mill uses this term to mean "one of those classes which are distinguished from all others, not by one or a few definite properties, but by an unknown multitude of them; the combination of properties on which the class is grounded being a mere index to an indefinite number of other distinctive attributes."

Going by the way Mr. Mill puts it, he seems to include in a nomenclature only the names of supposed species. For he says: "A nomenclature may be defined, the collection of names of all kinds with which any branch of knowledge is conversant; or more properly, of all the lowest kinds, or infimæ species, those which may be subdivided indeed, but not into kinds, and which generally accord with what in natural history are termed simply species." But the fact is that naturalists have now given up the idea that a species is any fixed form. Many species are already divided into subspecies and varieties, or even varieties of varieties, and on the principles of Darwin's theory the dividing could go on without end. Surely the most reasonable view is that the natural kingdoms of plants and animals are arranged in an endless series of classes and subclasses, and that all the names attached to any of these classes belong to the nomenclature.

Again, Mill does not count as part of the nomenclature those general names that stand for ideas formed artificially in the course of induction and investigation. So, besides a terminology fit for describing the individual facts we observe with precision, there is, in his account, a branch of language containing "a name for every common property of any importance or interest, which we detect by comparing those facts: including (as the concretes corresponding to those abstract terms) names for the classes which we artificially construct in virtue of those properties, or as many of them, at least, as we have frequent occasion to predicate any thing of." As examples of this kind of name he gives Circle, Limit, Momentum, Civilization, Delegation and Representation. While the nomenclature contains the names of natural classes, this third branch of language would apparently contain the names of artificial ideas or classes.

But I find it very hard to give a clear account of Mill's views on this subject. And since my aim in these lessons does not allow me to discuss unsettled questions, I must end by referring any reader who wants to take the subject further to the 4th and 6th chapters of the 4th Book of Mr. Mill's System of Logic, which deal with the Requisites of a Philosophical Language.

See Dr. Whewell's "Aphorisms concerning the Language of Science," at the end of his Philosophy of the Inductive Sciences.

Thomson's Outline of the Laws of Thought contains very interesting remarks on the general nature and use of language, §§ 17–31.
