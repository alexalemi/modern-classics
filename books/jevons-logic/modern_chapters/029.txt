Lesson 29: Methods of Quantitative Induction

The methods of Induction described in the last Lesson dealt only with whether the event whose cause we were looking for happened or did not happen. That is how we learned that friction is one cause of heat. We saw that two solid bodies rubbed together, even two pieces of ice, produced heat, and that when they were not rubbed, no heat was produced. But this is a very elementary sort of experiment. As an investigation moves forward, we always need to measure the exact quantity of an effect, if it is the kind of thing that can be more or less, and to connect it with the quantity of the cause. In fact there is a natural course of progress that we follow in every inquiry of this kind, and it can be laid out as a series of questions:

1. Does the antecedent invariably produce an effect?

2. In what direction is that effect?

3. How much is that effect in proportion to the cause?

4. Is it uniformly in that proportion?

5. If not, according to what law does it vary?

Take, for instance, the way heat changes the size of bodies. The first question is whether heating a solid body, say a bar of iron, changes its length. The simple method of difference lets us answer that it does. The next inquiry shows that almost all substances get longer, or larger in size, when heated, but that a very few, such as India rubber, and water below 4.08° Centigrade (Celsius), get smaller. Next we work out the proportion of the change to each degree of temperature, which is called the coefficient of expansion. Iron, for example, expands by 0.0000122 of its own length for every 1° Centigrade between 0° and 100°.

Still more careful inquiry shows, however, that the expansion is not uniformly proportional to the temperature. Most metals expand faster and faster the hotter they get. But we don't need to go into the details of the subject here.

Or take the fixed stars. They have come up often in these Lessons, but the reader probably knows that they are not really fixed. For any particular star, the astronomer actually has to answer the five questions below.

Firstly. Does the star move?

2ndly. In what direction does it move?

3rdly. How much does it move in a year or a century?

4thly. Does it move uniformly?

5thly. If not, according to what law does the motion vary in direction and speed?

Every science, and every question in a science, starts out as a matter of fact only. Then it becomes a matter of quantity, and little by little it becomes more and more precisely quantitative. Thirty years ago most of the phenomena of electricity and electromagnetism were known only as facts. Now most of them can be exactly measured and calculated.

As soon as phenomena can be measured like this, we can use a further Method of Induction, and a very important one. It is really the Method of Difference again, but used under far more favorable conditions, where every degree and quantity of a phenomenon gives us a new experiment and a new proof of the connection between cause and effect. It may be called the Method of Concomitant Variations, and Mr. J. S. Mill states it like this, in what he calls the Fifth Canon of Induction:

"Whatever phenomenon varies in any manner whenever another phenomenon varies in some particular manner, is either a cause or an effect of that phenomenon, or is connected with it through some fact of causation."

Sir John Herschel states the same method as follows: "Increase or diminution of the effect, with the increased or diminished intensity of the cause, in cases which admit of increase and diminution," and he adds, "Reversal of the effect with that of the cause."

The examples of this method are countless. Mr. Joule, of Manchester, proved beyond doubt that friction is a cause of heat. He spent exact quantities of force (in today's terms, energy) rubbing one substance against another, and showed that the heat produced was greater or less in exact proportion as the force was greater or less. We can also apply the method to many cases that had earlier been handled by the simple method of difference. Instead of striking a bell in a complete vacuum, for instance, we can strike it with a very little air left in the receiver (the glass jar) of the air pump. We then hear a very faint sound, which grows louder or softer every time we increase or decrease the density of the air. This experiment fully satisfies anyone that air is the cause of the transmission of sound.

This method often lets us detect the material connection that exists between two bodies. For a long time it was doubtful whether the red flames seen during total eclipses of the sun belonged to the sun or to the moon. But during the last eclipse of the sun, it was noticed that the flames moved with the sun, and that the moon gradually covered and uncovered them at successive moments of the eclipse. From then on, no one could doubt that they belonged to the sun.

Again, whenever phenomena go through Periodic Changes, increasing and decreasing by turns, we should look for other phenomena that go through changes in exactly the same periods, and there will probably be a connection of cause and effect between them. This is how the tides are proved to be caused by the attraction of the moon and the sun. The periods of high and low tides, and of spring and neap tides, follow one another at intervals that match the apparent revolutions of those bodies around the earth. The moon turns on its own axis in exactly the same period in which it goes around the earth, so that for unknown ages the same side of the moon has always faced the earth. This is a perfect case of concomitant variations, and it proves beyond doubt that the earth's attraction governs the moon's turning on its own axis.

The most extraordinary case of variations, however, is the connection that has been shown in recent years between the Aurora Borealis, magnetic storms, and the spots on the sun. Only in the last 30 or 40 years has it become known that the magnetic compass needle is subject from time to time to very slight but curious movements. At the same moments there are usually natural currents of electricity running in telegraph wires, strong enough to interfere with sending messages. These disturbances are known as magnetic storms, and they are often observed when a fine display of the Northern or Southern Lights is taking place somewhere on the earth. Observations over many years have shown that these storms reach their worst at the end of every eleven years (the maximum falling around the present year, 1870), and then weaken until the next period of eleven years has passed. Close observation of the sun over 30 or 40 years has shown that the size and number of the dark spots, which are gigantic storms going on at the sun's surface, increase and decrease at exactly the same periods as the magnetic storms at the earth's surface. No one can doubt, then, that these strange phenomena are connected, although how they are connected is quite unknown. It is now believed that the planets Jupiter, Saturn, Venus and Mars are the real causes of the disturbances, because Balfour Stewart and Warren de la Rue have shown that the motions of these planets correspond exactly to the periods of the sunspots. This is a most remarkable and far-reaching case of concomitant variations.

Next we have to consider a method of Induction that must be used when several causes act at once and their effects all blend together, producing a joint effect of the same kind as the separate effects. Suppose that in one experiment friction, combustion, compression and electric action are all going on at the same time. Each of these causes will produce a quantity of heat, the quantities will add together, and it will be hard or impossible to say how much is due to each cause separately. We may call this a case of the homogeneous intermixture of effects. The name means that the joint effect is of the same kind as the separate effects. Mr. Mill distinguishes it from cases of the heterogeneous, or, as he calls it, the heteropathic intermixture of effects, where the joint effect is totally different in kind from the separate effects. If we bend a bow too far, for example, it breaks instead of bending further. If we warm ice, it soon stops rising in temperature and melts. If we warm water, it rises in temperature homogeneously for a while, but then it suddenly stops, and an effect of a totally different kind follows: the production of vapor, or possibly an explosion.

Now, when the joint effect is of a heterogeneous kind, the method of difference is enough to find the cause of its occurrence. Whether a bow or a spring will break under a given weight is easy to test, and whether water will boil at a given temperature, at any given reading of the barometer, is just as easy to find out. But in the homogeneous intermixture of effects our task is more complicated. There are several causes, each producing part of the effect, and we want to know how much is due to each one. In this case we must use a further Inductive Method, which Mr. Mill calls the Method of Residues and states in his Fourth Canon:

"Subduct from any phenomenon such part as is known by previous inductions to be the effect of certain antecedents, and the residue of the phenomenon is the effect of the remaining antecedents."

If we know that the joint effect a, b, c is due to the causes A, B, and C, and we can prove that a is due to A and b to B, it follows that c must be due to C. There can be no simpler case of this than finding the exact weight of a load of goods in a cart. We weigh the cart and load together, and then subtract the tare, the weight of the cart alone, which we found earlier. In the same way we can find how much of the spring tides is due to the attraction of the sun, provided we have already worked out the height of the tide due to the moon, which will be about the average height of the tides over the whole lunar month. Subtract the moon's tide, and what remains is the sun's tide.

Newton used this method in a beautiful experiment to measure the elasticity of different substances. He made balls of the substances, let them swing against each other, and then observed how far they bounced back compared with how far they had originally fallen. But the loss of motion is due partly to imperfect elasticity and partly to the resistance of the air. He measured the second effect in the simplest possible way: he let the balls swing without striking each other, and observed how much smaller each swing was than the one before. This made it easy for him to calculate the amount that had to be subtracted for the resistance of the air.

This is the method we use when we make allowance for errors, or apply the necessary corrections, in observations. Few thermometers are quite accurate. But if we put a thermometer into melting snow, which has a temperature of exactly 0° Centigrade, or 32° Fahrenheit, we can see exactly how far below or above the true point the mercury stands. That tells us how much we should add to or subtract from the thermometer's readings to make them correct. The height of the barometer is affected by several causes besides changes in the pressure of the air. It is lowered by the capillary repulsion between the glass tube and the mercury. It is raised by the expansion of the mercury with heat, if the temperature is above 32° Fahrenheit. And it may be raised or lowered by any error in the length of the scale used to measure the height. In an accurate observation, all these effects are calculated and allowed for in the final result.

In chemical analysis this method is used constantly to find the proportional weights of substances that combine with each other. The composition of water, for example, is found like this. We take a known weight of copper oxide, pass hydrogen over it in a heated tube, and condense the water produced in a tube containing sulfuric acid. Subtracting the original weight of the condensing tube from its final weight tells us how much water was produced. The quantity of oxygen in that water is found by subtracting the final weight of the copper oxide from its original weight. If we then subtract the weight of the oxygen from the weight of the water, we learn the weight of the hydrogen that we have combined with the oxygen. When the experiment is done very carefully, as described in Dr. Roscoe's Lessons in Elementary Chemistry (p. 38), we find that 88.89 parts by weight of oxygen unite with 11.11 parts of hydrogen to form 100 parts of water.

This method is used in every science that allows quantities to be measured, but above all in astronomy, the most exact of all the sciences. Almost all the causes and effects in astronomy have been discovered as residual phenomena. That is, astronomers calculate the effects of all the known attractions on a planet or a satellite, and then observe how far it is from the place those calculations predicted. When this was done very carefully for Uranus, the planet was still found to be sometimes ahead of and sometimes behind its true place. This residual effect pointed to some cause of attraction that was not yet known, and as a result that cause was soon discovered in the shape of the planet Neptune. The motions of several comets have been calculated in the same way, but it is observed that they fail to return at the expected time. There is a discrepancy that points to some obstructing power in the space they pass through, whose nature is not yet understood.

Questions on the Methods of Induction

1. Define exactly what is meant by a cause of an event, and distinguish between cause, occasion, and antecedent.

2. Point out all the causes involved in the following phenomena:

(1) The burning of a fire.

(2) The ordinary growth of plants.

(3) The cracking of a glass by hot water.

3. State and explain in your own words Mr. Mill's first three Canons of Inductive Method.

4. Point out exactly how the Joint Method differs from the simple Method of Difference.

5. Give some examples of simple experiments that completely fulfill the conditions of the Method of Difference.

(2) The sun is thought to move through space.

(3) A ray of light passing into or out of a denser medium is bent aside. Point out the successive questions that would have to be settled in investigating each of the phenomena above.

8. Find some simple examples of the homogeneous and the heterogeneous intermixture of effects, and of the methods of concomitant variations and of residues.

9. Since 1842 there has been a great reform of the British tariff, and a great increase in British trade. Does this coincidence prove that the first circumstance is the cause of the second?

10. Suppose we did not know the causes of the following phenomena. By what methods should we investigate each one?

(1) The connection between the barometer and the weather.

(2) A person poisoned at a meal.

(3) The connection between the hands of a clock.

(4) The effect of the Gulf Stream on the climate of Great Britain.
