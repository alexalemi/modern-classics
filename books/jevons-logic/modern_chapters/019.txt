Lesson 19: Of Conditional Arguments

You will remember that when we dealt with propositions we divided them into two distinct kinds: Categorical Propositions and Conditional Propositions. So far we have looked only at the first kind. Now we must go on to describe Conditional propositions, and the arguments that can be built out of them.

Logicians have usually described a Conditional proposition as two or more Categorical propositions joined by a conjunction. They can be joined in two ways, and this gives two very different species of conditional, which we will call Hypothetical Propositions and Disjunctive Propositions. We can also describe a conditional proposition as one that makes a statement under a certain condition, or qualification, which restricts how far the statement applies. In the hypothetical form the condition is brought in by the conjunction if, or by some other word that does the same job. So

	"If iron is impure, it is brittle"

is a hypothetical proposition made up of two distinct categorical propositions. The first, "Iron is impure," is called the Antecedent. The second, "It is brittle," is called the Consequent. Here "impurity" is the condition, or qualification, that limits the application of the predicate brittle to iron. In his celebrated book The Diversions of Purley, Horne Tooke claimed that all conjunctions are the remains, or worn-down forms, of verbs. This is certainly true of the hypothetical conjunction. In old English the word if is written gif or gyf, and it undoubtedly comes from the verb to give. Even today we can put in its place any verb of similar meaning, such as grant, allow, or suppose. So we can say:

	"Grant that iron is impure, and it is brittle."
	"Supposing that iron is impure, it is brittle."

The hypothetical proposition could be used in arguments of various forms, but only two of these are important enough to have been given special names. The hypothetical syllogism has two premises, called the major and the minor, just like the ordinary syllogism. The major premise is hypothetical in form. The minor premise is categorical, and depending on whether it is affirmative or negative, the argument is called a Constructive or a Destructive hypothetical syllogism. So the form

	If A is B, C is D;
	But A is B;
	Therefore C is D,

is a constructive hypothetical syllogism.

Notice carefully that the minor premise affirms the antecedent of the major premise. That is why the argument is said to be in the modus ponens, the mood that posits, or affirms. It is probably one of the most familiar and common kinds of argument there is. The form

	If A is B, C is D;
	But C is not D;
	Therefore A is not B,

is the matching Destructive hypothetical syllogism, also called the modus tollens, the mood that removes the consequent. Notice carefully, again, that it is the consequent, not the antecedent, that is denied.

Only one rule is needed for testing whether such syllogisms are valid, and it sums up what we have just seen: either the antecedent must be affirmed, or the consequent denied. Break this rule in either direction and you commit a serious fallacy. Take this apparent argument:

	If A is B, C is D;
	But C is D;
	Therefore A is B.

It is really a fallacy, which we can call the fallacy of affirming the consequent. It is easy to see why it fails. Nothing in the premise says that "A being B" is the only condition under which C is D. C might be D when E is F, or when G is H, or in a hundred other circumstances. So the mere fact that C is D is not enough to prove that A is B. For example, if a man has an avaricious, or greedy, character, he will refuse to give money for useful purposes. But it does not follow that everyone who refuses to give money for such purposes is avaricious. There may be many good reasons or motives for refusing. He may have no money; he may think the purpose is not a useful one; or he may have more useful purposes in mind.

A matching fallacy comes from denying the antecedent, as in this form:

	If A is B, C is D;
	But A is not B;
	Therefore C is not D.

The mistake can be explained the same way. Nothing says that "A being B" is the only condition of C being D. So we may deny that this one condition holds, and the consequent may still happen to be true for other reasons we know nothing about. If a man is not avaricious, then, we cannot conclude that he is sure to give money whenever he is asked. Or take this example:

"If the study of Logic furnished the mind with a multitude of useful facts, like the study of other sciences, it would deserve cultivation; but it does not furnish the mind with a multitude of useful facts; therefore it does not deserve cultivation."

This argument is plainly fallacious, because picking up a multitude of useful facts is not the only reason for recommending the study of a science. The reason Logic deserves to be cultivated is that it corrects and exercises our powers of judgment and reasoning. The argument above ignores that this other purpose exists, and it plainly commits the fallacy of denying the antecedent.

Books on logic usually describe the hypothetical proposition and syllogism as if they were different in nature from the categorical proposition and syllogism. But it has long been known that hypotheticals can be reduced to the categorical form and brought under the ordinary rules of the syllogism. As a general rule, a hypothetical proposition can easily be turned into a universal affirmative proposition (A) with exactly the same meaning. Our example, "If iron is impure, it is brittle," simply becomes "Impure iron is brittle." When we make this change in a hypothetical syllogism, we find we have to supply a new minor term. Take the case

	If iron is impure it is brittle;
	But it is impure;
	Therefore it is brittle.

Here we have to put "the iron in question" in place of the indefinite pronoun it, and we get a correct categorical syllogism in the mood Barbara:

	Impure iron is brittle;
	The iron in question is impure iron;
	Therefore the iron in question is brittle.

Sometimes the reduction needs a bigger change of wording. For instance,

	If the barometer is falling, bad weather is coming;
	But the barometer is falling;
	Therefore bad weather is coming,

can be put in this form:

	The circumstances of the barometer falling are the circumstances of bad weather coming;
	But these are the circumstances of the barometer falling;
	Therefore these are the circumstances of bad weather coming.

As an example of the Destructive Hypothetical syllogism we can take:

	If Aristotle is right, slavery is a proper form of society;
	But slavery is not a proper form of society;
	Therefore Aristotle is not right.

As a categorical, this becomes:

	The case of Aristotle being right is the case of slavery being a proper form of society;
	But this is not the case of slavery being a proper form of society;
	Therefore this is not the case of Aristotle being right.

When no other way of wording will reduce a hypothetical, the words "case of" always will.

Now we can easily show that the fallacy of affirming the consequent is really a breach of the third rule of the syllogism, and leads to an undistributed middle term. We can use the same example as before:

	If a man is avaricious he will refuse money;
	But he does refuse money;
	Therefore he is avaricious.

As a categorical syllogism this becomes:

	All avaricious men refuse money;
	But this man refuses money;
	Therefore this man is avaricious.

This is the mood AAA in the second figure. The middle term, refusing money, is undistributed in both premises, so the argument is entirely fallacious.

In the same way, the fallacy of denying the antecedent amounts to the illicit process of the major. Our earlier example (the one about the study of Logic, given earlier in this lesson) can be put like this:

"A science which furnishes the mind with a multitude of useful facts deserves cultivation; but Logic is not such a science; therefore Logic does not deserve cultivation."

This apparent syllogism is in the mood AEE in the first figure. It breaks the fourth rule of the syllogism, because the major term, deserving cultivation, is distributed in the negative conclusion but not in the affirmative major premise.

We now move on to the disjunctive proposition. Instead of a single predicate, it has several alternatives joined by the disjunctive conjunction or, and any one of them may be affirmed of the subject. "A member of the House of Commons is either a representative of a county, or of a borough, or of a University" is an example of such a proposition. It has three alternatives, but there can be any number, from two upward.

The disjunctive syllogism has a disjunctive major premise, with a categorical proposition, either affirmative or negative, as its minor premise. That gives two moods. The affirmative one is called by the Latin name modus ponendo tollens (the mood that denies by affirming), and it can be stated like this:

	A is either B or C,
	But A is B;
	Therefore A is not C.

This form of argument assumes that if one alternative of a disjunctive proposition is held to be true, the others cannot be true as well. Thus "the time of year must be either spring, summer, autumn or winter," and if it is spring, it cannot be summer, autumn or winter, and so on. But Whately, Mansel and Mill, along with many earlier logicians, have objected that this does not always hold. Suppose we say that "a good book is valued either for the usefulness of its contents or the excellence of its style." It certainly does not follow that because the contents of a book are useful, its style is not excellent. We generally choose alternatives that are inconsistent with each other, but logic does not require it.

The other form of disjunctive syllogism, called the modus tollendo ponens (the mood that affirms by denying), is always, necessarily, conclusive. It goes like this:

	A is either B or C,
	But A is not B;
	Therefore A is C.

So if we suppose that a book is valued only for the usefulness of its contents or for the excellence of its style, it follows that if a book is valued, but not for the first reason, it must be valued for the second; and the other way around. If the time of year is not spring, it must be summer, autumn or winter. If it is neither autumn nor winter, it must be either spring or summer, and so on. In short, if any of the alternatives are denied, the rest remain to be affirmed as before. Notice that the disjunctive syllogism is governed by completely different rules from the ordinary categorical syllogism. In the disjunctive syllogism a negative premise gives an affirmative conclusion; in the categorical syllogism it gives a negative one.

One more form of argument remains. It is called the Dilemma, because it starts by assuming two alternatives, usually called the horns of the dilemma, and still proves something whichever one holds (from the Greek δι- (di-), two, and λῆμμα (lēmma), assumption). Mr. Mansel defines this argument as "a syllogism, having a conditional major premise with more than one antecedent, and a disjunctive minor." It can be stated in at least three forms. The first is called the Simple Constructive Dilemma:

	If A is B, C is D; and if E is F, C is D;
	But either A is B, or E is F;
	Therefore C is D.

For example: "if a science furnishes useful facts, it is worth cultivating; and if studying it exercises the reasoning powers, it is worth cultivating; but either a science furnishes useful facts, or studying it exercises the reasoning powers; therefore it is worth cultivating."

The second form is the Complex Constructive Dilemma, which goes like this:

	If A is B, C is D; and if E is F, G is H;
	But either A is B, or E is F;
	Therefore either C is D, or G is H.

It is called complex because the conclusion is in disjunctive form. As an example we can take this argument: "If a statesman who sees that his former opinions were wrong does not change his course, he is guilty of deceit; and if he does change his course, he is open to a charge of inconsistency; but either he does not change his course or he does; therefore he is either guilty of deceit, or open to a charge of inconsistency." In this case, as in most dilemmas, the terms A, B, C, D and so on are not all different.

The Destructive Dilemma is always complex, because otherwise it could be broken up into two unconnected destructive hypothetical syllogisms. Its form is:

	If A is B, C is D; and if E is F, G is H;
	But either C is not D, or G is not H;
	Therefore either A is not B, or E is not F.

For instance: "If this man were wise, he would not speak irreverently of Scripture in jest; and if he were good, he would not do so in earnest; but he does it either in jest or in earnest; therefore he is either not wise, or not good."

Footnote: Whately.

Still, dilemmatic arguments are more often fallacious than not. It is rarely possible to find cases where two alternatives cover every possibility, unless one of them is simply the negative of the other, in keeping with the law of excluded middle (see Lesson 14). Suppose we argued that "if a pupil is fond of learning he needs no stimulus, and if he dislikes learning no stimulus will be of any use; but since he is either fond of learning or dislikes it, a stimulus is either needless or of no use." We would plainly be assuming the disjunctive minor premise without any right to it. Fondness and dislike are not the only two possible alternatives. Some pupils are neither fond of learning nor dislike it, and for them a stimulus in the shape of rewards may be a good thing. You can prove almost anything if you are allowed to pick out two of the possible alternatives that suit you and argue from those alone.

A dilemma can often be retorted, that is, rebutted by producing an equally conclusive dilemma that proves the opposite. According to Aristotle, an Athenian mother spoke to her son like this: "Do not go into public life; for if you say what is just, men will hate you; and if you say what is unjust, the gods will hate you." Aristotle suggests the following retort: "I ought to go into public life; for if I say what is just, the gods will love me; and if I say what is unjust, men will love me."

Footnote: Mansel's Aldrich, Appendix, Note I, on the Hypothetical Syllogism.

Questions and Exercises

1. What are the kinds of conditional propositions, and by what signs can you recognize them?

2. What are the rules of the hypothetical syllogism?

3. Which categorical fallacies do breaches of these rules correspond to?

4. Pick out those of the following that are valid arguments, and reduce them to the categorical form; explain what is fallacious in the reasoning of the others.

(1) Rain has fallen if the ground is wet; but the ground is not wet; therefore rain has not fallen.

(2) If rain has fallen, the ground is wet; but rain has not fallen; therefore the ground is not wet.

(3) The ground is wet, if rain has fallen; the ground is wet; therefore rain has fallen.

(4) If the ground is wet, rain has fallen; but rain has fallen; therefore the ground is wet. Note: in these, as in other logical examples, the student must argue only from the premises, and not from any other knowledge of the subject matter.

5. Show that the canons of the syllogism (see Lesson 14) can be stated equally well in the hypothetical or the categorical form.

6. State the following as a Disjunctive or a Dilemmatic argument, and name the kind it belongs to.

If pain is severe it will be brief; and if it lasts long it will be slight; therefore it is to be patiently borne.
