Lesson 22: The Quantification of the Predicate

In the last three lessons the syllogism has been explained almost exactly as it has been taught for more than two thousand years. Geometry has been taught in the way and the order first laid down by the ancient Greek writer Euclid, and in just the same way logic has been taught very nearly as Aristotle taught it, around the year 335 B.C.

But within the last few years, teachers in England have at last come to the conclusion that Euclid's ideas of geometry are not as perfect as they might be. And over the last thirty or forty years it has gradually become clear that Aristotle's syllogism is not an absolutely perfect system of logical deduction either. In fact, certain eminent writers, especially Sir William Hamilton, Professor De Morgan, Archbishop Thomson and George Boole, have shown that we need to make improvements from the very foundations of the science.

This reform in logic goes by the rather mysterious name of the quantification of the predicate. But a reader who has found no impossible difficulty in the lessons so far need not fear one here. To quantify the predicate simply means to state whether the whole of the predicate, or only part of it, agrees with or differs from the subject. Take this proposition:

	"All metals are elements."

Here the subject is quantified, but the predicate is not. We know that all metals are elements, but the proposition does not say plainly whether the metals make up the whole of the elements or not. Now take the quantified proposition

	"All metals are some elements."

The little word "some" says clearly that the metals really form only a part of the elements. Aristotle avoided using any mark of quantity on the predicate. As we have seen, he assumed that every affirmative proposition has a particular predicate, like the example just given, and that only negative propositions have a distributed, or universal, predicate. The fact is, however, that he was entirely wrong about this. As a result he shut out of his system an infinite number of affirmative propositions that are universal in both terms. It is true that "All equilateral triangles are all equiangular triangles," but this proposition could have appeared in his system only in the mutilated form

	"All equilateral triangles are equiangular."

A proposition such as

	"London is the capital of England,"

or "Iron is the cheapest metal," had no proper place at all in his syllogism. In each of these, both terms are singular and identical with each other, and so both are universal.

As soon as we allow the quantity of the predicate to be stated, the forms of reasoning become much simpler. Let us first look at the process of conversion. In our lesson on that subject we had to distinguish between conversion by limitation and simple conversion. Now a single process, simple conversion, is enough for every kind of proposition. So the quantified proposition of the form A,

	"All metals are some elements,"

is converted simply into

	"Some elements are all metals."

The particular affirmative proposition

	"Some metals are some brittle substances"

becomes, just by swapping the terms around,

	"Some brittle substances are some metals."

The particular negative proposition

	"Some men are not (any) trustworthy persons"

is also converted simply, into

	"Not any trustworthy persons are some men,"

though the result may seem less satisfactory in this form than in the affirmative form that follows:

	"Some men are some not-trustworthy persons,"

converted simply into

	"Some not-trustworthy persons are some men."

The universal negative proposition E is converted simply, as before. And finally we have a new affirmative proposition, universal in both subject and predicate, as in "All equilateral triangles are all equiangular triangles." This can obviously be converted simply into

	"All equiangular triangles are all equilateral triangles."

This doubly universal affirmative proposition is extremely common: every definition and every singular proposition is one. I may give as examples "Honesty is the best policy," "The greatest truths are the simplest truths," "Virtue alone is happiness below," and "Self-exaltation is the fool's paradise."

When affirmative propositions are put in quantified form, every immediate inference can easily be drawn from them by one rule: whatever we do to one term, we should do to the other. So from the doubly universal proposition "Honesty is the best policy" we infer that "what is not the best policy is not honesty," and also that "what is not honesty is not the best policy." In fact we can draw two contrapositives from this proposition. But the reader should carefully remember that from the ordinary, unquantified proposition A we can draw only one contrapositive (see Lesson 10). So if "metals are elements," we must not say that "what are not metals are not elements." But if we quantify the predicate, as in "All metals are some elements," we may infer that "what are not metals are not some elements." Immediate inference by added determinant and by complex conception can also be applied in either direction to quantified propositions, without any fear of the errors pointed out in Lesson 10.

Once we admit the mark of quantity in front of the predicate, it is clear that we double the number of propositions the syllogism must take in, because the predicate of each of the four propositions A, E, I, O may be either universal or particular. So we arrive at a list of eight conceivable kinds of proposition, set out in the following table.

	U | All X is all Y. | Affirmative.
	I | Some X is some Y. | Affirmative.
	A | All X is some Y. | Affirmative.
	Y | Some X is all Y. | Affirmative.
	E | No X is (any) Y. | Negative.
	ω | Some X is not some Y. | Negative.
	η | No X is some Y. | Negative.
	O | Some X is no Y. | Negative.

The letters X and Y stand for any subject and any predicate, and by putting in various terms the reader can easily make up propositions of each kind. The symbolic letters on the left were proposed by Archbishop Thomson as a convenient way of referring to each of the eight propositions, and they are very well chosen. The doubly universal affirmative proposition is called U. The simple converse of A is called Y. The Greek letter η (Eta, ē) is given to the proposition we get by changing the universal predicate of E into a particular predicate, and the Greek ω (Omega, ō) to the proposition we get in the same way from O. Sir William Hamilton uses all eight of these propositions, but Archbishop Thomson considers that two of them, η and ω, are never really used. It is a remarkable fact that a complete table of these eight propositions was given by Mr. George Bentham in a work called Outline of a New System of Logic, published in 1827, several years before the earliest of Hamilton's writings on logic. But Mr. Bentham considered that some of the propositions can hardly be told apart from others: Y from A, of which it is the simple converse, for instance, or η from O.

Even using only the two extra propositions U and Y brought in by Thomson extends the list of possible syllogisms a great deal, to 62 in all, without counting the fourth figure, which Hamilton and Thomson do not use. When all eight propositions are admitted, we have to extend the list of possible syllogisms to 12 affirmative and 24 negative moods in each of the first three figures. Sir William Hamilton also invented a curious system of notation for showing all the moods of the syllogism clearly. He always used the letter M for the middle term of the syllogism, and the two letters C and Γ (the Greek capital letter Gamma) for the two terms that appear in the conclusion. He showed the copula of the proposition by a line thickened toward the subject, so that C ——— M means "C is M." To show the quantity of the terms, Hamilton put a colon (:) between the term and the copula when the quantity is universal, and a comma (,) when it is particular. Sir William Hamilton also proposed a new law, or supreme canon, of the syllogism, by which the validity of every form of the syllogism could be tested. He stated it in these words: "What worse relation of subject and predicate subsists between either of two terms and a common third term, with which both are related, and one at least positively so—that relation subsists between these two terms themselves."

By a "worse" relation, Sir William means that a negative relation is worse than an affirmative one, and a particular relation worse than a universal one. So this canon expresses the rules that if there is a negative premise the conclusion must be negative, and if there is a particular premise the conclusion must be particular. Special canons were also worked out for each of the three figures. But in making the system so complicated, the advantages of the quantified form of proposition seem to be lost.

Professor De Morgan also discovered the advantages of the quantified predicate, and invented a system very different from Hamilton's. It is fully explained in his Formal Logic, in The Syllabus of a new System of Logic, and in various important papers on the syllogism in the Transactions of the Cambridge Philosophical Society. These works also give a complete explanation of the "Numerically Definite Syllogism." De Morgan pointed out that two particular premises can often give a valid conclusion, provided that the actual quantities of the two terms are stated and that, added together, they exceed the quantity of the middle term. Suppose a majority of a public meeting vote for the first resolution, and a majority also vote for the second. Then it follows necessarily that some who voted for the first also voted for the second. The two majorities added together are more than the whole number of people at the meeting, so they cannot be made up of entirely different people. They may, indeed, be made up of exactly the same people. But all we can deduce from the premises is that the excess of the two majorities added together over the number at the meeting must have voted for both resolutions. Hamilton said that this kind of inference depends on ultra-total distribution, and the name Plurative Propositions has been proposed for all those propositions that give a definite idea of what fraction or number of the subject the assertion is about.

Questions and Exercises

1. What does the quantification of the predicate mean?

2. Give each of the following propositions its proper symbol, and give the symbol of its converse:

(1) Knowledge is power.

(2) Some rectangles are all squares.

(3) Only the honest ultimately prosper.

(4) Princes have only their titles for their glories.

(5) In man there is nothing great but mind.

(6) The end of philosophy is the detection of unity.

3. Draw all the contrapositive propositions and immediate inferences you can from the following propositions:

(1) London is a great city.

(2) London is the capital of England.

(3) All ruminant animals are all cloven-footed animals.

(4) Some members of parliament are all the ministers.

4. Write out the moods Baroko, Darapti, Felapton and Bokardo in Hamilton's notation.
