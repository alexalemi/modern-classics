Lesson 13: Pascal and Descartes on Method

It is doubtful whether anyone has ever had a sharper or more perfect mind than Blaise Pascal. He was born in 1623 at Clermont, in Auvergne, and showed signs of a remarkable character from his earliest years. At first his father tried to stop him studying geometry. But Pascal's genius for the subject, and his love of it, were so strong that by the age of twelve he had worked out many of the propositions of Euclid's first book by himself, with no help from any person or any book. It is hard to say what he deserves most admiration for: his mathematical discoveries, his invention of the first calculating machine, his wonderful Provincial Letters written against the Jesuits, or his profound Pensées, or Thoughts, a collection of his reflections on scientific and religious subjects.

Among these Thoughts is a remarkable fragment on logical method, and the substance of it also appears in the Port Royal Logic. It is the second article of the Pensées, and its title is Réflexions sur la géométrie en général (Reflections on Geometry in General). I know of no piece of writing that comes closer to perfect truth and perfect clearness of expression. So in this lesson I am going to give a free translation of the most important parts of this fragment, and add to it the rules of method from the Port Royal Logic and from Descartes's famous Essay on Method. Pascal's words are nearly as follows.

"The true method would give us demonstrations of the highest excellence, if only it were possible to use it fully. It consists in keeping two main rules. The first rule is never to use any term whose meaning we have not clearly explained. The second rule is never to put forward any proposition that we cannot demonstrate from truths already known. In a word: define all the terms, and prove all the propositions. But if I am to keep the rules of the very method I am explaining, I must first say what is to be understood by Definition.

"In geometry we recognize only the definitions that logicians call Nominal Definitions. These are definitions that give a name to things that have been clearly pointed out in terms that are perfectly understood. Those are the only definitions I am talking about."

What such definitions are worth, and what they are for, is to make what we say clearer and shorter. They let us "express in the one name we give what could otherwise be said only in several words. The condition is that the name we give must be stripped of any other meaning it might have, so that it carries only the meaning we intend it to stand for.

"For example, suppose we need to tell apart the numbers that can be divided into two equal parts from those that cannot. To avoid repeating this distinction over and over, we give it a name, like this: we call every number that can be divided into two equal parts an Even Number.

"This is a geometrical definition. We have first clearly pointed out a thing, namely any number that can be divided into two equal parts. Then we give it a name, stripped of every other meaning the name might have, so that it carries only the meaning we have pointed out.

"It follows that definitions are entirely free, and that they can never be contradicted. Nothing could be more permissible than giving any name we like to a thing we have clearly pointed out. We need only take care not to abuse this freedom to name things by giving the same name to two different things. Even that would be permissible, as long as we did not mix up the results and carry what is true of one over to the other. But if we do fall into this fault, we have a sure and unfailing remedy. It is to put the definition, in our minds, in place of the thing defined. We must keep the definition so constantly in mind that every time we speak of an even number, say, we understand exactly that it is a number divisible into two equal parts. The two must be so joined and inseparable in our thinking that whenever one is spoken of, the mind goes straight to the other.

"Geometers, and everyone who works methodically, give names to things only to make what they say shorter, not to reduce or alter their ideas of the things they are talking about. They expect the mind always to fill in the whole definition behind the short terms, which they use only to avoid the confusion that a mass of words would cause.

"Nothing stops the cunning fallacies of the sophists faster or more effectively than this method. We should always use it, and it is enough by itself to clear away every kind of difficulty and equivocation.

"With these things well understood, I return to my explanation of the true method, which, as I said, consists in defining everything and proving everything.

"This method would certainly be an excellent one, if it were not absolutely impossible. Plainly, the first terms we tried to define would need earlier terms to explain them. In the same way, the first propositions we tried to prove would depend on other propositions that came before them in our knowledge. So it is clear that we would never reach the first terms or the first propositions.

"So as we push our inquiries further and further back, we necessarily arrive at basic words we cannot define, and at principles so clear that we cannot find any clearer ones to prove them by. It follows that human beings are by nature, and unavoidably, unable to treat any science whatever by a perfect method. But it does not follow that we should give up every kind of method. The most perfect method open to us is neither to define everything and demonstrate everything, nor to define nothing and demonstrate nothing. It is to take the middle course: not to define things that are clear and understood by everyone, but to define all the rest; and not to prove truths that everyone knows, but to prove all the rest. People go wrong in this method both ways: those who try to define and prove everything, and those who fail to do it for things that are not self-evident."

This admirable passage makes one thing plain: as long as we use words, we can never avoid appealing in the end to things themselves. Every definition of a word needs one or more other words, and those words need defining too, and so on forever. Nor may we ever circle back to words we have already defined. If we define A by B, and B by C, and C by D, and then D by A, we commit what may be called a circulus in definiendo, a "circle in defining." This is a very serious fallacy, because it can lead us to think we know the nature of A, B, C and D when in fact we know nothing about them at all.

Pascal summed up his view of the geometrical method clearly in the following rules, which he put into the Port Royal Logic.

Footnote: Mr. Spencer Baynes's translation, p. 317.

1. To admit no terms in the least obscure or equivocal without defining them.

2. To employ in the definitions only terms perfectly known or already explained.

3. To demand as axioms only truths perfectly evident.

4. To prove all propositions which are at all obscure, by employing in their proof only the definitions which have preceded, or the axioms which have been accorded, or the propositions which have been already demonstrated, or the construction of the thing itself which is in dispute, when there may be any operation to perform.

5. Never to abuse the equivocation of terms by failing to substitute for them, mentally, the definitions which restrict and explain them.

The reader will easily see that these rules are much easier to lay down than to follow. Even geometers do not agree about which are the simplest axioms to assume, or which are the best definitions to make. There are many different opinions about the true definition of parallel lines, and about the simplest assumptions to make about their nature. If that is so in geometry, how much harder must it be to follow Pascal's rules with any confidence in the less certain branches of science? After geometry, mechanics is perhaps the most perfect science. Yet the best authorities have been far from agreeing on the exact definitions of such ideas as force, mass, moment, power and inertia, and very different opinions are still held about the simplest axioms from which the law of the composition of forces can be proved. All the same, if we keep steadily in mind, while studying each science, that we need to define every term as far as we can, and to prove every proposition that can be proved from a simpler one, we will do a great deal to clear away error and confusion.

I also want to give here the rules that the famous Descartes proposed for guiding the reason toward the truth. They are as follows:

1. Never to accept anything as true unless we clearly know it to be true. That is, we must carefully avoid haste and prejudice, and include nothing more in our judgments than what presents itself to the mind so clearly and distinctly that we have no room at all to doubt it.

2. To divide each difficulty we examine into as many parts as possible, or as many as are needed to solve it.

3. To direct our thoughts in an orderly way, starting with the simplest objects and the easiest to know, so as to climb step by step to knowledge of the most complex.

4. In every case, to make our lists so complete and our reviews so wide that we can be sure we have left nothing out.

Descartes first stated these rules in his admirable Discourse on Method, where he sets out his thoughts on the right way to conduct the reason and to search for truth in any of the sciences. This little book is easy to get hold of in the original French, and it has also been translated into English by Mr. Veitch. I strongly advise the reader to study it. It is impossible always to follow the rules of Descartes and Pascal, or even to know whether we are following them properly in every case. But it must still be valuable to know what we ought to aim at.

Read Locke's short Essay on the Conduct of the Understanding, which contains admirable remarks on how to acquire exact and logical habits of thought.

Questions and Exercises

(1) What is the use of nominal definitions?

(2) How must we use definitions in order to avoid confusion?

(3) How far can we be said to be free to use any name for any object?

(4) According to Pascal, what is the true method of avoiding error?

(5) How do we learn the meanings of words that cannot be defined?

(6) Give examples of words that can be clearly defined, and of others that cannot.

(7) State the five rules of method given in the Port Royal Logic.

(8) Explain Descartes's rules for reaching the truth.
