Lesson 6: The Growth of Language

We have seen that words become equivocal in at least three different ways. Different words can get confused by accident. A word can change its meaning because it is habitually associated with things other than the thing it first meant. And a word can be carried over, by analogy, to objects of a similar nature. We now need to look more closely at certain changes in language that come from that last cause, because they are going on all the time. In fact, we can almost watch language being created and extended, and for the logician the subject is a very instructive and important one. Two great processes, working in opposite directions, change language in this way:

1. Generalization, by which a name comes to be applied to a wider class of objects than before, so that the extension of its meaning is increased and its intension diminished.

2. Specialization, by which a name comes to be restricted to a narrower class, so that its extension is decreased and its intension increased.

The first change happens in the most obvious way. We notice a resemblance between some new object that has no name and some object we know well, and to express the resemblance we instinctively apply the old name to the new object. We are all well acquainted with glass, for example. If we come across some substance with the same glassy nature and look, we will be quick to call it a kind of glass, and if we meet this new kind of glass often enough, it will probably come to share the name equally with the old, original kind. The word coal has gone through a change like this. Originally it was the name of charked, or charred, wood, which was the main fuel in use five hundred years ago. When mineral coal came into use, it took its name from the older fuel, which it resembled more than it resembled anything else, but at first it was marked off as sea-coal or pit-coal. Now that it is by far the more common of the two, it has taken the plain name, and we mark off the charred wood as charcoal. Paper has gone through a similar change. It originally meant the papyrus used in the Roman Empire, and it was carried over to the new writing material made of cotton or linen rags, which came in at some quite uncertain date. The word character is interesting because of the way logic uses it. The Greek χαρακτήρ meant, strictly, a tool for engraving. By association it was carried over to the marks or letters engraved with that tool, and the word still keeps this meaning when we speak of Greek characters or Arabic characters, meaning figures or letters. But objects often have natural marks, signs or tokens that point them out just as well as artificial characters do, so the name was generalized. It now means any peculiar or distinctive mark or quality by which an object is easily recognized.

Changes of this kind are usually made by no one in particular and with no definite purpose. They come about through a sort of unconscious instinct among the many people who use the name. In the language of science, though, changes are often made on purpose, with a clear understanding of the generalization involved. In everyday life, for example, soap means only a compound of soda or potash with fat. Chemists, however, have deliberately widened the name to include any compound of a metallic salt with a fatty substance. So there are such things as lime-soap and lead-soap, and lead-soap is used in making ordinary diachylon plaster (a sticking plaster). Alcohol at first meant the product of ordinary fermentation, commonly called spirits of wine. Then chemists discovered that many other substances had a theoretical composition very like that of spirits of wine, and the name was adopted for the whole class. A long list of different kinds of alcohols can be found in Dr. Roscoe's lessons on chemistry, and the number of known alcohols can go on growing without limit as discoveries are made. Every one of the chemical terms acid, alkali, metal, alloy, earth, ether, oil, gas and salt can be shown to have gone through great generalizations.

The other sciences supply hardly fewer examples. A lens originally meant a lentil-shaped, or double convex, piece of glass, because that was the kind of glass opticians used most often. But as glasses of other shapes came into use alongside lenses, the name was extended to concave pieces of glass, and even to perfectly flat ones. The words lever, plane, cone, cylinder, arc, conic section, curve, prism, magnet, pendulum, ray, light and many others have been generalized in the same way.

In ordinary language we can see that even proper, or singular, names are often generalized. In Cicero's time, for example, a good actor was called a Roscius, after an actor of outstanding talent. The successor of Julius Caesar adopted the name Caesar as an official name of the Emperor, and it gradually came to mean the same thing as Emperor, so that in the present day the Kaisers of Austria and the Czars of Russia both take their title from Caesar. The abstract name Caesarism has even been formed, to express a kind of imperial system like the one Caesar set up. A king of Egypt built a famous tower on the island of Pharos, at the entrance to the harbor of Alexandria, and because of it lighthouses are called phares in French, and were once called pharos in English. And from the famous Roman general Quintus Fabius Maximus, anyone who avoids bringing a contest to a showdown is said to follow a Fabian policy.

In science, too, singular names are often extended, as when the fixed stars are called distant suns, or the companions of Jupiter are called his moons. There is even a theory, and a likely one, that all general names were created by this process of generalization in the early ages of human progress. Grasping general notions takes a higher intellect than grasping single, concrete things, so it seems natural that names should at first have stood for individual objects, and only afterward been extended to classes. We catch a glimpse of this process in the case of the Aboriginal Australians who had been used to calling a large dog Cadli. When horses were first brought into the country, they took up this name as the nearest description of a horse. Captain Cook tells a very similar story about the people of Otaheite (Tahiti). It can be objected, though, that some act of judgment must have been made before anyone could see that a name suited a particular thing. So it seems likely that specialization as well as generalization was at work at the very beginning of language, much as both are now.

Specialization is the exact opposite of generalization, and it is almost as important. It consists in narrowing the extension of a general name, so that it comes to be the name only of an individual, or of a small part of the original class. This is how we get the names we need for the many new tools, occupations and ideas we deal with as civilization advances. The name physician comes from the Greek φυσικός, natural, and φύσις, nature, so properly it means someone who has studied nature, especially the nature of the human body. It has become restricted, however, to people who use this knowledge for medical purposes, and those who investigate natural science have had to adopt the new name physicist. The name naturalist has been restricted in the same way, to people who study living things. The name surgeon originally meant a handicraftsman. It is a corrupted form of chirurgeon, which comes from the Greek χειρουργός, hand-worker. But it has long been specialized to the people who carry out the mechanical parts of the healing art.

Language is full of examples just as good. Minister originally meant a servant, someone who acted as the minor, or lesser, of another. Now it often means, specifically, the most important man in the kingdom. A chancellor was a clerk, or even a doorkeeper, who sat in a space closed off by bars, or cancelli, in the offices of the Roman Emperor's palace. Now it is always the name of a high official, or even the highest. A peer was an equal (Latin, Par), and we still talk about being tried by our peers; but now, through the strange accidents of language, it means the few who rank above the rest of the Queen's subjects. Deacon, Bishop, Clerk, Queen, Captain and General are all words that have been through a similar process of specialization. In words like telegraph, rail, signal and station, and many other words to do with new inventions, we can follow the change happening within a single lifetime.

One effect of specialization is that it very soon creates a difference between any two words that happen, for some reason, to be synonymous. Two or more words are called synonymous (from the Greek σύν, with, and ὄνομα, name) when they have the same meaning, as perhaps teacher and instructor do, or similarity and resemblance, beginning and commencement, sameness and identity, hypothesis and supposition, intension and comprehension. But the truth is that words commonly called synonymous are seldom perfectly so. There are almost always shades of difference in their meaning or use, which are explained in books like Crabb's English Synonyms. A process that Coleridge called desynonymization, and Herbert Spencer called differentiation, is always going on, and it tends to specialize one word of a synonymous pair to one meaning and the other to another. Wave and billow, for example, originally meant exactly the same physical effect. But poets have now taken over the word 'billow,' while wave is used mainly in practical and scientific matters. Undulation is a third synonym, and in time it will probably become the only scientific term for a wave. Cab was originally just a short form of cabriolet, and so meant much the same thing, but it is now specialized to mean almost nothing but a hackney cab, a cab for hire. In America, car is coming to be restricted to the meaning of a railway car.

We may note that it is a logical defect in a language to have a great many synonymous terms. We get into the habit of using them interchangeably, without being sure that they are free of ambiguities and hidden differences of meaning. English suffers especially from this inconvenience, because it has a complete series of words from Greek or Latin roots that are nearly synonymous with other words of Saxon or French origin. The same statement can, in fact, be put into either Saxon or classical English, and, as Whately has rightly remarked, we often seem to prove a statement merely by repeating it in different words. The wealth and variety of words may add to the rhetorical power of the language, but it also sets traps for all kinds of fallacies. (See Lessons 20 and 21.)

Besides generalization and specialization, a vast number of additions and changes come into language through the analogical, or metaphorical, extension of the meaning of words. No doubt this change could be said to be a kind of generalization, since there must always be some resemblance between the new and the old uses of the term. But the resemblance is often a very distant and obscure one, the kind we would call analogy rather than identity. Every word used metaphorically, or as a comparison, is a case of this process of extension. The name metaphor comes from the Greek words μετά, over, and φέρειν, to carry, and apparently expresses the carrying over of a word from its ordinary use to a special one. The old comparison of a ruler to the pilot of a ship, for example, has given rise to many metaphors, as when we say the Prime Minister is at the Helm of the State. In fact the word governor, with everything derived from it, is one result of this metaphor, since it is just a corrupted form of gubernator, steersman. The words compass, polestar, ensign, anchor and many others connected with navigation are constantly used metaphorically. Horses and hunting give us another set of metaphors: taking the reins of government, overturning the government, taking the bit between the teeth, the Government Whip, being heavily weighted, and so on. No doubt we could show that every other familiar occupation in life has supplied its own stock of metaphors.

It is easy to show, however, that this process does not only go on consciously today. It must have been at work throughout the history of language, and we owe to it almost all, and probably all, the words that express refined mental or spiritual ideas. The very word spirit, now the most refined and immaterial of ideas, is only the Latin spiritus, a gentle breeze or breathing. Inspiration, esprit (or wit) and many other words come from this metaphor. It is a truly curious fact that almost all the words in different languages for mind or soul imply the same comparison with breath. Soul is from the Gothic root meaning a strong wind or storm. The Latin words animus and anima are thought to be connected with the Greek ἄνεμος, wind. ψυχή certainly comes from ψύχω, to blow. πνεῦμα, air or breath, is used in the New Testament for Spiritual Being. And our word ghost has been said to have a similar origin.

Almost all the terms used in mental philosophy, or metaphysics, for the actions and phenomena of the mind come in the end from metaphors. Apprehension is putting out the hand to take hold of something; comprehension is taking things together in a handful; extension is spreading out; intention, bending toward; explication, unfolding; application, folding to; conception, taking up together; relation, carrying back; experience is going thoroughly through a thing; difference is carrying apart; deliberation, weighing out; interruption, breaking between; proposition, placing before; intuition, seeing into. The list could be extended almost without end. Our English name for reason, the understanding, obviously contains some physical metaphor that has never been fully explained, and the Latin intellect contains a metaphor too.

Every sense gives rise to words with refined meanings. Sapience, taste, insipidity and gout (in the sense of taste or relish) come from the sense of taste, and sagacity from the dog's extraordinary power of smell. But the sense of sight is by far the sharpest and most intellectual, so it gives rise to the larger part of language. Clearness, lucidity, obscurity, haziness, perspicuity and countless other expressions come from this sense.

It is truly astonishing how much power language has, through generalization, specialization and metaphor, to create many words from one single root. Professor Max Müller has given a remarkable example in the root spec, which means sight. It appears in the Aryan (Indo-European) languages: in the Sanskrit spas, in the Greek σκέπτομαι (with the consonants swapped round), in the Latin specio, and even in the English spy. Here is an incomplete list of the words developed from this one root: species, special, especial, specimen, spice, spicy, specious, speciality, specific, specialization, specie (gold or silver coin), spectre, specification, spectacle, spectator, spectral, spectrum, speculum, specular, speculation. The same root also combines with various prefixes, and so we get a series of words: suspect, aspect, circumspect, expect, inspect, prospect, respect, retrospect, introspection, conspicuous, perspicuity, perspective. Each of these, in turn, has a number of derivatives attached to it. From suspect, for example, we get suspicion, suspicable, suspicious, suspiciously, suspiciousness. By my own count there are at least 246 words in all, used at one time or another in the English language, that undoubtedly come from the one root spec.

Questions and Exercises

1. Trace the generalization or specialization that has taken place in any of the following words: kind, genus, class, species, order, rank, Augustus, president, speaker, Utopia, rock, Commons, doctor.

2. Point out metaphors that come from the notions of weight, straightness, rock and wind.

3. Distinguish as precisely as you can the meanings of the following synonyms: sickness, malady; mud, mire; confutation, refutation; boundary, limit; mind, intellect; recollection, reminiscence; procrastination, dilatoriness; converse, reverse, obverse, inverse.

4. Make lists of all the words derived from any of the following roots:

(1) Tendere, to stretch, as in intention, attention.

(2) Ponere, to place, as in position, supposition.

(3) Genus, tribe or kind, as in genus, generation.

(4) Munus, gift, as in remuneration, common (Latin, Communis).

(5) Modus, shape or fashion, as in mood, moderate.

(6) Scribere, to write, as in scribe, inscription, describe.

(7) Capere, to take, as in deception, incipient.
