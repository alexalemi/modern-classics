Lesson 1: Definition and Sphere of the Science

The shortest definition of logic is that it is the Science of Reasoning. More often it is called the Science of the Laws of Thought, and some logicians like to be more exact still and call it the Science of the Formal, or Necessary, Laws of Thought. Before definitions like these can do us any good, we need to be clear about what the words in them mean. Once we are, we will probably find that there is not much difference between them.

By a Law of Thought we mean a certain uniformity: a way in which everyone thinks and reasons alike, and must think alike, as long as they don't make what we call mistakes, or contradict themselves, or fall into fallacies. The laws of thought are natural laws. We have no power to interfere with them, and they must never be confused with the man-made laws of a country, which people invent and can change. Every science is busy finding and describing the natural laws that the things it studies obey without exception. Astronomy, for example, studies the uniform way in which the heavenly bodies, and in fact all material things, tend to fall toward one another, the way a stone falls toward the earth, or to move around one another under the pull of that tendency. The universal law of gravitation is the natural law, or uniformity, that physical astronomy deals with.

In chemistry, the law of equivalent proportions describes the well-established fact that each chemical substance combines with every other only in certain fixed proportions. Exactly eight parts by weight of oxygen combine with one part of hydrogen to make water, for instance, and sixteen parts of oxygen combine with six parts of carbon to make carbonic acid gas (carbon dioxide) whenever an ordinary flame or fire burns. Wherever we can find uniformities or similarities, we are to that extent creating science and arriving at natural laws. But there may be, and there are, many things so changeable, so complicated and so uncertain that we can never be sure we have found laws they will always obey, and in those cases no science, in the proper sense of the word, is possible. There is no real science of human character, for example, because the human mind is too variable and too complicated to study that way. No two people are so alike that you can be sure one will act in every situation exactly as the other would. So it is impossible to sort people into classes in such a way that everyone in the same class will act the same way in any given situation.

But there is a science of human reason, or thought, separate from all the other workings of the mind that make up a person's character. That is because there are ways in which everyone does think and reason alike, and must. For example: if two things are identical with a third thing, they are identical with each other. This is a law of thought, and a very simple and obvious one, and we can notice two things about it:

1. Everyone thinks in agreement with it, and agrees that they do as soon as they understand what it means.

2. They think in agreement with it no matter what subject they are thinking about.

Suppose the things we are considering are these:

	London,
	The capital,
	The most populous city in Great Britain.

Since "the capital is identical with London," and "London is identical with the most populous city in Great Britain," it follows necessarily, in every mind, that "the capital is identical with the most populous city in Great Britain."

Or compare these three things:

	Iron,
	The most useful metal,
	The cheapest metal.

If we grant that "the most useful metal is iron" and "iron is the cheapest metal," it follows necessarily, in every mind, that "the most useful metal is the cheapest." These are two examples of the general truth that things identical with the same thing are identical with each other. We can call this a general, or necessary, form of thought and reasoning.

Now compare these three:

	The earth,
	Planets,
	Bodies revolving in elliptical orbits.

This time we can't say, as before, that "the earth is identical with the planets." It is identical with only one of the planets, so we say that "it is a planet." In the same way we can say that "the planets are bodies revolving in elliptical orbits," but they are only some of all the bodies that revolve that way. Even so, it follows that if the earth is among the planets, and the planets are among the bodies revolving in elliptical orbits, then the earth is among those bodies too.

The most elementary chemistry lets us argue the same way about these:

	Iron,
	Metals,
	Elementary substances.

Iron is one of the metals, and the metals are elements, that is, simple substances that cannot be broken down any further. The metals are among the elements, part of them, not the whole of them. It follows necessarily that "iron is one of the elementary substances." So we now have two examples of a fixed and necessary form of thought, one that holds true whatever things we apply it to. This form of argument can be put in several ways, and we will look at it closely in the lessons on the syllogism. One way to put it is: "part of a part is part of the whole." Iron is part of the class of metals, which is part of the class of elements, so iron is part of the class of elements.

So if I now offer another definition of logic, and say it is "the science of the necessary forms of thought," I hope the reader will see clearly what "necessary forms of thought" means. A form is something that can stay the same while the matter poured into it changes. Medals struck from the same dies have exactly the same form, but they can be made of different matter: bronze, copper, gold or silver. A building of exactly the same form could be built of stone or of brick. Furniture of exactly the same shape can be made of oak, mahogany, walnut and so on. We recognize the difference between form and substance in everyday objects without thinking about it, and in logic we can see in just the same way that the form of an argument is one thing, and the subject or matter that the argument happens to be about is quite another. Logic, then, is the science that works out and describes all the general forms of thought we must use as long as we reason validly. These forms are very many, though the principles they are built on are few and simple. This makes logic the most general of all the sciences. We need its help more often than the help of any other science, because each particular science deals with only some of the things that exist, and the sciences grow into very different and often unconnected branches of knowledge. Logic, though, deals with the principles and forms of thought that must be used in every branch of knowledge. It deals with the very origin and foundations of knowledge itself. It is true that the logical method used in one science may differ a little from the method used in another. But whatever its particular form, the method must be logical, and it must obey the laws of thought. In short, there is something all the sciences must have in common, something they must all obey as long as they hold to what is true and consistent, and the work of logic is to explain this common basis of all science.

One name that has been given to logic, the Science of Sciences, describes very well how far the reach of logical principles extends. People who work in the particular branches of knowledge seem to have known perfectly well that they owe allegiance to the highest of the sciences, because the names they have usually chosen say so. The word "logic" itself turns up in almost all the names recently adopted for the sciences. These are often casually called the "ologies," but they are really the "logics," since the "o" is only a connecting vowel or the end of the word before it. So geology is logic applied to explaining how the earth's crust was formed; biology is logic applied to the facts of life; psychology is logic applied to the nature of the mind; and the same goes for physiology, entomology, zoology, teratology (the study of abnormal growths), morphology, anthropology, theology, ecclesiology (the study of churches), thalattology (the study of the sea) and the rest. Each of these sciences openly admits, in its own name, that it is a special kind of logic. The name "logic" comes from the common Greek word λόγος (logos), which usually means a word, the sign and outward expression of a thought inside. But the same word was also used for the inward thought or reasoning that words express, and that is probably how later Greek writers on reasoning came to call their science ἐπιστήμη λογική, "logical science," and also τέχνη λογική, "logical art." The adjective λογική, used on its own, soon became the name of the science, in the same way that mathematics, rhetoric and other names ending in "-ic" started out as adjectives and were turned into nouns.

Footnote: Except philology, which is formed differently and means the love or study of words. If its name had been formed on the same plan, the science would be called "logology."

A good deal of rather trivial argument has gone into the question of whether logic should be thought of as a science only, an art only, or both at once. Sir William Hamilton even took the trouble to sort nearly every writer on logic according to which opinion they held. But it seems correct, and enough, to say this: logic is a science insofar as it simply investigates the necessary principles and forms of thought, and so teaches us what correct thinking consists of. It becomes an art when it sets about making rules to help people catch false reasoning. A science teaches us to know, and an art teaches us to do, and all the more advanced sciences lead to useful arts that go with them. Astronomy is the foundation of the art of navigating the ocean, and of arranging the calendar and keeping track of dates. Physiology is the basis of the art of medicine, and chemistry is the basis of many useful arts. In the same way, logic has been thought of as the basis of an art of correct reasoning, or investigation, which ought to teach the true method to be followed in every science. The famous British logician Duns Scotus, who lived in the thirteenth century, called logic the Science of Sciences, and he also called it the Art of Arts, which says everything about its preeminence. Others have defined it like this: "Logic is the art of directing the reason aright in acquiring the knowledge of things, for the instruction both of ourselves and others." Dr. Isaac Watts took this view of logic and called his well-known book "the Art of Thinking."

Still, it is fair to say that logic is more a science than an art, and here is the reason. Everyone picks up the ability and the habit of reasoning long before they have even heard the word "logic." They pick it up by the natural use of their minds, or by constantly and unconsciously copying other people. So in all very simple cases they follow the principles of the science correctly, but without knowing it. Yet the contradictory opinions and absurd fallacies put forward by people with no training show that this unaided use of the mind can't be trusted once the subject under discussion gets at all difficult or complicated. So studying logic cannot be useless. It explains the principles on which everyone has often reasoned correctly already, and it also points out the dangers of arguing wrongly. The reasoner becomes a correct reasoner on purpose, and learns to avoid the traps of fallacy on purpose. To say that people can reason well without the science of logic is about as true as saying they can stay healthy without medicine. So they can, as long as they are healthy; and reasoners can do without the science of reasoning, as long as they do reason correctly. But how many people can? A man might as well claim his body is immortal as claim his mind is infallible.

And if it is worth saying a few words in defense of logic as an art, because of misunderstandings that have arisen in the history of the subject, does anything need to be said in praise of it as a science? Everything great in science, in art or in literature is the work of the intellect. In the form of our bodies we are related to the animals, and in the part of us that perishes we are nothing but matter. What raises human beings above everything else on earth is a conscious intellect, the power of reasoning with general ideas. Who can say, then, that the nature and workings of this intellect are not very nearly the highest and most interesting subject we could ever study? No one could seriously deny the truth of Sir William Hamilton's favorite saying:

	In the world there is nothing great but man.
	In man there is nothing great but mind.

Questions and Exercises

1. What do we mean by a Law of Nature, and by a Law of Thought?

2. Explain the difference between the Form of Thought and the Matter of Thought.

3. In what sense can logic be called the Science of Sciences?

4. Where does the name "logic" come from?

5. How does a science differ from an art, and why is logic more a science than an art?

6. Can we say that logic is a necessary aid to correct reasoning, when people who have never studied logic reason correctly?
