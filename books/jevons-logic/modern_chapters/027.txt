Lesson 27: Observation and Experiment

It is safe to say that all knowledge must ultimately rest on experience. "Experience" is simply a general name for all the different feelings and impressions the mind receives at any time in its existence. The mind never creates completely new knowledge independent of experience. All our reasoning powers can do is work out the full meaning of the facts we already have. In earlier centuries, people of the very highest ability believed that the mind, by its own power alone and by thinking hard enough, could discover what the things outside us ought to be, and would turn out to be when examined. They thought we could anticipate Nature, working out from the human mind an idea of what the Creator would have made. The famous philosopher Descartes, for example, held that whatever the mind can clearly conceive may be taken as true. But we can conceive of mountains of gold or oceans of fresh water, and in fact they do not exist. Anything we can clearly conceive must agree with the laws of thought, and so, as far as our intellect is concerned, its existence is not impossible. But the shapes and sizes and ways in which it has pleased the Creator to make things, in this or any other part of the universe, cannot possibly be anticipated by the very limited wisdom of the human mind. They can only be learned by actually examining the things that exist.

In the second half of the 13th century the great Roger Bacon taught clearly, in England, that experience is supremely important as the basis of knowledge. By a curious coincidence, the same doctrine was taken up again in the 17th century by the great Lord Chancellor Francis Bacon, and it has been called the Baconian Philosophy after him. I believe that Roger Bacon was an even greater man than Francis, though Francis is the better known. But the words in which Francis Bacon proclaimed the importance of experience and experiment deserve to be remembered forever. At the beginning of his great work, the Novum Organum, or New Instrument, he points out our proper position as learners in the world of Nature:

"Man, the Servant and Interpreter of Nature, can do and understand as much as he has observed concerning the order of nature in outward things or in the mind; beyond this he can neither know nor do."

That is the first of the aphorisms, or short numbered paragraphs, with which the Novum Organum begins. In the second aphorism he says that the mind on its own can do little and is liable to go wrong. It needs help, in the form of a definite logical method, and supplying that method was the purpose of his New Instrument. The 3rd and 4th aphorisms must be given in full. They are:

"Human science and human power coincide, because not knowing a cause deprives us of the effect. For Nature is not conquered except by obeying her; and what we discover as a cause when we contemplate becomes a rule when we act."

"Man can do nothing for himself except move natural bodies toward or away from each other; Nature working within does the rest."

It would be impossible to express more clearly and completely how we discover science by interpreting the changes we observe in Nature, and then turn that knowledge to useful purposes in the arts and manufacturing. We cannot create or destroy a single particle of matter. It is now known that we cannot even create or destroy force (in today's terms, energy). Nor can we really alter the inner nature of any substance we deal with. All we can do is observe carefully how one substance acts on another by its natural powers, and then, by bringing them together at the right time, accomplish what we want. As Bacon says, "Nature working within does the rest." If it had not been the nature of heat, applied to water, to produce steam with elastic power (the power to push back when compressed), the steam engine obviously could never have been made. The invention of the steam engine came from noticing how useful the force of steam was, and putting it to work accordingly. It is in this sense that the Roman poet Virgil called that person happy who knows the causes of things:

	Felix qui potuit rerum cognoscere causas,

and that Bacon said, "Knowledge is Power." To the extent that we have observed how things happen in Nature, and on what occasions particular effects come about, we can avoid those effects or make use of them as we wish. We do this not by altering the natures of things, but by letting them show their own proper powers at suitable times and in suitable circumstances. In this way, as Tennyson has put it so well, we

	"Rule by obeying Nature's Powers."

Inductive logic deals with the methods of reasoning by which we can successfully interpret Nature and learn the natural laws that different substances obey in different circumstances. In this lesson we consider the first requirement of induction: the experience, or examination of Nature, that supplies us with facts. We get this experience either by observation or by experiment. To observe is simply to notice events and changes produced in the ordinary course of Nature, without being able to control or vary them, or at least without trying to. The early astronomers, for example, observed the motions of the sun, moon and planets among the fixed stars, and gradually detected many of the laws, or regular returns, of those bodies. In the same way the meteorologist observes the ever-changing weather. He notes the height of the barometer, the temperature and humidity of the air, the direction and force of the wind, and the height and character of the clouds, without being able to control any of these things in the least. The geologist, again, is generally a simple observer when he investigates the nature and position of rocks. The zoologist, the botanist and the mineralogist usually rely on mere observation when they examine animals, plants and minerals in the natural condition in which they find them.

In experiment, by contrast, we vary the combinations of things and circumstances as we choose, and then observe the result. This is how the chemist discovers the composition of water: he uses an electric current to separate it into its two constituents, oxygen and hydrogen. The mineralogist may use experiment when he melts two or three substances together to find out how a particular mineral might have been formed. Even the botanist and the zoologist are not limited to passive observation. By moving animals or plants to different climates and different soils, and by what is called domestication, they can test how far the natural forms and species can be altered.

Clearly, where experiment can be used at all, it is the most powerful and direct way of getting facts. We might have to wait years or centuries to come across, by accident, facts that we can easily produce at any moment in a laboratory. Most of the chemical substances now known, and many extremely useful products, would probably never have been discovered at all if we had waited for Nature to present them to us of her own accord. Many forces and changes, too, may be going on in Nature all the time, but so faintly that they escape our senses, and we need some experimental means to detect them. Electricity is no doubt at work in every particle of matter, perhaps at every moment. Even the ancients could not help noticing its action in the lodestone (a natural magnet), in lightning, in the Aurora Borealis (the northern lights), and in a piece of rubbed amber (in Greek, electrum, from which "electricity" takes its name). But in lightning, electricity was too intense and dangerous to study; in the other cases it was too weak to be properly understood. The science of electricity and magnetism could only advance once we had regular supplies of electricity from the ordinary electric machine (a friction generator) or the galvanic battery, and could make powerful electromagnets. Most, if not all, of the effects electricity produces must go on in Nature too, but far too faintly to be observed.

Experiment is also indispensable for another reason. On the surface of the earth we usually meet substances under certain uniform conditions, so observation alone could never teach us what those substances would be like under other conditions. Carbonic acid (carbon dioxide), for example, is met with only as a gas, given off when carbon burns. But when it is exposed to extreme pressure and cold, it condenses into a liquid, and can even be turned into a solid, like snow. Many other gases have been liquefied or solidified in the same way, and there is reason to believe that every substance can take all three forms, solid, liquid and gas, if only the conditions of temperature and pressure can be varied enough. Mere observation of Nature would have led us to the opposite belief: that nearly all substances were fixed in a single state, and could not be turned from solid into liquid, or from liquid into gas.

We must not suppose, however, that we can draw a precise line between observation and experiment and say where one ends and the other begins. The difference is one of degree rather than of kind. All we can say is that the more we vary the conditions artificially, the more we are using experiment. I have said that meteorology is a science of almost pure observation. But if we deliberately climb mountains to observe how the air becomes thinner and colder with height, or go up in balloons for the same purpose, as Gay-Lussac and Glaisher did, we vary our way of observing so much that it becomes almost experimental. Astronomers, again, can almost be said to experiment rather than merely observe when they set up instruments at the same moment as far north and as far south on the earth's surface as possible, to observe how far the position of Venus appears to shift as it crosses the face of the sun in a transit. In this way they can compare the distances of Venus and the sun with the size of the earth.

Sir John Herschel has described this difference excellently in his Discourse on the Study of Natural Philosophy. "Essentially they are much alike, and differ rather in degree than in kind; so that perhaps the terms passive and active observation might better express their distinction; but it is, nevertheless, highly important to mark the different states of mind in inquiries carried on by their respective aids, as well as their different effects in promoting the progress of science. In the former, we sit still and listen to a tale, told us, perhaps obscurely, piecemeal, and at long intervals of time, with our attention more or less awake. It is only by after rumination that we gather its full import; and often, when the opportunity is gone by, we have to regret that our attention was not more particularly directed to some point which, at the time, appeared of little moment, but of which we at length appreciate the importance. In the latter, on the other hand, we cross-examine our witness, and by comparing one part of his evidence with the other, while he is yet before us, and reasoning upon it in his presence, are enabled to put pointed and searching questions, the answer to which may at once enable us to make up our minds. Accordingly it has been found invariably, that in those departments of physics where the phenomena are beyond our control, or into which experimental enquiry, from other causes, has not been carried, the progress of knowledge has been slow, uncertain and irregular; while in such as admit of experiment, and in which mankind have agreed to its adoption, it has been rapid, sure, and steady."

Footnote: Herschel's Discourse, p. 77.

Often, though, Nature has, so to speak, made experiments on a scale and over a length of time that we cannot possibly match. We do not need to test which soil and situation suit a given plant best, for example. We only have to look around and notice the habitat, or place, where it grows naturally in the most flourishing condition, and we can be sure that this shows the result of ages of natural experiment. We would probably never have known the distances of the fixed stars if the earth, by traveling in an orbit 182,000,000 miles across, did not give us a kind of experimental base line for observing them. Because of it we see the stars in very slightly different positions, and so can judge their distances compared with the size of the earth's orbit. Eclipses, transits, occultations (when the moon or a planet passes in front of a star) and remarkable conjunctions of the planets are also kinds of natural experiment. They were often recorded in early times, and so give us data of the greatest value.

Logic can do little or nothing to make someone a sharp or accurate observer. No definite rules can be laid down on the subject. Observing well is an art that can only be gained by practice and training, and one of the greatest advantages of studying the Natural Sciences is that it develops the ability to observe clearly and steadily. Logic can, however, give us one caution, which Mr. J. S. Mill has pointed out well: we must distinguish accurately between what we really observe and what we only infer from the facts we observe. As long as we only record and describe what our senses have actually witnessed, we cannot make a mistake. The moment we assume or infer anything, we can go wrong. For example, suppose we examine the sun's surface through a telescope. We observe that it is intensely bright except where there are small breaks, or round openings, in the surface with a dark interior. We are irresistibly led to conclude that the inside of the sun is colder and darker than the outside, and we record as a fact that we saw the dark interior of the sun through certain openings in its luminous atmosphere. But that record would contain a mistaken inference. We saw nothing but dark spots, and as observers we should have done no more than record the shape, size, appearance and changes of those spots. Whether they are dark clouds above the luminous surface, or glimpses of the dark interior, or, as is now (in the 1870s) almost certainly inferred, something entirely different from either, can only be proved by comparing many unprejudiced observations.

The reader cannot keep in mind too often this caution against confusing the facts observed with the inferences drawn from them. It is not too much to say that nine-tenths of what we seem to see and hear is inferred, not actually felt. Every sense has what are called acquired perceptions: the power, built up by long experience, of judging unconsciously many things that cannot be perceived directly. The eye cannot see distance. Yet we constantly imagine, and say, that we see things at such and such a distance, without realizing that this is the result of judgment. As Mill remarks, it is saying too much to say "I saw my brother." All I positively know is that I saw someone who closely resembled my brother, as far as I could observe. It is only by judgment that I can say he was my brother, and that judgment might be wrong.

Nothing is more important in observation and experiment than not letting any prejudice or theory influence us, so that we record the facts observed correctly and give them their proper weight. Someone who fails to do this will almost always be able to find facts that support an opinion, however mistaken. For example, most uneducated people still believe firmly that the moon has a great influence on the weather. The changes of the moon, full, new and half moon, happen four times every month, and it is supposed that any change can influence the weather at least on the day before or the day after it. So there will be twelve days out of every 28 on which any change in the weather would be put down to the moon, and over a year many changes will probably be recorded in this way as supporting the opinion. The uneducated observer is struck by these cases and remembers them carefully. But he fails to notice, or at least to remember, that changes of weather also often happen when there is no change of the moon at all. The question could only be settled by a long course of careful and unbiased observation, in which all the facts, favorable and unfavorable, were recorded equally. All the observations that have been published go against the idea that the moon has any such influence as the popular mind gives it.

At the same time, it would be a mistake to think that the best observer or experimenter is the one who holds no previous opinions or theories about the subject he is investigating. On the contrary, the great experimenter is the one who always has a theory, or even a whole crowd of theories or ideas, in his mind, but is always putting them to the test of experience and throwing out the ones that are false. The number of things that could be observed and experimented on is infinite. If we simply set to work recording facts without any definite purpose, our records will be worthless. We need some opinion or theory to guide our choice of experiments, and we are more likely to hit on the truth that way than by working at random. But the great requirement of the true scientist is that he be completely unbiased, and give up any opinion as soon as facts are observed that are inconsistent with it.

The famous Turgot (the French economist and statesman) put it well: "the first thing is to invent a system; the second thing is to be disgusted with it." That is, we ought to have some idea of the truth we are looking for, but we should immediately put it to a severe trial, as if we were inclined to distrust and dislike it, rather than be biased in its favor. Probably few people have entertained more false theories than Kepler and Faraday; few have discovered or established truths of greater certainty and importance. Faraday himself has said:

"The world little knows how many of the thoughts and theories which have passed through the mind of a scientific investigator, have been crushed in silence and secrecy by his own severe criticism and adverse examination; that in the most successful instances not a tenth of the suggestions, the hopes, the wishes, the preliminary conclusions have been realized."

The student is strongly advised to read Sir John Herschel's Preliminary Discourse on the Study of Natural Philosophy (Lardner's Cabinet Cyclopædia), especially Part II, Chapters 4 to 7, on Observation, Experiment, and the Inductive Processes generally.

Questions and Exercises

1. What is the false method of science that Bacon protested against?

2. Explain exactly what Bacon meant when he said that man is the Servant and Interpreter of Nature, and that Knowledge is Power.

3. How does experiment differ from observation?

4. Classify the sciences according to whether they use passive observation, experiment, or both.

5. Name the chief ways in which experiment is better than mere observation.

6. What is the chief precaution needed in observation?

7. Explain how it is possible to anticipate Nature and yet base all our conclusions on the results of experience.
