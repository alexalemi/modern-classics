Lesson 32: Classification, and Abstraction

In Lesson 12, on the Predicables, we looked at the doctrine of classification as logicians treated it many centuries ago. Over the last two centuries, though, the progress of science has drawn a great deal of attention to the true principles by which we can put a huge number of different objects in order. So we now have to ask what makes a system of classification natural and perfect.

In fact, you could say that our subject here covers the whole science of logic. All thought and all reasoning, as far as it deals with general names or general notions, can be said to consist in classification. Every common, or general, name is the name of a class, and every name of a class is a common name. "Metal" is the name of one class of substances, the one we have used so often in our syllogisms; "element" is the name of another class, of which the first is a part. Reasoning has been described, quite plausibly, as affirming of the parts of a class whatever can be affirmed of the whole. Every law of nature we arrive at lets us class a number of facts together. It would hardly be going too far to define logic as the theory of classification.

Here, however, we are dealing with the more deliberate and clear-cut arrangement of objects or notions that is used above all in the natural sciences, such as botany, zoology, mineralogy and paleontology.

The history of the word class is rather curious. In ancient Rome the whole people was summoned together at certain times, and this ceremony was called a classis, from the Greek κλάσις (klasis) or κλῆσις (klēsis), which comes from καλέω (kaleō), to call together. Servius Tullius is said to have divided the people into six orders according to how much tax they could pay, and these orders were, naturally enough, called the classes of the people. From there the name gradually came to be used for any organized body of people, such as an army. Then it was carried over to a fleet of ships drawn up in a fixed order, and finally, by analogy, it was stretched to cover any collection of objects carefully arranged. It is curious that when we speak today of the lower or higher classes of the people, we are bringing the word back very close to its original meaning.

Classification is perhaps best defined as the arrangement of things, or of our notions of them, according to their resemblances or identities. Every class should be made up of objects that resemble each other exactly in certain definite qualities, and those qualities are stated in the definition of the class. The more numerous and wide-reaching the resemblances that a system of classes points out, the more perfect and useful we must consider that system.

Mr. J. S. Mill describes his view of what classification means this way: "Classification is a contrivance for the best possible ordering of the ideas of objects in our minds; for causing the ideas to accompany or succeed one another in such a way as shall give us the greatest command over our knowledge already acquired, and lead most directly to the acquisition of more. The general problem of classification, in reference to these purposes, may be stated as follows: To provide that things shall be thought of in such groups, and those groups in such an order, as will best conduce to the remembrance, and to the ascertainment of their laws."

A collection of objects can usually be classified in an unlimited number of ways. Any quality that some of them have and others lack can be taken as the first difference, and the groups it marks off can then be subdivided, one after another, by any other qualities we like. A library of books, for example, could be arranged (1) by size, (2) by the language they are written in, (3) by the alphabetical order of their authors' names, (4) by subject, and in various other ways. Large libraries and catalogues use these arrangements and combine them in different ways. Each arrangement has its own particular convenience, and we must choose the one that best serves the special purpose of the library or catalogue. The population of a country, again, can be classified in an almost endless number of ways, for different purposes or different sciences. The people of the United Kingdom can be divided by place of birth, as English, Welsh, Scottish, Irish, colonial-born, and foreigners. The ethnographer would divide them into Anglo-Saxons, Cymri (the Welsh Britons), Gaels, Picts, Scandinavians, and so on. The statistician arranges them by age; by marital condition, as married, unmarried, widowed, and so on; and by state of body, as able-bodied, incapacitated, blind, or intellectually disabled. The economist looks at the countless trades people carry on and classifies them in a complicated way. The lawyer, again, treats everyone as a minor, an adult, a feme sole (an unmarried woman, who held property in her own name), a feme couverte (a married woman, whose legal identity the law then merged with her husband's), a guardian, ward, trustee, felon, and so on.

In the natural world, too, we can make various classifications. Plants can be arranged by the country they come from; by the kind of place, or habitat, they thrive in; by how long they live, as annual, biennial, perennial; by size, as herbs, shrubs, trees; or by their uses, as food plants, drugs, or poisons. All of these are different from the classifications that botanists devise to represent the natural affinities, or relationships, of plants. So it is clear that when we make a classification there is no single fixed method that can be found by rule. Usually an unlimited number of choices are open to us. In cases like this logic cannot do much; it is really the job of the special sciences to work out what kind of classification is needed. All logic can do is point out certain general requirements and principles.

The first requirement of a good classification is that it suits the purpose in hand. That is, the points of resemblance chosen to form the leading classes should be the ones that matter for the practical use of the classification. Everything that needs to be treated alike must be grouped together, and things that need to be treated differently must be separated. A lawyer, for instance, has no need to classify people by the English county they were born in, because the law is the same whatever the county. But a Scot, a Manxman, or a foreigner lives under laws different from those of a man born in England, and to that extent we will have to classify them separately. A gardener is quite right to classify plants as annuals, biennials, perennials; as herbs, shrubs, trees; as evergreen and deciduous; or by the soil, temperature and other conditions that affect them. These are exactly the points that must guide him in treating some plants differently from others.

A second requirement, and from a scientific point of view the most important one, is that a good classification lets us make the greatest possible number of general assertions. This is the criterion, as Dr. Whewell stated it, that distinguishes a natural system of classification from an artificial one, and we need to look carefully at what it means. Clearly, a good classification is more than just an orderly arrangement. It involves a process of induction that brings to light all the more general relations among the things being classified. An arrangement of books will usually be artificial: the octavo volumes have nothing in common except being octavo size. An alphabetical arrangement of names, again, is extremely convenient and suitable for many purposes, but it is artificial, because it allows few or no general assertions. We cannot make any general assertion at all about people just because their names happen to begin with an A or a B, a P or a W. Even the people who share the name Smith or Taylor or Robinson could be put through the inductive method of agreement without our discovering any common circumstance that could be stated as a general proposition, or law. It is true that if we looked into the backgrounds of the Evanses and the Joneses, we would find nearly all of them to be Welsh, and the Campbells to be Scottish, and people with a very unusual name would often turn out to descend from common ancestors. To that extent even an alphabetical arrangement has something natural in it, and lets us make general assertions. In fact, hardly any arrangement can be made that does not show some traces of important relations and resemblances. But what we want is a system that will reveal all the most important general truths.

For this purpose we must choose, as the basis for grouping things together, the characters that carry the most other characters with them. In Lesson 12 we described a property (proprium) as a quality that belongs to the whole of a class without being part of the class's definition. Now, we should frame the definition of a class so that it contains as few characters as possible, while as many other characters, properties, or propria as possible can be attributed to the things in the class. Everyone can see, for instance, that animals form one great group of living beings with many characters in common, and that plants form another. Animals have sensation and voluntary motion; they consume carbon-containing food and give off carbonic acid (carbon dioxide); they have a stomach; and they produce fat. Plants lack sensation and voluntary motion; they build carbon-containing tissue, absorb carbonic acid, and give off oxygen; they have no stomach; and they produce starch. At one time it might have been thought that almost any one of these characters was enough to tell which group a living being belonged to. Whatever had a stomach was an animal, and whatever did not was a plant. Whatever produced starch or gave off oxygen was called a plant; whatever absorbed oxygen or produced fat was an animal. These statements are still generally true today, so we can make assertions in the form of the proposition U (the doubly universal affirmative of Lesson 22): "all animals are all beings that give off carbonic acid, and all plants are all beings that absorb carbonic acid." But in reality there are many exceptions, and the more research we do, the clearer it becomes that no definite line can be drawn between animal and vegetable life. This, of course, is not a failure of logical science. It is a highly significant fact about the things themselves.

When we classify plants, we again meet very deep and natural distinctions, this time between the great classes called Exogens, Endogens, and Acrogens (roughly, the groups botanists now call dicots, monocots, and the spore-bearing plants such as ferns and mosses). The Acrogens have no true flowers with sexes and no seeds. They are made almost entirely of cellular tissue, and their outer skin, or epidermis, has no pores. The other two classes have much in common: they have true flowers, woody tissue and pores in the skin, and so they can be united into one wider class, the Vasculares. But exogens and endogens are also very sharply distinguished from each other. Exogens have a stem or trunk made of distinct bark, pith, and wood in concentric layers; leaves with net-like veins; and seeds with two seed-leaves and a naked radicle (the root of the embryo). Generally, too, the parts of the flower come in some multiple of two or five. Endogens, on the other hand, have no distinct bark, pith, and wood, and no concentric layers; their leaves have parallel veins; their seeds have one seed-leaf and a radicle that is not naked; and the parts of the flower generally come in multiples of three.

These are the very widest classes in what is called the natural system of botanical arrangement, but the same principles hold in all its smaller classes. Botanists are constantly working to bring the great multitudes of plants together into species, genera, orders, classes, and various groups in between, so that the members of each group have the greatest number of points of resemblance to each other and the fewest points of resemblance to members of other groups. This is how the great purpose of classification is best fulfilled. Classification reduces multiplicity to unity, and it lets us infer of all the other members of a class what we know of any one member, provided we distinguish properly between the qualities that are likely, or known, to belong to the class and those that are peculiar to the individual. As Professor Huxley has remarked, it is a necessary condition of correct classification that the definition of a group should hold exactly true of all the members of the group, and not of the members of any other group. In the natural sciences, though, this condition is very hard to carry out, because kinds of plants or animals keep being discovered that sit halfway between classes that would otherwise be well distinguished. Ferns, for example, are a real embarrassment to the fundamental division of plants. They have no true flowers, and in this and other ways they agree with the other acrogens; yet they have plenty of woody fiber, which would entitle them to rank with the vasculares, the larger group that exogens and endogens are subdivisions of.

We might add that the progress of chemistry is quickly turning it into a science of classification. In fact the whole theory of chemical combination now depends on grouping the elements and compounds correctly. Dr. Roscoe, in his Lessons in Elementary Chemistry, lists no fewer than eleven classes of metals, each class having a number of properties in common. The metals of the alkalies, for instance, namely potassium, sodium, cesium, rubidium and lithium, form a remarkably natural class. They are all soft, melt easily, and turn to vapor at high temperatures. They combine very violently with oxygen and decompose water at any temperature, forming oxides that dissolve very readily in water and become powerfully caustic and alkaline substances, from which heat cannot drive the water out. Their carbonates dissolve in water, and each metal forms only one compound with chlorine.

The metals of the alkaline earths, calcium, strontium and barium, also form a very natural class. What distinguishes them is that their carbonates do not dissolve in pure water but do dissolve in water that has carbonic acid dissolved in it. The gold class contains the rare or valuable metals gold, platinum, palladium, rhodium, ruthenium, iridium and osmium. Nitric acid does not act on them, and they can only be dissolved by chlorine or by the mixture of acids called aqua regia. Their oxides can be reduced, or have their oxygen removed, simply by heating them.

Natural classifications give us the deepest resemblances and relations, and they may lead us in the end to know how the different varieties of things came to be produced. So they are essential to a true science; you could almost say they make up the framework of the science. It does not follow, though, that they suit every purpose. When all we want is to recognize which chemical element, plant or animal we have in front of us, its character as defined in a natural system will give us little or no help. A chemist does not detect potassium by getting it into the state of a metal and testing whether it decomposes water. He simply looks for whichever compounds of potassium have the most clearly marked and unusual characters. A compound of potassium, platinum and chlorine, for example, is the most distinctive, or characteristic, of the metal, and it is generally used as a way of recognizing it. The fine violet color that potash (a potassium compound) gives to the flame of a lamp was also used as a sign of potassium's presence long before the spectroscope was brought in to analyze such colors. An artificial classification of the elements is therefore necessary for detecting substances. Accordingly, any book on chemical analysis will contain arrangements of the elements by characters of very minor importance, which are chosen because they can be observed easily and reliably.

In botany, again, the natural system of classification is far from well suited to finding out the name of a plant. Its classes are often defined by the shape of tiny parts of the seed, the arrangement of the seed-vessel, and other parts that are usually hard, and sometimes impossible, to examine. So botanists usually arrange their genera and species in the order of the natural system, but devise a sort of key, or artificial arrangement, that uses the simplest and most obvious characters, often called characteristics, to tell the plants apart. The best arrangement of this kind for British plants is found in Bentham's British Flora. In fact, the famous Linnaean arrangement of plants was intended by its author to serve exactly this purpose. Linnaeus was far too deep a thinker to suppose that the numbers of stamens and pistils generally expressed the real relationships of plants. Many of his classes were genuinely natural classes, but he chose the stamens and pistils as the general guide to the classes and orders because they were very plain and obvious marks.

Closely connected with classification is the process of abstraction. To abstract is to separate the qualities common to all the individuals of a group from the peculiarities of each individual. The notion "triangle" is the result of abstraction, to the extent that we can reason about triangles without paying any attention to the particular size or shape of any one triangle. All classification involves abstraction, because in forming and defining a class I must separate the common qualities from the peculiarities. When I abstract, I also form a general conception, that is, one that usually takes in many objects. But if the quality I abstract is a peculiar property of the class, one that belongs to the whole class and to no other objects, I may not increase the extent of the notion at all. So Mr. Herbert Spencer is perhaps right to hold that we can abstract without generalizing. We often use this word generalization, and the process can be defined as inferring of a whole class what we know only of a part of it. Whenever we regard the qualities of a thing as not confined to that thing alone but as extending to other objects, and in fact whenever we consider a thing only as a member of a class, we are said to generalize. If, after studying the properties of the circle, we go on to those of the ellipse, the parabola and the hyperbola, we soon find that the circle is just one case of a whole class of curves called the conic sections, which correspond to equations of the second degree. I generalize when I regard certain properties of the circle as shared by many other curves.

Dr. Whewell added to the oversupply of terms for the same processes when he introduced the expression Colligation of facts. Whenever two things are found to have similar properties, so that they are placed in the same class, they can be said to be connected together. We connect together the positions of a planet as it moves around the sun when we think of them as points on one and the same ellipse. Whenever we join together facts that were unconnected before, by means of a suitable general notion or hypothesis, we are said to colligate them. Dr. Whewell adds that the general conceptions we use must be (1) clear and (2) appropriate. But it is very doubtful whether there is anything in these processes really different from the general process of natural classification that we have been considering.

Questions and Exercises

1. Define classification, and give the derivation of the word.

2. What do you mean by important characters in classification?

3. State Dr. Whewell's criterion of a good natural arrangement.

4. Distinguish between a natural and an artificial system of classification.

5. What do you mean by a characteristic quality? Is it always an important quality?
