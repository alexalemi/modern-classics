Lesson 17: Reduction of the Imperfect Figures of the Syllogism

To make the nineteen valid and useful moods of the syllogism easier to remember, logicians invented, at least six centuries ago, a very curious system of artificial words, put together into verses that are easy to learn by heart. However clever the device is, it is barbarous and entirely unscientific. Still, a student of logic is expected to know how it is built and how to use it, so the verses are given and explained below:

	Barbara, Celarent, Darii, Ferioque, prioris;
	Cesare, Camestres, Festino, Baroko, secundæ;
	Tertia, Darapti, Disamis, Datisi, Felapton,
	Bokardo, Ferison, habet; Quarta insuper addit
	Bramantip, Camenes, Dimaris, Fesapo, Fresison.

The words that are not mood names are real Latin words. They tell us that the four moods whose artificial names are Barbara, Celarent, Darii and Ferio belong to the first figure ("prioris," of the first); that the next four belong to the second ("secundæ"); that the third ("Tertia") has six more; and that the fourth figure ("Quarta") adds five moods besides. Each artificial name contains three vowels, and these give the propositions that make up a valid mood. So CElArEnt stands for the mood of the first figure that has E for its major premise, A for its minor premise, and E for its conclusion. Taken all together, the artificial words contain exactly the combinations of vowels shown in the table of valid moods in Lesson 16, except those in brackets.

These verses also contain directions for proving each mood of the second, third and fourth figures by reducing it to a matching mood of the first figure. Aristotle regarded the first figure as an especially clear and convincing form of argument, because the Dictum de omni et nullo applies to it directly, and so he called it the Perfect Figure. He never recognized the fourth figure at all. It is often called the Galenian figure, because the famous Greek physician Galen is supposed to have discovered it. Aristotle knew the second and third figures as the Imperfect Figures. They had to be reduced to the first figure by certain conversions of the premises and by changing the order of the premises, and the artificial words contain the directions for doing this. The directions are as follows:

The letter s means that the proposition marked by the vowel just before it is to be converted simply.

The letter p means that the proposition is to be converted per accidens, or by limitation.

The letter m means that the premises of the syllogism are to be transposed: the old major premise becomes the minor premise of a new syllogism, and the old minor becomes the new major. The m comes from the Latin mutare, to change.

B, C, D, F, the consonants the names begin with, tell us which mood of the first figure the reduction produces. So Cesare, Camestres and Camenes reduce to Celarent; Darapti and the others beginning with D reduce to Darii; Fresison reduces to Ferio; and so on.

The letter k means that the mood must be reduced, or proved, by a separate process called Indirect reduction, or reductio ad impossibile, which we will come to shortly.

Now let us take a syllogism, say one in Camestres, and follow the directions for reducing it. Let the example be:

	All stars are self-luminous (1)
	No planets are self-luminous (2)
	Therefore no planets are stars (3)

The first s in Camestres tells us to convert the minor premise simply. The m tells us to change the order of the premises, and the final s tells us to convert the conclusion simply. When we have made all these changes, we get:

	No self-luminous bodies are planets, converse of (2)
	All stars are self-luminous (1)
	Therefore no stars are planets, converse of (3)

This, as we can check, is a syllogism in Celarent, which we could have known from the initial C of Camestres.

For another example, let us take Fesapo:

	No fixed stars are planets,
	All planets are round bodies;
	Therefore some round bodies are not fixed stars.

The directions in the name tell us to convert the major premise simply, and the minor premise by limitation. We then have this syllogism in Ferio:

	No planets are fixed stars,
	Some round bodies are planets;
	Therefore some round bodies are not fixed stars.

The reader will find it easy to apply the same process of conversion or transposition to the other moods, following the directions in their names. The only moods we need to look at specially are Bramantip, Baroko and Bokardo. As an example of Bramantip we may take:

	All metals are material substances.
	All material substances are gravitating bodies;
	Therefore some gravitating bodies are metals.

The name contains the letter m, which tells us to transpose the premises, and the letter p, which means conversion by limitation. Making these changes, we have:

	All material substances are gravitating bodies,
	All metals are material substances;
	Therefore some metals are gravitating bodies.

This is not a syllogism in Barbara, as we might have expected. It is the weakened mood AAI of the first figure. Plainly these premises yield the conclusion "all metals are gravitating bodies," so in this mood we must take the letter p to mean that the conclusion is weaker than it could be. The truth is that the fourth figure is so imperfect and unnatural in form that Aristotle, the founder of logical science, never admitted that it existed at all. It contains nothing but badly arranged syllogisms, which would have been better stated in the first figure. It is a pity that such a needless addition was made to the forms of the syllogism, which are complicated enough already.

The two odd moods called Baroko and Bokardo cause a good deal of trouble, because they cannot be reduced directly to the first figure. To show how to handle them, we will let X, Y and Z stand for the major, middle and minor terms of the syllogism. Baroko can then be stated like this:

	All X's are Y's,
	Some Z's are not Y's;
	Therefore some Z's are not X's.

Now if we convert the major premise by contraposition (see Lesson 10), we get "all not-Y's are not X's." Making this the major premise of the syllogism, we have:

	All not-Y's are not X's,
	Some Z's are not-Y's;
	Therefore some Z's are not X's.

Both of these premises look negative, but this is really a valid syllogism in Ferio. Two of the negative particles only affect the middle term (see Lesson 15), and so we have carried out the reduction of the syllogism.

Stated in the same way, Bokardo is:

	Some Y's are not X's,
	All Y's are Z's;
	Therefore some Z's are not X's.

To reduce this, convert the major premise by negation, and then transpose the premises. We get:

	All Y's are Z's,
	Some not-X's are Y's;
	Therefore some not-X's are Z's.

This conclusion is the converse by negation of the original conclusion. So the truth of the original conclusion is proved by reducing the argument to a syllogism in Darii.

Both of these moods, Baroko and Bokardo, can also be proved by a special process of Indirect reduction, very much like the indirect proofs Euclid often uses in geometry. The process works like this. We suppose that the conclusion of the syllogism is false, and that its contradictory is therefore true. We can then easily build a new syllogism that leads to a conclusion contradicting one of the original premises. Now in logic it is absurd to call the truth of our own premises into question. The whole purpose of argument, or syllogism, is to draw a conclusion that will be true when the premises are true. The syllogism lets us restate, in a new form, the information already contained in the premises, just as a machine can hand back to us in a new form the material we put into it. The machine, or rather its maker, is not responsible for the quality of the materials fed into it. In the same way, the logician is not in the least responsible for the truth of his premises, only for handling them correctly. If he handles them at all, he must treat them as true. So a conclusion that requires one of our premises to be false is completely absurd.

To apply this method, we may take Baroko as before:

	All X's are Y's (1)
	Some Z's are not Y's (2)
	Therefore some Z's are not X's (3)

If this conclusion is not true, then its contradictory, "all Z's are X's," must necessarily be taken as true (see Lesson 9). Making this the minor premise of a new syllogism, with the original major premise, we have:

	All X's are Y's (1)
	All Z's are X's, contradictory of (3)
	Hence all Z's are Y's.

Now this conclusion, in A, is the contradictory of our old minor premise, in O. So we must either admit that one of our own premises is false or grant that our original conclusion is true. Of course the second is the one we choose.

We treat Bokardo in a very similar way:

	Some Y's are not X's (1)
	All Y's are Z's (2)
	Therefore some Z's are not X's (3)

If this conclusion is not true, then "all Z's are X's" must be true. Now we can make the syllogism:

	All Z's are X's, contradictory of (3)
	All Y's are Z's (2)
	Hence all Y's are X's.

This conclusion is the contradictory of (1), the original major premise. Since that cannot be allowed, we must either suppose that (2), the original minor premise, is false, which is just as impossible, or grant that our original conclusion is true.

Notice that in both of these cases of Indirect Reduction, or Indirect Proof, we use a syllogism in Barbara, and that is what the initial letters of Baroko and Bokardo indicate. The same process of indirect proof can be applied to any of the other moods, but it is not usual to do so, because the simpler process of direct reduction, often called ostensive reduction, is enough.

You will remember that when we looked at the rules of the syllogism toward the end of Lesson 15, there were two supplementary rules, the 7th and 8th, about particular premises. These were not at all self-evident, and they need to be proved from the six more fundamental rules. We have now come far enough to look at this proof with profit. The 7th rule forbids us to draw any conclusion from two particular premises. Two such premises must be II, IO, OI or OO. Of these, II contains no distributed term at all, so it must break the 3rd rule, which requires the middle term to be distributed. The premises OO plainly break the 5th rule, against negative premises. With the pair IO, the conclusion must be negative by the 6th rule, because one premise is negative. The major term will therefore be distributed in the conclusion. But the major premise is a particular affirmative, so the major term cannot be distributed there, and drawing the conclusion would commit the fallacy of illicit process of the major, against rule 4. Lastly, the premises OI contain only one distributed term, the predicate of the major premise. But since the conclusion must be negative by the 6th rule, the major term must be distributed. So we would need two distributed terms in the premises, one for the middle term and the other for the major term. The premises contain only one, so if we try to draw any conclusion at all, we must commit either the fallacy of undistributed middle or that of illicit process of the major term. We see, then, that in no possible case can a pair of particular premises give a valid conclusion.

The 8th rule of the syllogism tells us that if one premise of a syllogism is particular, the conclusion must also be particular. The only way to show this is true is to go through all the possible cases and observe that the six principal rules of the syllogism always require the conclusion to be particular. Suppose, for instance, the premises are A and I. Then they contain only one distributed term, the subject of A, and rule 3 requires it for the middle term. So the minor term cannot be distributed in the conclusion without breaking rule 4, and the conclusion must be the proposition I. The premises AO would contain two distributed terms, the subject of A and the predicate of O. But if we drew the conclusion E from them, both the major and the minor terms would have to be distributed, and the middle term would be left undistributed, against rule 3. The reader can easily prove the other cases, such as EI, by counting the distributed terms in the same way. It will always turn out that the premises do not distribute enough terms to allow a universal conclusion.

Questions and Exercises

1. State and explain the mnemonic lines Barbara, Celarent, and so on.

2. Construct syllogisms in each of the following moods, taking X, Y and Z for the major, middle and minor terms respectively, and show how to reduce them to the first figure:

Cesare, Festino, Darapti, Datisi, Ferison, Camenes, Fesapo.

3. What is the use of Reduction?

4. Prove that the following premises cannot give a universal conclusion: EI, IA, OA, IE.

5. Prove that the third figure must have an affirmative minor premise and a particular conclusion.

6. Reduce the moods Cesare and Camenes by the Indirect method, or Reductio ad Impossibile.
