Lesson 28: Methods of Induction

We now have to look at the methods that can be laid down to guide us when we search for general truths, or laws of nature, among the facts we have gathered by observation and experiment. Induction means inferring from particulars to generals: spotting a general truth among the particular cases in which it shows up. In physical science, though, the truths to be discovered are usually about the connection of cause and effect, and we generally call them laws of causation or natural laws. By the Cause of an event we mean the circumstances that must have come before it in order for the event to happen. And it is usually impossible to say that an event has one single cause and no more. Producing an effect normally takes many different things, conditions or circumstances, and all of them must be counted as causes, or as necessary parts of the cause. Take the loud bang of a gun going off. Its cause is not simply the pulling of the trigger, which is only the last visible cause, or the occasion, of the explosion. The qualities of the powder, the right shape of the barrel, a charge in the barrel to resist the blast, the proper arrangement of the percussion cap and the powder, and the air surrounding the gun are all among the circumstances needed for that loud report. If any one of them had been missing, it would not have happened.

Again, the cause of water boiling is not just heating it up to a certain temperature. The vapor must also be able to escape once it reaches a certain pressure. In the same way, the freezing of water does not depend merely on drawing off heat until the temperature falls below 0° Centigrade (Celsius). So the work of Induction is to detect the circumstances that will always produce a given effect. As soon as we know them, we have a law, or uniformity of nature, of greater or less generality.

In this lesson and the ones that follow I will often need, besides cause and effect, the words antecedent and consequent, and the reader should notice what they mean. An antecedent is any thing, condition or circumstance that exists before an event or phenomenon, or possibly at the same time as it. A consequent is any thing, circumstance, event or phenomenon that is different from all the antecedents and follows after they have come together. An antecedent is not necessarily a cause, because the effect might have happened without it. The sunlight, for example, may be an antecedent of a house burning down, but it is not the cause, because the house would burn just as well at night. A necessary, or indispensable, antecedent, however, is the same thing as a cause: it is that without which the effect would not happen.

I will also often use the word phenomenon. It simply means anything that appears, and so is observed by the senses. The word comes from the Greek φαινόμενον (phainomenon), "that which appears," and this origin matches its use in logic exactly.

The first method of Induction is the one Mr. J. S. Mill has aptly called the Method of Agreement. It rests on this rule: "If two or more instances of the phenomenon under investigation have only one circumstance in common, the circumstance in which alone all the instances agree, is the cause (or effect) of the given phenomenon." I think the meaning of this First Canon of inductive inquiry could be put more briefly: the sole invariable antecedent of a phenomenon is probably its cause.

To apply this method, we collect as many instances of the phenomenon as we can and compare their antecedents. The causes will be somewhere among them. But if we notice that certain antecedents are sometimes present and sometimes absent without seeming to affect the result, we conclude that they cannot be necessary antecedents. So it is the one antecedent, or group of antecedents, that is always present when the effect follows that we take to be the cause. Here is an example. Bright rainbow colors appear on bubbles, on films of tar floating on water, on thin sheets of mica, on cracks in glass, and between two pieces of glass pressed together. When we examine all these cases, they seem to agree in nothing except the presence of a very thin layer or plate, and it seems to make no noticeable difference what kind of matter the plate is made of, whether solid, liquid or gas. So we conclude that these colors are caused simply by the thinness of the plates, and the theory of the interference of light proves this conclusion true. Sir David Brewster gave a beautiful proof, along the same lines, that the colors seen on mother-of-pearl are caused not by the nature of the substance but by the shape of its surface. He took impressions of mother-of-pearl in wax and found that, although the substance was now entirely different, the colors were exactly the same. It was later found that a metal plate whose surface was marked with very fine, close-set grooves would show iridescent colors like those of mother-of-pearl. So it is clear that the shape of the surface, the only invariable antecedent or condition needed to produce the colors, must be their cause.

The Method of Agreement runs into a serious difficulty, which Mill calls the Plurality of Causes: the same effect may be due to different causes in different instances. If we look carefully into the cause of heat, for example, we find it is produced by friction, by burning or combustion, by electricity, by pressure and so on. So even if one and the same thing happened to be present in every case we examined, it would not follow that this thing was the cause. The second method of induction, which we will look at now, is free from this difficulty. It is known as the Method of Difference, and Mill's Second Canon states it like this:

"If an instance in which the phenomenon under investigation occurs, and an instance in which it does not occur, have every circumstance in common save one, that one occurring only in the former; the circumstance in which alone the two instances differ, is the effect, or the cause, or an indispensable part of the cause, of the phenomenon."

In other words: the antecedent that is always present when the phenomenon follows, and always absent when the phenomenon is absent, with the other circumstances staying the same, is the cause of the phenomenon in those circumstances.

This lets us prove clearly that friction is one cause of heat. When two sticks are rubbed together they get hot; when they are not rubbed, they do not. Sir Humphry Davy showed that even two pieces of ice rubbed together in a vacuum produce heat, as their melting shows, and so he completely demonstrated that the friction is the source and cause of the heat. We prove that air is what carries sound to our ears by striking a bell inside the receiver (the glass jar) of an air pump, as Hawksbee first did in 1705. When the receiver is full of air we hear the bell; when it holds little or no air we do not. We learn that sodium, or any of its compounds, produces a spectrum with a bright yellow double line by noticing two things: there is no such line in the spectrum of a light when no sodium is present, but if the smallest quantity of sodium is thrown into the flame or other source of light, the bright yellow line appears instantly. Oxygen is the cause of breathing and life, because if an animal is put into a jar of ordinary air from which the oxygen has been removed, it soon suffocates.

This is essentially the great method of experiment. How useful it is depends mainly on one precaution: varying only one circumstance at a time and keeping all the others exactly as they were. Thomson and Tait put this into one of the rules for conducting experiments in their great treatise on Natural Philosophy, Vol. I. p. 307:

"In all cases when a particular agent or cause is to be studied, experiments should be arranged in such a way as to lead if possible to results depending on it alone; or, if this cannot be done, they should be arranged so as to increase the effects due to the cause to be studied till these so far exceed the unavoidable concomitants, that the latter may be considered as only disturbing, not essentially modifying the effects of the principal agent."

Here is what an imperfect and unsatisfactory experiment would look like. Suppose we took air whose oxygen had been turned into carbonic acid (carbon dioxide) by burning carbon in it, and argued that, because an animal dies in that air, oxygen is the cause of breathing. Instead of simply removing the oxygen, we have brought in a new substance, carbonic acid, which is perfectly capable of killing the animal by its own poisonous properties. In fact the animal would suffocate even if a good deal of oxygen were left. So the presence of the carbonic acid is a disturbing circumstance that confuses the experiment and spoils it.

We can prove that the force of gravity exists, and even measure how strong it is, by delicately hanging up a small ball about the size of a marble and then suddenly bringing a very heavy lead ball, weighing a ton or more, close to it. The small ball will be attracted and set moving. But the experiment would be worthless unless it were done with the greatest care. Obviously, moving the big lead ball suddenly would disturb the air, shake the room, set up air currents by being colder or warmer than the air around it, and even cause electrical attractions or repulsions. All of these would probably disturb the small ball far more than the force of gravitation does.

As Sir John Herschel has pointed out, beautiful examples of experiment by this method can be found in the research by which Dr. Wells discovered the cause of dew. On a clear, calm night, stretch a sheet or some other covering a foot or two above the ground, so that it screens the ground beneath it from the open sky. Dew will form on the grass around the screen but not underneath it. Since the temperature, the moisture of the air and the other circumstances are exactly the same, the open sky must be an indispensable antecedent of dew. Nature in fact tries the same experiment for us. If we observe the dew on two nights that differ in nothing except that one is cloudless and the other cloudy, we will find that a clear open sky is needed for dew to form.

Often we cannot apply the Method of Difference perfectly, by varying only one circumstance at a time. For example, we generally cannot test the qualities of the same substance as a solid and as a liquid without any other change in the circumstances, because to melt it or freeze it we have to change its temperature. The temperature might then be the cause of what we put down to the substance being liquid or solid. In cases like this we have to fall back on what Mill calls the Joint Method of Agreement and Difference. It consists of applying the Method of Agreement twice: first to a number of instances where an effect is produced, and second to a number of quite different instances where the effect is not produced. It must be clearly understood, though, that the negative instances differ from the positive ones in several circumstances. If they differed in only one, we could use the simple Method of Difference. Iceland spar, for instance, has the curious power of making things seen through it look double. This phenomenon, called double refraction, belongs to many other crystals as well. We could prove at once that it is due to crystalline structure if we could get some transparent substance in both a crystallized and an uncrystallized state, with no other change at all. We can't, but we do have a fairly satisfactory proof. We observe that uniform, transparent, uncrystallized substances agree in not having double refraction, and that crystalline substances, on the other hand, agree in having that power, apart from certain exceptions that are easy to explain. The principle of the Joint Method can be stated in the following rule, which is Mill's Third Canon:

"If two or more instances in which the phenomenon occurs have only one circumstance in common, while two or more instances in which it does not occur have nothing in common save the absence of that circumstance; the circumstance in which alone the two sets of instances (always or invariably) differ, is the effect, or the cause, or an indispensable part of the cause, of the phenomenon."

I have put in the words in parentheses myself, because without them the canon seems to me to say exactly the opposite of what Mill means.

It may help in understanding these inductive methods exactly if I give a symbolic picture of them, in the way Mill does. Let A, B, C, D, E and so on be antecedents that can be combined in various ways, and let a, b, c, d, e and so on be the effects that follow from them. Suppose we can collect the following sets of antecedents and effects:

	Antecedents. | Consequents.
	ABC | abc
	ADE | ade
	AFG | afg
	AHK | ahk
	...... | ......
	...... | ......

Then we can apply the Method of Agreement, and little doubt will remain that A, the sole invariable antecedent, is the cause of a.

The Method of Difference is represented well enough by this:

	Antecedents. | Consequents.
	ABC | abc
	BC | bc

Here B and C stay completely unchanged, and we find that the presence or absence of A brings about the presence or absence of a. So A is the cause of a when B and C are present. But the reader should be warned against thinking that this proves A is the cause of a under every circumstance whatever.

The Joint Method of Agreement and Difference is represented in the same way:

	Antecedents. | Consequents.
	ABC | abc
	ADE | ade
	AFG | afg
	AHK | ahk
	...... | ......
	PQ | pq
	RS | rs
	TV | tv
	XY | xy
	...... | ......

Here the presence of A is followed by a, as in the simple Method of Agreement, and the absence of A, in circumstances different from the earlier ones, is followed by the absence of a. So it is very highly probable that A is the cause of a. But it is easy to see that A is not the only circumstance in which the two sets of instances differ; if it were, we could apply the simple Method of Difference to any pair of them. What we can say is that the presence of A is a circumstance in which one set invariably, or uniformly, or always, differs from the other set. So the Joint Method stands in for the simpler Method of Difference in cases where that method cannot properly be put to work.
