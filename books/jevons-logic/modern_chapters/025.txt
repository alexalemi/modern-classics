Lesson 25: Perfect Induction and the Inductive Syllogism

In the earlier lessons we have been looking at deductive reasoning. Deduction combines two or more propositions synthetically and arrives at a conclusion that is less general than the premises: a proposition or truth that applies to fewer individual instances than the separate premises it was inferred from. Take the general truth that "metals are good conductors of heat," and combine it with the truth that "aluminum is a metal." A syllogism in the mood Barbara then lets me infer that "aluminum is a good conductor of heat." This proposition is about one metal only, so it is plainly less general than the premise, which was about every metal there is. Induction goes the other way. In induction we move from less general facts, or even from individual facts, to more general propositions or truths, or, as we will often call them, Laws of Nature. Once we know that Mercury moves around the sun in an elliptical orbit, and that Venus, the Earth, Mars, Jupiter and the rest do too, we can arrive at the simple, general truth that "all the planets move in elliptical orbits around the sun." That is an example of inductive reasoning.

It is true that we can also reason without making our conclusion either more or less general than the premises. For example:

	Snowdon is the highest mountain in England or Wales.
	Snowdon is not so high as Ben Nevis.
	Therefore the highest mountain in England or Wales is not so high as Ben Nevis.

Or again:

	Lithium is the lightest metal known.
	Lithium is the metal indicated by one bright red line in the spectrum.
	Therefore the lightest metal known is the metal indicated by a spectrum of one bright red line.

Footnote: Roscoe's Lessons in Elementary Chemistry.

In these examples every proposition is a singular proposition. Each one simply asserts that two singular terms are identical, so the generality never changes. Each conclusion applies to exactly the same sort of object as each premise does. This kind of reasoning has been given the apt name of traduction.

Induction is a much harder and much more important kind of reasoning than traduction, or even deduction. Its work is to discover the general laws or uniformities, the relations of cause and effect, in short all the general truths we can assert about the countless and very varied events that happen in the natural world around us. Most of our knowledge, and perhaps all of it, as some philosophers think, comes in the end from inductive reasoning. The mind, people plausibly say, does not come supplied with knowledge already made up into general propositions and stamped on it. Instead it is given powers of observation, comparison and reasoning. When these powers are well trained and exercised, they are enough to get us knowledge of the world outside us and of the world inside the human mind. Even when we argue synthetically and deductively from simple ideas and truths that seem to be ready-made in the mind, as we do in geometry, we may in fact have gathered those simple ideas and truths earlier, by observation or by a kind of induction we were hardly aware of. This is a disputed point, and I will not take a firm position on it here. But if it is true, then induction is the way all the materials of knowledge are brought into the mind and analyzed. Deduction will then be the almost equally important process by which we put that knowledge to use, and by which, as we will see, new and more complicated inductions become possible.

An induction, that is, one act of inductive reasoning, is called perfect when all the possible cases or instances the conclusion can refer to have been examined and listed in the premises. Usually it is impossible to examine every case, since some may occur in the future, or in distant parts of the earth, or in other regions of the universe. Then the induction is called imperfect. The statement that every month of the year is shorter than thirty-two days comes from perfect induction, and it is a certain conclusion. The calendar is a human institution, so we know beyond doubt how many months there are, and we can easily check that each of them is shorter than thirty-two days. But the statement that all the planets move around the sun in one direction, from west to east, comes from imperfect induction. There may be planets farther out than Neptune, the most distant planet known in the 1870s, and the statement would of course apply to such a planet too.

So there is obviously a great difference between perfect and imperfect induction. Imperfect induction includes some process that lets us make statements about things we have never seen or examined, or even known to exist. But we must also be careful to remember that no imperfect induction can give a certain conclusion. It may be highly probable, or nearly certain, that the unexamined cases will be like the ones we have examined, but it can never be certain. It is quite possible, for instance, that a newly found planet might go around the sun in the opposite direction from the others. Among the satellites of the planets, more than one exception of this kind has already been discovered, and science has made mistakes again and again by expecting every new case to be exactly like the old ones. Imperfect induction, then, gives only a certain degree of probability, or likelihood, that all the instances will agree with the ones examined. Perfect induction, on the other hand, gives a necessary and certain conclusion, but it asserts nothing beyond what the premises already asserted.

Mr. J. S. Mill, in fact, disagrees with almost every other logician here. He holds that perfect induction is wrongly called induction at all, because it does not lead to any new knowledge. He defines induction as inference from the known to the unknown, and he counts the unexamined cases that seem to be brought into our knowledge as the only thing the reasoning gains. So perfect induction seems to him to have no scientific value whatever, because its conclusion merely says the premises over again in a shorter form, a mere summing up. I would point out, though, that even if perfect induction were nothing more than a way of abbreviating, it would still be very important, and we need it constantly in science and in everyday life. Without it we could never make a comprehensive statement at all. We would have to list every particular case. Suppose we examined the books in a library and found that they were all English books. Without perfect induction we could not sum up our results in the single proposition "all the books in this library are English books." We would have to go through the list of books every time we wanted to tell anyone what the library held. The truth is that the power to express a great number of particular facts in a very small space is essential to the progress of science. The whole art of arithmetic is nothing but a set of processes for shortening addition and subtraction, so that we can handle a great number of units in a very short time. In just the same way, perfect induction is absolutely necessary if we are to handle a great number of particular facts in a very small space.

Perfect induction is usually set out in the form of an inductive syllogism, as in this example:

	Mercury, Venus, the Earth, and so on, all move around the sun from west to east.
	Mercury, Venus, the Earth, and so on, are all the known planets.
	Therefore all the known planets move around the sun from west to east.

This argument is a true perfect induction. The conclusion makes a statement only about all the known planets, which shuts out any reference to planets that might be discovered later, and we may suppose that all the known planets have been listed in the premises. The argument looks like a syllogism in the third figure, namely Darapti, with the group of known planets as the middle term. In reality, though, it is not an ordinary syllogism. The minor premise does not say that Mercury, Venus, the Earth, Neptune and so on are contained among the known planets. It says that they are those planets, that they are identical with them. So this premise is a doubly universal proposition, of a kind (see Lesson 22) that the Aristotelian syllogism does not recognize. That is why the conclusion here is a universal proposition, something not allowed in the third figure of the syllogism.

As another example of perfect induction, we can take this:

	January, February, ... December, each contain less than 32 days.
	January ... December are all the months of the year.
	Therefore all the months of the year contain less than 32 days.

Sir William Hamilton rejected the idea completely, but it seems worth asking whether the inductive syllogism is not really a disjunctive syllogism. I would be inclined to set out the last example in this form:

A month of the year is either January, or February, or March, ... or December. But January has less than 32 days; and February has less than 32 days; and so on until we come to December, which has less than 32 days.

It clearly follows that a month, whichever it is, must have less than 32 days. There are only twelve possible cases, and in each one this is asserted. The fact is that the major premise of the syllogism above is a compound sentence with twelve subjects, so it is equivalent to twelve separate logical propositions. The minor premise is either a disjunctive proposition, as I have set it out, or something quite different from anything we have met elsewhere.

From perfect induction we will have to go on to imperfect induction. But logicians do not agree about what entitles us to take only some of the instances and conclude that what is true of those is true of all. Suppose we take the example found in many books and say:

	This, that, and the other magnet attract iron;
	This, that, and the other magnet are all magnets;
	Therefore all magnets attract iron.

Here we are obviously using a false minor premise, because this, that and the other magnet that we examined cannot possibly be all the magnets in existence. However we put it, there has to be an assumption that the magnets we examined are a fair sample of all magnets, so that what we find in some we may expect in all. Archbishop Whately thinks this assumption ought to be stated in one of the premises, and he sets out induction as a syllogism in Barbara, like this:

	That which belongs to this, that, and the other magnet, belongs to all;
	Attracting iron belongs to this, that, and the other;
	Therefore it belongs to all.

This is no doubt a correct statement of the assumption made in an imperfect induction. But it does not explain in the least what entitles us to make the assumption, or in what circumstances such an assumption is likely to turn out true. Some writers have claimed that there is a principle called the Uniformity of Nature, which lets us affirm that what has often been found true of anything will go on being found true of the same sort of thing. We must notice, though, that if there is such a principle, it has exceptions. Many facts that held true up to a certain point have later turned out not to be always true. For example, there was a wide and unbroken induction that seemed to show that all the satellites in the planetary system went around their planets in one and the same direction. Yet when the satellites of Uranus were discovered, they were found to move in a retrograde direction, that is, in the opposite direction from every satellite known before. The satellite of Neptune, discovered more recently (the only one known in the 1870s), has the same peculiarity.

We can leave to the next lesson the question of how the degree of certainty that induction gives varies from one branch of knowledge to another.

Questions and Exercises

1. Define deduction, induction and traduction, and distinguish them from one another.

2. Find an example of reasoning by traduction.

3. Distinguish perfect and imperfect induction.

4. How does Mr. Mill define induction, and what does he think of perfect induction?

5. What is the use of perfect induction?

6. Construct some examples of the inductive syllogism, and show that they can be put into a disjunctive form.
