Lesson 8: Kinds of Propositions

A term standing alone cannot express a truth. All it does is direct the mind to some object, or class of objects, about which something may be affirmed or denied; the term itself affirms or denies nothing. "Sun," "air," "table" each call up an object of thought in every mind, but we cannot say that "sun is true," or "air is mistaken," or "table is false." Before words or terms can express the reasoning acts of the mind that we call true or false, we have to join them into sentences, or propositions. "The sun is bright," "the air is fresh," "the table is unsteady" are statements that may be true or may be false, and in any circumstances we can at least ask whether they are true. Now, just as the logical term was defined as any combination of words expressing an act of simple apprehension, a logical proposition is any combination of words expressing an act of judgment. In short, the proposition is the result of an act of judgment put into the form of language.

What the logician calls a proposition, the grammarian calls a sentence. But although every proposition is a sentence, we must not suppose that every sentence is a proposition. There are in fact several kinds of sentence that are more or less distinct from a proposition: the Sentence Interrogative, or Question; the Sentence Imperative, or Command; the Sentence Optative, which expresses a wish; and the Exclamatory Sentence, which expresses an emotion of wonder or surprise. These kinds of sentence can perhaps be turned, by a more or less roundabout way of speaking, into the form of a Sentence Indicative, which is the grammatical name for a proposition. But until that has been done they have no proper place in logic, or at least no place that logicians have so far explained well enough.

The name proposition comes from the Latin words pro, before, and pono, I place. It means laying or placing before someone the result of an act of judgment. Every act of judgment, or comparison, must involve the two things being compared. So every proposition will naturally have three parts: the two terms, or names, standing for the things compared, and the copula, or verb, showing the connection between them as it was found in the act of judgment. Take the proposition "Gold is a yellow substance." It expresses an agreement in color between gold and certain other substances already called yellow. Gold and yellow substance are clearly the two terms, and is is the copula.

The first term of a proposition is always called the subject, because it stands for the underlying matter, so to speak, about which something is asserted (from the Latin sub, under, and jactum, laid). The second term is called the predicate, which simply means what is affirmed or asserted. This name comes from the Latin prædicare, to assert, which also gave French its word prédicateur, worn down in English into our "preacher." Don't confuse this Latin verb with the rather similar prædicere, which has an entirely different meaning: to predict, or foretell. I strongly suspect that newspaper writers and others who pedantically use the verb "to predicate" sometimes fall into this confusion and really mean to predict. In any case, a purely technical term like predicate should not be dragged into ordinary language without need, when there are so many other good words that could be used instead. This and every other technical term of science should be kept to its proper scientific use. Neglecting that rule damages the language of everyday life and the language of science at the same time.

Propositions fall into two kinds, depending on whether they make their statement conditionally or unconditionally. The proposition "If metals are heated they are softened" is conditional. It does not make an assertion about metals in general, only about metals in the circumstance of being heated. Any circumstance that has to be granted or supposed before the assertion applies is a condition. Conditional propositions are of two kinds, Hypothetical and Disjunctive, but it is best to leave them for a later lesson (Lesson 19). For some time to come we shall be concerned only with unconditional propositions. These are usually called Categorical Propositions, from the Greek verb κατηγορέω (kategoreo, to assert or affirm).

Now we need to look carefully at the different kinds of categorical propositions. They are classified according to quality and according to quantity. As regards quality they are either affirmative or negative; as regards quantity they are either universal or particular.

An affirmative proposition is one that asserts a certain agreement between the subject and the predicate, so that the qualities or attributes of the predicate belong to the subject. The proposition "gold is a yellow substance" states that gold agrees with other yellow substances in such a way that we know it has the color yellow, along with whatever qualities are implied in the name substance. A negative proposition, on the other hand, asserts a difference or disagreement, so that some at least of the qualities of the predicate do not belong to the subject. "Gold is not easily fusible" denies that the quality of being easily melted belongs to gold.

Propositions are also divided according to quantity into universal and particular propositions. If the proposition affirms that the predicate belongs to the whole of the subject, it is a universal proposition. An example is "all metals are elements," which affirms that the quality of being undecomposable, or simple in nature, is true of all metals. But if we say "some metals are brittle," the quality of brittleness is affirmed only of some indefinite portion of the metals, and nothing in the proposition lets us be sure that any particular metal you might name is brittle. The name particular comes from the diminutive of the Latin pars, a part, so you would expect it to mean a small part. In logic, though, it must be carefully understood to mean any part at all, from the smallest fraction up to nearly the whole. Particular propositions do not include cases where a predicate is affirmed of the whole of the subject, or of none of it, but they include every case between those limits. So we can count all propositions like the following as particular:

A very few metals are less dense than water.

Most elements are metals.

Many of the planets are comparatively small bodies.

Not a few distinguished men have had distinguished sons.

The reader must pay careful attention to a rather subtle point, explained further on: a particular proposition, although it asserts the predicate only of a part of the subject, does not deny that it is true of the whole.

Aristotle, in fact, held that there were four kinds of proposition altogether as regards quantity, namely:

	Proposition:
	    Universal.
	    Particular.
	    Singular.
	    Indefinite.

A singular proposition is one that has a singular term for its subject, as in:

	Socrates was very wise.
	London is a vast city.

But we can fairly treat a singular proposition as a universal one, because it clearly refers to the whole of its subject, which in this case is one single individual thing.

Indefinite, or indesignate, propositions are those that carry no mark of quantity at all, so that the wording gives us no way of judging whether the predicate applies to the whole of the subject or only to part of it. "Metals are useful" and "comets are subject to the law of gravitation" are indefinite propositions. In reality, however, such propositions have no distinct place in logic at all, and the logician cannot properly deal with them until their true and precise meaning has been made clear. The predicate must be true either of the whole of the subject or of part of it, so the proposition as it stands is plainly incomplete. But if we try to fix this by supplying the marks of quantity ourselves, we step outside the proper boundaries of logic, because we are assuming that we know the subject matter, or the science, that the proposition is about. We can safely take the examples above to mean "some metals are useful" and "all comets are subject to the law of gravitation," but not on logical grounds. So we may strike the class of indefinite propositions out of logic altogether, on the understanding that they must be made definite before we deal with them. I should point out, though, that in the lessons that follow I shall often use propositions in the indefinite form as examples, on the understanding that where no sign of quantity appears, the universal quantity is to be assumed. Probably, wherever a term is used on its own, it ought to be taken to mean the whole of its class. But however that may be, we need not recognize the indefinite proposition as a separate kind. And since singular propositions have been resolved into universals, only two kinds remain: Universal and Particular.

The vowels placed on the right are symbols, or shorthand names, that are always used for the four kinds of proposition. Their meaning is easy to remember once we notice that A and I occur in the Latin verb affirmo, I affirm, and E and O in nego, I deny.

There will usually be no difficulty in putting a proposition we meet in our reading into its proper class. The mark of universality is usually some adjective of quantity, such as all, every, each, any, none; but whenever the predicate is clearly meant to apply to the whole of the subject, we may treat the proposition as universal. The signs of a particular proposition are the adjectives of quantity some, certain, a few, many, most, or any others that clearly indicate part at least.

A negative proposition is recognized by the adverbial particle not joined to the copula. But in the proposition E, that is, the universal negative, we often put the particle no or none in front of the subject instead. So "no metals are compound" and "none of the ancients were acquainted with the laws of motion" are familiar forms of the universal negative.

The student must also always be ready to meet misleading or ambiguous ways of putting things. The proposition "all the metals are not denser than water," for instance, might be taken as E or as O, depending on whether we read it as "no metals are denser than water" or as "not all the metals are denser than water," and the second, of course, is the true sense. The little adjective few is especially liable to a subtle ambiguity of this kind. If I say "few books are at once learned and amusing," I may fairly be taken to assert that a few books certainly are both; but what I really mean to draw attention to is my belief that "the greater number of books are not at once learned and amusing." A proposition of this kind should generally be classed as O rather than I. The word some suffers from exactly the same sort of ambiguity: it can mean "some but not all," or "some at least, and perhaps all." The second appears to be the correct interpretation, as the next lesson shows (see the table in Lesson 9 of how the truth of each proposition bears on the others).

As we meet propositions in ordinary language, they come with various inversions and other departures from the simple logical form.

(1) It is quite common, especially in poetry, to find the predicate placed first, for emphasis or variety, as in "Blessed are the merciful," "Comes something down with eventide," "Great is Diana of the Ephesians." Such an inversion of the terms is usually easy to spot, and the sentence must then be put back into the regular order before logic can deal with it.

(2) The subject can sometimes be mistaken for the predicate when it is described by a relative clause standing at the end of the sentence, as in "no one is free who is enslaved by his appetites." Here free is clearly the predicate, although it stands in the middle of the sentence, and "one who is enslaved by his appetites" is the real subject. This proposition is clearly of the form E.

Propositions are also expressed in various ways that differ from the simple logical order, and we must notice some of the kinds that result.

Exclusive propositions contain words such as only, alone, none but, which limit the predicate to the subject. In "elements alone are metals," we are told that the predicate "metal" cannot be applied to anything except "elements," but we are not to understand that "all elements are metals." The same meaning is expressed by "none but elements are metals," or again by "all that are not elements are not metals," and we shall see in the next lesson that this is really equivalent to "all metals are elements." Arguments that look fallacious at first sight will often turn out to be correct when they contain exclusive propositions and these are properly interpreted.

Exceptive propositions affirm a predicate of all the subject except certain stated cases, to which, by implication, the predicate does not belong. "All the planets, except Venus and Mercury, are beyond the earth's orbit" is clearly a proposition equivalent to two: that Venus and Mercury are not beyond the earth's orbit, and that the rest are. If the exceptions are not actually named, an exceptive proposition must often be treated as a particular one. Suppose I say "all the planets in our system except one agree with Bode's law" and do not name that one exception. Then the reader cannot, on the strength of the proposition, positively assert of any planet that it does agree with Bode's law.

Some propositions are distinguished as explicative, or essential, because they simply affirm of their subject a predicate that everyone who can define the subject knows belongs to it. Such propositions only unfold what is already contained in the subject. "A parallelogram has four sides and four angles" is an explicative, or essential, proposition. "London, which is the capital of England, is the largest city of Europe" contains two propositions, and one of them only directs our attention to a fact everyone may be supposed to know, namely that London is the capital of England.

Ampliative propositions, on the other hand, join a new predicate to the subject. So, for anyone who does not know how the cities of Europe compare in size, the last example contains an ampliative proposition. Most propositions are of this kind.

Tautologous, or Truistic, propositions are those that simply affirm the subject of itself and give no information whatever, as in "whatever is, is," or "what I have written, I have written."

It is no part of formal logic to teach us how to interpret the meanings of sentences as we meet them in our reading; that is more the work of the grammarian and the philologist. Logic deals with the relations between different propositions and the inferences that can be drawn from them. Even so, it is a good idea for the reader to get some familiarity with the real logical meaning of conventional or peculiar forms of expression, and a number of examples will be found in the Examples of Propositions at the end of Lesson 10, which the reader is asked to classify and treat as directed.

Besides the distinctions already noticed, it has long been usual to distinguish propositions as pure or modal. The pure proposition simply asserts that the predicate does or does not belong to the subject. The modal proposition asserts this cum modo, that is, "with a mode": with some indication of the mode or manner in which the predicate belongs to the subject. Any adverb of time, place, manner, degree and so on, or any expression equivalent to an adverb, makes a proposition modal. "Error is always in haste," "justice is ever equal," "a perfect man ought always to be conquering himself" are examples of modal propositions in this sense of the name. Other logicians, however, have taken a different view, and treat modality as the degree of certainty or probability with which a judgment is made and asserted. So we may say "an equilateral triangle is necessarily equiangular," "men are generally trustworthy," "a falling barometer probably indicates a coming storm," "Aristotle's lost treatises may possibly be recovered," and each of these assertions is made with a different degree of certainty, or modality. Dr. Thomson is no doubt right in holding that modality does not affect the copula of the proposition, and that the subject could only be properly dealt with in a work on Probable Reasoning.

Many logicians have also divided propositions into true and false, and this might well seem an important distinction. All the same, it lies wholly outside the logician's province to consider whether a proposition is true or not in itself. All the logician has to determine is the comparative truth of propositions, that is, whether one proposition is true when another is. Strictly speaking, logic has nothing to do with a proposition on its own. The work of reasoning consists only in converting or transforming certain propositions into certain others, and the truth of the conclusion is in question only insofar as it follows from the truth of what we shall call the premises. It is the duty of each special science, in its own sphere, to decide which propositions are true and which are false. If logic took that duty on itself, it would be just another name for the whole of knowledge.
