A Vocabulary of Logical Terms

Abscissio Infiniti (the cutting off of the infinite or negative part), the process by which we find the place of an object in a system of classes, by comparing it with one class after another and rejecting each class it does not belong to.

Accidental definition is a definition that gives the properties of a species, or the accidents of an individual; it is more commonly called a Description.

Analogue, a thing that is analogous to some other thing.

Analytics (τὰ Ἀναλυτικά), the title given in the second century to parts of the Organon, the logical treatises of Aristotle; they were divided into the Prior and the Posterior Analytics.

Analytic syllogism, a syllogism in which the conclusion is put first and the premises follow as the reasons for it. See Synthetic syllogism; the distinction is unimportant.

Antinomy (ἀντί, against; νόμος, law), the opposition of one law or rule to another. Kant.

Argument (Latin, argus, from ἀργός, clear, manifest), the process of reasoning: showing or proving what is doubtful by means of what is known. See Inference (Lesson 10). The middle term of a syllogism is sometimes called the argument in a special sense.

Argumentum a fortiori, an argument in which we prove that the case in question is even stronger or more probable than one already admitted to be strong or probable enough.

Argumentum ad judicium, an appeal to the common sense of humankind.

Argumentum ad ignorantiam, an argument that rests on the ignorance of one's opponents.

Argumentum ad verecundiam, an appeal to our respect for some great authority.

Argumentum ex concesso, a proof drawn from a proposition the other side has already granted.

Assertion (ad, to; sero, to join), a statement or proposition, whether affirmative or negative.

Association of ideas (associo, to accompany; socius, a companion), the natural connection in the mind between impressions that have been together before, or that are similar. Any idea tends to bring its associated ideas into the mind, following the two great laws of association: the Law of Contiguity and the Law of Similarity.

Assumption (assumo, to take for granted), any proposition taken as the basis of an argument; in a special sense, the minor premise of a categorical syllogism.

Attribute (attribuo, to give or ascribe to), a quality or circumstance that can be affirmed (or denied) of a thing; the opposite of Substance, which see.

Belief, assent to a proposition. It can have any degree of strength, from the slightest probability to the fullest certainty; see Probability.

Categories, the summa genera, the widest classes into which things can be sorted. There are ten of them: Οὐσία, Substance; Ποσόν, Quantity; Ποιόν, Quality; Πρός τι, Relation; Ποιεῖν, Action; Πάσχειν, Passion, or being acted on; Ποῦ, Place; Πότε, Time; Κεῖσθαι, Position; Ἔχειν, Habit or condition. Everything that can be affirmed must fall under one or another of these highest predicates. Aristotle described them in the first treatise of his Organon, called the Categories.

Cause, Aristotle distinguished four kinds of cause for the existence of a thing: 1. The Material Cause, the substance or matter it is made of; 2. The Formal Cause, the pattern, type or design it is shaped by; 3. The Efficient Cause, the force used in shaping it; 4. The Final Cause, the end, motive or purpose of the work.

Chance, ignorance of the causes that are at work; see Probability.

Cognition (cognosco, to know), knowledge, or the action of the mind in acquiring knowledge.

Colligation of Facts, Dr. Whewell's expression for binding facts together in the mind by some suitable conception.

Comparison (com, together; par, equal or like), the action of the mind by which we judge whether two objects of thought are the same or different in certain respects. See Judgment (Lesson 2).

Compatible terms are terms that are distinct but not contradictory, and so can be affirmed of the same subject, such as "large" and "heavy," or "bright-colored" and "nauseous."

Composition of Causes, the principle shown in every case where the joint effect of several causes is identical with the sum of their separate effects. J. S. Mill.

Concept, that which is conceived, the result of the act of conception; nearly the same as general notion, idea, thought.

Conception (con, together; capio, to take). An ambiguous term. Properly it means the action of the mind in which it takes several things together so as to form a general notion; or else the action in which it forms "a mental image of the several attributes given in any word or combination of words." Mansel.

Conjugate words, words that come from the same root or stock, such as known, knowing, knowingly, knowledge.

Consciousness, the immediate knowledge the mind has of its sensations and thoughts, and, in general, of everything it is doing at present. Reid.

Consectary = Corollary.

Consequence, the connection between antecedent and consequent; but often used, ambiguously, for the consequent itself.

Consilience of Inductions, the agreement of inductions drawn from different and independent series of facts, as when we learn that the earth moves by entirely different kinds of observation and reasoning. Whewell.

Contingent (contingo, to touch), that which may or may not happen; the opposite of both the necessary and the impossible.

Continuity, Law of, the principle that nothing can pass from one extreme to another without passing through all the degrees in between; motion, for instance, cannot be produced or destroyed in an instant.

Corollary, a proposition that follows directly from another that has been proved.

Criterion (κριτήριον, from κρίνω, to judge), any fact, rule, knowledge or means needed for forming a judgment that will settle a doubtful question.

Data (plural of datum, that which is given), the facts or assertions from which an inference is to be drawn.

De facto, what actually or really happens: the opposite of de jure, what ought to happen by law or right.

Demonstration (demonstro, to point out), strictly, pointing out the connection between premises and conclusion. The term is more generally used for any argument or reasoning regarded as proving the conclusion it asserts. A demonstration is either Direct or Indirect. In an indirect demonstration we prove the conclusion by disproving its contradictory, that is, by showing that the conclusion cannot be supposed untrue.

Determination, the marking off of parts of a genus by joining the genus and a difference together again. See Division (Lesson 12).

Dialectic (διαλεκτικὴ τέχνη, the art of discourse, from διαλέγεσθαι, to discourse). The original name of Logic, perhaps invented by Plato. It has also been used to mean the Logic of Probable Matter (Aristotle), the right use of Reason and Language, and the Science of Being, so it is a highly ambiguous term.

Disbelief, the state of mind in which we are fully convinced that some opinion is not true. J. S. Mill. It amounts to belief in the contradictory opinion or assertion, and must not be confused with Doubt, which see.

Doubt (dubito, to go two ways), the state of mind in which we hesitate between two or more inconsistent opinions. See Disbelief.

Drift of a proposition, the different meanings the same sentence can be given depending on where the stress falls. See Fallacy of accent (Lesson 20).

Empiricism (ἐμπειρία, experience), the doctrine of those who hold that all knowledge comes merely from experience.

Essence (essentia, from esse, to be), "the very being of anything, whereby it is what it is." Locke. It is an old scholastic word that cannot really be defined, and it should be dropped from use.

Evidence (e, and videre, to see), literally the seeing of anything. The word now means any facts taken in by the mind and made the grounds of knowledge and belief.

Experimentum crucis, an experiment that decides between two rival theories and shows which one to adopt, just as a signpost (a "finger-post") shows which of two roads to take.

Exposita, a proposition given to be worked on by some logical process.

Extremes of a proposition are its ends or terms, the subject and the predicate.

Fundamentum relationis, the ground of a relation, that is, the series of events or circumstances that sets up a relation between two correlative terms.

Galenian or 4th figure of the syllogism; see Lesson 17.

Homologue, whatever is homologous.

Homology, a special term for the analogy between parts of different plants and animals, such as between the wing of a bird and the foreleg of a four-footed animal, or between the scales of a fish and the feathers of a bird.

Idea (ἰδέα, εἶδος, image), a term used ambiguously, but usually equivalent to thought, notion, concept. Locke defines it as "Phantasm, notion, species, or whatever it is which the mind can be employed about in thinking." To have an idea of a thing is to think of that thing.

Idol (εἴδωλον, εἶδος, image), Bacon's figurative name for the sources of error. He listed four kinds: Idols of the Tribe, which affect all people; Idols of the Cave, which belong to one individual; Idols of the Forum, which arise from people's dealings with one another; and Idols of the Theatre, which come from the systems of philosophers.

Illation (illatum, past participle of infero, to bring in). See Inference (Lesson 10).

Illative, that which can be inferred.

Inconsistent terms imply qualities that cannot exist together in the same thing. See Compatible terms.

Indefinite or infinite term is a negative term, which marks out an object only by shutting it out of a class.

Individual, that which cannot be divided without losing its name and distinctive qualities, although it can usually be physically divided, or partitioned; see Partition (Lesson 12).

Intention, first and second, a distinction between terms, defined by Hobbes like this:—"Of the first intention are the names of things, a man, stone, &c.; of the second are the names of names, and speeches, as universal, particular, genus, species, syllogism, and the like." A term of the second intention expresses the way the mind regards or classifies terms of the first intention.

Lemma (λαμβάνω, to take or assume), a proposition, a premise that is granted; in geometry, a preliminary proposition.

Matter is defined by J. S. Mill as "the external cause to which we ascribe our sensations," or as the Permanent Possibility of Sensation.

Membra dividentia, the parts into which a class is divided; the species that make up a genus.

Metaphysics (τὰ μετὰ τὰ Φυσικά), the works of Aristotle that came after his Physics, or were studied after it. Hence First Philosophy, the so-called science of things in their own nature; ontology, or the science of Being.

Method (μέθοδος, μετά and ὁδός, way), a mode, way or instrument for reaching an end.

Metonymy (μετά, and ὄνομα, name), the grammatical name for transferring the meaning of a word to a closely connected thing, as when we say the church and mean the people in it. See Transfer of meaning (Lesson 4).

Necessity (ne, not; and cesso, to cease), that which always is and cannot help but be.

Notion (nosco, to know), the action of grasping or taking note of the various qualities of an object; or, more commonly, the result of that action. See Idea, Concept.

Objective, that which belongs to the object of thought, the non-ego (the not-self); the opposite of Subjective, which see.

Obversion is the name Professor Alexander Bain uses for the process previously called Immediate Inference by Privative Conception (Lesson 10).

Organon (ὄργανον, Latin Organum, Instrument), a name for Aristotle's logical treatises, first in general use in the 15th century. It implies that they can be regarded as an instrument to help the mind. Bacon adopted the name for his Novum Organum.

Paradox (παρά, δόξα, contrary to opinion), an assertion that goes against common opinion, and which may or may not turn out to be true; often wrongly used to mean something self-contradictory and absurd.

Paralogism (παραλογίζομαι, to reason wrongly), a purely logical fallacy, that is, a breach of the rules of deductive logic.

Parity of reasoning, an expression meaning that once one case has been demonstrated, other similar cases can be demonstrated by the same line of reasoning.

Peripatetic Philosophy (περιπατέω, to walk about), the name usually given to the doctrines of Aristotle and his followers, who are said to have carried on their studies and discussions while walking about the halls and walkways of the Lyceum.

Physical definition gives the parts into which a thing can be separated by partition, or physical division.

Polylemma, an argument with the same form as a dilemma, but with more than two alternatives.

Postulate (postulatum, a thing demanded), a proposition that has to be demanded as a basis for argument; in geometry, the postulates lay down the practical conditions required.

Predicaments (prædicamenta, what can be predicated), see Categories.

Principle (principium, beginning), the first source of anything; sometimes used specially to mean the major premise of a syllogism.

Probability, quantity or degree of belief, or, more truly, quantity of information about an uncertain event. It is measured by the ratio of the number of cases favorable to the event to the total number of possible cases.

Problem (πρόβλημα, that which is thrown down), an assertion put forward to be proved or disproved.

Proof, giving a reason or argument in support of a given proposition.

Ratiocination, a name equivalent to Syllogism or Deduction, adopted by J. S. Mill.

Reason (ratio, from reor, to think), a term with a wide and ambiguous meaning; it has sometimes been used specially for the minor premise of a syllogism.

Reductio ad absurdum or ad impossibile, an indirect demonstration based on the impossibility of a contradictory supposition.

Relation (relatum, past participle of refero, to bear back), any connection, in thought or in fact, between two things.

Scholastic Philosophy, a general name for the systems of philosophy taught in the Middle Ages, from the 9th to the 16th century, at their height chiefly in the 13th and 14th centuries. The subject was mainly the logic of Aristotle, mixed with theology, metaphysics, grammar or rhetoric.

Secundi adjacentis of the second adjacent, an expression in incorrect Latin, applied to a grammatical sentence or proposition that has only two parts, the subject and the verb, with no separate copula.

Significates of a term are the things it denotes or signifies.

Sophism (σόφισμα, from σοφία, wisdom), a false argument; the name often implies that the false argument is being used knowingly, to deceive.

Subjective, that which belongs to the thinking subject, the ego, or the mind engaged in thought; the opposite of Objective, which see.

Substance (sub, under; stans from stare, to stand), that which underlies and supports phenomena or attributes. Strictly speaking it is either mind or matter, but it is more commonly used in the material sense.

Subsumption (sub, under; sumo, to take or put), Sir William Hamilton's name for the minor premise of a syllogism, because it brings, or subsumes, a special case under the rule stated in the major premise, or sumption.

Subsumption of a law is Mr. Mill's expression for the third way of explaining a law: by showing it to be a particular case of a more general law.

Sumption (sumo, to take), Hamilton's name for the major premise of a syllogism.

Synthetic syllogism, a syllogism in which the conclusion comes last; see Analytic syllogism.

System (σύστημα, from συνίστημι, to put together), a connected body of knowledge.

Tertii adjacentis of the third adjacent, an expression in incorrect Latin, applied to a grammatical sentence or proposition in which the subject, copula and predicate are all stated separately.

Theory (θεωρία, contemplation), knowledge of principles, as opposed to practice; used ambiguously.

Thesis (θέσις, from τίθημι, to place), an assertion or proposition put forward to be proved or supported by arguments.

Totum divisum, a class or notion that is divided into parts by a difference.

Trilemma, an argument like a dilemma, but with three alternatives.

Truth, the agreement of our knowledge with the things known.
