Lesson 10: Conversion of Propositions, and Immediate Inference

We infer whenever we draw one truth from another truth, or pass from one proposition to another. As Sir William Hamilton puts it, Inference is "the carrying out into the last proposition what was virtually contained in the antecedent judgments." Teaching the principles on which this act of inference must be carried out is the true business of logic. Everything we have said so far about terms and propositions is useful, or even relevant, only so far as it helps us understand how inference works. So we now have to look, one after another, at all the ways in which the same information can be recast into different forms of words, forms that often seem to say quite different things. Logicians do not agree exactly about what should be counted as inference and what should not. Everyone would agree that we make an inference when we see drops of water on the ground and believe that it has rained. That is a fairly complicated act of inference, and we will come to it in later lessons, under the heading of Induction. Few people, or none, would say that we make an inference when we pass from "The Duke of Cambridge is the Commander-in-chief" to "The Commander-in-chief is the Duke of Cambridge." (The Duke of Cambridge was then the head of the British Army.) But without worrying too much about what to call the process, I will point out in this lesson every way in which we can pass from a single proposition of the form A, E, I or O to another proposition.

We are said to convert a proposition when we swap its subject and predicate around. The proposition we get is called the converse, or converted proposition, and the one we started from is called the convertend, meaning the one to be converted. For the converse to be validly inferred from the convertend, we must keep two rules: (1) the quality of the proposition, affirmative or negative, must stay the same; and (2) no term may be distributed in the converse unless it was distributed in the convertend.

Suppose that in "all metals are elements" we simply swapped the terms, giving "all elements are metals." That would claim some knowledge about all elements. But we have already seen clearly that the predicate of A is undistributed, so the convertend really tells us nothing about all elements. All we can infer is that "some elements are metals." This converse obeys the rule, and the process by which we pass in this way from A to I is called Conversion by Limitation, or per accidens.

When the converse has exactly the same form as the convertend, the process is called simple conversion. From "some metals are brittle substances" I can infer "some brittle substances are metals," because every term here is undistributed. So I is simply converted into I.

Again, from "no metals are compounds" I can pass straight to "no compounds are metals." Both of these propositions are E propositions, so all their terms are distributed. Euler's diagram in Lesson 9 (Figure 3) shows clearly that if all the metals are kept apart from all the compounds, then all the compounds are necessarily kept apart from all the metals. So the proposition E is simply converted into E.

When we try to convert the proposition O, though, we run into a peculiar difficulty. Its subject is undistributed, and yet conversion would make that subject the predicate of a negative proposition, and a negative proposition distributes its predicate. Take the proposition "some existing things are not material substances." Converted directly, it would become "all material substances are not existing things," which is plainly absurd. The fallacy comes from the fact that "existing things" is distributed in the converse, while it was particular in the convertend; and the rules of Aristotle's logic do not allow us to put a sign of particular quantity in front of the predicate. The converse would be just as untrue and fallacious if we made the subject particular, as in "some material substances are not existing things." So we must conclude that the proposition O cannot be dealt with either by simple conversion or by conversion by limitation. We need a new process, which we may call Conversion by Negation. It consists in first changing the convertend into an affirmative proposition, and then converting it simply. If we attach the negation to the predicate instead of to the copula, our proposition becomes "some existing things are immaterial substances." Converting this simply, we get "some immaterial substances are existing things," and this can truly be inferred from the convertend. So the proposition O can be converted only by this exceptional method, by negation.

There is another process of conversion that can be applied to the proposition A, known as conversion by contraposition. From "all metals are elements" it necessarily follows that "all not-elements are not metals." If this is not obvious at first, a little thought will make it so. Figure 5 shows that if all the metals are among the elements, then whatever is not an element, that is, whatever lies outside the circle of elements, must also lie outside the circle of metals. If we may look ahead to what Lesson 23 contains, we can also prove that the contrapositive proposition is true in this way. Suppose something that is not-element were a metal. Then by the original proposition it would have to be an element, so it would be an element and not an element at once. That is impossible by the Primary Laws of Thought (Lesson 14), since nothing can both have and not have the same property. It follows that whatever is not-element must be not-metal.

It is easy to make mistakes in contrapositive conversion, for a reason that will become clearer in Lesson 22. We are very apt to infer from a proposition of the form "all metals are elements" that all not-metals are not-elements. That is not only a false statement in itself; it is not in the least justified by the original proposition. Figure 5 makes it plain that a thing which lies outside the circle of metals does not necessarily lie outside the circle of elements, which is wider than the circle of metals. All the same, people make this mistake often in everyday life. The reader will do well to remember that conversion by contraposition consists only in this: we take the negative of the predicate of the proposition A as a new subject, and we affirm of it, universally, the negative of the old subject.

Contrapositive conversion cannot be applied at all to the particular propositions I and O, nor to the proposition E as it stands. But we can change E into A by attaching the negation to the predicate, and then the process can be applied. So "no humans are perfect" can be changed into "all humans are not-perfect," that is, "are imperfect." Then by contraposition we infer "all not-imperfect beings are not-humans." But not-imperfect really means the same as perfect, so our new proposition really amounts to "all perfect beings are not humans," or "no perfect beings are humans" (E), which is the simple converse of the original proposition.

That leaves certain deductions that can be drawn from a proposition without converting its terms. We may call them immediate inferences, and Archbishop Thomson has described them very clearly in his "Outline of the Necessary Laws of Thought" (pp. 156, &c.).

Immediate Inference by Privative Conception consists in passing from any affirmative proposition to a negative proposition that is implied in it or equivalent to it, or the other way round, passing from a negative proposition to the affirmative that corresponds to it. It is also called Obversion.

The following table takes a proposition of each kind and changes it, by privative conception, into an equivalent proposition:

	A all metals are elements. | E no metals are compounds.
	E no humans are perfect. | A all humans are imperfect.
	I some humans are trustworthy. | O some humans are not untrustworthy.
	O some humans are not trustworthy. | I some humans are untrustworthy.

The truth of any of these can be clearly shown by diagrams. For instance, it will be plain that if the whole circle of metals lies inside the circle of elements, no part of it can lie outside that circle, among the compounds. Any of the propositions above can be converted, but the results will mostly be ones we have already obtained. The simple converse of "no metals are compounds" is "no compounds are metals," which is to say "no not-elements are metals," the contrapositive of "all metals are elements." From the last example in the table we also get, by simple conversion, "some untrustworthy beings are humans," which is obviously the converse by negation explained above. Applying this kind of conversion to "some humans are not untrustworthy," we get "some not-untrustworthy beings are humans." Finally, from "all humans are imperfect" we can obtain, by conversion by limitation, "some imperfect beings are humans."

Immediate Inference by Added Determinants consists in attaching an adjective, or some similar qualification, to both the subject and the predicate of a proposition, so that the meaning of each term becomes narrower, or more fully determined. As long as nothing else is altered, the truth of the new proposition follows necessarily from the truth of the original in almost every case.

So from "all metals are elements" we may infer that "all very heavy metals are very heavy elements." From "a comet is a material body" we infer "a visible comet is a visible material body." But if we use this kind of inference too boldly, we can arrive at fallacious and absurd results. From "all kings are men" we might infer "all incompetent kings are incompetent men." But it does not follow at all that someone who is incompetent as a king would be incompetent in other positions. In this case and many others, the qualifying adjective is apt to carry different meanings in the subject and in the predicate, and the inference is necessarily true only when the meaning is exactly the same in both. This kind of inference will seldom work with comparative terms. From "a cottage is a building," for example, we cannot infer "a huge cottage is a huge building," since a cottage may be large compared with other cottages but not compared with buildings in general.

Immediate Inference by Complex Conception is very like the last kind. It consists in using the subject and predicate of a proposition as parts of a more complex conception. From "all metals are elements" I can pass to "a mixture of metals is a mixture of elements." From "a horse is a quadruped" I infer "the skeleton of a horse is the skeleton of a quadruped." But here again the reader must beware of applying the process where the new complex conception means something different in the subject and in the predicate. From "all Protestants are Christians," it does not follow that "a majority of Protestants are a majority of Christians," nor that "the most excellent of the Protestants is the most excellent of the Christians."

I recommend that the student get thoroughly familiar with all the transformations of propositions, or immediate inferences, described in this lesson, and plenty of examples are given for the purpose. A good exercise is to put the same proposition through a series of changes so that it comes back to its original form at the end, which proves that all the changes along the way were sound. But if conversion by limitation has been used anywhere in the chain, the original universal proposition cannot be recovered, only the particular proposition that corresponds to it.

On Immediate Inference, see Archbishop Thomson, Outline of the Laws of Thought, §§ 85—92.

Questions and Exercises

1. Define inference and conversion.

2. What are converse and convertend propositions?

3. State the rules of valid conversion.

4. Name all the kinds of conversion.

5. By what process do we pass from each of the following propositions to the next?

(1) No knowledge is useless.

(2) No useless thing is knowledge.

(3) All knowledge is not-useless.

(4) All knowledge is useful.

(5) What is not useful is not knowledge.

(6) What is useless is not knowledge.

(7) No knowledge is useless.

6. Give the logical opposites of the following proposition, and the converse of its contradictory: "He cannot become rich who will not labor."

7. Apply negative conception to the proposition "All humans are fallible"; then convert it, and show that the result is the contrapositive of the original.

8. Sort the propositions below into the following four groups (an expedient act is one that is useful or advantageous): a. Those that can be inferred from (1). b. Those from which (1) can be inferred. c. Those that do not contradict (1), but cannot be inferred from it. d. Those that contradict (1).

(1) All just acts are expedient acts.

(2) No expedient acts are unjust.

(3) No just acts are inexpedient.

(4) All inexpedient acts are unjust.

(5) Some unjust acts are inexpedient.

(6) No expedient acts are just.

(7) Some inexpedient acts are unjust.

(8) All expedient acts are just.

(9) No inexpedient acts are just.

(10) All unjust acts are inexpedient.

(11) Some inexpedient acts are just acts.

(12) Some expedient acts are just.

(13) Some just acts are expedient.

(14) Some unjust acts are expedient.

Examples of Propositions

For each one, say whether it is affirmative or negative; universal, particular, singular or indefinite; pure or modal; exclusive or exceptive; and so on. Where a proposition is stated irregularly, put it into the simple logical order. Then convert the proposition, and draw immediate inferences from it by any process that applies.

(21) Unsafe are all things unbecoming.
