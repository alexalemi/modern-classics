Lesson 18: Irregular and Compound Syllogisms

It may seem surprising, but the arguments we meet in books or in conversation are hardly ever set out in the form of regular syllogisms. Even when a complete syllogism does turn up now and then, it is usually there as a mere show of logical precision. In earlier centuries, it is true, every student at the universities had to take part in public disputations. In these, one side put forward elaborate syllogistic arguments and the other side refuted them with precise syllogisms of its own. The practice was given up at the University of Oxford not very long ago, and it is said to survive still at some universities on the Continent. But outside disputations of that kind, we have to admit, perfectly formal syllogisms are seldom used.

The truth is, though, that syllogistic arguments are not missing at all. Wherever one of the conjunctions therefore, because, for, since, hence, inasmuch as, consequently appears, we can be sure that an inference is being drawn, and very probably it is being drawn by a true syllogism. What usually gets left out is only the complete statement of the premises and the conclusion. The reader generally knows one premise or the other already, or can easily guess what is being assumed, and it is tedious, even irritating, to spell out at full length what the reader already knows. Suppose I say, "atmospheric air must have weight because it is a material substance." I am certainly using a syllogism. But I think it quite unnecessary to state the premise whose truth I am plainly assuming, namely "whatever is a material substance has weight." The conclusion of the syllogism is the first proposition, "atmospheric air has weight." The middle term is "material substance," which does not appear in the conclusion. The minor term is "atmospheric air," and the major term is "having weight." The complete syllogism is plainly this:

	All material substances have weight,
	Atmospheric air is a material substance;
	Therefore atmospheric air has weight.

This is in the very common and useful mood Barbara.

A syllogism that is stated incompletely is usually called an enthymeme. The name is often supposed to come from two Greek words, ἐν (en), "in," and θυμός (thymos), "mind." On that reading it means that some piece of knowledge is held in the mind and supplied as a tacit premise, that is, a silent or understood one. Most often the missing premise is the major premise, and then the enthymeme can be said to be of the First Order. Less often it is the minor premise that goes unstated, and then the enthymeme is of the Second Order. Here is an argument of that kind: "Comets must be subject to the law of gravitation; for this is true of all bodies which move in elliptical orbits." It is so obviously implied that comets move in elliptical orbits that it would be tedious to state it as the minor premise of a complete syllogism in the mood Barbara, like this:

	All bodies moving in elliptical orbits are subject to the law of gravitation;
	Comets move in elliptical orbits;
	Therefore comets are subject to the law of gravitation.

Occasionally it is the conclusion of a syllogism that is left unstated, and then the enthymeme can be said to belong to the Third Order. This happens with epigrams and other witty sayings, where the whole wit often lies in making an unstated truth obvious. Sir William Hamilton gives, as an example of this kind of enthymeme, the famous epigram that the English scholar Porson wrote about a German scholar of his own day, Gottfried Hermann:

	"The Germans in Greek
	Are sadly to seek;
	Not five in five score,
	But ninety-five more;
	All, save only Hermann,
	And Hermann's a German."

The writer pretends to make an exception of Hermann. But he cleverly slips in the suggestion that, since Hermann is a German, he does not really know Greek either. In the same way, the wonderful speech Antony makes over Caesar's body, in Julius Caesar, Shakespeare's greatest historical play, contains a whole series of syllogistic arguments whose conclusions are only hinted at.

Even a single proposition can have the force of a syllogism, if it clearly brings to mind a second premise from which a conclusion can then be drawn. A saying of the radical writer John Horne Tooke, "Men who have no rights cannot justly complain of any wrongs," seems to be a case in point. Very few people have never felt wronged at one time or another, and so they would be likely to argue, whether their premises were true or false, like this:

	Men who have no rights cannot justly complain of any wrongs;
	We can justly complain;
	Therefore we are not men who have no rights.

In other words, we have rights.

Syllogisms can be joined and combined in various ways, and it is convenient to have special names for the different parts of a complex argument. A syllogism that proves, or gives a reason for, one of the premises of another syllogism is called a Prosyllogism. A syllogism that has as one of its premises the conclusion of another syllogism is called an Episyllogism.

Take this example:

	All B's are A's,
	And all C's are B's;
	Therefore all C's are A's.
	But all D's are C's;
	Therefore all D's are A's.

This plainly contains two syllogisms in the mood Barbara. The first is a Prosyllogism with respect to the second, and the second is an Episyllogism with respect to the first.

The special name Epicheirema is given to a syllogism in which either premise is proved or supported by a reason, so that a prosyllogism is implied but only partly expressed. So the form

	All B's are A's, for they are P's,
	And all C's are B's, for they are Q's;
	Therefore all C's are A's,

is a double Epicheirema, since it gives reasons for both premises. The reader will easily break it down into three complete syllogisms in the mood Barbara.

A more interesting form of reasoning is the chain of syllogisms usually called the Sorites, from the Greek word σωρός (sōros), meaning a heap. It is usually stated like this:

	All A's are B's,
	All B's are C's,
	All C's are D's,
	All D's are E's;
	Therefore all A's are E's.

The chain can be carried on to any length, as long as it is perfectly continuous, so that every term except the first and the last appears twice, once as a subject and once as a predicate. It hardly needs pointing out that a sorites really contains a series of syllogisms stated incompletely, like this:

	First Syllogism. | Second Syllogism. | Last Syllogism.
	B's are C's, | C's are D's, | D's are E's,
	A's are B's; | A's are C's; | A's are D's;
	∴ A's are C's. | ∴ A's are D's. | ∴ A's are E's.

Each syllogism supplies a premise to the one after it, and so it is the prosyllogism of that one. Equally, any syllogism in the chain can be regarded as the episyllogism of the one before it.

In the sorites above, all the premises were universal and affirmative. But a sorites may contain one particular premise, provided it is the first, and one negative premise, provided it is the last. The reader can easily check by trying it that if any premise except the first were particular, the fallacy of undistributed middle would be committed. That is because one of the middle terms would then be the predicate of one affirmative premise and the subject of another, particular, premise. And if any premise except the last were negative, there would be a fallacy of illicit process of the major term.

We should not suppose that the forms of the syllogism described so far are the only kinds of reasoning actually used in science or in everyday life. Besides the hypothetical and disjunctive syllogisms, and some other forms to be described in later lessons, there are in fact many ways of reasoning that logicians have so far paid little attention to. This was clearly pointed out more than two hundred years ago by the writers of the Port Royal Logic. The book was first printed in 1662, but it has been reprinted many times since and translated into a great many languages. It takes its name from Port Royal, a place near Paris where a small religious community lived. The authors of the book, Arnauld and Nicole, and one contributor to it, the great philosopher and mathematician Pascal, were the most famous members of that community. The Port Royal Logic was to a large extent the basis of the well-known Watts's Logic, but the reader can now be referred to an excellent translation of the original work by Professor Spencer Baynes of St Andrews.

Many improvements to logic can be found in this book, such as the doctrine of extension and intension explained in Lesson 5. Moreover, in the 9th Chapter of the 3rd Part it wisely points out that "little pains are taken in applying the rules of the syllogism to reasonings of which the propositions are complex, though this is often very difficult, and there are many arguments of this nature which appear bad, but which are nevertheless very good; and besides, the use of such reasonings is much more frequent than that of syllogisms which are quite simple." The book gives some examples of the complex syllogisms it means, such as this one:

	The sun is a thing without feeling,
	The Persians worship the sun;
	Therefore the Persians worship a thing without feeling.

This argument cannot be proved by the rules of the syllogism. Yet it is not only plainly true, it is an extremely common kind of argument. Here is another example:

	The Divine Law commands us to honor kings;
	Louis XIV is a king;
	Therefore the Divine Law commands us to honor Louis XIV.

The reader will also find arguments that are perfectly valid and syllogistic, but are worded so that they seem to have four different terms, and so to break one of the rules of the syllogism. Suppose I say, "Diamonds are combustible, for they are composed of carbon and carbon is combustible." Four terms are used here: diamonds, combustible, composed of carbon, and carbon. But it is easy to rearrange the propositions so as to get a simple syllogism without really changing the sense, and then we have:

	What is composed of carbon is combustible;
	Diamonds are composed of carbon;
	Therefore diamonds are combustible.

Among the Miscellaneous Examples at the end of Lesson 21 are some short arguments, taken from Bacon's Essays and other writings, which the student can reduce to syllogistic form by easy changes of wording. But it should be clearly understood that changes like these are not part of logic itself. They belong more properly to the science of language.

I should explain here that the syllogism and the sorites can be expressed either in the order of extension or in the order of intension. In terms of the number of individual things, the noble metals are part of the metals, and the metals are part of the elements. But in terms of intension, that is, the qualities the names imply, element is part of metal, and metal is part of noble metal. Again, in extension the plant genus Anemone is part of the order Ranunculaceae (the buttercup family), and that order is part of the great class of Exogens (roughly, the dicots). But in intension the character of Exogen is part of the character of Ranunculaceae, and that is part of the character of Anemone. Syllogistic reasoning is just as valid and just as obvious either way, and we could show the two modes in ordinary language like this:

	Extensive Syllogism.
	All Ranunculaceae are Exogens;
	The Anemone is one of the Ranunculaceae;
	Therefore the Anemone is an Exogen.
	Intensive Syllogism.
	All the qualities of Ranunculaceae are qualities of Anemone;
	All the qualities of Exogen are qualities of Ranunculaceae;
	Therefore all the qualities of Exogen are qualities of Anemone.

Any sorites can be shown in the same way, in either extension or intension.

On the Aristotelian doctrine of the Enthymeme, see Mansel's Aldrich, App. Note F, and Hamilton's Lectures on Logic, Lecture XX. Port Royal Logic, translated by T. Spencer Baynes, 7th ed., Edinburgh.

Questions and Exercises

1. Explain the meaning of each of these terms: Enthymeme, Prosyllogism, Episyllogism, Epicheirema, Sorites.

2. Make up an example of a syllogism that has two prosyllogisms.

3. Construct a sorites with four premises, and break it down into separate syllogisms.

4. What rules must a sorites obey?

5. The reader is asked to analyze the following arguments, to pick out the ones that are false, and to find which rules of the syllogism they break. If an argument seems valid, the reader should find its figure and mood, state it in correct logical form, and then, if it is in an imperfect figure, prove it by reducing it to the first figure. The first six examples should be arranged in both the extensive and the intensive orders.

1. None but mortals are human beings. Monarchs are human beings. Therefore monarchs are mortals.

2. Personal deformity is an affliction of nature. Disgrace is not an affliction of nature. Therefore personal deformity is not disgrace.

3. Some statesmen are also authors; for such are Mr. Gladstone, Lord Derby, Lord Russell, and Sir G. C. Lewis.

4. This explosion must have been caused by gunpowder; for nothing else would have had enough force.

5. Every person should be moderate; for excess will cause disease.

6. Blessed are the merciful; for they shall obtain mercy.

7. Since almost all the organs of the body have a known use, the spleen must have some use.

8. Cogito, ergo sum. (I think, therefore I exist.)

9. Some speculative people are unworthy of trust; for they are unwise, and no unwise person can be trusted.

10. No idle person can be a successful writer of history; therefore Hume, Macaulay, Hallam and Grote must have been hard-working.

11. Whoever spares the rod hates his child; therefore the parent who loves his child does not spare the rod.

12. Comets must consist of heavy matter; for otherwise they would not obey the law of gravitation.

13. Lithium is an element; for it is an alkali-producing substance, which is a metal, which is an element.

14. Rational beings are accountable for their actions; brute animals, not being rational, are therefore exempt from responsibility.

15. A singular proposition is a universal one; for it applies to the whole of its subject.

16. Whatever tends to draw the mind away from low pursuits deserves to be encouraged; classical learning does this, since it gives us a taste for intellectual pleasures; therefore it deserves to be encouraged.

17. Bacon was a great lawyer and statesman; and since he was also a philosopher, we may infer that any philosopher may be a great lawyer and statesman.

18. Immoral companions should be avoided; but some immoral companions are intelligent people, so some intelligent people should be avoided.

19. Mathematical study undoubtedly improves the reasoning powers; but since the study of logic is not mathematical study, we may infer that it does not improve the reasoning powers.

20. Every fair-minded man acknowledges merit in a rival; every learned man does not do so; therefore every learned man is not fair-minded.
