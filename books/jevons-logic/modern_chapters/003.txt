Lesson 3: Terms, and Their Various Kinds

In the last lesson we saw that every assertion or statement expresses the agreement or difference of two things, or of two general notions. So when we put an assertion into words, we need two kinds of words. We need words that draw the mind's attention to the things being compared, and we need words that give the result of the comparison, that is, whether the things agree or differ. The words that point out the things, or classes of things, are called Terms. The words that express the comparison are said to form the Copula. A complete assertion or statement therefore consists of two terms and a copula, and when it is put this way it forms a Proposition. Take the proposition "Dictionaries are useful books." The two terms are dictionaries and useful books. The copula is the verb are, and it expresses a certain agreement between the class of dictionaries and the class of useful books: the class of dictionaries forms part of the class of useful books. Here each term is only one or two words long, but a term can need any number of words to describe the notions or classes being compared. In the proposition "the angles at the base of an isosceles triangle are equal to each other," the first term takes nine words, and the second term takes four (equal to each other). There is no limit to the number of words that can go into a term.

A term gets its name because it forms one end (Latin terminus, an end or boundary) of a proposition, and strictly speaking it is a term only as long as it stands in a proposition. But we commonly use "term" or "name" to mean any noun, substantive or adjective, or any group of words that denotes an object of thought. As we will soon see, that object may be a single thing, a group of things, a quality of things, or a group of qualities. No one has defined a name or term better than Hobbes: "A name is a word taken at pleasure to serve for a mark, which may raise in our mind a thought like to some thought which we had before, and which, being pronounced to others, may be to them a sign of what thought the speaker had before in his mind."

Every term or name is made of words, but not every word can make a name by itself. We can't properly say "Not is agreeable" or "Probably is not true." Nothing can be asserted about a preposition, an adverb or certain other parts of speech, except that they are prepositions, adverbs and so on. Only a noun substantive, or a group of words used as a noun substantive, can form the subject, or first term, of a proposition. Only a noun substantive, an adjective, something equivalent to an adjective, or a verb can form the second term, or predicate. It is even open to question whether an adjective can ever form a term on its own. In "Dictionaries are useful," it may be said that the substantive things or books is understood in the predicate, so that the complete sentence is "Dictionaries are useful books." But since this is a disputed point, we will assume that words divide into two kinds, as follows.

Words that stand, or seem to stand, alone as complete terms, namely the substantive, the adjective and certain parts of a verb, are called categorematic words, from the Greek word κατηγορέω (katēgoreō), to assert or predicate.

The other parts of speech, such as prepositions, adverbs and conjunctions, can only form parts of names or terms. These are called syncategorematic words, because they have to be used with other words to make up terms (Greek σύν, syn, with, and κατηγορέω). We need not pay any more attention to syncategorematic words, except where they form part of categorematic terms.

Next we need to look at the different kinds of terms and their peculiarities, so that we get a clear idea of what they mean. The first division of terms is into singular or individual terms, and general or common terms. It is a very obvious division, but an important one. A Singular term is one that can denote only a single object, at least as long as it is used in exactly the same meaning. The Emperor of the French, the Atlantic Ocean, St Paul's, William Shakespeare and the most precious of the metals are all singular terms. All proper names belong to this class. John Jones is the name of many men, it is true, but it is not used to mean any one of those men you like. It is used to mean some single man. In short, it has a different meaning in each case, just as London, the name of our capital, has no connection in meaning with London in Canada.

General terms, by contrast, apply in the same sense, and equally well, to any one of an indefinite number of objects that resemble each other in certain qualities. Metal is a general name, for example, because it can be applied without preference to gold, silver, copper, tin, aluminum, or any of about fifty known substances. It is no more the name of one of these than of any other. In fact it is applied to any substance that has a metallic luster, cannot be decomposed, and has certain other qualities that chemists easily recognize. Nor is the number of substances in the class fixed: as new kinds of metal are discovered from time to time, they are added to it. Again, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn and the rest are singular terms, since each can denote only one planet. But the term planet is a general one, because it applies to as many bodies as may be found to revolve around the sun as the earth does.

We must be careful not to confuse general terms with collective terms. A collective term is the name of a number of things all joined together as one whole, like the soldiers of a regiment, the men of a jury, or the crew of a ship. A collective term is the name of all of them, but not of each. A general term, on the other hand, is the name of a number of things, but of each of them separately, or, to use the technical expression, distributively. Soldier, juryman and sailor are general names that may belong to John Jones, Thomas Brown and so on. But we can't say that John Jones is a regiment, or Thomas Brown a jury. Pointed out like this, the distinction is extremely obvious. But it can turn up in less obvious forms, and then it is likely to lead to faulty reasoning, as we will see in Lesson 20. Notice that we cannot divide terms into those that are general and those that are collective, because the same term will often be both, depending on how we look at it. Library is collective with respect to the books in it, but general with respect to the great number of different libraries, private or public, that exist. Regiment is a collective term with respect to the soldiers who make it up, but general with respect to the hundred different regiments, the Coldstream Guards, the Highland regiment, the Welsh Fusiliers and the rest, that make up the British standing army. Army, in its turn, is a collective whole, because it is made of a number of regiments organized together. Year is collective with respect to the months, weeks or days it consists of, but general because it is the name of 1869, or of 1870, or of any period marked by one revolution of the earth around the sun.

English does not always give us a convenient way to tell the general use of a term from its collective use. Latin expressed the difference exactly: omnes meant all distributively, and cuncti meant all taken together (it is a shortened form of conjuncti, joined together). In English, all men may mean any man, or all men together. Even the more exact word every is sometimes misused, as in the old proverb, "Every little makes a mickle" (a mickle is a large amount). Plainly, no little portion can make much all by itself. It can only do so when joined to other little portions.

A second important distinction is between concrete terms and abstract terms. It can't be described better than in the words of Mr. Mill: a concrete name is the name of a thing, and an abstract name is the name of a quality, attribute or circumstance of a thing. So red house is the name of a thing that physically exists, and it is concrete. Redness is the name of one quality of the house, and it is abstract. The word abstract means drawn from (Latin abstractus, from abstrahere, to draw away from). It tells us that the quality redness is thought of in the mind apart from all the other qualities that belong to the red house, or to any other red object. But although we can think of a quality by itself, we cannot suppose that the quality can exist physically apart from the matter in which it shows itself to us. Redness means either a notion in the mind, or whatever it is in red objects that excites that notion.

The reader should notice carefully that adjectives are concrete, not abstract. If we say that a book is useful, it is the book we apply the adjective useful to, and usefulness is the abstract noun that denotes the quality. In the same way, the adjectives equal, grateful, reverent and rational are names of things, and the abstract nouns that go with them are equality, gratitude, reverence and rationality. This distinction will become clearer when you read Lesson 5.

It is good practice to try to find pairs of matching concrete and abstract names. Animal has animality; miser, miserliness; old, agedness or old age; substance, substantiality; soap, soapiness; shrub, shrubbiness; and so on. But it does not follow at all that there is an abstract word for every concrete one. Table hardly has an abstract tabularity, and although ink has inkiness, we would not find an abstract for pen. Whether we have an abstract name or not depends on the accidents of the history of language, and as time and science move on, people keep inventing new abstract words.

Unfortunately, concrete and abstract names are often confused, and it is far from easy to keep their meanings apart. Relation, properly speaking, is the abstract name for the position two people or things stand in toward each other, and the people themselves are properly called relatives (Latin relativus, one who is related). But nowadays we constantly speak of relations, meaning the people themselves, and when we want to point to the abstract relation between them we have to invent a new abstract name, relationship. Nation has long been a concrete term, though judging by its form it was probably abstract at first. Yet the misuse of language has gone so far, especially in newspaper writing, that we hear of a nationality meaning a nation. Of course, if nation is the concrete, then nationality ought to be the abstract, meaning the quality of being a nation. In the same way, action, intention, extension, conception and a great many other names that are properly abstract are used loosely for the matching concrete words, namely act, intent, extent, concept and so on. Production is properly the condition or state of someone who is producing, or drawing something forth. But it has become confused with the thing produced, so that we constantly talk about the productions of a country, meaning its products. The logical terms Proposition, Deduction, Induction and Syllogism are all properly abstract words, yet they are used concretely for a Proposition, a Deduction, an Induction, a Syllogism. And it has to be admitted that logicians are nearly as bad as everyone else at confusing abstract and concrete terms. This misuse does a lot of harm to the language.

Another very obvious division of terms is between positive terms and negative terms. The usual way of describing the difference is to say that positive terms signify that a quality exists or is possessed, as in grateful, metallic and organic, while the matching negatives signify that the same qualities are absent, as in ungrateful, non-metallic and inorganic. Negative terms may be adjectives, like these, or substantives, either concrete or abstract. Ingratitude, inequality and inconvenience are abstract negative terms; individuals, unequals and so on are concrete negatives. We usually treat as negative any term with a negative prefix such as not, non, un or in. But a great many terms work as negatives without carrying any mark of it. Darkness is the negative of light or lightness, since it means the absence of light. Compound is the negative of element, since we give the name compound to whatever can be decomposed, and an element is what cannot be decomposed. In theory, every term has a matching negative, but it does not follow at all that the language supplies that term ready-made. Table has the matching adjective tabular, but there is no negative untabular to go with it. One person may be called a bookworm, but there is no negative for people who are not bookworms, because no one has felt the need for the expression. New negative terms are being invented all the time, perhaps faster than is good for us. When an idea is not mentioned often, it is better to express it with a phrase than to lengthen the dictionary with a newly made word.

In many cases, it seems, a negative term implies that some definite quality or fact is present. Inconvenience no doubt implies the absence of convenience, but it also implies the presence of real trouble or pain caused by that absence. Unhappiness is a negative term, but exactly the same notion is expressed by the positive term misery. The negative of healthy is unhealthy, but the positive term sickly does just as well. So whether a positive or a negative term is used for a particular notion seems to be more a matter of accident than anything else. All we can really say is that every positive term necessarily implies the possibility of a matching negative term, which is the name of all the things the positive name cannot be applied to. Whether that negative term has actually been invented is an accident of language; in logic we may assume it exists.

The reader should be warned not to assume that every term that looks negative because it has a negative prefix really is negative. The participle unloosed certainly looks like the negative of loosed, but the two words mean exactly the same thing: the prefix un is not really a negative here. Invaluable, again, does not mean having no value. It means so valuable that the value cannot be measured. And a shameless action can just as well be called by the positive term, a shameful action. No doubt other examples could be found.

Take great care not to confuse terms that express the presence or absence of a quality with terms that describe its degree. Less is not the negative of greater, because there is a third possibility, equal. The true negative of greater is not-greater, and that amounts to either equal or less. In the same way, we may say that disagreeable is not the simple negative of agreeable, because some things may be neither one nor the other, but simply indifferent to us. It would not be easy to say offhand whether every action that is not honest is dishonest, or whether there may be actions of an in-between character. The rule is this. Wherever the question is one of degree or quantity, a middle ground is possible, and the subject belongs to the science of quantity rather than to simple logic. Where the question is whether a quality is present or absent, there cannot be more than two alternatives, according to one of the Primary Laws of Thought, which we will look at in Lesson 14. In the case of quantity, we may call the extreme terms opposites: less is the opposite of greater, and disagreeable of agreeable. In the case of simple negation, we may call the terms negatives or contradictories. From a logical point of view, it makes no difference which of a pair of contradictory terms we treat as the positive and which as the negative. Each is the negative of the other.

Logicians have set apart from simple negative terms a class of terms called privative, such as blind, dead and so on. Such terms express that a thing has been deprived of a quality that it had before, or could have had, or usually does have. A man may be born blind, so that he never did see; but he has the organs that would have let him see if not for some accident. A stone or a tree could not have had the faculty of sight under any circumstances. No mineral substance can properly be said to die or to be dead, because it was never capable of life. But it may be called uncrystallized, because it could have been in the form of a crystal. So we apply a privative term to anything that lacks a quality it was capable of having, and a negative term to anything that lacks a quality and could not have had it. It is doubtful, though, whether this distinction can be properly carried through, and it does not matter very much.

It is also usual to divide terms into relative and absolute, that is, non-relative. The adjective absolute means whatever is "loosed from connection with anything else" (Latin ab, from, and solutus, loosed). Relative means whatever is carried, in thought at least, into connection with something else. So a relative term denotes an object that cannot be thought of without reference to some other object, or as part of a larger whole. A father cannot be thought of except in relation to a child, a monarch except in relation to a subject, a shepherd except in relation to a flock. So father, monarch and shepherd are relative terms, while child, subject and flock are their correlatives (Latin con, with, and relativus): the objects that are necessarily joined in thought with the first ones. The very meaning of father, in fact, is that he has a child; of monarch, that he has subjects; and of shepherd, that he has a flock. As examples of terms with no obvious relation to anything else, I might mention water, gas and tree. Nothing seems to me so habitually connected with water that we must think of it as part of the same idea. Gas, tree and a great many other terms also denote objects that have no notable or permanent relations of the kind that would earn the terms the name of relatives. So they may be considered absolute, or non-relative, terms.

The truth, though, is that everything really must have relations to something else: water to the elements it is made of, gas to the coal it is manufactured from, the tree to the soil it is rooted in. And by the very laws of thought, we cannot think of any thing, or class of things, except by separating it from the other existing things it differs from. I cannot use the term mortal without immediately splitting all existing or conceivable things into two groups, mortal and immortal. Metal, element, organic substance and every other term one could mention would necessarily imply that a matching negative term exists: non-metallic, compound, inorganic substance. In this respect, then, every term is certainly relative. Logicians, however, have been content to treat as relative terms only those that imply some special and striking kind of relation, arising from position in time or space, from the connection of cause and effect, and so on. It is in this special sense that the student should use the distinction.

Now that the most important kinds of terms have been explained, the reader should get thoroughly familiar with them by working through the exercises at the end of this lesson. For each of the terms given there, the reader is to decide:

1. Whether it is a categorematic or syncategorematic term.

2. Whether it is a general or a singular term.

3. Whether it is collective or distributive.

4. Whether it is concrete or abstract.

5. Whether it is positive, or negative, or privative.

6. Whether it is relative or absolute.

The next lesson will show in full that most terms have more than one meaning. One meaning may be general and another singular, one concrete and another abstract, and so on. So it is absolutely necessary for the reader first of all to choose one exact meaning of the term being examined. And in answering the questions, it is a good idea to state which way you are taking it. Take the word sovereign. We may first pick the meaning in which it is equivalent to monarch. In this meaning it is a general term, insofar as it is the name of any one of many monarchs, living or dead. But it is singular with respect to the people of any one country. It is clearly categorematic, concrete and positive, and obviously relative to the monarch's subjects.

Read Mr. Mill's chapter on Names, System of Logic, Book I. chap. 2.
