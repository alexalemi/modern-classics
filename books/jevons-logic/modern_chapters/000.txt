Preface

In writing these Lessons I have tried to show that logic, even in its traditional form, can be made a really useful subject of study and a powerful way of exercising the mind. With that aim I have avoided unnecessary technical terms, and I have kept out of questions that are purely speculative or metaphysical. Books on logic too often use childish illustrations. In their place I have generally put examples drawn from the clear-cut objects and ideas of the natural and experimental sciences. In this and in other ways I have tried to make these Lessons a fitting companion to a series of science schoolbooks.

Logic is not only an exact science; it is the simplest and most elementary of all the sciences, and so it certainly ought to have some place in every course of education. The relations between propositions and the forms of argument make as precise a subject to teach, and as vigorous an exercise for the mind, as the properties of geometrical figures or the rules of algebra. Yet every schoolboy is made to learn mathematical problems he will never use in later life, while he is left completely ignorant of the simple principles and forms of reasoning that will come into his thoughts every hour of the day. Logic should no longer be thought of as an elegant, learned accomplishment. It should take its place as an essential study for every well-informed person. I hope these Lessons will bring to the science many people who have neither the time nor the inclination to read longer and more detailed treatises, and many who would not be drawn in by the numerous, but rather dry and brief, summaries published in past years.

Lessons in logic ought to be the basis of plenty of exercises, and for that purpose I have supplied a large number of questions and examples at the end of the book, some of them taken from the examination papers of the universities of Oxford, London and Edinburgh. In my own classes I have always found that working out logical questions, examining arguments and spotting fallacies is just as practical and useful an exercise of the mind as doing calculations and solving problems in a mathematics class.

Except in a few places, where I say so, I have not put forward any views that teachers of logic do not commonly accept. Throughout, I have spent more effort on describing clearly and simply the doctrines that logicians generally agree on than on discussing the points where opinions differ. The recent logical discoveries of Sir William Hamilton, Mr. George Bentham, Professor De Morgan and, above all, the late Professor Boole cannot yet be fully adopted in an elementary book, but I have tried to give a clear idea of the results they are bound to lead to.

In the later Lessons, which deal with induction, I have generally followed Sir John Herschel, Dr. Whewell and Mr. J. S. Mill, as the recognized authorities on the subject. In fact, these Lessons can be treated as an easy introduction to some of the most important parts of Mr. Mill's treatise on logic.

At the end of almost every Lesson the student will find references to the books in which he can most profitably go on reading about the subject of that Lesson, so that this little volume can serve as a guide to a wider course of study.

Hampstead, November, 1876.
