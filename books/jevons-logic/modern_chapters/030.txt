Lesson 30: Empirical and Deductive Methods

So far we have treated deduction and induction as if they were two entirely separate and independent methods. In reality they are often blended together, or used by turns, in the search for truth. In fact, all the more important and far-reaching investigations in science can be said to rely on one just as much as on the other. Probably the greatest merit of Mr. J. S. Mill's writings on logic is that he shows how completely inadequate the so-called Baconian Method is for detecting the more obscure and difficult laws of nature. Bacon advised that we should always begin by collecting facts, sort them according to how they agree and differ, and gradually gather from them laws of greater and greater generality. He protested against "anticipating nature" altogether, that is, against forming our own hypotheses and theories about what the laws of nature probably are. He seems to have thought that arranging facts systematically would take the place of every other method. The reader will soon see that the progress of science has not borne out his opinions.

When a law of nature has been found purely by induction from certain observations or experiments, and has nothing else to guarantee its truth, it is called an empirical law. As Mill puts it, "Scientific inquirers give the name of Empirical Laws to uniformities which observation or experiment has shown to exist, but on which they hesitate to rely in cases varying much from those which have been actually observed, for want of seeing any reason why such a law should exist." The name comes from the Greek word ἐμπειρία (empeiria), meaning experience or trial. Examples of such laws are plentiful. We learn empirically that a certain strong yellow color at sunset, or an unusual clearness in the air, warns of rain; that a quick pulse is a sign of fever; that horned animals are always ruminants (animals that chew the cud); that quinine does good to the nervous system and to the health of the body in general; and that strychnine has a terrible effect of the opposite kind. We know all of these are true from repeated observation, but we can give no other reason for their truth. That is, we cannot bring them into harmony with any other scientific facts, and we could never have deduced them or predicted them from what we knew before. The connection between sunspots, magnetic storms, auroras and the motions of the planets, mentioned in the last lesson, is perhaps the most remarkable empirical induction known in the 1870s, for no one has yet given any hint of how these magnetic influences could act across the vast distances of the planetary system. The properties of the various alloys of metals are also good examples of empirical knowledge. When two or three metals are mixed in a given proportion for the first time, no one can tell in advance what the mixture will be like. No one could have predicted that brass would be both harder and more ductile than either of the metals it is made from, copper and zinc; that copper, mixed with the very soft metal tin, would make hard, ringing bell-metal; or that a certain mixture of lead, bismuth, tin and cadmium would melt at a temperature (65° centigrade, or 149° Fahrenheit) far below that of boiling water.

However useful empirical knowledge may be, it matters very little compared with the well-connected and fully explained body of knowledge that makes up an advanced, deductive science. In fact, a science becomes perfect exactly in proportion as it becomes deductive and lets us bring more and more facts that seem unconnected under the same law. Whoever knows exactly why a thing happens will also know exactly in which cases it will happen, and what difference in the circumstances will stop it from happening. Take the simple fact that hot water cracks glass. Most people learn this empirically. They have a vague idea that hot water has a natural and unavoidable tendency to break glass, and that thin glass, being more fragile than other glass, will be broken more easily. Physical science, however, gives a very clear reason for the effect. It shows that this is just one case of the general tendency of heat to make substances expand. The crack happens when the heated glass manages to expand in spite of the colder glass it is joined to. But then we see at once that the same will not be true of thin glass vessels. The heat passes through them so quickly that the whole of the glass is heated almost equally. And so chemists regularly use thin, even glass vessels to hold or boil hot liquids, without any fear of the cracks that would be sure to happen in thick glass vessels or bottles.

The history of science would show beyond question that deduction was the key to all the greatest discoveries. Newton, who after Galileo was the chief founder of experimental science, had without doubt the greatest power of deductive thought any human being has ever had. It is striking to compare his results in optics with his results in chemistry, or rather alchemy. It is not widely known that Newton really was an alchemist. He spent days and nights experimenting in his laboratory, trying to discover the secret of turning metals into gold. But in this research everything was purely empirical, and he had no clue to guide him toward successful experiments. A few lucky guesses in his famous Queries (the questions he added at the end of his Opticks) are all that came of this labor. In optics it was quite different. There he grasped general laws, and every experiment led him to design several more and to predict their results, each more beautiful than the last. In this way he was able to lay, beyond all doubt, the foundations of the science of the spectrum, which is now producing such wonderful results. Some people may suppose that Newton, living not long after Bacon, adopted the Baconian method. But I believe there is no mention of Bacon anywhere in Newton's works, and it is certain that he did not use Bacon's method. The Principia, though it appeals to experiment and observation all the way through, is nonetheless the product of a constant, sustained effort of deductive mathematical reasoning.

What Mill has called the Deductive Method, but which I think might more fittingly be called the Combined or Complete Method, consists of using induction and deduction by turns. It can be said to have three steps:

1. Direct Induction.

2. Deduction, or, as Mill calls it, Ratiocination.

3. Verification.

The first step is a rough and simple appeal to experience, enough to give us a glimpse of the laws at work but not enough to prove them true. Taking these laws as true for the time being, we then reason out what their effects would be in other cases. A further appeal to experience then either verifies the laws we assumed or shows them to be false. In short, there are two appeals to experience, joined by reasoning in between. Newton, for example, passed a ray of sunlight through a glass prism and found that it spread out into a band of colors like those of the rainbow. He adopted the theory that white light is really a mixture of lights of different colors, which get separated as they pass through the prism. He saw that if this were true, then if he passed a single ray from the spectrum, say the yellow ray, through a second prism, it ought not to break up into different colors again. It should stay yellow, whatever was done with it afterward. When he tried it, he found that this was so. He then devised a whole series of similar experiments confirming it, which verified his theory beyond all possible doubt.

It was no mere accident that led Pascal to have a barometer carried to the top of the mountain Puy de Dôme in France. Galileo, it is true, learned by accident that water will not rise more than 33 feet in an ordinary pump, and this led him to claim that it was the limited weight of the atmosphere that made the water rise. Torricelli reasoned from this theory. Mercury is fourteen times as heavy as water, so, he saw, it ought not to rise more than one-fourteenth of that height, or about 29 or 30 inches. The experiment was tried, and it verified the theory. But it took the genius of Pascal to see that the experiment needed to be varied in another way, by carrying the mercury barometer to the top of a mountain. If the weight of the atmosphere really was what held the mercury up, the mercury ought to stand lower on the mountain than at the bottom, because only the upper part of the atmosphere would be pressing down on the mountaintop. The experiment succeeded, and completely verified the original hypothesis. The progress of the experimental sciences depends mainly on this way in which one experiment leads to others and brings to light new facts. In all probability we would never have noticed those facts if we had kept to the purely Baconian method of collecting the facts first and doing the induction afterward.

The greatest achievement of the deductive method is nothing less than the theory of gravitation, which is a perfect example of how the method works. Here the first induction was, we may suppose, the famous fall of the apple, which happened while Newton was sitting in an orchard, during the time he had left London because of the Great Plague. The fall of the apple, so we are told, led Newton to reflect that there must be some power tending to pull bodies toward the earth, and he asked himself why, in that case, the moon did not fall onto the earth. The Lancashire astronomer Horrocks had brought another fact to his mind: when a stone tied to a string is whirled around, it pulls on the string with a force often called centrifugal force. Horrocks had remarked that the planets, as they revolve around the sun, must in the same way tend to fly off from the center. Newton knew Horrocks's views, and so he may have been led to suppose that the earth's attractive force might exactly balance the moon's centrifugal tendency, and so keep the moon going around and around.

But it so happened that the world already had certain empirical laws about the motions of the planets, and without them Newton could hardly have gone any further. Kepler had spent a lifetime observing the heavenly bodies and making up hypotheses to explain their motions. Most of his ideas were wild and baseless, but a lifetime of work was rewarded when he established the three laws that bear his name. They describe the shape of the orbits the planets travel in, and the relation between the size of a planet's orbit and the time the planet takes to go around it. Newton was able to show by geometrical reasoning that if one body revolved around another, attracted toward it by a force that decreases as the square of the distance increases, it would necessarily travel in an orbit for which Kepler's laws hold true, and which would therefore be exactly like the orbits of the planets. This was a partial verification of his theory by an appeal to the results of experience. But several other thinkers had already got that far in the subject. Newton's chief claim to honor is that he kept on with his deductions and verifications until he reached complete proof. To do this, he first had to show that the moon really does fall toward the earth just as fast as a stone would if it were in the same position. Using the best figures then available for the distance of the moon, Newton calculated that the moon falls through 13 feet in one minute, but that a stone raised to the same height would fall through 15 feet. Most people would have taken this near agreement as proof of the theory. But Newton's love of certain truth set him apart even from most scientists, and the difference made him lay "aside at that time any further thoughts of this matter."

It was not until many years later (probably fifteen or sixteen) that Newton heard of more exact data for calculating the distance of the moon, and was able to explain the difference. His theory of gravitation was then verified as far as the moon was concerned. But for him this was only the start of a long series of deductive calculations, each ending in a verification. If the earth and the moon attract each other, and so do the sun and the earth, there is no reason why the sun and the moon should not attract each other too. Newton worked out the consequences of this inference, and showed that the moon would not move as if it were attracted by the earth alone, but would go sometimes faster and sometimes slower. Comparison with Flamsteed's observations of the moon showed that this was the case. Newton argued further that since the waters of the ocean are not rigidly fixed to the earth, they might attract the moon, and be attracted by it in return, independently of the rest of the earth. That would cause certain daily motions of the water exactly like the tides, and there were the tides to verify it. What makes Newton stand above every other scientist is the almost superhuman power with which he traced out the consequences of his theory geometrically and tested them again and again against experience.

What he began has been going on ever since. The positions of the moon and planets are calculated for every day on the assumption that Newton's law of gravitation is absolutely true. Every night their positions are observed, as far as possible, at Greenwich or some other observatory. When the observed position is compared with the predicted one, there is always some degree of error, and if the two ever coincided exactly it would only be by accident. The theory is never proved completely true, and never can be. But the more accurately its results are calculated, and the more perfect the astronomer's instruments become, the closer the agreement gets. So the rough observations of Kepler, and the few small facts that worked on Newton's mind, became the foundation of a theory that gave us endless ways of predicting new facts. By constant verification, as far as human accuracy can reach, it has been placed beyond all reasonable doubt.

If there were room, it could be shown that all the other great theories have followed much the same course. The wave theory (undulatory theory) of sound was in fact very nearly verified by Newton himself, although when he calculated the speed of sound from it there was again a difference, which only later research could explain. This theory no doubt suggested the matching theory of light. When Young, Fresnel and others worked that theory out, its results always turned out in the end to agree with observation. It even let mathematicians predict results that the most eager imagination could hardly have guessed, and that experimenting at random might never have revealed. Dalton's laws of equivalent proportions in chemistry, if not his atomic theory, were founded on experiments made with the simplest and roughest apparatus, yet results deduced from them are verified every day in the most delicate processes of modern chemical analysis. The still more recent theory of the Conservation of Energy had been vaguely anticipated by Bacon, Rumford, Montgolfier, Seguin, Mayer and possibly others. Mr. Joule brought it to the test of experimental verification in some of the most beautiful and decisive experiments on record. It will be a long time before scientists have traced out all the consequences of this grand principle, but the way it agrees with the facts already puts it far beyond doubt.

I think it will now be clear that, although observation and induction must always be the ground of all certain knowledge of nature, on their own they could never have produced the results of modern science. A person who merely collects and digests facts will seldom come to understand their laws. A person who builds a theory and is satisfied with his own deductions from it, as Descartes did, will only astonish the world with his misused genius. The best student of science is the one who, with a plentiful store of theories and fancies, has the greatest power of foreseeing their consequences, the greatest care in comparing them with facts beyond doubt, and the greatest honesty in admitting the ninety-nine mistakes he has made on the way to the one true law of nature.

Questions and Exercises

2. What are the three steps of the Deductive Method?

3. Trace some of the successive steps in the progress of the theory of gravitation, showing that it was established by this method.
