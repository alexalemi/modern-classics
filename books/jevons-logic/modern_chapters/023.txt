Lesson 23: Boole's System of Logic

In an elementary book like this one, it would be quite impossible to give any real idea of the system of indirect inference first discovered by the late George Boole, Professor of Mathematics at Queen's College, Cork. As I mentioned in the last lesson, his system was founded on the Quantification of the Predicate. But Boole regarded Logic as a branch of Mathematics, and he believed he could reach every possible inference by the principles of algebra. The process he actually used is very obscure and difficult, and so far hardly anyone has tried to bring it into elementary textbooks of Logic.

I have been able to reach exactly the same results as Boole without using any mathematics at all. The very simple process I am going to describe can hardly be called Boole's logic in the strict sense, but it is very similar to it, and it can prove everything Boole proved. This Method of Indirect Inference is founded on the three primary Laws of Thought stated in Lesson 14. Any reader who thought those laws were useless truisms may be surprised to find how wide and elegant a system of deduction can be built on them.

The law of excluded middle lets us assert that anything must either have a given quality or not have it. If the thing is iron, say, and the quality is combustibility, anyone can see that

	"Iron is either combustible or incombustible."

We can repeat this division into alternatives as often as we like. Let Book be the class of things to be divided, and let English and Scientific be two qualities. Then any book must be either English or not English. An English book, in turn, must be either Scientific or not Scientific, and the same goes for books that are not English. So we can divide books straight away into four classes:

	Books, English and Scientific.
	Books, English and not-Scientific.
	Books, not-English and Scientific.
	Books, not-English and not-Scientific.

We may call this an exhaustive division of the class Books. There is no possible book that does not fall into one or other of these four divisions, for the simple reason that if it does not fall into any of the first three, it must fall into the last. The process can go on without end, as long as anyone can suggest a new circumstance to divide by. We might divide each class again according to whether the books are octavo (a standard mid-sized format) or not octavo, bound or unbound, published in London or elsewhere, and so on. This process of twofold division is really the process of Dichotomy described in Lesson 12. We shall call it the development of a term, because it always lets us develop the greatest number of alternatives that need to be considered.

As a general rule, it is unlikely that all the alternatives unfolded, or developed, in this way can actually exist. The next step is to find out how many do or may exist. The Law of Contradiction asserts that nothing can combine contradictory attributes or qualities, so if we meet any term that contradicts itself in this way, we are entitled to strike it out of the list at once. Now take our old example of a syllogism:

	Iron is a metal;
	All metals are elements;
	Therefore iron is an element.

We can easily prove this conclusion by the indirect method. If we develop the term iron, we get four alternatives:

	Iron, metal, element.
	Iron, metal, not-element.
	Iron, not-metal, element.
	Iron, not-metal, not-element.

Now compare each of these alternatives with the premises of the syllogism, and it becomes clear that several of them cannot exist. We are told that iron is a metal, so no class of things "iron, not-metal" can exist. The first premise therefore lets us strike out both of the last two alternatives, since they combine iron with not-metal. The second alternative combines metal with not-element. But the second premise tells us that "all metals are elements," so that one must be struck out too. Only one alternative remains that can exist if the premises are true. And since there cannot possibly be any alternatives beyond the ones we have considered, it follows by strict demonstration that iron occurs only together with the qualities of metal and element; in short, that iron is an element.

But we can prove more than the ordinary syllogistic conclusion. We can prove any other conclusion that can be drawn from the same premises. In fact, the syllogistic conclusion is usually only one of many that can be got from a given set of premises. Suppose, for instance, that we want to know what the term or class not-element is like, as far as the premises we have just used can tell us. We can develop the alternatives of this term just as we did those of iron, and we get the following:

	Not-element, iron, metal.
	Not-element, iron, not-metal.
	Not-element, not-iron, metal.
	Not-element, not-iron, not-metal.

Compare these combinations with the premises, as before. The first plainly cannot exist, because all metals are elements, and the third cannot exist for the same reason. The second is excluded as well, because iron is a metal and cannot exist together with the qualities of not-metal. So only one combination remains to represent the class we wanted, namely

	Not-element, not-iron, not-metal.

So the premises teach us that every not-element is not a metal and is not iron.

For another example of this kind of deduction, I will take a case of the Disjunctive Syllogism in the negative mood:

	A fungus is either plant or animal,
	A fungus is not an animal;
	Therefore it is a plant.

If we develop all the possible ways in which fungus, plant and animal can be combined, we get, for the term fungus:

	(1) Fungus, plant, animal.
	(2) Fungus, plant, not-animal.
	(3) Fungus, not-plant, animal.
	(4) Fungus, not-plant, not-animal.

Of these, the 4th cannot exist, because according to the premise a fungus must be a plant, or, if not a plant, an animal. The 1st and 3rd cannot exist either, because the minor premise tells us that a fungus is not an animal. That leaves only the second combination,

	Fungus, plant, not-animal,

and from it we learn the syllogistic conclusion that "a fungus is a plant."

The chief strength of this way of deducing is that it is not tied to a fixed series of forms, as the syllogism is. Without any extra rules, it applies to every kind of proposition or problem that can be conceived and stated. There may be any number of premises, and they may contain any number of terms. To get any possible inference, all we have to do is develop the term we want into all its alternatives and then see how many of them agree with the premises. Whatever is left after this examination is, necessarily, the description of the term. The method's only drawback is that the number of alternatives to examine grows very fast as the number of terms grows, and writing them all out soon becomes tedious. We can shorten the work by putting single letters in place of the terms, somewhat as in algebra. We may take A, B, C, D, and so on, to stand for the affirmative terms, and a, b, c, d, and so on, for the corresponding negative ones. For a first example, take the premises:

	Organic substance is either vegetable or animal.
	Vegetable substance consists mainly of carbon, hydrogen, and nitrogen.
	Animal substance consists mainly of carbon, hydrogen, and nitrogen.

It would take a long time to write out all the combinations of the four terms that occur here. But if we put letters in place of them, like this,

	A = organic substance,
	B = vegetable substance,
	C = animal substance,
	D = consisting mainly of carbon, hydrogen, and nitrogen,

we can easily set out all the combinations that can belong to the term A:

	(1) | ABCD | AbCD | (5)
	(2) | ABCd | AbCd | (6)
	(3) | ABcD | AbcD | (7)
	(4) | ABcd | Abcd | (8)

Now the premises amount to the statements that

	A must be either B or C,
	B must be D,
	C must be D.

Combinations (7) and (8) are inconsistent with the first premise; combinations (2) and (4) with the second premise; and (6) is inconsistent with the third premise. That leaves only

	ABCD
	ABcD
	AbCD.

From this we learn at once that "organic substance (A) always consists mainly of carbon, hydrogen and nitrogen," because it always occurs together with D. The reader may notice that the combination ABCD implies that an organic substance may be both vegetable (B) and animal (C). If the first premise is taken to mean that this is impossible, then of course this combination should be struck out as well. Whether the alternatives of a disjunctive proposition can exist together or not is an unsettled question (see Lesson 19), but I much prefer the view that they can. And as a matter of fact, it is quite likely that there are very simple kinds of living things that cannot be said for certain to be only vegetable or only animal, but share the nature of both.

To show the power of this system on a harder problem, let us take the premises Boole treated in his Laws of Thought, p. 125:

"Similar figures consist of all whose corresponding angles are equal, and whose corresponding sides are proportional.

Triangles whose corresponding angles are equal have their corresponding sides proportional; and vice versa.

Triangles whose corresponding sides are proportional have their corresponding angles equal."

Now suppose we choose our letters as follows:

	A = similar figure,
	B = triangle,
	C = having corresponding angles equal,
	D = having corresponding sides proportional.

Then the premises turn out to amount to the statements that

	A is identical with CD,

and that

	BC is identical with BD.

In other words, all A's must be CD's, and CD's must be A's; all BC's must be BD's, and all BD's must be BC's.

The letters can be combined in 16 possible ways, shown in the following table:

	ABCD | aBCD
	ABCd | aBCd
	ABcD | aBcD
	ABcd | aBcd
	AbCD | abCD
	AbCd | abCd
	AbcD | abcD
	Abcd | abcd

Comparing each of these combinations with the premises, we see that ABCd, ABcD, ABcd and others must be struck out, because every A has to be CD as well. The combinations aBCD and abCD are struck out because every CD must also be A. Again, aBCd is inconsistent with the condition that every BC must also be BD. If the reader carefully carries the same examination through to the end, only six combinations will be left that agree with all the premises:

	ABCD | aBcd
	AbCD | abCd
	"" | abcD
	"" | abcd

From these combinations we can draw any description we like of the classes of things that agree with the premises. The class A, or similar figures, is represented by only two combinations, or alternatives. The negative class a, or dissimilar figures, is represented by four. So we may draw the following conclusion: "Dissimilar figures consist of all triangles that do not have their corresponding angles equal and their sides proportional (aBcd), and of all figures that are not triangles and that have either their angles equal but their sides not proportional (abCd), or their corresponding sides proportional but their angles not equal (abcD), or neither their corresponding angles equal nor their corresponding sides proportional (abcd)."

Anyone who works this method of inference soon sees that it runs in a very simple, mechanical way, and that its only drawback is the large number of alternatives, or combinations, to be examined. So I have invented several ways of cutting down the labor. The simplest is to engrave the series of 16 combinations in the table above, which turn up again and again in problems, along with larger and smaller sets, on an ordinary writing slate. Then the excluded combinations can easily be struck out with an ordinary slate pencil, and the slate can still be used again for any later logical question. A second device, which I have called the "Logical abacus," is made by printing the letters on slips of wood fitted with pins, arranged so that any part or class of the combinations can be picked out mechanically with very little trouble. A logical problem is then solved by the hand rather than by the head. More recently, though, I have reduced the system to a completely mechanical form, and so built the whole indirect process of inference into what may be called a Logical Machine. On the front of the machine are some movable wooden rods carrying the set of 16 combinations of letters shown in the table above. At the foot are 21 keys like those of a piano. Eight keys toward the left are marked with the letters A, a, B, b, C, c, D, d, and they stand for these terms when they occur in the subject of a proposition. Eight more keys toward the right stand for the same letters, or terms, when they occur in the predicate. The copula of a proposition is represented by a key in the middle of the row, and the full stop by a key at the far right. Two other keys serve for the disjunctive conjunction or, one for when it occurs in the subject and one for when it occurs in the predicate. Now let the letters stand for the terms of a syllogism, or of any other logical argument, and press the keys of the instrument in exactly the order of the words of the premises. The 16 combinations will be sorted and arranged by this so that at the end only the possible combinations remain in view. Any question can then be put to the machine, and the combinations that remain will give an infallible answer. So the internal workings of the machine actually perform the work of inference that, in Boole's system, was done by a very complicated mathematical calculation. I should add that there is one last key at the far left, which wipes out all the previous operations and puts all the combinations back in their original places, so that the machine is ready for a new problem.

An account of this logical machine can be found in the Proceedings of the Royal Society for January 20, 1870; on that day the machine was shown working to the Fellows of the Society. The principles of the method of inference described here are set out more fully in The Substitution of Similars and in the Principles of Science, which I published in 1869 and 1874. I may add that each of these works contains certain views about the real nature of the process of inference that I do not think it wise to bring into an elementary book like this one, because they are speculative. The process of inference I have taken from Boole's system, on the other hand, is so self-evident, and so clearly proved true by being reduced to a mechanical form, that I do not hesitate to bring it to the reader's attention.

Questions and Exercises

1. Apply this system of inference to prove the syllogisms in Cesare and Camestres given in Lesson 16.

2. Show that if no A's are B's, then no B's are A's; and that if all A's are all B's, then all not-A's are all not-B's.

3. Develop the term substance with respect to the terms vegetable, animal, organic. Then pick out the combinations that agree with these premises: "What is vegetable is not animal but is organic; what is animal is organic."

4. Test whether this argument is valid: "Good always triumphs, and vice always fails; therefore the victor cannot be wrong, nor the vanquished right."

5. It is known of a certain class of things that:

(1) Where the quality A is, B is not.

(2) Where B is, and only where B is, C and D are. What can we infer from these premises about the class of things in which A is not present but C is present?

6. If all A's are B's, all B's are C's, and all C's are D's, show that all A's are D's, and that all not-D's are not A's.
