Lesson 12: The Predicables, Division, and Definition

Before we go any further, the reader should get a precise grasp of a few logical terms known as the Predicables. These are the kinds of terms, or attributes, that can always be predicated of any subject. There are five of them: genus, species, difference, property, and accident. Used properly, they are extremely useful and important in logic. Ancient writers gave the predicables many different and subtle meanings, and it would be neither possible nor worthwhile to try to describe them all in this book. All we can give here is the simplest and most useful view of the subject.

Any class of things can be called a genus (from the Greek γένος, genos, race or kind) if we think of it as made up of two or more species. "Element" is a genus when we think of it as divided into the two species "metallic and non-metallic." Triangle is a genus with respect to the species acute-angled, right-angled, and obtuse-angled.

A species, on the other hand, is any class that we think of as forming part of the next larger class. So the terms genus and species are relative to each other. The genus is the larger class that gets divided, and the species are the two or more smaller classes it is divided into.

It is essential, though, to take these words in the two senses of extension and intension. From the explanation of those two senses in Lesson 5, it should be clear that the extent of a genus or species is simply the number of individuals it includes, and that there will always be fewer individuals in the species than in the genus. In extent, the genus book includes all books of every size, language, and subject. If we divide it by size, the species of book are folio, quarto, octavo, duodecimo, and so on (the old printers' names for book sizes, from the number of times the printed sheet was folded). Each of these species, of course, contains far fewer individual books than the whole genus.

In intension, the genus means not the individual things it contains but the sum of the qualities common to all those things, enough to mark them out clearly from other classes. In the same way, the species means the sum of the qualities common to all the individuals in that species, enough to mark them out from the rest of the genus as well as from everything else. So there must be more qualities implied in the meaning of the species than in the meaning of the genus. The species must contain all the qualities of the genus, plus some extra quality or qualities that tell the species apart from one another. These extra qualities form the difference. The difference can be defined as the quality, or sum of qualities, that marks out one part of a genus from the other part or parts. The difference (in Latin differentia, in Greek διαφορά, diaphora) has a meaning only in intension. When we use all these terms purely in intension, we can say that the difference added to the genus makes the species. If "building" is the genus, for example, and we add the difference "used for a dwelling," we get the species "house." If we take "triangle" as the genus, it means the sum of the qualities "three-sided rectilineal figure" (a figure bounded by straight lines). If we add the quality "having two sides equal," we get the species "isosceles triangle."

It is easy to see that the same class of things can be a genus and a species at the same time, depending on whether we think of it as divided into smaller classes or as part of a larger class. Triangle, which is a genus with respect to isosceles triangle, is a species with respect to right-lined geometrical figures. House is a species of building, but a genus with respect to mansion, cottage, villa, and the other kinds of houses. In fact we can have an almost endless chain of genera and species, each class being a species of the class just above it and a genus with respect to the class just below. Take the genus British subject. It has the species Born in the United Kingdom, Colonial-born, and Naturalized. Each of these becomes a genus with respect to the species male and female. Each of those species can be divided again into adult and minor; educated and uneducated; employed in some occupation or unemployed; self-supporting, supported by friends, or a pauper (living on public poor relief); and so on. The subdivision can go on like this until we reach a class so narrow in extent that it cannot be divided any further except into individuals. That class is called the lowest species, or infima species. All the genera and species in between are called subaltern (from the Latin sub, under, and alter, the other of two), because they stand one under another. If there is a genus that is not thought of as a species, that is, as part of any higher genus, it is called the summum genus, the highest genus, or genus generalissimum, the most general genus.

It is doubtful whether we can really put any such upper limit on the chain of classes. The class British subject is certainly not an absolute summum genus, since it is only a species of man, which is a species of animal, living being, part of the earth, substance, and so on. If there were any real summum genus, it would probably be "Being," or "Thing," or "Object that can be conceived." But we can usefully use the term to mean the highest class of things taken in by any science or classification. So "material substance" is the summum genus that the science of chemistry examines, and "inhabitant of the United Kingdom" is the summum genus that the British census counts and classifies. Logical terms are only a species of words or phrases, but they are the summum genus as far as logic is concerned, because logic has nothing to do with the parts of speech or with the relations of words, syllables, and letters that grammarians study.

Several very useful expressions come from the words genus and species. When a thing is so peculiar and so unlike other things that it can't easily be put into one class with them, we say it is sui generis, or "of its own genus." The rings of Saturn, for instance, are so different from anything else among the heavenly bodies that they can fairly be called sui generis. In zoology, the Ornithorhynchus, or Australian duck-bill (the platypus), the Amphioxus (the lancelet), and a few other animals are so peculiar that they can be called sui generis. When a substance is the same in all its parts, or when a number of things are all alike, we say they are homogeneous (from the Greek ὁμός, homos, like, and γένος, genos, kind), that is, of the same nature. Otherwise they can be called heterogeneous (from the Greek ἕτερος, heteros, other).

We must carefully separate the purely logical use of genus and species from their special use in natural history. There, a species is a class of plants or animals supposed to have descended from common parents and to be the narrowest class that has a fixed form, and the genus is the next class up. But if we accept Darwin's theory of the origin of species, this definition of species becomes entirely illusory, because according to that theory different genera and species must have descended from common parents. Species then means just an arbitrary amount of resemblance that naturalists choose to settle on, and that can't be defined any more exactly. So this use of the word has nothing whatever to do with the logical use, in which any class of things at all is a species, as long as we think of it as part of a wider class, or genus.

The fourth of the Predicables is Property (in Latin proprium, in Greek ἴδιον, idion, one's own). It is hard to define in a way that is free from objections and difficulties, but perhaps the best description is this: a property is any quality that is common to the whole of a class but is not needed to mark that class out from other classes. For example, it is a property of the genus "triangle" that its three inside angles add up to two right angles. This is a very remarkable fact, and it is always true of triangles. But it is not made part of the genus, and it is not used in defining a triangle, because having three straight sides is enough of a mark. Geometrical figures have a great many properties. The Second Book of Euclid is taken up with proving a few properties of rectangles, and the Third Book does the same for circles. As we usually use the word, a property may or may not belong to other things as well as the ones in question. Some properties of the circle may also belong to the ellipse, and some properties of human beings, such as the power of memory or of anger, may belong to other animals too.

Logicians have invented various subtle divisions of properties, but it is enough to say this. A peculiar property is one that belongs to the whole of a class and to that class only, as laughter is supposed to belong only to human beings; the property of enclosing the greatest area within a boundary of a given length is peculiar to circles. A property that is not peculiar may belong to other classes of things as well as to the class it is called a property of. We can also distinguish the Generic Property, which belongs to the whole of the genus, from the Specific Property, which belongs to the whole of a lowest species.

Last, an accident (in Latin accidens, in Greek συμβεβηκός, symbebekos) is any quality that may belong to a class or not, as the case may be, without affecting the class's other qualities. The word means what befalls or happens by chance, and has no necessary connection with the nature of a thing. The absolute size of a triangle, for example, is a pure accident as far as its geometrical properties go. Whether the side of a triangle is 1/10 of an inch or a million miles, whatever Euclid proves true of one is true of the other. A man's birthplace is an accident with respect to him, and so are the clothes he is wearing, the position he is resting in, and so on. Some writers distinguish separable and inseparable accidents. The clothes a man is wearing are a separable accident, because they can be changed, and so can his position and many other circumstances. But his birthplace, his height, his first name, and so on are inseparable accidents, because they can never be changed, even though they have no necessary or important connection with his general character.

To illustrate part of the scheme of classification described under the name of the Predicables, I will give here, as logic textbooks usually do, the Tree of Porphyry. It is a sort of model classification invented by one of the early Greek logicians, Porphyrius (Porphyry). I have simplified the usual form of it by translating the Latin names and leaving out unnecessary words.

In this Tree we see a succession of genera and species: Substance, Body, Living Being, Animal, and Man. Of these, Substance is the summum genus, because it is not treated as a species of any higher class. Man is the infima species, because it is a class that is not divided into any lower class, only into individuals, and the usual individuals to name are Socrates and Plato. Body, Living Being, and Animal are called subaltern genera and species, because each one is a species with respect to the next genus up and a genus with respect to the next species down. The qualities meant by the adjectives Corporeal, Animate, Sensible (that is, capable of feeling), and Rational are the successive differences that divide each genus into species. Notice that the negative parts of the genera, namely Incorporeal Substance, Inanimate Body, and so on, could also be subdivided. That has not been done, to avoid cluttering the figure.

Logical division is the name for the process by which we pick out the species a genus is made up of. We are said to divide the genus "book," for instance, when we think of it as made up of the groups folio, quarto, octavo, duodecimo books, and so on. The size of the books is in this case the ground, basis, or principle of division, usually called the Fundamentum Divisionis. For a quality or circumstance to serve as the basis of a division, it must be present in some members and absent in others, or it must vary among the different species the genus contains. A generic property, of course, is present in the whole of the genus, so it can't be used for dividing. A sound and useful division must obey three rules:

1. The constituent species must exclude each other.

2. The constituent species, added together, must be equal to the genus.

3. The division must be founded on one principle or basis.

It would obviously be absurd to divide books into folio, quarto, French, German, and dictionaries. These species overlap: there may be French or German dictionaries that happen to be quarto or folio, and so belong to three different species at once. A division like this is called a Cross Division, because it uses more than one principle of division, and so the species cross one another and cause confusion. If I divided rectilineal figures into triangles, parallelograms, rectangles, and polygons of more than four sides, I would commit every possible fault in a single division. The species parallelogram and rectangle don't exclude each other, since all rectangles must be parallelograms. The species added together don't make up the whole genus rectilineal figure, since irregular four-sided figures that are not parallelograms have been left out. And there are three principles of division at work: the number of sides, the directions of those sides, and the angles they contain. But when we subdivide, treating each species as a genus that can be split up further, we may, and in fact must, use a new principle of division each time. From a logical point of view, the most perfect divisions come from dividing each genus again and again into two species by a difference. The Tree of Porphyry is an example of this. The process is called Dichotomy (from the Greek δίχα, dicha, in two, and τέμνω, temno, to cut). It is also called Exhaustive Division, because it always and necessarily obeys the second rule and provides a place for every possible thing. By a Law of Thought that we will consider in Lesson 14, everything must either have a given quality or not have it, so it must fall into one part or the other of the genus. This process of exhaustive division will turn out to be quite important in Lesson 23. In practice, though, it is by no means always necessary or convenient. We know beyond all doubt that every figure must have 3, 4, 5, 6, or more sides, and that no figure can belong to more than one of these groups. So it is much better to list the parts straight away: Triangle, Quadrilateral, Pentagon, Hexagon, and so on. Again, it would be very awkward to divide the counties of England into Middlesex and not-Middlesex, the second of these into Surrey and not-Surrey, and that again into Kent and not-Kent. Dichotomy is useless in cases like these, and even looks absurd, because a much shorter division lets us obey the rules of division with certainty. But in less certain branches of knowledge, our divisions can never be safe from a possible oversight unless they proceed by dichotomy. Suppose we divide the population of the world into three branches, Aryan (Indo-European), Semitic, and Turanian, as the scholars of the 1870s did. Some race might eventually be discovered that is distinct from all of these, and for which no place has been provided. But suppose we had proceeded like this:

Then the new race would obviously fall into the last group, the one that is neither Aryan, Semitic, nor Turanian. All the divisions of naturalists are exposed to this inconvenience. If we divide Vertebrate Animals into Mammalia, Birds, Reptiles, and Fish, a new form might be discovered at any time that belongs to none of these, and so upsets the division.

One more precaution is needed in Division: don't go straight from a high or wide genus to a low or narrow species. As the saying goes, divisio non faciat saltum, "the division should not make a leap." The species should always be those of the proximate genus, the genus immediately above them. It would obviously be awkward, for instance, to begin by dividing geometrical figures into those that have parallel sides and those that don't. Yet this principle of division is perfectly proper when it is applied to the proximate genus.

Logical division must not be confused with physical division, or Partition, in which an individual object, such as a tree, is thought of as made up of its separate parts: root, trunk, branches, leaves, and so on. There is even a third, distinct process, called Metaphysical Division, which consists in thinking of a thing as a collection of qualities and separating these in our minds, as when we distinguish the shape, color, taste, and smell of an orange.

Closely connected with the subject of this Lesson is the process of Logical Definition, by which we settle the common qualities, or marks, of the objects that belong to a given class. A definition must give the briefest possible statement of the qualities that are enough to distinguish the class from other classes and to fix its position in the general classification of our ideas. We can do this by treating the class as a species and giving the proximate genus and the difference. Here the word genus is used in its intensive meaning: it stands for the qualities that belong to everything in the genus and are enough to mark those things out. Since the difference then marks out the part of the genus we want, we get a perfect definition of the species. But we should be careful not to put any superfluous marks into a definition. If the extra marks are accidents and do not belong to the whole class, the definition is wrongly narrowed, as it would be if we defined Quadrilateral Figures as figures with four equal sides. If the extra marks belong to everything being defined, they are Properties, and they make no difference to the definition at all. Suppose I define parallelograms as "four-sided rectilineal figures, with the opposite sides equal and parallel, and the opposite angles equal." I have added two properties, the equality of the opposite sides and the equality of the opposite angles. These follow necessarily from the sides being parallel, and they only make the definition more complicated without making it any more precise.

Logic books usually give certain rules that sum up the precautions needed in definition.

1. A definition should state the essential attributes of the species defined. As far as the expression "essential attributes" can be given any exact meaning, it means, as explained above, the proximate genus and the difference.

2. A definition must not contain the name defined. The purpose of a definition is to make the species known, and as long as the species is not known, it can't serve to make itself known. When this rule is broken, there is said to be circulus in definiendo, or "a circle in defining," because the definition brings us right back round to the word we started from. This fault is usually committed by putting into the definition a word that is really a synonym of the name being defined. Examples would be defining "Plant" as "an organized being possessing vegetable life," or elements as simple substances, since vegetable is really equivalent to plant, and simple to elementary. If I defined metals as "substances possessing metallic luster," I would either be committing this fault, or else using the term metallic luster in a sense that would let in other substances too, and so breaking the next rule.

3. The definition must be exactly equivalent to the species defined. That is, it must be an expression whose denotation is neither narrower nor wider than the species, so that it takes in exactly the same objects. In short, the definition must denote the species, the whole species, and nothing but the species, and this can really be taken as a description of what a definition is.

4. A definition must not be expressed in obscure, figurative, or ambiguous language. In other words, every term used in the definition must be exactly known. Otherwise the purpose of the definition, which is to make us acquainted with the sufficient marks of the species, is obviously defeated. There is no worse logical fault than to define ignotum per ignotius, the unknown by the still more unknown. Aristotle's definition of the soul as "The Entelechy, or first form of an organized body which has potential life," certainly seems open to this objection.

5. Last, a definition must not be negative where it can be affirmative. This rule, though, often does not apply, and it is by no means always binding.

On the nature of Classification and the five Predicables, read Mr. J. S. Mill, System of Logic, Book I. Chap. VII. For the ancient Scholastic views on Definition, see Mansel's Artis Logicae Rudimenta (Aldrich), App. Note C.

Questions and Exercises

1. Define each of the five predicables.

2. In what sense can we say that the genus is part of the species, and in what sense that the species is part of the genus?

3. From the terms in question 6 of Lesson 5, pick out the ones that are genera, species, highest genera, or lowest species of other terms.

4. Explain the expressions sui generis, homogeneous, heterogeneous, summum genus, infima species, Tree of Porphyry.

5. Name a property and an accident of each of the following classes: Circle, Planet, Bird, Member of Parliament, Ruminant Animal.

6. What are the rules of correct logical division?

7. The first name in each of the following series of terms is a class. Divide and subdivide it so as to include all the smaller classes listed after it, in accordance with the laws of division.

	(1) People: Laity, Aliens, Naturalized Subjects, Peers, Natural-born Subjects, Clergy, Baronets, Commons.
	(2) Triangle: Equiangular, Isosceles, Right-angled, Scalene, Obtuse-angled.
	(3) Reasoning: Induction (Imperfect), Deduction, Mediate Inference, Induction, Hypothetical Syllogism, Disjunctive Syllogism.

8. Divide any of the following classes: Governments, Sciences, Logical terms, Propositions.

9. What does a logical definition consist of?

10. What are the rules of correct definition?

11. Which rules do the following definitions break?

(1) Life is the sum of the vital functions.

(2) Genus is the material part of the species.

(3) Illative conversion is that in which the truth of the converse can be inferred from that of the convertend.

(4) Mineral substances are those which have not been produced by the powers of vegetable or animal life.

(5) An equilateral triangle is a triangle whose sides and angles are respectively equal.

(6) An acute-angled triangle is one which has an acute angle.
