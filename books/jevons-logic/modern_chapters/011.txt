Lesson 11: Logical Analysis of Sentences

The propositions we meet in ordinary writing and speech seldom show the simple form we have seen to be the proper logical construction: a subject, a copula and a predicate joined together. Often the copula is mixed up with the predicate. Often, too, several propositions are packed into one grammatical sentence. For a full account of how sentences are analyzed, I will point the reader to several excellent little books on the subject. Here I will only try to sketch the different ways a sentence can be built.

Footnote: W. S. Dalgleish's Grammatical Analysis, or J. D. Morell's Analysis of Sentences. Alex. Bain's English Composition and Rhetoric, pp. 91–117, deals with the construction of sentences.

In ordinary language the copula is so often joined to the predicate that grammarians treat a proposition as having only two parts: the subject, and the predicate, or verb. Take the proposition "The sun rises." It seems to contain nothing but a subject, "the sun," and a predicate, "rises." But it really means the same as "the sun is rising," where the copula is plainly shown. So we will treat the verb, or grammatical predicate, as containing both the copula and the logical predicate. In Latin a single word can hold all three parts of a proposition, as in sum, "I am." Caesar's famous exclamation Veni, vidi, vici, "I came, I saw, I conquered," holds three separate and complete propositions in three words. But these odd cases arise only because the parts of the proposition have been blended together and disguised inside one word. In the Latin sum, the letter m is a leftover of the pronoun me, which is the real subject of the proposition. If we knew the grammar of any language perfectly, it would probably not contradict the logical view of a sentence. Instead it would probably explain how the separate parts of the complete proposition got blended together and seemingly lost, just as the words will and not are blended in the everyday "I won't."

A grammatical sentence can contain any number of separate propositions. They could be pulled apart, but they are combined to save words. In the sentence

	"Art is long and Time is fleeting,"

there are two separate subjects, Art and Time, and two predicates, "long" and "fleeting." So we simply have two propositions joined by the conjunction and. But we can also have several separate subjects with one and the same predicate, as in

	"Thirty days hath September,
	April, June, and November."

In this well-known couplet the predicate, "having thirty days," is put first for emphasis, and there are four subjects, September, April and the rest, and the predicate is affirmed of each of them. So these lines really contain four separate propositions.

Or there can be one subject with several predicates, so that several different propositions are asserted without repeating the subject and the copula. The sentence

"Nitrogen is a colourless, tasteless, inodorous gas, slightly lighter than air," has only one subject, Nitrogen, but four or five predicates ("inodorous" means odorless). It plainly means the same as "Nitrogen is colourless," "Nitrogen is tasteless," "Nitrogen is a gas," and so on.

Last, we can have several subjects and several predicates all combined in one sentence with only one copula, so that each predicate is asserted of each subject. Then a great many separate propositions are squeezed into one short sentence. In the sentence "Iron, Copper, Lead and Zinc are abundant, cheap and useful metals," we clearly have four subjects, and we can say we have four predicates: "abundant," "cheap," "useful" and "metal." Nothing stops us from applying each predicate to each subject, so the sentence really contains 16 separate propositions in only 11 words: "Iron is abundant," "Iron is cheap," "Copper is abundant," "Copper is cheap," and so on. In this curious sentence,

"Hearts, tongues, figures, scribes, bards, poets, cannot think, speak, cast, write, sing, number, his love to Antony," Shakespeare has joined six subjects and six predicates, or verbs, so that, strictly speaking, there are six times six, or thirty-six, propositions.

Footnote: Antony and Cleopatra, Act III. Sc. i.

In all the cases above, the sentence is said to be compound, and the separate propositions combined in it are said to be coordinate with each other, that is, of the same order or rank. They are called that because they do not depend on each other or affect each other's truth in any way. There is no need to state how abundant, cheap or useful iron is in the same sentence as the qualities of copper, lead or zinc. But since the predicates happen to be the same, we save a good deal of trouble in speaking or writing by attaching as many subjects as we can to the same set of predicates. It is truly said that brevity is the soul of wit, and one of the great arts of writing is to pack as many statements as possible into the fewest words, as long as the meaning does not get muddled.

Propositions are combined in a completely different way when one proposition forms part of the subject or the predicate of another. In the sentence "The man who is upright need not fear accusation," there are two verbs and two propositions, but one of them only describes the subject of the other. "Who is upright" clearly limits the predicate "need not fear accusation" to one part of the class "man." The meaning of the whole sentence could be put like this:

	"The upright man need not fear accusation."

Here we can clearly see that the clause, or apparent proposition, has taken the place of an adjective. A clause or proposition like this is called subordinate, because it only helps to build the principal sentence and has no meaning apart from it. Any sentence that contains a subordinate clause is said to be complex. Almost any part of a sentence can be replaced by a subordinate clause in this way. In "Oxygen and Nitrogen are the gases which form the largest part of the atmosphere," a subordinate clause makes up part of the predicate, and the meaning could be expressed almost as well like this: "Oxygen and Nitrogen are the gases forming the largest part of the atmosphere."

Take a modal proposition (see Lesson 8), one that states the manner in which the predicate belongs to the subject. Its mode can be expressed either by an adverb or by a subordinate clause. "As a man lives so he dies" is a proposition of this kind. It means "a man dies as he lives," and "as he lives" does the job of an adverb: if he lives well, he dies well; if he lives badly, he dies badly. Adverbs or adverbial clauses can also specify the time, the place, or any other circumstance the truth of the main proposition depends on.

Assuming the reader knows the grammatical terms involved, we can now list the parts that even the most complex sentence must be made of. The subject may be made up of:

1. A noun, as in "The Queen reigns."

2. A pronoun, as in "She reigns."

3. An adjective turned into a noun, as in "The brave are honored."

4. A gerund, as in "Seeing is believing."

5. An infinitive, as in "To see is to believe."

6. A subordinate clause, as in "Who falls from virtue is lost."

The subject may be qualified or restricted by joining an attribute to it, and the attribute can be expressed in any of these ways:

1. An adjective, as in "Fresh air is wholesome."

2. A participle, as in "Falling stars are often seen."

3. A noun used as an adjective, as in "Iron ships are now much employed."

4. A noun and a preposition, as in "ships of iron are now much employed."

5. A possessive case, as in "Chatham's son was the great minister Pitt."

6. A noun in apposition, as in "The Metropolis London is the most populous of cities."

7. A gerund or a dative infinitive (an infinitive saying what something is for), as in "The desire to go abroad is common in Englishmen."

The predicate is almost always made up of a verb, which often has an object or some qualifying words. So it may be:

1. A simple tense of a complete verb, as in "The sun rises."

2. A compound tense, as in "The sun has risen."

3. An incomplete verb and its complement, as in "The sea seems rough."

4. The verb "to be" and an adjective, as in "Time is fleeting."

5. A verb with an object, as in "Warmth melts ice."

6. A verb with an adverbial, as in "The snow falls thickly."

The object of a verb is usually a noun or a pronoun, but any of the six kinds of expression that can serve as a subject can also serve as an object.

The adverbial that qualifies a verb, and expresses the manner, time, place or some other circumstance affecting the proposition, may be:

1. An adverb, as in "The days pass slowly."

2. A noun and a preposition, as in "The resolution was passed by a large majority."

3. An absolute phrase, as in "The snow melts, the sun having risen."

4. A dative infinitive, as in "She stoops to conquer."

5. Any phrase that does the work of an adverb, as in "The dividends are paid twice a year."

People have invented various ways of showing how sentences are built, using symbols and names for the different parts. But I believe that by far the simplest and most effective way is to show the construction as a diagram. Any two or more parts of a sentence that are coordinate with each other, or that stand in the same relation to some other part, are written side by side and joined by a bracket. So the diagram

shows clearly that there are four coordinate subjects and four coordinate predicates in the example we took earlier.

Whenever one part of a sentence is subordinate to another part, we can connect the two with a line drawn in whatever direction is convenient. The analysis of the following sentence is easily shown by the diagram below it:

"No one who is a lover of money, a lover of pleasure, and a lover of glory, is likewise a lover of mankind; but only he who is a lover of virtue."

[Figure lover]

We see that the sentence is both compound and complex. That is, it contains two principal coordinate propositions with a shared predicate, "a lover of mankind." The first proposition is negative, and its subject is described by three subordinate clauses. The second proposition is affirmative and has one subordinate clause.

I will end this rather long lesson by analyzing a few sentences. The first is made up of some remarkably complex lines from a poem by Burbidge:

	"He who metes, as we should mete,
	Could we His insight use, shall most approve,
	Not that which fills most space in earthly eyes,
	But what—though Time scarce note it as he flies—
	Fills, like this little daisy at my feet,
	Its function best of diligence in love."

[Figure burbidge]

	"Most sweet it is with unuplifted eyes
	    To pace the ground, if path there be or none,
	While a fair region round the traveller lies
	    Which he forbears again to look upon;
	Pleased rather with some soft ideal scene,
	    The work of fancy, or some happy tone
	Of meditation slipping in between,
	    The beauty coming, and the beauty gone."
	                                        Wordsworth.

[Figure wordsworth]

In this sentence there is clearly one subject, "to pace the ground," which is connected by the pronoun it to the predicate "most sweet." But the main part of the sentence is made up of three adverbials, which express the manner and the surrounding circumstances, and the third adverbial is developed in a very complicated way. The sentence is not compound, but it is complex, because of its four subordinate propositions.

In the next sentence there is, strictly speaking, only one principal proposition, "We find." But this is only a way of introducing what the sentence is really saying: "the two classes of intellectual operations have much that is different, much that is common."

"When the notions with which men are conversant in the common course of life, which give meaning to their familiar language and which give employment to their hourly thoughts, are compared with the ideas on which exact science is founded, we find, that the two classes of intellectual operations have much that is different, much that is common."

[Figure notions]

Here "the two classes" make a collective term, and they have two coordinate predicates, which so far makes the sentence a compound one. But most of the sentence is a complicated subordinate sentence that works like an adverbial: it expresses the time or occasion when this is found to be the case.

As a last example we take the sentence below:

"The law of gravitation, the most universal truth at which human reason has yet arrived, expresses not merely the general fact of the mutual attraction of all matter; not merely the vague statement that its influence decreases as the distance increases, but the exact numerical rate at which that decrease takes place; so that when its amount is known at any one distance it may be exactly calculated for any other."

[Figure gravitation]

Questions and Exercises

1. How does the grammatical predicate differ from the logical predicate?

2. Explain the difference between a compound sentence and a complex sentence, and between coordinate and subordinate propositions.

3. List the grammatical expressions that can form

(1) A subject.

(2) An attribute.

(3) A predicate.

(4) An object.

(5) An adverbial.

4. Examine the following sentences. Work out which are compound and which are complex, and point out the coordinate or subordinate propositions.

(1) Happy is the man that findeth wisdom, and the man that getteth understanding.

(2) Heat, being motion, can be converted into mechanical force.

(3) Ceres, Pallas, Juno, and Vesta are minor planets, or asteroids.

(4) Knowledge comes, but wisdom lingers.

(5) Fortune often sells to the hasty what she gives to those who wait.

(6) Thousands at His bidding speed, And post o'er land and ocean without rest; They also serve who only stand and wait.

(7) Pride that dines on vanity, sups on contempt.

(8) Nobody can be healthful without exercise, neither natural body, nor politic.

(9) Nature is often hidden, sometimes overcome, seldom extinguished.

(10) It is impossible to love and be wise.

(11) Though gods they were, as men they died.

(12) He that is not industrious envieth him that is.

(13) Ye are my friends, if ye do whatsoever I command you.—John 15:14.

(14) The wisdom that is from above is first pure, then peaceable, gentle, and easy to be intreated, full of mercy, and good fruits, without partiality, and without hypocrisy.—James 3:17.

5. Analyze any of the following sentences in the form of a scheme or diagram:

(1) The first aphorism of Bacon's Novum Organum, quoted in Lesson 27.

(2) Some judgments are merely explanatory of their subject, having for their predicate, a conception which it fairly implies, to all who know and can define its nature.

(3) There be none of the affections which have been noted to fascinate or bewitch, but love and envy: they both have vehement wishes; they frame themselves readily into imaginations and suggestions; and they come easily into the eye, especially upon the presence of the objects, which are the points that conduce to fascination, if any such there be.

For more examples to analyze, see Dalgleish's Grammatical Analysis with Progressive Exercises (Oliver and Boyd, Edinburgh, 1866; price 9d.).
