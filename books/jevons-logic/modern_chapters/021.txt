Lesson 21: Material Fallacies

We come now to the Material fallacies. They matter a great deal, although they are not easy to illustrate with short examples. Aristotle listed seven kinds, and later logicians have kept his list:

1. The Fallacy of Accident.

2. The Converse Fallacy of Accident.

3. The Irrelevant Conclusion.

4. The Petitio Principii.

5. The Fallacy of the Consequent, or Non sequitur.

6. The False Cause.

7. The Fallacy of Many Questions.

The first two are most easily described together. The fallacy of accident is the mistake of arguing from a general rule to a special case where some accidental circumstance makes the rule not apply. The converse fallacy is the mistake of arguing from a special case to a general rule. This second fallacy is usually described by the Latin phrase a dicto secundum quid ad dictum simpliciter, which means "from a statement made under a condition to a statement made simply, without that condition." Professor De Morgan, in his very interesting Chapter on Fallacies, has pointed out that we ought to add a third fallacy of this kind: arguing from one special case to another special case.

Footnote: Formal Logic, Chapter XIII.

I will try to illustrate these kinds of fallacy with a few examples. Be warned, though: it is often very hard to say which of the three a particular example belongs to. Here is a very ancient example, repeated in almost every logic handbook: "What you bought yesterday you eat today; you bought raw meat yesterday; therefore you eat raw meat today." The conclusion makes its claim about meat with the accidental quality of rawness added. But the first premise is plainly talking about the meat as a substance, without regard to its accidental condition. So this is a case of the direct fallacy. If, on the other hand, someone argues that because wine acts as a poison when taken in excess it is always a poison, we have the converse fallacy.

Here is another case of the direct fallacy of accident: to infer that a magistrate is justified in using his power to advance his own religious views, because every man has a right to teach his own opinions. Clearly a magistrate, as a man, has the same rights as other men. But in his capacity as a magistrate he is set apart from other men, and he must not claim for his special powers what is true only of his rights as a man. For another instance, take this: "Anyone who thrusts a knife into another person should be punished; a surgeon does this when he operates; therefore he should be punished." The fallacy here is absurdly obvious, but it is not so obvious how we should classify the error. We might say that, as a general rule, whoever stabs or cuts another person is to be punished, unless it can be shown that it was done under exceptional circumstances, as by a properly qualified surgeon acting for the patient's good. Read that way, the example belongs to the direct fallacy of accident. Read another way, the first premise might mean only the special case of thrusting a knife into someone out of malice. To argue from that case to the case of a surgeon would then be to infer from one special case to another special case.

It is certainly true that giving to beggars encourages begging and does harm. But if we take this to mean that help should never be given to anyone who asks for it, we fall into the converse fallacy of accident: we infer about everyone who asks for charity what is true only of those who beg for a living. In the same way, it is a very good rule to avoid lawsuits and quarrels, but only as a general rule, because circumstances often arise in which going to law is a plain duty. Nearly all the difficulties we meet in matters of law and moral duty come from this: we can never be sure exactly which cases a legal or moral rule covers and which it does not. That is why there are endless differences of opinion, even among the judges of the land.

The Third Material Fallacy is the Irrelevant Conclusion, technically called the Ignoratio Elenchi, which means literally Ignorance of the Refutation. It consists in arguing to the wrong point: proving one thing in such a way that people suppose something else has been proved. Here again it is hard to give short examples, because this fallacy usually turns up in the course of long speeches, where the flood of words and figures leaves room for muddled thinking and forgetfulness. In fact, this fallacy is the great standby of anyone who has to defend a weak case. It is not unknown in the legal profession. The story goes that a solicitor for the defendant in a lawsuit once handed the barrister (the lawyer who would argue the case in court) his brief marked: "No case; abuse the plaintiff's attorney." Anyone who argues like this is using what is called the argumentum ad hominem, an argument that rests not on the merits of the case but on the character or position of the people involved in it, and he commits this fallacy. If a man is accused of a crime, it is no answer to say that the prosecutor is just as bad. If a great change in the law is proposed in Parliament, it is an Irrelevant Conclusion to argue that the person proposing it is not the right man to bring it forward. Everyone who gives advice lays himself open to the retort that he should practice what he preaches, or that people who live in glass houses should not throw stones. Even so, there is no necessary connection between the character of the person giving advice and the quality of the advice.

The argumentum ad populum is another form of Irrelevant Conclusion. It consists in addressing to a crowd arguments designed to stir up their feelings and keep them from judging the matter in hand calmly. It is the great weapon of orators and demagogues.

Petitio Principii is a familiar name, and the fallacy it names is exactly described by the phrase begging the question. Another good name for it is circulus in probando, "a circle in the proof." It consists in using the conclusion itself as one of the premises of the argument. Of course, the conclusion of a syllogism must always be contained in, or implied by, the premises. But it is contained in them only when the premises are put together, and the premises must be assertions clearly different from the conclusion. Take this syllogism:

	B is C,
	A is B,
	therefore A is C.

Here the conclusion is proved by being deduced from two propositions, neither of which is the same as the conclusion. But suppose the truth of one of these premises itself depends on the following syllogism:

	C is B,
	A is C,
	therefore A is B.

Then plainly we are trying to prove a proposition by means of itself, which makes as much sense as trying to hold a body up by itself. This kind of fallacy is not easy to illustrate with examples, because it usually happens in long arguments, and especially in wordy metaphysical writing. We are, however, very likely to fall into it when we mix plain Anglo-Saxon words with Latin or Greek ones, so that we seem to prove one proposition by another which is really the same proposition in different words. For example: "Consciousness must be immediate cognition of an object; for I cannot be said really to know a thing unless my mind has been affected by the thing itself."

This fallacy is especially likely to happen when we use the disjunctive syllogism. If we list only the alternatives that favor one view and forget the others, it is easy to prove anything at all. There is an instance of this in the famous sophism by which some of the ancient Greek philosophers proved that motion was impossible. A moving body, they said, must move either in the place where it is or in the place where it is not. Now it is absurd to say that a body can be where it is not; and if it moves, it cannot be in the place where it is. Therefore it cannot move at all. The error lies in assuming a premise that begs the question. The fact, of course, is that the body moves between the place where it is at one moment and the place where it is at the next.

Jeremy Bentham pointed out, however, that even a single name can carry a Petitio Principii with it. Suppose a church assembly, or synod, is discussing whether a certain doctrine ought to be condemned. It would be a Petitio Principii to argue that the doctrine is heresy and therefore ought to be condemned. To assert that it is heresy is to beg the question, because everyone understands by heresy a doctrine that is to be condemned. In the same way, in Parliament a bill is often opposed on the ground that it is unconstitutional and therefore ought to be rejected. But since no one can give a precise definition of what is or is not constitutional, this means little more than that the opponent dislikes the measure. Bentham gave names used in this fallacious way a good title: Question-begging Epithets. We beg the question in just the same way when we oppose a change by saying that it is un-English.

The Fallacy of the Consequent is better known by the familiar phrase non sequitur ("it does not follow"). We may give this name to any argument so loose and disconnected that no one can find any force in it at all. It amounts to little more than asserting a conclusion that has no connection with the premises. Professor De Morgan gives this example (episcopacy means the government of a church by bishops): "Episcopacy is of Scripture origin; the Church of England is the only episcopal Church in England; ergo, the Church established is the Church that should be supported."

By the Fallacy of the False Cause I mean the fallacy usually referred to by the Latin phrase non causa pro causâ (the non-cause taken for the cause). In this fallacy we assume, without sufficient grounds, that one thing is the cause of another. People still blame a change in the weather on the new moon or full moon that came shortly before it, although it has been shown over and over again that the moon can have no such effect. In earlier centuries, any plague or other public disaster that followed the appearance of a comet or an eclipse was thought to be its result. The Latin phrase post hoc ergo propter hoc ("after this, therefore because of this") describes exactly what is wrong with conclusions like these. We no longer fear signs and omens, but we still commit the fallacy often enough. We do it when we assume that all the prosperity of England comes from the national character, forgetting that the country's plentiful coal and its position on the sea have contributed to our material wealth. It is, no doubt, just as fallacious to give national character no importance at all; or to argue that because England has misgoverned Ireland in past centuries, all the present evils of Ireland are due to that misgovernment.

Last comes the rather trivial Fallacy of Many Questions. It is committed by anyone who combines two or three questions into one in such a way that no true answer can be given. I cannot think of a better example than the crude joke of asking, "Have you stopped beating your mother?" Questions just as unfair are constantly asked by barristers examining witnesses in court, and no one can fairly be required to answer Yes or No to every question that may be put to him. As Aristotle says, "Several questions put as one should be at once decomposed into their several parts. Only a single question admits of a single answer: so that neither several predicates of one subject, nor one predicate of several subjects, but only one predicate of one subject, ought to be affirmed or denied in a single answer."

Read Professor De Morgan's excellent and amusing Chapter on Fallacies, Formal Logic, Ch. xiii.

Whately's remarks on Fallacies, in his Elements of Logic, Book III, are often very original and sharp.

Questions on Fallacies

1. Classify the fallacies.

2. Explain the following expressions:

A dicto secundum quid ad dictum simpliciter; ignoratio elenchi; argumentum ad hominem; argumentum ad populum; petitio principii; circulus in probando; non sequitur; post hoc ergo propter hoc.

3. What is arguing in a circle, and what is a question-begging epithet?

4. What different meanings can be given to the following sentence by changing which word is stressed?

"Newton's discovery of gravitation is not generally believed to have been at all anticipated by several philosophers in England and Holland."

5. Point out the ways in which each of the following sentences could be misunderstood.

(1) He went to London and then to Brighton by the express train.

(2) Did you make a long speech at the meeting?

(3) How much is five times seven and nine?

Miscellaneous Examples

(Continued from the questions to Lesson 18.)

The following examples are partly sound arguments and partly false ones. The reader is asked to deal with them as follows:

1. If the example is not in a simple and complete logical form, put it into whatever complete form seems most fitting.

2. Decide whether it is a valid argument or a fallacy.

3. Give the exact name of the argument, or of the fallacy, as the case may be.

4. If it is a categorical syllogism, reduce it to the first figure.

5. If it is a hypothetical syllogism, state it in categorical form.

21. Elementary substances alone are metals. Iron is a metal; therefore it is an elementary substance.

22. No Athenians could have been Helots (the serfs of Sparta); for all the Helots were slaves, and all Athenians were free men.

23. Aristotle must have been a man of extraordinary industry; for only such a man could have produced his works.

24. Nothing is better than wisdom; dry bread is better than nothing; therefore dry bread is better than wisdom.

25. Pitt (William Pitt the Younger, the Prime Minister) was not a great and useful minister; for although he would have been one if he had carried out Adam Smith's doctrines of Free Trade, he did not carry out those doctrines.

26. Only the virtuous are truly noble; some who are called noble are not virtuous; therefore some who are called noble are not truly noble.

27. Ireland is idle and therefore starves; she starves, and therefore rebels.

28. No designing person ought to be trusted; engravers are designers by profession; therefore they ought not to be trusted.

29. Logic as the schoolmen (the philosophers of the medieval universities) practiced it proved a fruitless study; therefore logic as it is practiced today must be a fruitless study too.

30. Is a stone a body? Yes. Then is not an animal a body? Yes. Are you an animal? I think so. So you are a stone, since you are a body. (Lucian.)

31. "If ye were Abraham's children, ye would do the works of Abraham." (John 8:39.)

32. "He that is of God heareth God's words: ye therefore hear them not, because ye are not of God." (John 8:47.)

33. Muhammad was a wise lawgiver; for he studied the character of his people.

34. Everyone desires virtue, because everyone desires happiness.

35. His weakness of character might have been inferred from his fondness for favorites; for all weak princes have this failing. (De Morgan.)

36. He is brave who conquers his passions; he who resists temptation conquers his passions; so he who resists temptation is brave.

37. Suicide is not always to be condemned; for it is only voluntary death, and many of the greatest heroes of the ancient world gladly embraced voluntary death.

38. Since all metals are elements, the rarest of all the metals must be the rarest of all the elements.

39. The express train alone does not stop at this station; and since the last train did not stop, it must have been the express train.

40. Peel's remission of taxes was beneficial; the taxes Peel remitted were indirect taxes; therefore the remission of indirect taxes is beneficial. (Sir Robert Peel, as Prime Minister in the 1840s, abolished or cut many duties on goods.)

41. Books are a source both of instruction and of amusement; a table of logarithms is a book; therefore it is a source both of instruction and of amusement.

42. All desires are not blameworthy; all desires are liable to excess; therefore some things liable to excess are not blameworthy.

43. Whoever intentionally kills another person should suffer death; so a soldier who kills his enemy should suffer death.

44. Schemers are unfit to be trusted; this man has formed a scheme; therefore he is unfit to be trusted.

45. Few towns in the United Kingdom have more than 300,000 inhabitants; and since all such towns ought to be represented by three members in Parliament, it is clear that few towns ought to have three representatives.

46. All the works of Shakespeare cannot be read in a day; therefore the play of Hamlet, being one of the works of Shakespeare, cannot be read in a day.

47. In moral matters we cannot stand still; therefore whoever does not go forward is sure to fall behind.

48. The people of the country are suffering from famine; and since you are one of the people of the country, you must be suffering from famine.

49. Substances that are lighter than water can float on it; the metals that can float on it are potassium, sodium, lithium, and so on; therefore potassium, sodium, lithium, and so on, are lighter than water.

50. The laws of nature must be discovered by Deduction, Traduction or Induction; but the first two are not enough for the purpose; therefore the laws of nature must be discovered by Induction.

51. A successful author must be either very hard-working or very talented; Gibbon was very hard-working; therefore he was not very talented.

52. You are not what I am; I am a man; therefore you are not a man.

53. The holder of some shares in a lottery is sure to win a prize; and since I am the holder of some shares in a lottery, I am sure to win a prize.

54. Gold and silver are wealth; and therefore a decrease of the gold and silver in the country by exporting it is a decrease of the wealth of the country.

55. Overly credulous people ought never to be believed; and since the Ancient Historians were in many instances overly credulous, they ought never to be believed.

56. Some mineral compounds are not decomposed by heat; all organic substances are decomposed by heat; therefore no organic substances are mineral compounds.

57. Whatever schools exclude religion are irreligious; non-sectarian schools do not allow the teaching of religious creeds; therefore they are irreligious.

58. Night must be the cause of day; for it always comes before it.

59. The ancient Greeks produced the greatest masterpieces of eloquence and philosophy; the Lacedaemonians (the Spartans) were ancient Greeks; therefore they produced the greatest masterpieces of eloquence and philosophy.

60. All presuming men are contemptible; this man, therefore, is contemptible; for he presumes to believe that his opinions are correct.

61. If a substance is solid it has elasticity, and so it does if it is liquid or gaseous; but all substances are either solid, liquid or gaseous; therefore all substances have elasticity.

62. If Parr's Life Pills (a popular patent medicine) are of any value, those who take them will improve in health; now my friend, who has been taking them, has improved in health; therefore they are of value.

63. He who calls you a man speaks truly; he who calls you a fool calls you a man; therefore he who calls you a fool speaks truly.

64. Whoever is most hungry eats most; whoever eats least is most hungry; therefore whoever eats least eats most.

65. Whatever produces drunkenness should be prohibited; the use of spirits (strong liquor) causes drunkenness; therefore the use of spirits should be prohibited.

66. What we eat grew in the fields; loaves of bread are what we eat; therefore loaves of bread grew in the fields.

67. If light consisted of material particles, it would have momentum; so it cannot consist of material particles, for it does not have momentum.

68. Everything that is morally right is allowed by law; indulgence in pleasures is allowed by law; therefore indulgence in pleasures is morally right.

69. All the trees in the park make a thick shade; this is one of them; therefore this tree makes a thick shade.

70. All visible bodies shine either by their own light or by reflected light. The moon does not shine by its own, therefore it shines by reflected light; but the sun shines by its own light, therefore it cannot shine by reflected light.

71. Honesty deserves reward, and a Black man is a fellow human being; therefore an honest Black man is a fellow human being who deserves reward.

72. Nearly all the satellites revolve around their planets from west to east; the moon is a satellite; therefore it revolves around its planet from west to east.

73. Italy is a Catholic country and is full of beggars; France is also a Catholic country, and therefore is full of beggars.

74. Every law is either useless or causes harm to someone; now a law that is useless ought to be abolished; and so ought every law that causes harm; therefore every law ought to be abolished.

75. The end of a thing is its perfection; death is the end of life; therefore death is the perfection of life.

76. When we hear that all righteous people are happy, it is hard not to exclaim: What! Are all the unhappy people we see to be thought unrighteous?

77. I am offered a sum of money to help this person get the office he wants; to help a person is to do him good, and no rule of morality forbids doing good; therefore no rule of morality forbids me to take the money for helping the person.

78. Ruminant animals (the cud-chewers) are those which have cloven hoofs, and they usually have horns; the extinct animal that left this footprint had a cloven hoof; therefore it was a ruminant animal and had horns. And again, since no beasts of prey are ruminant animals, it cannot have been a beast of prey.

79. We must either indulge our vicious tendencies or resist them; the first course will involve us in sin and misery; the second requires self-denial; therefore we must either fall into sin and misery or practice self-denial.

80. The stonemasons benefit from the masons' union; the bricklayers from the bricklayers' union; the hatmakers from the hatmakers' union; in short, every trade from its own union; so it is clear that if all workmen had unions, all workmen would benefit from them.

81. Every moral aim requires the rational means of reaching it; these means are the establishment of laws; and since happiness is the moral aim of human beings, it follows that reaching happiness requires the establishment of laws.

82. Whoever can swim need not despair of flying; for to swim is to fly in a coarser fluid, and to fly is to swim in a finer one.

83. If the Helvetii went through the country of the Sequani, they were sure to meet various difficulties; and if they went through the Roman province, they faced the danger of opposition from Caesar; but they had to go one way or the other; therefore either they were sure to meet various difficulties, or they faced the danger of opposition from Caesar. (Caesar, De Bello Gallico, Book I, 6.)

84. "Riches are for spending, and spending for honour and good actions; therefore extraordinary expense must be limited by the worth of the occasion." (Bacon.)

85. If light is not refracted near the surface of the moon, there cannot be any twilight there; but if the moon has no atmosphere, light is not refracted near its surface; therefore if the moon has no atmosphere, there cannot be any twilight.

86. The preservation of society requires exchange; whatever requires exchange requires a fair valuation of property; this requires the adoption of a common measure; so the preservation of society requires the adoption of a common measure.

87. The fact that the various species of brute animals were created to prey on one another proves that the human species was intended to prey on them.

88. The more correct the logic, the more certainly the conclusion will be wrong if the premises are false. Therefore, where the premises are wholly uncertain, the best logician is the least safe guide.

89. If our rulers could be trusted always to look to the best interests of their subjects, monarchy would be the best form of government; but they cannot be trusted; therefore monarchy is not the best form of government.

90. If people were prudent, they would act morally for their own good; if they were benevolent, they would do so for the good of others. But many people will not act morally, either for their own good or for that of others; such people, therefore, are not prudent or benevolent.

91. Whoever bears arms at the command of the magistrate does what is lawful for a Christian; the Swiss in the French service, and the British in the American service, bore arms at the command of the magistrate; therefore they did what was lawful for a Christian. (Whately.)

92. "A man that hath no virtue in himself ever envieth virtue in others; for men's minds will either feed upon their own good or upon others' evil; and who wanteth [lacks] the one will prey upon the other." (Bacon.)

93. The object of war is lasting peace; therefore soldiers are the best peacemakers.

94. Confidence in promises is essential to the dealings of human life; for without it most of our conduct would be left to chance. But there could be no confidence in promises if people were not obliged to keep them; therefore the obligation to keep promises is essential to the same ends, and in the same degree.

95. If the majority of those who use pubs (public houses) are prepared to close them, legislation is unnecessary; but if they are not prepared for such a measure, then to force it on them by outside pressure is both dangerous and unjust.

96. Whoever believes himself always to be in the right in his opinion lays claim to infallibility; you always believe yourself to be in the right in your opinion; therefore you lay claim to infallibility. (Whately.)

97. If we never find skins except as the coverings of animals, we may safely conclude that animals cannot exist without skins. If color cannot exist by itself, it follows that nothing colored can exist without color either. In the same way, if language without thought is unreal, thought without language must be unreal too.

98. No soldiers should be brought into the field who are not well qualified to do their part; none but veterans are well qualified to do their part; therefore none but veterans should be brought into the field. (Whately.)

99. The minimum visibile is the smallest size that can be seen; no part of it alone is visible, and yet every part of it must affect the mind in order for it to be visible; therefore every part of it must affect the mind without being visible.

100. The scarlet poppy belongs to the genus Papaver, of the natural order Papaveraceae; this order is part of the subclass Thalamiflorae, which belongs to the great class of Dicotyledons. So the scarlet poppy is one of the Dicotyledons.

101. Improbable events happen almost every day; but what happens almost every day is a very probable event; therefore improbable events are very probable events. (Whately.)
