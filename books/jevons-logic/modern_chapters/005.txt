Lesson 5: Of the Twofold Meaning of Terms, in Extension and Intension

There is no part of logic I would more urgently ask the reader to pay attention to than the one I will try to explain clearly in this lesson. I mean the double meaning that most logical terms have: the meaning in extension, and the meaning in intension. I believe that a reader who once gets a thorough grasp of the difference between these two meanings, and learns to keep it always in mind, will have very little further trouble in the study of logic.

The meaning of a term in extension consists of the objects the term can be applied to. Its meaning in intension consists of the qualities that objects bearing that name must have. A simple example will make the distinction very clear. What is the meaning of the name "metal"? The first and most obvious answer is that metal means gold, or silver, or iron, or copper, or aluminum, or some other of the 48 substances that chemists know of and consider to be metallic in nature. These substances, then, make up the plain and ordinary meaning of the name, and this is the meaning in extension. But suppose someone asks why the name is given to all these substances and only to these. The answer has to be: because they have certain qualities that belong to the nature of a metal. So we cannot know which substances we may call metals, and which we may not, unless we know the qualities that are indispensable to the character of a metal. Chemists lay these down roughly as follows. (1) A metal must be an element, or simple substance, which cannot be decomposed or separated into simpler substances by any known means. (2) It must be a good conductor of heat and electricity. (3) It must have a great and peculiar power of reflecting light, known as metallic luster.

Footnote: It is doubtful whether all metals really do have metallic luster, and chemists would find it very hard to give any consistent account of how they use the name. But the statements in the text are true enough to serve as an example.

These properties are common to all metals, or nearly all, and they are what mark a metal out and distinguish it from other substances. So in a certain way they form the meaning of the name metal. This is called the meaning in intension, to distinguish it from the first kind of meaning.

Almost any other common name has a double meaning in the same way. "Steamship" denotes in extension the Great Eastern, the Persia, the Himalaya, or any one of the thousands of steamships that exist or have existed. In intension it means "a vessel propelled by steam-power." Monarch is the name of Queen Victoria, Victor Emmanuel (the king of Italy), Louis Napoleon (Napoleon III of France), or any one of a fair number of people who rule countries single-handed. The people themselves form the meaning in extension; the quality of ruling alone forms the intensive meaning of the name. Animal is, in extension, the name of any one of billions of creatures alive now, and of far greater numbers that have lived or will live. In intension it implies that all those creatures have a certain animal life and feeling, or at least the power of digesting food and exerting force, which are the marks of animal nature.

I should say here that logicians have described this distinction of extension and intension in several different ways of speaking. It is the special misfortune of logic to have too many names, or synonyms, for the same idea. So the intension of a term means the same as its comprehension, or connotation, or depth; and the extension means the same as its denotation, or breadth. This can be stated most clearly in the form of a scheme:

	The extension, extent, breadth, denotation, domain, sphere or application of a name consists of the individual things to which the name applies. | The intension, intent, depth, connotation, or implication of a name consists of the qualities the possession of which by those things is implied.

Of these words, denotation and connotation are used chiefly by Mr. J. S. Mill among modern writers on logic, and they suit the purpose very well. To denote is to mark down, and the name marks the things it can be applied or attached to; so metal denotes gold, silver, copper and so on. To connote is to mark along with (from the Latin con, together, and notare, to mark), so the connotation consists of the qualities described above, the qualities we imply a thing has when we use the name metal.

When we compare different but related terms, we can see that they differ in the quantity of their extension and of their intension. The term element, for example, has a greater extension of meaning than metal, because it takes in all the metals and other substances as well. But at the same time it has less intension of meaning, because the qualities of a metallic substance must include the qualities of an element, besides the other qualities peculiar to a metal. Or compare the terms metal and malleable metal. The second term plainly does not include the metals antimony, arsenic and bismuth, which are brittle substances. So malleable metal is a term of narrower meaning in extension than metal. But it is also deeper in intension, because it connotes, or implies, the quality of malleability on top of the general qualities of a metal. White malleable metal is narrower again in extension, because it does not include gold and copper. And I could go on narrowing the meaning with more qualifying adjectives until the term denoted only a single metal.

The reader will now see clearly that a general law of great importance connects the quantity of extension with the quantity of intension. It is this: as the intension of a term is increased, the extension is decreased. We must not suppose, though, that there is any exact proportion between how much one meaning grows and how much the other shrinks. If we join the adjective red to metal, for example, we narrow the meaning much more than if we join the adjective white, because there are at least twelve times as many white metals as red ones. Again, the term white man covers a considerable fraction of the extension of the term man, but the term blind man covers only a small fraction of it. So it is obvious that when we increase the intension of a term, we may decrease its extension by any amount.

In understanding this law we must carefully tell apart the cases where the intension of a term is only apparently increased from those where the increase is real. If I add the term elementary to metal, I shall not really change the extension of the meaning, because all the metals are elements, and the elementary metals are neither more nor less numerous than the metals. But then the intension of the term is really unchanged too, because the quality of being an element is already found among the qualities of a metal, and there is no point in stating it all over again. A quality that belongs without exception to the whole of a class of things is usually called a property of the class (see Lesson 12), and we cannot qualify or restrict a term by adding its own property.

This is a convenient place to note a distinction between terms that are connotative and terms that are non-connotative. Non-connotative terms are the ones that simply denote things, without implying any knowledge of their qualities. Mr. Mill thinks this distinction is very important, so it will be best to quote his own words:

Footnote: System of Logic, Vol. i. p. 31, 6th ed. Book I. Chap. ii. §5.

"A non-connotative term is one which signifies a subject only, or an attribute only. A connotative term is one which denotes a subject, and implies an attribute. By a subject is here meant anything which possesses attributes. Thus John, or London, or England, are names which signify a subject only. Whiteness, length, virtue, signify an attribute only. None of these names, therefore, are connotative. But white, long, virtuous, are connotative. The word white denotes all white things, as snow, paper, the foam of the sea, &c., and implies, or, as it was termed by the schoolmen, connotes the attribute whiteness. The word white is not predicated of the attribute, but of the subjects, snow, &c.; but when we predicate it of them, we imply, or connote, that the attribute whiteness belongs to them.

"All concrete general names are connotative. The word man, for example, denotes Peter, James, John, and an indefinite number of other individuals, of whom, taken as a class, it is the name. But it is applied to them, because they possess, and to signify that they possess, certain attributes.... What we call men, are the subjects, the individual Styles and Nokes; not the qualities by which their humanity is constituted. The name therefore is said to signify the subjects directly, the attributes indirectly; it denotes the subjects, and implies, or involves, or indicates, or, as we shall say henceforth, connotes, the attributes. It is a connotative name....

"Proper names are not connotative: they denote the individuals who are called by them; but they do not indicate or imply any attributes as belonging to those individuals. When we name a child by the name Paul, or a dog by the name Caesar, these names are simply marks used to enable those individuals to be made subjects of discourse. It may be said, indeed, that we must have had some reason for giving them those names rather than any others; and this is true; but the name, once given, is independent of the reason. A man may have been named John, because that was the name of his father; a town may have been named Dartmouth, because it is situated at the mouth of the Dart. But it is no part of the signification of the word John, that the father of the person so called bore the same name; nor even of the word Dartmouth to be situated at the mouth of the Dart. If sand should choke up the mouth of the river, or an earthquake change its course, and remove it to a distance from the town, the name of the town would not necessarily be changed."

I quote this in Mr. Mill's own words because, although it states very clearly the view held by Mr. Mill and many others, that view is nevertheless probably wrong. It confuses the connotation of a name with its etymological meaning, that is, with the circumstances that caused the name to be attached to a thing. Surely no one who uses the name England, and knows what it denotes, can be ignorant of the particular qualities and circumstances of the country, and these form the connotation of the term. To anyone who knows the town of Dartmouth, the name must imply that it has the circumstances that mark out that town at the present time. If the river Dart were destroyed or moved, the town would be changed to that extent, and the meaning of the name would change with it. The name would no longer denote a town on the Dart, but a town that used to be on the Dart, and it would be a mere accident of history that the form of the name no longer seemed to suit the town. In the same way, any proper name, such as John Smith, is almost meaningless until we know which John Smith is meant. It is true that the name alone connotes the fact that he is a Teuton (of Germanic stock) and a male. But as soon as we know exactly which individual it denotes, the name surely implies his particular features, appearance and character as well. In fact, the only way we can ever recognize a thing is by its particular qualities, features or circumstances. So no name could have any fixed meaning unless we attached to it, in our minds at least, enough of a definition of the kind of thing it denotes for us to know whether any given thing was denoted by it or not. If the name John Smith does not call up in my mind the qualities of John Smith, how am I to know him when I meet him? He certainly does not have his name written on his forehead.

Footnote: Further objections to Mr. Mill's views on this point will be found in Mr. Shedden's Elements of Logic. London, 1864. pp. 14, &c.

This, however, is a question that is far from settled. Since Mr. Mill is generally considered the best authority on the subject, the reader may do well to accept his opinion for the time being: that singular or proper names are non-connotative, and that all concrete general names are connotative. Abstract names, on the other hand, can hardly have any connotation at all. They already denote the attributes or qualities of something, so nothing is left over to form the connotation of the name. Mr. Mill does think that abstract names can often be considered connotative, as when the name fault connotes the attribute of hurtfulness as belonging to the fault. But if fault is a true abstract word at all, I would count hurtfulness as part of its denotation. I am inclined to think that faultiness is the abstract name, and that fault is usually used concretely, as the name of a particular action or thing that is faulty, or has faultiness. But the subject cannot be properly discussed here, and the reader should note Mr. Mill's opinion: abstract names are usually non-connotative, but may be connotative in some cases.

The subject of Extension and Intension can be followed up in Hamilton's Lectures on Logic, Lect. VIII., or in Thomson's Laws of Thought, Sections 48 to 52. It is also discussed at length in Spalding's Logic (Encyclopaedia Britannica, 8th ed.).

Questions and Exercises

1. Distinguish very carefully between the meanings in extension and in intension of these terms: quadruped, railway, human being, engine, mountain, Member of Parliament.

2. List the synonyms, or other names, that are used instead of extension and intension.

3. By what law is the quantity of extension connected with the quantity of intension? Show that the law holds true of the following series of terms:

(1) Iron, metal, element, matter, substance.

(2) Matter, organized matter, animal, man.

(3) Ship, steamship, screw-steamship, iron screw-steamship, British iron screw-steamship.

(4) Book, printed book, dictionary, Latin dictionary.

4. Distinguish between the connotation and the denotation of a term.

5. From the list of terms in question 8 of Lesson 3, pick out the terms that are non-connotative according to Mr. Mill's views.

6. Arrange the following terms in series as in question 3, placing each term of greater extension before a term of less extension. Point out which are the terms of greatest and of least intension in each series.

	Emperor | Animal | Planet
	Teacher | Dissenter | Mammalian
	Baptist | Individual | Matter
	Timber | Jupiter | Solicitor
	Person | Ruler | Quadruped
	Horse | Organized substance | Being
	Heavenly body | Lawyer | Napoleon III.
	Christian | Alexander | Episcopalian
