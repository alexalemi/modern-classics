Lesson 9: The Opposition of Propositions

We have found that logicians recognize four distinct kinds of propositions: the Universal affirmative, the Particular affirmative, the Universal negative, and the Particular negative, usually marked by the symbols A, I, E, O. Now we need to compare these four kinds fairly closely, looking at what each one means and how each is used. That way we will learn clearly how the truth of one affects the truth of the others, and how the same truth can be put into different forms of words.

The proposition A expresses the fact that the thing, or class of things, denoted by the subject is included in the class of things denoted by the predicate, and forms part of it. So "all metals are elements" means that the metals form a part of the class of elements, but not the whole of it. In the 1870s, 63 elements were known altogether, and 48 of them were metals, so we cannot say that all elements are metals. In fact the proposition does not tell us anything about elements in general. It is not really about elements at all: metals are the subject, and it is the metals it gives us information about. The best way to show this is with a kind of diagram first used by the famous mathematician Euler, in his letters to a German princess. In Fig. 1, imagine the metals shut inside the small circle, rather as sheep might be shut in a pen; this circle holds all the metals and nothing else. Imagine the larger circle holding, in the same way, all the elements and nothing else. Since the small circle lies wholly inside the larger one, all the metals must be counted as elements. But about the part of the elements lying outside the circle of metals, the proposition tells us nothing.

[Figure 1]

The particular affirmative proposition I means exactly the same kind of thing as A, except that only part of the subject is brought into question. When I say that "some metals are brittle," I mean that if we gathered together all the different metals, at least a few could be picked out that would turn out to be brittle. But the word some is extremely vague. It shows neither exactly how many metals are brittle, nor how we are to tell them from the others, except, of course, by testing whether they are brittle. In Euler's way, this proposition is properly shown by two intersecting circles, one supposed to enclose all metals and the other all brittle substances. The mere fact that the two circles intersect proves that some part of one class must coincide with some part of the other, which is just what the proposition means to express. About the parts of the circles that do not overlap, the proposition tells us nothing.

[Figure 2]

The universal negative proposition E denies that there is any agreement or coincidence at all between the subject and the predicate. From "no metals are compound substances," for example, we learn that no metal is to be found among compound substances. And it follows necessarily that no compound substance can be found among the metals. For if there were a compound substance among the metals, there would plainly be at least one metal among the compound substances. Euler's method shows this complete separation of the two classes in thought very well, by two circles that do not touch.

[Figure 3]

The reader will easily see what sets the proposition E apart from A and I. E gives us some information about the whole of the predicate, because we learn that none of the things included in the predicate can be found among the things included in the subject. The affirmative propositions, on the other hand, entitled us to hold that the things denoted by the subject, or some particular part of them, were included in the predicate. But they give us no right to say that any particular part of the predicate is in the subject. If all we know about a substance is that it is an element, the proposition "all metals are elements" does not tell us whether it is a metal or not. And from the proposition "some metals are brittle" we certainly cannot find out whether any particular brittle substance is a metal. We have to get that information from somewhere else. But from "no metals are compounds" we learn, of any compound substance, that it is not a metal, and also, of any metal, that it is not a compound substance.

In technical language, this important difference is expressed by saying that the proposition E distributes its predicate, while the affirmative propositions A and I do not distribute their predicates. The distribution of a term simply means taking it universally, or referring to all parts of it. Whether an argument or syllogism is valid will usually depend on whether its terms are sufficiently distributed, so we cannot pay too much attention to this point.

Judging from the examples we have had, we can see that the universal affirmative distributes its subject, but not its predicate: it gives us some information about all metals, but not about all elements. The particular affirmative distributes neither subject nor predicate: our example teaches us nothing about all metals, or about all brittle substances. But the universal negative distributes both subject and predicate, because we learn something about all metals and also about all compound substances.

The particular negative proposition O turns out to distribute its predicate, but not its subject. When I say that some metals are not brittle, I deliberately refer to only part of the metals, and I shut them out of the class of brittle substances. But in doing so I cannot help referring at the same time to the whole of the brittle substances. If the metals in question coincided with any part at all of the brittle substances, they could not be said to be shut out of that class. To shut a thing out of any space, such as one particular room of a house, it is not enough to remove it from some part of the room; it must be kept out of every part, out of the whole of that space or room. We can set out the result we have now reached in the following form:

	"" | "" | Subject. | Predicate.
	Universal | Affirmative A. | Distributed. | Undistributed.
	Universal | Negative E. | Distributed. | Distributed.
	Particular | Affirmative I. | Undistributed. | Undistributed.
	Particular | Negative O. | Undistributed. | Distributed.

Now we can very easily work out how the four propositions are related to one another, each to each; that is, the ways in which they are opposed to each other. Clearly, the truth of one proposition may interfere, more or less completely, with the truth of another proposition that has the same subject and predicate. If "all metals are elements," then it is impossible that "some metals are not elements," and still more obviously impossible, so to speak, that "no metals are elements." So the proposition A is inconsistent with both E and O, and, the other way around, E and O are inconsistent with A. In the same way, E is inconsistent with A and I. But there is an important difference to notice here. If A is false, O is necessarily true, but E may or may not be true. If it is not true that "all men are sincere," it follows that "some men are not sincere," but it does not in the least follow that "no men are sincere." We express this difference by saying that A and O are contradictory propositions, while A and E are called contrary propositions. A and E, as in "all men are sincere" and "no men are sincere," plainly stand for the greatest possible opposition of circumstances. To prove that A is false, it is enough to establish that O is true; it is unnecessary to prove E, even if that could be done. In the same way, E is disproved by proving I, and it is unnecessary to prove A. Anyone who asserts a universal proposition, either A or E, takes on the burden of explaining away or disproving every single exception that is brought against it. An opponent can always stick to the much easier job of finding cases that seem to contradict, or really do contradict, the universality of the statement. But if he takes it on himself to assert the direct contrary, he is the one open to easy attack. Suppose someone asserted, for instance, that "All Christians are more moral than Pagans." It would be easy to bring forward examples showing that "Some Christians are not more moral than Pagans." But it would be absurd to think it necessary to go to the opposite extreme and show that "No Christians are more moral than Pagans." In short, A is sufficiently, and best, disproved by O, and E by I. And it is easy to see that, the other way around, O is disproved by A, and I by E. In fact, there is no other way at all of disproving these particular propositions.

When we compare the propositions I and O, we find that they are opposed in nature in a certain sense, since one is affirmative and the other negative, and yet they are consistent with each other. It is true, for instance, that "Some metals are brittle": antimony, bismuth and arsenic, for example. But it is also true that "Some metals are not brittle." And the reader will notice that when I assert "Some metals are elements," nothing in this prevents "Some metals are not elements" from being true as well, although we know on other grounds that it is not true. The propositions I and O are each called the subcontrary of the other. The name implies a lesser degree of contrariety than there is between A and E.

As for the relation of A to I, and of E to O, the truth of the universal plainly includes and requires the truth of the particular. Whatever can be affirmed or denied of all parts of a class can certainly be affirmed or denied in the same way of some part of the class. But from the truth of the particular we have no right to infer either the truth or the falsity of the universal of the same quality. These pairs of propositions are called subalterns, meaning "one under the other" (from the Latin sub, under, and alter, the other of two). Or, to put it more exactly, I and O are the subalternates of A and E respectively, and each of A and E is a subalternans.

It is so very important to grasp the consistency or opposition of propositions completely and quickly that I will put the matter in another form. If we take any two propositions with the same subject and predicate, they must come under one of the following statements:

1. Of contradictory propositions, one must be true and one false.

2. Of contrary propositions, both cannot be true, and both may be false.

3. Of subcontrary propositions, only one can be false, and both may be true.

4. Of subalterns, the particular is true if the universal is true; but the universal may or may not be true when the particular is true.

I put the same matter in yet another form in the following table, which shows how the truth of each of A, E, I and O affects the truth of each of the others.

	"" | A is | E is | I is | O is
	If A be true | true | false | true | false.
	If E be true | false | true | false | true.
	If I be true | doubtful | false | true | doubtful.
	If O be true | false | doubtful | doubtful | true.

Clearly, we get more information from affirming universals than from affirming particulars. It follows that we can get more information from denying particulars than from denying universals: that is, fewer cases are left doubtful, as the table above shows.

The reader should be warned, though, against an ambiguity that has misled even some of the most eminent logicians. In particular propositions, the adjective some must be carefully understood as meaning some, and there may or may not be more, or all. If we took it to mean some, but not more and not all, it would really give the proposition the force of I and O combined. If I say "some men are sincere," I must not be taken to imply that "some men are not sincere." I must be understood to predicate sincerity of some men, and to leave the character of the rest entirely untouched. It follows that when I deny the truth of a particular, I must not be understood to imply the truth of the universal of the same quality. It might seem very natural to deny the truth of "some men are mortal," on the ground that not just some but all men are mortal. But then the proposition being denied would really be "some men are not mortal," that is, O, not I. So when I deny that "some men are immortal," I mean that "no men are immortal"; and when I deny that "some men are not mortal," I mean that "all men are mortal."

It has long been the custom to compare propositions according to the kind of subject matter they refer to. What is technically called the matter was divided into three kinds: necessary, contingent, and impossible. Necessary matter is any subject in which the proposition A can be affirmed; impossible matter is any subject in which E can be affirmed. Any subject or branch of knowledge in which universal statements usually cannot be made is called contingent matter, and it implies the truth of I and O. So "comets are subject to gravitation," although it is an indefinite or indesignate proposition (see Lesson 8), may be read as A, because it belongs to a part of natural science where general laws of that kind hold. But "men are sincere" would properly be read as particular, or I, because the matter is clearly contingent. The truth of the following statements is evident.

In necessary matter A and I are true; E and O false.

In contingent matter I and O are true; A and E false.

In impossible matter E and O are true; A and I false.

In reality, though, this part of logical doctrine is thoroughly illogical. As we have already explained (in Lesson 8), when we deal with a proposition we have no right to assume that we know the science it refers to. Our job is to draw out the exact consequences of whatever statements we are given. In logic we must learn to transform information in every possible way, but not to add facts from outside.

Questions and Exercises

1. State the quantity of the subject and of the predicate in each of the propositions A, E, I, O.

2. From the following propositions, pick out pairs of contrary, contradictory, subaltern, and subcontrary propositions:

(1) Some elements are known.

(2) No elements are known.

(3) All elements are known.

(4) Not all elements are known.

(5) Some elements are not known.

(6) All elements are not known.

3. What propositions are true, false, or doubtful:

(1) when A is false,

(2) when E is false,

(3) when I is false,

(4) when O is false?

4. Prove, by means of the contradictory propositions, that subcontrary propositions cannot both be false.

5. Show, by means of the subcontrary propositions, that contrary propositions may both be false.

6. What quantity would you assign to each of the following propositions?

(1) Knowledge is power.

(2) Nebulae are material bodies.

(3) Light is the vibration of an ether.

(4) Men are more to be trusted than we think.

(5) The Chinese are industrious.

7. Why is it better, in an argument, to refute a statement by its contradictory rather than by its contrary?
