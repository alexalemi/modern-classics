Lesson 31: Explanation, Tendency, Hypothesis, Theory, and Fact

In the last few lessons I have used several expressions without ever defining what they mean. Now is a good time to show how these terms are used, with examples, and to get as clear an understanding as we can of their proper meanings.

Explanation literally means making something plain, or level, so that there is nothing uneven or obscure to block our view. Scientific explanation consists in harmonizing fact with fact, or fact with law, or law with law, so that we can see both to be cases of one uniform law of causation. Suppose we hear of a great earthquake in some part of the world, and later hear that a nearby volcano has erupted. We say that the earthquake is now partly explained. The eruption shows that great forces were at work beneath the earth's surface, and the earthquake is obviously an effect of causes like these. Or take the scratches that can be plainly seen on the surface of rocks in certain parts of Wales and Cumberland (in the English Lake District). They are explained by the fact that glaciers once existed in those mountains: the scratches match exactly the effects of the glaciers that still exist in Switzerland, Greenland and elsewhere. We may call these explanations of fact by fact.

A fact may also be explained by a general law of nature. That is, we can point out the cause and the way the fact was produced, and show that it is the same cause that operates in many cases that look quite different. So the cracking of glass by heat was explained (in Lesson 30) as one result of the universal law that heat increases the dimensions of solid bodies. The trade-winds are explained as one case of the general tendency of warm air to rise and be pushed aside by cold, dense air. The very same simple laws of heat and mechanics that make a draft flow up a chimney when there is a fire below also make the winds blow from each hemisphere toward the equator. At the same time, the simplest laws of motion explain why the winds come from the east. The earth rotates from west to east, and its surface moves much faster at the equator than nearer the poles. The air coming from the north or south tends to keep its slower rate of motion, so as the earth near the equator moves on beneath it, the wind seems to blow from east to west.

According to Mr. J. S. Mill, there are three distinct ways in which one law may be explained by other laws, or brought into harmony with them.

The first is the case where two or more separate causes are really at work, and their results are combined, or added together, homogeneously. As was explained before (in Lesson 29), the homogeneous intermixture of effects means that the joint effect is simply the sum of the separate effects, and is of the same kind as they are. Our last example, the trade-winds, really comes under this case. We find one law, or tendency, that makes winds blow from the arctic regions toward the equator, and a second tendency that makes them blow from east to west. These tendencies combine, and make the trade-winds blow from the northeast in the northern hemisphere and from the southeast in the southern hemisphere. Another example is the law that governs the temperature of the air in any part of the earth. It is a very complicated one. It depends partly on the law that governs the sun's heating power, and partly on the earth's power to radiate the heat away into space, but perhaps even more on the way currents of air or water bring warmth in or carry it away. The path of a cannon-ball, or any other projectile, is likewise fixed by several laws acting together. First comes the simple law of motion, by which any moving body tends to keep moving onward at a uniform rate in a straight line. Second comes the law of gravity, which keeps bending the body's path toward the earth's surface. Third comes the resistance of the air, which tends to reduce its velocity.

The reader may have noticed how often I use the word tendency, and that I have repeatedly spoken of a cause as tending to produce its effect. If the joint, homogeneous action of causes has been made clear, it will now be clear what a tendency is. A tendency means a cause that will produce an effect unless there are opposite causes which, combined with it, counteract and disguise that effect. When we throw a stone into the air, for instance, the attractive power of the earth tends to make it fall, but the upward motion we have given it disguises the result for a certain time. The endless circling motion of the moon around the earth is the result of two balanced tendencies: one toward the earth, and one to go straight on in a line. The laws of motion and gravity are such that this balance must always be kept. If anything brought the moon nearer to the earth, its tendency to fly off would increase, and would outweigh the effect of gravity until the moon had got back to its proper distance. A tendency, then, is a cause that may or may not be counteracted.

In the second case of explanation, an effect is shown to be due, not directly to the supposed cause, but to an intermediate effect of that cause. Instead of A being the cause of C, we find that A is the cause of B, and B the cause of C, so that B forms an intermediate link. This kind of explanation may seem to make the matter more complicated, but it really simplifies it. The connection of A with B may be a case of some familiar and simple law, and so may the connection of B with C, while the law that A produces C may be purely empirical and seem out of harmony with everything else. In lightning, for example, it looks as if electricity had the power of creating a loud explosion. In reality electricity only produces heat, and it is the heat that makes the sound, by suddenly expanding the air. So thunder comes into harmony with the sound of artillery, which is also caused by the sudden expansion of the heated gases given off by the gunpowder. Again, when chlorine was discovered, it was soon found to have a strong power of bleaching, and today almost all bleaching is done with chlorine instead of by the sun, as it used to be. Inquiry showed, however, that it was not really the chlorine that destroyed the color. Oxygen is the intermediate and active agent. Chlorine breaks down water, takes the hydrogen, and leaves the oxygen in a state of great activity, ready to destroy the organic coloring matter. In this way a number of facts are harmonized. We learn why dry chlorine does not bleach, and why several other substances resemble chlorine in their bleaching power: for instance ozone, hydrogen peroxide, sulfurous acid, and a peculiar oxide of vanadium recently discovered by Dr. Roscoe. We could not understand the effect at all unless we knew that in all these cases it is probably due to active oxygen, or ozone, even in the old method of bleaching by laying things out in the sun.

The third case of explanation, and much the most important, is where one law is shown to be a case of a more general law. As was explained in Lesson 24, we naturally discover the less general laws first, and only gradually make our way to the simpler but deeper secrets of nature. It has often happened that scientists possessed several well-known laws without seeing the bond that connected them. People had long known, for instance, that all heavy bodies tend to fall toward the earth, and before Newton's time Hooke, Huygens and others knew that some force probably connected the earth with the sun and moon. It was Newton, however, who clearly brought these and many other facts under one general law, so that each fact, or less general law, throws light on every other.

The science of Electricity now harmonizes a vast series of partial laws and facts, among which it was truly hard to find any resemblance. The chief properties of the magnet had been fairly well known since the time of Gilbert, the physician of Queen Elizabeth. Ordinary frictional electricity was carefully studied by Otto von Guericke, Aepinus, Coulomb and others. Galvanism (electricity produced by chemical action, as in a battery) was investigated in great detail almost as soon as Galvani and Volta discovered that the chemical action of one substance on another can produce electricity. In the early part of this century there were three distinct sciences, Magnetism, Electricity and Galvanism; now there is only one. Oersted of Copenhagen supplied the first link between them in 1819, when he pointed out that an electric current can make a compass-needle move. Ampère and Faraday worked out the complicated relations among the three sciences, and in the end took them all into a wider science. We may call it Electro-magnetism, or perhaps, more conveniently, we may widen the name Electricity to take in all the phenomena connected with it.

A number of minor laws and separate facts are taken in and explained by the theory now generally accepted: that heat, electricity, light, and in fact all the phenomena of nature, are only different forms in which one and the same kind of energy shows itself. The total amount of energy in the universe is held to be fixed and unchangeable, like the quantity of matter. Sometimes it is disguised, because it affects only the molecules too small to perceive. At other times we see it produce visible mechanical effects, as in the fall of a stone or the expansion of steam. Now it had been known ever since the time of the Greeks that a simple lever greatly alters the character of a force, making its action slower or faster, but does not alter its amount: the more intense the force, the slower and more limited its action. In modern times a similar truth was proved of every kind of machine, and it was recognized that, apart from friction, no machine of any kind either creates or destroys energy. Quite separately, it had been recognized that the electricity produced in a galvanic battery is exactly proportional to the amount of chemical action, and that almost any one of the forces just named can be converted into any of the others. All facts like these are now taken in under one general theory. Its details are gradually being made more certain and accurate, but its main principle is that a certain amount of mechanical energy is equal to a certain amount of heat, or of electricity, or of chemical action, or even of muscular effort.

The word hypothesis is used a great deal in connection with the subject we are discussing, and we must consider what it means. It comes from the Greek words ὑπό (hupo), under, and θέσις (thesis), placing, so it means exactly the same as the Latin word suppositio, a placing under, which gives us our everyday word supposition. In science it seems to mean imagining some thing, force or cause that lies underneath the phenomena we are examining and is the agent that produces them, but that cannot be directly observed. When we make a hypothesis, we assert that a cause exists on the strength of the effects we observe. How probable it is that the cause exists depends on how many different facts or partial laws it lets us explain, or bring into harmony. To be of any value at all, a hypothesis must harmonize at least two different facts. Suppose we account for the effects of opium by saying, with Molière, that it possesses a dormitive power (a power of putting people to sleep), or say that a magnet attracts because it has a magnetic power. Everyone can see that we gain nothing. We know neither more nor less about the dormitive power or the magnetic power than we did about the opium or the magnet. But suppose we say that a magnet attracts because currents of electricity are circulating inside it. The hypothesis may seem very improbable, but it is valid, because it draws a certain analogy between a magnet and a coil of wire carrying electricity. Such a coil of wire attracts other coils in exactly the way one magnet attracts another, so this hypothesis lets us harmonize several different facts.

Take another example: the existence of intense heat in the interior of the earth. It is hypothetical in the sense that we cannot actually see the heat and measure it directly. But it harmonizes so many facts drawn from different sources that we can hardly doubt it is there. Hot springs and volcanoes are facts in its favor, though they might be explained on other grounds. The empirical law that the heat increases as we sink mines, in any part of the earth's surface, is stronger evidence. The intensely heated condition of the sun and the other stars strongly confirms it, because it shows that other bodies really do exist in the condition we suppose the earth's interior to be in. The cool state of the earth's surface is perfectly consistent with the earth's comparatively small size and with the known facts and laws about how heat is conducted and radiated. And the more we learn about the way the sun's heat is kept up by the fall of meteoric matter into it (the explanation most favored in the 1870s), the more probable it becomes that the earth, too, was once intensely heated like the sun, although it has been slowly cooling for an immense length of time. A supposition that agrees with so many facts, laws and other probable hypotheses almost stops being hypothetical, and its high probability makes us regard it as a known fact.

As long as it is consistent with the laws of thought, there is nothing we may not have to accept as a probable hypothesis, however hard it may be to imagine and understand. The force of gravity is hypothetical in the sense that we know it only by its effects on the motions of bodies. The way it weakens with distance does indeed match exactly the way that light, sound, electric or magnetic attraction, and in fact every influence that spreads out from a point through space, grows weaker. So it is probable that the law of the inverse square is absolutely true. But in other respects gravity goes strongly against all our ideas. If sound could travel to the sun as fast as it travels in the earth's atmosphere, it would take nearly fourteen years to get there. If the sun and the earth were joined by a solid, continuous bar of iron, a strong pull at one end would not be felt at the other for nearly three years. Light, it is true, comes from the sun in a little more than eight minutes. But what are we to think of the force of gravity, which seems to reach the sun in an instant, so short that no calculation has yet been able to detect any interval at all? In fact there seems to be some reason to suppose that gravity is felt instantaneously throughout the immeasurable regions of space.

The undulatory hypothesis of light (the hypothesis that light is a wave) has features just as extraordinary and inconceivable. That light really does consist of tiny but extremely rapid vibrations of something that fills space is almost certain. This hypothesis brings great harmony into the enormously varied and complicated phenomena of light, and it explains the analogy between light and sound. It is hard, of course, to imagine that anything can vibrate so fast as to strike the retina of the eye 831,479,000,000,000 times in one second, which is what must happen with violet light according to this hypothesis. But that is nothing compared with the difficulty of imagining space filled with a solid ether (the medium that was then believed to carry light), extremely rigid and elastic, which nevertheless offers no noticeable resistance to ordinary matter passing through it, and has no weight of its own. It has indeed been claimed that comets come back later than they should because of friction against this ether, and Mr. Balfour Stewart believes he has produced heat by the friction of a metal disc against the ether in a vacuum. If these claims prove true, we will have new facts in harmony with the theory of light, which will then be less hypothetical than before.

Now there is no difficulty in seeing the part that hypothesis plays in the deductive method of scientific investigation, which we considered in the last lesson. The preliminary induction is replaced, more or less completely, by imagining agents that we think could produce the known effects in question. Suppose our object is to explain what causes ebbing and flowing wells, which are found in many parts of the world (springs that flow and stop, or rise and fall, at intervals). We cannot possibly begin by exploring the inside of the earth until we find the source of a spring and observe its circumstances. We have to imagine cavities and channels of various shapes, until we think of an arrangement that would, according to the known laws of hydrostatics, make the water flow irregularly in the way we observe. If we can show that cavities of a particular shape will produce that effect, and can think of no other way it could be produced, the hypothesis becomes established as almost a certain fact.

It is the same with any great hypothesis, such as the theory of light. We have no way of directly observing and measuring the qualities of the ether that is the medium of light. All we know about this ether at present comes from the observed phenomena of light. So we are driven to invent something and give it qualities from which we can calculate, by certain principles of mechanics, what effects to expect. Then, finding that these effects can be made to harmonize with the effects actually observed, we rely on this agreement to prove that the ether exists. The truth of a hypothesis therefore depends entirely on its later verification, its agreement with observed facts. To invent hypotheses that cannot be verified in this way, or to invent them and then neglect to verify them, leads either to no result at all or to fallacy. But when the verification is careful and complete, no one can fairly object to the use of hypothesis. It may then be as certain as any other way of investigating, and it is indispensable in any case. In fact there was little truth or reason in Newton's famous protest against the use of hypothesis, "Hypotheses non fingo" (I do not frame hypotheses). The truth is that his theory of gravitation rested on the greatest and most successful of all hypotheses, while his views of the material nature of light, and of the causes of its peculiar phenomena, involved a false hypothesis that has long since been completely disproved.

The word theory has been used constantly in the last few lessons, and it deserves some examination. It comes from the Greek θεωρία (theoria), meaning contemplation, reflection or speculation, but that gives us little clue to how we use it now. In reality the word is highly ambiguous. Sometimes it is used as the equivalent of hypothesis, and at other times as the equivalent of a general law or truth. When people form theories about comets, the sun, the cause of earthquakes and so on, they imagine a great many things that may or may not exist. Theories like these are really complicated hypotheses, and should be called so. In this sense there are two theories of electricity. One supposes that there is a single fluid, which collects in some places and then tends to discharge itself toward places where there is too little of it, just as water always tends to find its level. The other supposes that there are two fluids, which are usually united but, when separated, tend to rush back together again. These so-called theories are really hypotheses, because we have no independent evidence that any such fluid exists, and it is now almost certain that there is no such thing. The atomic theory, again, is really a hypothesis put forward by Dalton to explain the remarkable laws he discovered in the proportions in which chemical elements combine. It is a valid hypothesis insofar as it really does explain why the quantities that combine are fixed. But as to the shapes, properties or absolute sizes of the atoms, it is purely hypothetical, because we have no facts in those respects for it to harmonize, and no apparent way of getting any.

In another, and more proper, sense, theory is opposed to practice, just as the general is opposed to the particular. The theory of gravitation means all the more general laws of motion and attraction on which Newton founded his system of the universe. We may know what those laws are without being able to work out the position of a planet or put them to any practical use. Skilled astronomers must calculate the particular results before navigators, travelers or anyone else can use them in practice to find latitude or longitude. When we speak of the mathematical theory of sound, the lunar theory or the theory of the tides, the word has no special reference to hypothesis. It simply means general knowledge, or science. It implies that we possess a complete series of general and accurate laws, but does nothing to set them apart from accurate knowledge in general. When a word like theory really is used in an equivocal way, it is not wise to try to give it an exact definition, which would be imaginary and artificial.

The word fact is used very often in this book, as in most books, and calls for a few remarks. It comes from factum, the past participle of facere, to do, and so would mean something done, an act or deed. But its meaning has obviously been greatly extended by analogy. We usually set fact and theory against each other. But just as theory seems to have two meanings that get confused, I believe that fact is ambiguous too. Sometimes it means what is certain and known by the evidence of the senses, as opposed to what is known only probably, by hypothesis and inference. At other times it is contrasted with a general law, and means a particular instance or case. A law of great generality may often be just as certain and true as the particular facts that come under it, especially in mathematics, so in this case the contrast must be between the general and the particular. We also often use the word in everyday life simply to mean a truth. We might say, for example, "It is a fact that the primary laws of thought are the foundation of reasoning." In short, just as theory means, ambiguously, what is hypothetical, general, abstract or uncertain, so fact is equally ambiguous, and means, in a confused way, what is intuitively known, particular, concrete or certain.

Questions and Exercises

1. What do you mean by the explanation of a fact?

2. State the three ways in which a law of nature may be explained, and suggest some more examples of each case.

3. Define tendency. Do all causes consist only of tendencies, or can you find examples to the contrary?

4. Give a definition of hypothesis. How can a valid hypothesis be told apart from an invalid one?

5. What place does hypothesis hold in the Deductive Method?

6. Explain the ambiguities of the words theory and fact.
