Lesson 4: Of the Ambiguity of Terms

No part of logic is more genuinely useful than the part that deals with the ambiguity of terms: the uncertainty, and the variety, of the meanings that words carry. Nothing matters more for good habits of thinking and reasoning than a thorough knowledge of how imperfect language is. Comparatively few terms have one clear meaning and one meaning only. And whenever two or more meanings get mixed up without our noticing, we are bound to commit a logical fallacy. Suppose someone argues that "punishment is an evil," and that by the principles of morality "no evil is to be allowed even with the purpose of doing good." At first we might not see how to escape the conclusion that "no punishments should be allowed," since they cause evil. A little thought shows that the word evil is being used here in two completely different senses. In the first statement it means physical evil, or pain. In the second it means moral evil, or wrongdoing. Moral evil must never be committed, but it does not follow that physical evils must never be inflicted, because they are often the very means of preventing moral evil.

Here is another very convincing fallacy, which has been put forward in many forms: "A thoroughly benevolent man cannot possibly refuse to relieve the poor; and since a person who cannot possibly act otherwise than he does can claim no merit for his actions, it follows that a thoroughly benevolent man can claim no merit for his actions." By this kind of argument, the more virtuous a person became, and so the harder he found it to act wrongly, the less credit he would deserve. Everyone feels sure that the conclusion is false. But the cause of the fallacy can only be found by noticing that the words cannot possibly have a double meaning. In the first case they refer to the pull of moral motives, or good character. In the second they refer to circumstances entirely beyond a person's control, such as being forced by the law, lack of money, or lack of personal freedom. The more closely you study the subtle shifts in the meaning of common words, the more convinced you will be that the tools you have to use in every conversation and argument are dangerous ones. So I must ask you to pay close attention to this lesson.

Terms are called univocal when they can bring only one single, definite meaning to mind. They are called equivocal, or ambiguous, when they have two or more different meanings. Notice, though, that a term is not equivocal just because it can be applied to many objects, as long as it is applied to all of them in the same sense. Cathedral, for instance, is the name of St Paul's, of York Minster, and of the principal churches of Salisbury, Wells, Lincoln and a number of other cities. But it is not ambiguous, because these are all just different instances of the same meaning: they are all objects of the same description, or kind. The word cathedral is probably univocal, with only one logical meaning. The word church, on the other hand, is equivocal. Sometimes it means the building where religious worship takes place. Sometimes it means the body of people who belong to one sect or denomination and meet in churches. And sometimes "the church" means the clergy, as distinct from the laity (the ordinary members). So there is a clear difference in the sense in which the word is used at different times.

Univocal terms are found mostly in technical and scientific language. Steam-engine, gasometer (a large gas-storage tank), railway train, permanent way (the finished track bed of a railway), and a great many other technical names for distinct, familiar objects are univocal enough. In everyday life the names penny, mantelpiece, teacup, and bread and butter have a definite and single enough meaning. In chemistry, too, oxygen, hydrogen, sulphate of copper, alumina, lithia and thousands of other terms are very precise. Often the words themselves were invented quite recently, and their meanings were fixed exactly and kept unchanged. Every science has, or ought to have, a set of terms just as precise and certain in meaning (see Lesson 33). The names of individual objects, buildings, events or people are also usually quite certain and clear: Julius Caesar, William the Conqueror, the first Napoleon, St Peter's, Westminster Abbey, the Great Exhibition of 1851, and so on.

But however many univocal terms we can list, equivocal terms are astonishingly common. They include most of the nouns and adjectives we use every day in ordinary life. They are called ambiguous, from the Latin verb ambigo, to wander, hesitate or be in doubt. They are also called homonymous, from the Greek ὁμός (homos), same, and ὄνομα (onoma), name. Whenever someone uses equivocal words in a way that mixes up their different meanings, and so falls into error, we can say he commits the fallacy of Equivocation, in the logical sense of that name (see Lesson 20). In everyday life, though, we only say someone equivocates when he uses words knowingly and dishonestly, in a way designed to confuse the true meaning with the apparent one.

I will now describe the different kinds and causes of ambiguity in words, following in part the interesting chapters on the subject in Dr. Watts's Logic. First, we can distinguish three classes of equivocal words, depending on whether they are:

	1. Equivocal in sound only.
	2. Equivocal in spelling only.
	3. Equivocal both in sound and spelling.

The first two classes matter comparatively little, and they don't often lead to serious error. They produce what we would call trivial mistakes. When spoken aloud, for example, we can confuse the words right, wright and rite (a ceremony); also rein, rain and reign, might and mite, and so on. Partly because of careless pronunciation, people have been known to confuse the four words air, hair, hare and heir.

Words equivocal in spelling but not in sound include tear (a drop from the eye) and tear, pronounced "tare," meaning a rip in cloth; or lead, the metal, and lead, as in following someone else's lead. Such resemblances can cause little more than a moment's misunderstanding, so we will move straight on to the class of words that are equivocal both in sound and in spelling. I will divide these into three groups, according to whether the equivocation comes:

1. From the accidental confusion of different words.

2. From the transfer of meaning by the association of ideas.

3. From the logical transfer of meaning to analogous objects.

I. In the first group we put a certain number of curious but not very important cases. Here the ambiguity comes from the confusion of entirely different words. They came from different languages, or from different roots in the same language, but over time they took on the same sound and spelling. Take the word mean. It can mean what is medium or mediocre, from the French moyen and the Latin medius, which are related to the Anglo-Saxon mid, or middle. Or it can mean what is small-minded and base, and then it comes from the Anglo-Saxon gemæne, meaning "belonging to the mæne, or many": in short, whatever is common or vulgar. The verb to mean can hardly be confused with the adjective mean, but it comes from a third, separate root, probably related to the Sanskrit verb for "to think."

Here are some other examples of this accidental kind of ambiguity. Rent can be a money payment, from the French rente (from rendre, to give back). Or it can be a tear, the result of the action of rending. This second word is of Anglo-Saxon origin, and belongs to the large group of words beginning with r or wr that imitate, more or less closely, the sound of the action they name. Pounds, from the Latin pondus, a weight, gets confused with pound in the sense of a village pinfold, the pen where stray cattle were shut up, which comes from the Saxon pyndan, to pen up. Fell, a mountain, is a completely different word from fell, a skin or hide (a sense already old-fashioned by the 1870s). And pulse, a throb or beat, and pulse, meaning peas, beans or a soup made from them, though both come from Greek or Latin, are probably quite unrelated words. Curiously, gin in the sense of a trap or machine is a shortened form of engine, while gin the alcoholic drink is a corruption of Geneva, the place where the spirit was first made.

Some important cases of confusion have been found in grammar. One example is the confusion between the number one, which comes from an Aryan root (what we now call Indo-European) through the Latin unus, and the indefinite pronoun one (as in "one ought to do one's duty"). The pronoun is really a corrupted form of the French word homme, meaning man. To this day the Germans use man in this sense, as in man sagt, that is, "one says."

2. By far the largest share of equivocal words became equivocal through a transfer of meaning. The word moved from the thing it first named to some other thing that was regularly connected with it, so that the two became closely linked in our thoughts. In the language of Parliament, for example, "the House" means either the chamber where the members meet, or the body of members who happen to be gathered in it at any time. In the same way, the word church originally meant the building (κυριακόν, kyriakon, "the Lord's House") where any religious worshippers gather, but from there it has picked up a variety of meanings. It can mean a particular group of worshippers who regularly meet in one place, which is the sense it has in Acts 14:23. It can mean any body of people who hold the same beliefs and are joined in one organization, as in the Anglican, Greek or Roman Catholic Church. It is sometimes used to include the laity as well as the clergy. But perhaps more often the clergy and religious authorities of a sect or country are so strongly associated with the act of worship that they are called the church in a special sense, as if they were the church above all others. It is also quite clear that the word means something entirely different depending on whether it is used by a member of the Anglican, Greek, Roman Catholic, Scottish Presbyterian, or any other existing church.

The word foot has gone through several curious but very clear transfers of meaning. It first meant the foot of a person or an animal, and it is probably distantly related to the Latin pes, pedis and the Greek πούς, ποδός (pous, podos). Because the length of a foot is a natural rough measure of length, the word came to be used for a fixed measure of length. And because the foot is at the bottom of the body, the name was extended by analogy to the foot of a mountain or the feet of a table. By a further extension, any position, plan, reason or argument that we stand on and rely on is called our foot, or footing. The same word also means soldiers who fight on their feet, that is, infantry, and the measured unit of a line of verse, which has a definite length. These very different meanings are naturally connected with the original one, and we can see this from the fact that the Latin and Greek words for foot go through exactly the same series of ambiguities.

It would be a long job to trace out all the various, and often contradictory, meanings of the word fellow. Originally a fellow was one who follows another, that is, a companion. From there it came to mean the other one of a pair, as one shoe is the fellow of the other, or simply an equal, as when we say that Shakespeare "hath not a fellow." From the plain meaning of companion, it also came to mean, vaguely, a person, as in the question "What fellow is that?" But there is a curious mixture of scorn and affection in the word. In the 1870s, to call a man a mere fellow, or simply a fellow in a certain tone of voice, was to show him severe contempt. Change the tone of voice, or the words around it, even slightly, and it becomes one of the warmest and most affectionate names there is, as when we speak of a dear fellow or a good fellow. We can add the technical meanings of the word too, as in a Fellow of a college, or of a learned society.

Another good example of many different meanings growing from a single root is the word post. Originally a post was something posited, that is, placed firmly in the ground, such as an upright piece of wood or stone. That meaning survives in a lamp-post, a gate-post, a signal-post, and so on. A post was often used to mark a fixed spot of ground, as a mile-post does, so the word came to mean the fixed or appointed place where the post stood, as in a military post, or the post of danger or of honor. In the days of the Roman Empire, the fixed stations where horses were kept ready, so that people could travel fast, were called posts in this way. From there the whole system for carrying people or news came to be called the posts. The word still has exactly this meaning in most of Europe, and we still use it in post-chaise (a hired fast carriage of the horse-drawn era), post-boy and post-horse, and in postillion (the rider on one of the horses). A postal system for letters has now been running for about two centuries in England and other countries, so this is perhaps the meaning most closely tied to the word post today, and a number of expressions have arisen from it, such as post-office, postage, postal-guide, postman, postmaster, postal-telegraph and so on. Curiously enough, we now have iron letter-posts (the pillar boxes where letters are mailed), and in them the word post is brought back to exactly its original meaning.

I chose the words above for their curious variety of meanings, but I do not hesitate to claim that most common nouns have several meanings, some more and some fewer. Dr. Watts, in his Logic, suggests that the words book, bible, fish, house and elephant are univocal terms, but the reader will easily find ambiguities in each of them. Fish, for instance, means something very different in natural history from what it means to people who are not scientists. They include under it not only true fishes but shellfish, or mollusks, and the cetacea, such as whales and seals; in short, every swimming animal, whether it has the character of a true fish or not. Elephant, in a stationer's or bookseller's shop, meant a large size of paper in the 1870s rather than a large animal. Bible sometimes means a particular copy of the Bible, and sometimes the collection of works that makes up the Holy Scriptures. The word man is remarkably ambiguous. Sometimes it means man as distinguished from woman. At other times it is certainly used to include both sexes. In certain recent election cases, the lawyers could not decide whether the word man, as used in the Reform Act of 1867, ought or ought not to be read as including women. On other occasions man means a grown male as distinguished from a boy, and it often means someone who is emphatically a man, with a manly character. Now and then it is used the way groom is, to mean a servant, as in the proverb, "Like master, like man." At other times it means a husband in particular.

3. Third, among ambiguous words we must pick out those that get their different meanings in a somewhat different way, namely by analogy, or real resemblance. When we speak of a sweet taste, a sweet flower, a sweet tune, a sweet landscape, a sweet face, a sweet poem, we are plainly applying one and the same word to very different things. Something as concrete as a lump of sugar can hardly be compared directly with something as intellectual as Tennyson's poem "The May Queen." Even so, if the word sweet is to be called ambiguous, it is ambiguous in a different way from the words we looked at before. All these things are called sweet because of a particular kind of pleasure they give, and that pleasure cannot be described except by comparing it with sugar. In the same way we call a pain sharp, a disappointment bitter, a person's temper sour, the future bright or gloomy, an achievement brilliant. All these adjectives imply a comparison with the simplest kinds of bodily sensation. The adjective brilliant comes from the French briller, to glitter or sparkle, and it keeps this meaning fully when we speak of a brilliant diamond, a brilliant star and so on. But by what a subtle analogy we speak of a brilliant position, a brilliant achievement, brilliant talents, a brilliant style! We cannot speak of a clear explanation, indefatigable perseverance, a perspicuous style, or a sore calamity, without using in each of these phrases a double analogy to physical impressions, actions or events. (Perspicuous means clear, literally "see-through"; indefatigable means "that cannot be tired out.") We will see in Lesson 6 that we owe the creation of all the names for mental feelings and mental things to this process.

Questions and Exercises

2. What other names are equivocal terms often called by?

3. Distinguish the three kinds of ambiguous terms, and find examples of each.

4. Distinguish the three causes that have produced the third and most important class of ambiguous terms.
