Lesson 24: On Method, Analysis and Synthesis

Many writers on logic have held that logical doctrine has a fourth part, in addition to the three parts that deal in turn with Terms, Propositions and Syllogisms. That fourth part is method. The doctrine of Judgment considers how terms are arranged and combined into propositions. The doctrine of the Syllogism considers how propositions are arranged so that they form arguments. In the same way, they said, there should be a fourth part, called Method, to govern how syllogisms are arranged and combined into a complete discourse. So method is defined as arranging the parts of a discourse so that the whole is as easy as possible to understand.

The famous Peter Ramus, who was killed in the Massacre of St. Bartholomew's Day (Paris, 1572), was the first to propose making method a part of the science of logic in this way. But we may well doubt whether any definite set of rules or principles can be given to guide us in arranging arguments. Every discourse has to be made of arguments arranged to suit the particular nature of its subject. Things vary without end in the way they need to be treated, and no general rules can be given for treating them. So the supposed general rules of method are no better than truisms: they tell us nothing we must not be supposed to know already. For example, we are told that in composing any discourse we should take care that:

1. Nothing should be missing or superfluous.

2. The separate parts should agree with each other.

3. Nothing should be treated unless it suits the subject or purpose.

4. The separate parts should be connected by suitable transitions.

But obviously the whole difficulty lies in deciding what is missing or superfluous, what is suitable or consistent. Rules of this kind simply tell us to do what we ought to do, without saying what that is.

All the same, there are certain general ways of treating any subject that can be clearly told apart, and the student of logic should understand them well. Logic cannot teach him exactly how and when to use each kind of method. But it can teach him what the methods are and what they can do, so that he is more likely to use them rightly. We must distinguish:

	1. The method of discovery,
	2. The method of instruction.

The method of discovery is the one we use in acquiring knowledge. It really consists of the processes of inference and induction by which we find out general truths from collecting and examining particular facts. This method will be the subject of most of our remaining lessons. The second method applies only when knowledge has already been acquired and expressed in the form of general laws, rules, principles or truths. Then all we have to do is get to know these, and learn the proper way to apply them to particular cases, and we will have a complete knowledge of the subject.

Take a student learning Latin, Greek, French, German, or any other well-known language. He is given a complete grammar and syntax that set out all the principles, rules and nature of the language. He receives these instructions and takes them as true on the authority of his teacher, or of the writer of the book. Once he has made them familiar, he has nothing left to do but combine and apply the rules in reading or writing the language. In short, he follows the method of instruction. But the scholar who has been handed some writings in an unknown language, and is trying to work out its alphabet, words, grammar and syntax, has to go about it in a completely different, opposite way. He doesn't have the laws of the grammar. He has words and sentences that obey those laws, and he has to detect the laws, if he can, by watching their effects on the written language. In short, he follows the method of discovery. It consists of a tedious comparison of letters, words and phrases, carried out so as to reveal the forms and combinations in which they most often occur. The process would be a strictly inductive one, of the kind I will partly illustrate in the lessons on induction. But it is far harder than the method of instruction, and it depends a great deal on making good use of conjecture and hypothesis, which takes a certain skill and inventiveness.

Exactly the same is true of investigating natural things and events. The principles of mechanics, such as the lever, the inclined plane and the other Mechanical Powers, or the Laws of Motion, seem fairly simple and obvious when they are explained to us in textbooks. But the early philosophers had no such books. They had only the Book of Nature, which sets out not the laws but the results of the laws. It took the most patient and skillful investigation, and hundreds of mistakes, before those laws were found out. Today it is very easy to understand the Copernican system of astronomy, which has the planets revolving around the sun in orbits of different sizes. Once we know the theory, we can easily see why the planets move in such different ways and appear in such different positions, and why they sometimes seem to stand still. It is easy to see, too, why on top of their own proper motions they all seem to go around the earth every day, because the earth turns on its axis daily. But all these changes were extremely puzzling to the ancients, who believed the earth stood still.

So the method of discovery begins with facts that are apparent to the senses, and it has the hard task of detecting the universal laws or general principles that only the intellect can grasp. It has been well said that the method of discovery goes from the things that are better known to us, that is, to our senses (nobis notiora), to the things that are simpler, or better known in nature (notiora naturæ). The method of instruction goes in the opposite direction. It begins with the things notiora naturæ and goes on to show or explain the things nobis notiora. The difference is almost like the difference between hiding and seeking. Whoever hid a thing knows where to find it. A discoverer is not in that position. He has no clue except what he may come across in his own careful and shrewd search.

Closely matching the distinction between the methods of discovery and instruction is the distinction between the methods of analysis and synthesis. It is very important that the reader understand clearly what these terms mean in their various uses. Analysis is the process of separating a whole into its parts, and synthesis is the combining of parts into a whole. An analytical chemist who is given a piece of mineral to examine may be able to separate completely the chemical elements it is made of, and find out what they are and in what proportions they are present. This is chemical analysis. At other times the chemist mixes carefully weighed amounts of certain simple substances and combines them into a new compound substance. This is chemical synthesis. Logical analysis and synthesis must not be confused with these physical actions. Even so, they are actions of the mind of a similar kind.

In logical synthesis we begin with the simplest possible notions or ideas and combine them. The best possible example is elementary geometry. In Euclid we begin with certain simple notions: points, straight lines, angles, right angles, circles and so on. Put three straight lines together and we make a triangle. Add to this the notion of a right angle, and we form the notion of a right-angled triangle. Join four equal lines at right angles to each other and we get the idea of a square. Now suppose we imagine such a square built on each side of a right-angled triangle, and reason from the necessary qualities of these figures. We discover that the two squares on the sides that contain the right angle must together be exactly equal to the square on the third side, as is shown in the 47th Proposition of Euclid's first book. This is a perfect example of combining simple ideas into more complex ones.

Often, though, in geometry we have to take the opposite course, analysis. We may be given a complicated geometrical figure, and in order to prove the properties it has, we may have to break it up into its separate parts and consider the properties of each part separately from the others.

A similar distinction between the analytic and the synthetic methods can be traced all through the natural sciences. By keeping exact records of how the weather looks and changes, we can easily build up an immense collection of facts. Each recorded fact involves a crowd of different circumstances occurring together. In any storm or shower of rain, for instance, we have to consider the direction and strength of the wind; the temperature and dampness of the air; the height and shapes of the clouds; the amount of rain that falls, or the lightning and thunder that come with it. If we tried to explain the changes of the weather by analysis alone, we would have to try breaking each storm or change of weather down into its separate circumstances, and comparing each with every other, to find out which circumstances usually go together. No doubt we could find out in this way, with considerable certainty, what kinds of clouds and what changes of wind, temperature, moisture and so on usually come before each kind of storm. In time we might even give some imperfect explanation of what goes on in the atmosphere.

But we could also make good use of the synthetic method. Earlier chemical investigations tell us that the atmosphere consists mainly of two permanent gases, oxygen and nitrogen, together with water vapor, whose amount varies a great deal. We can test by experiment what happens when portions of such air, at various degrees of dampness, are compressed or allowed to expand, or are mixed together, as often happens in the atmosphere. In this way we discover that whenever moist air is allowed to expand, cloud is produced, and perhaps drops of rain. Dr. Hutton, too, found that whenever cold moist air is mixed with warm moist air, cloud is again produced. We can safely argue from small experiments like these to what happens in the atmosphere. By putting together synthetically everything that the sciences of chemistry, mechanics and electricity tell us about air, wind, cloud and lightning, we can explain what happens in a thunderstorm far more completely than we could by simply watching what happens in the storm. Here, however, we are getting ahead of ourselves, into the methods of inductive investigation, which we must consider in the following lessons. It will turn out that induction is equivalent to analysis, and that the deductive kinds of reasoning we have dealt with in the earlier lessons are synthetic in character.

It has been said that the synthetic method usually corresponds to the method of instruction, and the analytic method to the method of discovery. But it is possible to discover new truths by synthesis and to teach old ones by analysis. Sir John Herschel, in his well-known Outlines of Astronomy, partly adopts the analytic method. He imagines a spectator who first surveys the appearances of the heavenly bodies and of the earth's surface and looks for an explanation. Then he leads him through a series of arguments showing that these appearances really point to the roundness of the earth, its turning on its own axis and its revolution around the sun, and its humble position as one of the smaller planets of the solar system. Mr. Norman Lockyer's Elementary Lessons in Astronomy is a clear example of the synthetic method of instruction. He starts by describing the sun, the center of the system, and then adds the planets and the other members of the system one after another, until at last we have the complete picture. The reader, who has for the time being taken everything on the writer's authority, then sees that the description matches the truth. Each method, we must admit, has its own advantages.

We must take careful note that the meaning of analysis, and so the meaning of synthesis, changes depending on whether we are looking at terms in intension or in extension. To divide, or analyze, a class of things in extension, I must add a quality, or difference. For example, I divide the class organism when I add the quality vegetable, and separate vegetable organism from what is not vegetable. So analysis in extension is the same process as synthesis in intension. And the other way around: whenever I separate, or analyze, a group of qualities, each part belongs to a larger class of things in extension. When I analyze the notion vegetable organism, and consider the notion organism apart from vegetable, I am obviously adding the whole class of animal organisms to the class I am considering. So analysis in intension is synthesis in extension. A reader who has thought carefully about Lessons 5 and 12 will probably see that this connection between the two processes is just a restatement of the law (see Lesson 5) that "as the intension of a term is increased, the extension is decreased."

To express the difference between knowledge gained deductively and knowledge gained inductively, the Latin phrases a priori and a posteriori are often used. By a priori reasoning we mean argument based on truths already known. A posteriori reasoning, on the other hand, works from the consequences of a general truth to infer what that general truth is. Many philosophers believe that the mind naturally possesses certain laws or truths that it must recognize in every act of thought. All such truths, if there are any, would be a priori truths. No one can doubt, for example, that in thinking we must always recognize the three Primary Laws of Thought discussed in Lesson 14. There we have a priori knowledge that "matter cannot both have weight and be without weight," or that "everything must be either self-luminous or not self-luminous." But no law of thought can force us to think that matter has weight while the luminous ether (the medium that was then believed to carry light) has no weight, or that Jupiter and Venus are not self-luminous but comets are to some extent self-luminous. No doubt these facts are necessary consequences of the laws of nature and the general make-up of the world. But since we are not born knowing all the secrets of creation, we have to learn them by observation, that is, by the a posteriori method.

Nowadays, however, the name a priori is not usually restricted to truths obtained entirely without the help of observation. Knowledge may start out a posteriori, and yet, once it has been held for a long time and has become as certain as anything can be, it may serve as the ground of deductions. It may then be said to give a priori knowledge. For example, all scientists now believe that force (in today's terms, energy) cannot be created or destroyed by any of the processes of nature. If this is true, the force that disappears when a bullet hits a target must be turned into something else, and on a priori grounds we can say that the result will be heat. It's true that we could easily learn the same thing a posteriori, by picking up pieces of a bullet that has just hit a target and noticing that they are warm. But a priori knowledge has a great advantage: we can often apply it where experiment or observation would be difficult. If I lift a stone and drop it, the most delicate instruments could hardly show that the stone was heated by hitting the ground. Yet on a priori grounds I know that it must have been, and I can easily calculate how much heat was produced. In the same way we know, without the trouble of observing it, that Niagara Falls and every other waterfall produce heat. This is a fair example of a priori knowledge, because no one, as far as I have heard, has tested the fact or proved it a posteriori. All the same, the knowledge is founded in the first place on the experiments of Mr. Joule, who measured, in certain well-chosen cases, how much force is equivalent to a given amount of heat. The reader should take care, though, not to confuse the meaning of a priori explained here with the meaning given to the words by the philosophers who hold that the mind possesses knowledge independently of all observation.

It is not hard to see that the a priori method is equivalent to the synthetic method (see earlier in this lesson) considered in intension, and the a posteriori method, of course, is equivalent to the analytic method. But the same difference is really expressed by the words deductive and inductive, and we will often need to consider it in the following lessons.

Questions and Exercises

1. What place did earlier writers on logic give to method, and what are the rules of method?

2. Explain the expressions nobis notiora and notiora naturæ.

3. What kind of method is the usual method of instruction?

4. Prove that analysis in extension is synthesis in intension, using some of the series of terms in question 6 of Lesson 5 as illustrations.

5. Explain the exact meanings of the expressions a priori knowledge and a posteriori knowledge.

6. To which kind does our knowledge of the following facts belong?

(1) The light of the stars takes a long time to reach us.

(2) Vaccination protects against smallpox.

(3) A meteor becomes heated as it passes through the air.

(4) There must be either some inhabitants or no inhabitants on Jupiter.
