Lesson 2: The Three Parts of Logical Doctrine

We saw in the last lesson that logic is the Science of Reasoning, or the Science of the Necessary Laws of Thought, the laws we must follow if we are to argue consistently with ourselves and avoid contradicting ourselves. So argument, or reasoning, is strictly speaking the proper subject in front of us. But the most convenient and usual way to study logic is to look first at the parts that any argument must be made of. An architect has to know the materials of a building, and a mechanic the materials of a machine, before either can claim to understand how the thing is built. In the same way, it makes sense to describe the materials and instruments we work with in reasoning before we go on to the actual forms of argument.

Take a simple argument like the one in the last lesson:

	Iron is a metal,
	Every metal is an element,
	Therefore Iron is an element,—

We see that it is made up of three statements, or assertions. Each of them contains, besides some minor words, two nouns, or names of things, and the verb "is." In short, two names, or terms, joined by a verb make up an assertion, or proposition; and three such propositions make up an argument, which in this case is called a syllogism. So it is natural and convenient to describe terms first, since they are the simplest parts. Next we go on to the nature and the different kinds of propositions that are built out of terms. Then we will be ready to deal with the syllogism as a whole. These, then, are the three parts of logical doctrine.

We can say that the three parts of logic deal with terms, propositions and syllogisms. But it is just as true, or truer, to say that our real subject is the acts of mind that these forms of language point to. Logicians' opinions on this point have varied, or perhaps it is only the way they express themselves that has varied. Archbishop Whately says plainly that logic is entirely about language. Sir William Hamilton, Mr. Mansel and most other logicians treat it as being about the acts or states of mind that the words point to. Mr. J. S. Mill goes back to the things themselves that we are arguing about. So is the subject of logic language, thought, or objects? The simplest and truest answer is that in a certain sense it deals with all three. No process of reasoning can be explained or passed on to another person without words, so in practice we are limited to reasoning that has been put into the form of language. That means we will always be dealing with words, but only insofar as they are the instruments for recording and referring to our thoughts. The grammarian deals with language too, but he treats it simply as language, and his science stops once it has described and explained the forms, kinds and relations of words. Logic also deals with language, but only as the necessary sign of what the mind is doing.

Again, as long as we think correctly, we must think of things as they are. The state of mind inside us must match the state of things outside us whenever we get a chance to compare them. It is impossible, and we cannot even conceive, that iron should turn out not to be an elementary substance, if it is a metal and every metal is an element. We cannot suppose, and there is no reason to suppose, that the way our minds are built forces us to think of things differently from the way they are. If, then, we can assume that things really agree or differ just as correct logical thought leads us to believe they will, the views of the logicians I named do not seem impossible to reconcile. We deal with things insofar as they are the objects of thought, and we deal with language insofar as it is the embodiment of thought. If the reader keeps this explanation in mind, he will be spared some confusion later, when he reads different books on logic and finds that they vary enormously in their way of treating the subject, or at least in their way of putting it.

If logic, once it is put into language, has three parts—terms, propositions and syllogisms—then there must be just as many different kinds of thought, or operations of the mind. They are usually called:

	1. Simple apprehension.
	2. Judgment.
	3. Reasoning or discourse.

The first of these, Simple Apprehension, is the act of mind by which we simply become aware of something, or have a notion, idea or impression of it brought into the mind. The adjective "simple" means apart from other things, and "apprehension" means the mind's taking hold of something. So the name, or term, Iron instantly makes the mind think of a strong and very useful metal, but it does not tell us anything about it or compare it with anything else. The words sun, Jupiter, Sirius and St. Paul's Cathedral are terms too. They call up in the mind certain well-known objects, which stay in our memory even when they are not in front of our senses. In fact, a term like these is used simply as a stand-in for showing the actual things it names.

Judgment is a different action of the mind. It consists in comparing two notions or ideas of objects, which we got by simple apprehension, to find out whether they agree or differ. Clearly, then, we cannot judge or compare unless we are aware of two things, or have the notions of two things in the mind, at the same time. Suppose I compare Jupiter and Sirius. First I simply apprehend each of them. But when I bring them into comparison, I notice that they agree in being small, bright, shining bodies that rise and set and move around the sky at what looks like the same speed. On close examination, though, I notice that Sirius gives a twinkling, flickering light, while Jupiter shines steadily. Watching for longer shows that Jupiter and Sirius do not really move at the same steady speed: Jupiter shifts its position in the sky from night to night in a rather complicated way. If I widen the comparison to other heavenly bodies that I can apprehend, or see, at the same time, I will find a multitude of stars that agree with Sirius in giving a twinkling light and in staying perfectly fixed in position relative to one another. Meanwhile two or three other bodies can be seen that resemble Jupiter in giving a steady light and also in changing their place from night to night among the fixed stars. Through the action of judgment I have now formed in my mind the general notion of fixed stars, by mentally grouping together a number of objects that agree; and out of several other objects I have formed the general notion of planets. When I compare these two general notions, I find that they do not have the same qualities or appearances, and I state this in the proposition, "Planets are not fixed stars."

I have used the expression "General Notion" as if the reader already knew it well. But although philosophers have been using the expressions general notion, idea, conception, concept and so on for more than two thousand years, they have never managed to agree exactly on what the terms mean. One group of philosophers, called Nominalists, say it is all a matter of names. When we put together Jupiter, Mars, Saturn, Venus and the rest and call them planets, the common name is what binds them together in our minds. Others, called Realists, have claimed that besides these particular planets there really is something that combines the properties they all share, without any of the differences of size, color or motion that set them apart. Today, however, everyone agrees that nothing can physically exist that corresponds to a general notion. Anything that exists must be here or there, of this size or that size, so it would be one particular planet and not any planet whatever. The Nominalists seem just as wrong, though, because language, if it is to be of any use, must stand for something, and must correspond, as we have seen, to acts of mind. So if proper names raise in our minds the images of particular things, like the sun or Jupiter, general names should raise general notions.

The true view seems to be that of the philosophers called Conceptualists. They say that the general notion is the mind's knowledge of the common properties, or resemblances, of the things the notion takes in. So the notion planet really means the awareness, in anyone's mind, that there are certain heavenly bodies which agree in giving a steady light and in moving around the sky differently from the fixed stars. I should add, however, that many people, including Sir William Hamilton, would be counted as Nominalists and yet hold that the general name carries with it an awareness of the resemblance between the things it denotes. It is not easy to draw a precise line between this form of the doctrine and conceptualism, and the subject is too much a matter of debate to follow any further in this book.

It will become clear over these lessons that the whole of logic, and the whole of any science, consists in arranging the individual things we meet into general notions, or classes, and giving them suitable general names, or terms, so that our knowledge of them can be made as simple and as general as possible. Every properly formed general notion allows us to state general laws, or truths, about it. Of the planets, for example, we can affirm that they move in elliptical orbits around the sun from west to east, that they shine with light reflected from the sun, and so on. Of the fixed stars we can affirm that they shine with their own light, that they are incomparably farther away than the planets, and so on. All reasoning, we will find, comes from this faculty of judgment, which lets us discover and affirm that a large number of objects have similar properties, so that whatever is known of some of them can be inferred and asserted of the others.

It is in applying knowledge like this that we use the third act of mind, called discourse or reasoning. By it, starting from certain judgments, we are able to form a new judgment without going back to the real objects at all. If we know that iron comes under the general notion of metal, and that this notion comes under the still wider notion of element, then without examining iron any further we know that it is a simple substance that cannot be broken down, the kind chemists call an element. Or suppose we learn from one source that Neptune is a planet, and from another that planets move in elliptical orbits. We can join these two pieces of knowledge together in the mind and draw out the truth that Neptune moves in an elliptical orbit.

Reasoning, or Discourse, can therefore be defined as the progress of the mind from one or more given propositions to a proposition different from those given. The propositions we argue from are called Premises, and the one we draw from them is called the Conclusion. The conclusion is said to follow from the premises, or to be concluded, inferred or collected from them. The premises get their name because they are put forward, or put at the beginning (Latin præ, before, and mitto, I send or put). The heart of the process is gathering up the truth that the premises contain when they are joined together, and carrying it with us into the conclusion, where it takes shape in a new proposition, or assertion. We extract from the premises all the information that is useful for the purpose in hand—and that is all that reasoning does.

I have now pointed out the three parts of logical doctrine—Terms, Propositions, and Reasoning or Syllogism—into which the subject is conveniently divided, and we will go on to look at each of them. It is worth mentioning, though, that a fourth part has often been added, called Method, which deals with the arrangement of the parts of any composition.

It is sometimes said that method is to the syllogism what the syllogism is to the proposition, and what the proposition is to the term, and that a fourth division is needed to complete the doctrine of logic. In any case, this fourth part is certainly much less important and less clearly marked off than the other three, and everything that will be said about it is in Lesson 24.

Questions and Exercises

1. Name the parts that a syllogism is made of.

2. How far is it correct to say that logic is concerned with language?

3. What are the three acts of mind considered in logic? Which of them is more especially the subject of the science?

4. Can you state exactly what is meant by a general notion, idea, or conception?

5. How do the Nominalists, Realists, and Conceptualists differ in their opinions about the nature of a general notion?

6. What is the supposed fourth part of logic?
