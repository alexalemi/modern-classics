Lesson 16: The Moods and Figures of the Syllogism

We now have in hand all the principles of reasoning, and the rules built on them, that let us tell a true syllogism from one that only looks like one. Our job in this lesson is to find out all the different shapes in which a mediate inference, or syllogism, can turn up. We know that every syllogistic argument must contain three propositions, and three distinct terms, each appearing twice in those propositions. As far as we know so far, each proposition of the syllogism can be either affirmative or negative, and either universal or particular. So it is not hard to work out the largest possible number of ways a syllogism could conceivably be put together. In short, any one of the four propositions A, E, I or O can be taken as the major premise; it can be joined to any one of the same four as the minor premise; and any one of the four again can be added as the conclusion. That gives us a series of combinations, or ways of joining the letters A, E, I, O. Here are a few of them written out:

	AAA | AEA | AIA | AOA | EAA | EEA
	AAE | AEE | AIE | AOE | EAE | EEE
	AAI | AEI | AII | AOI | EAI | EEI
	AAO | AEO | AIO | AOO | EAO | &c.

Clearly there will be 4x4x4, or 64, such combinations in all, and only 23 of them are given above. The student can easily write out the rest by carrying on the same orderly changes of the letters. Starting with AAA, we change the right-hand letter to E, then I, then O. Then we do the same starting from AEA instead. Once the middle letter has been carried through all its changes, we start changing the left-hand letter. With each change of the left-hand letter we have to repeat all sixteen changes of the other two, so there are plainly 64 different conceivable ways of arranging propositions into syllogisms.

We call each of these triplets of propositions a mood, or form, of the syllogism (from the Latin modus, shape). Now we have to work out how many of these forms can really be used in valid arguments, as opposed to those that break one or more of the rules of the syllogism. The mood AEA, for example, would break the 6th rule, that if one premise is negative the conclusion must be negative too. AIE breaks the other half of the same rule, that a negative conclusion can only be proved by means of a negative premise. EEA, EEE and so on break the 5th rule, which forbids us to reason at all from two negative premises. Examples of any of these moods are easy to invent, and their falsity is very obvious. For AEA we might take:

	All Austrians are Europeans,
	No Australians are Europeans;
	Therefore, all Australians are Austrians.

Many of the 64 conceivable moods are ruled out by the 7th and 8th rules of the syllogism. AIA and EIE break the rule that if one premise is particular, the conclusion must be particular too. IIA, IOO, OIO and many others break the rule against two particular premises. Some combinations break more than one rule. OOO has both negative premises and particular premises, and OOA breaks the 6th rule as well. It is an excellent exercise in using the rules of the syllogism to write out all 64 combinations and then strike out every one that breaks a rule. If you go about it systematically, the job will not be as long or as tedious as it might seem. You will find that only twelve moods escape being ruled out, and so far these can be considered good forms of reasoning. They are:

	AAA | EAE | IAI | OAO
	AAI | EAO | (IEO) | ""
	AEE | EIO | "" | ""
	AEO | "" | "" | ""
	AII | "" | "" | ""
	AOO | "" | "" | ""

Of these, though, IEO will soon have to be thrown out, because we will find that it really breaks the 4th rule and involves an Illicit process of the major term. So there are only eleven moods of the syllogism that are really valid, and we can now account for all sixty-four moods:

	Excluded by | "" | Number of moods.
	Negative premises, | Rule 5 | 16
	Particular premises | Rule 7 | 12
	One negative premise | Rule 6 | 12
	One premise particular | Rule 8 | 8
	Negative conclusion | Rule 6 | 4
	Illicit major | Rule 4 | 1
	Total excluded | "" | 53
	Valid moods | "" | 11
	Total | "" | 64

We have by no means used up all the possible varieties of the syllogism yet. So far we have only settled the character of the propositions: affirmative or negative, general or particular. We have not settled the ways the terms can be arranged inside them. The major term must be the predicate of the conclusion, but it can be either the subject or the predicate of the major premise. In the same way, the minor term, the subject of the conclusion, can be either the subject or the predicate of the minor premise. This gives four different ways in which the terms can be arranged, and these are called Figures. The four figures of the syllogism are shown in the following scheme, taking

	X to denote the major term
	Y to denote the middle term
	Z to denote the minor term

	"" | 1st Fig. | 2nd Fig. | 3rd Fig. | 4th Fig.
	Major Premise | Y X | X Y | Y X | X Y
	Minor Premise | Z Y | Z Y | Y Z | Y Z
	Conclusion | Z X | Z X | Z X | Z X

These figures must be carefully learned by heart, and the best way to do it is to notice where the middle term stands. In the 1st Figure it stands first, as the subject of the major premise. In the 2nd Figure it stands second, as the predicate of both premises. In the 3rd Figure it stands first again, as the subject of both premises. In the 4th Figure it takes an in-between position. In the conclusion, of course, the major and minor terms always have the same fixed places, so once the middle term is correctly placed in any figure, the rest of the syllogism is easy to fill in.

The reader will hardly be pleased to hear that each of the eleven valid moods has to be examined in each of the four figures separately. That leaves 44 possible cases, and the valid syllogisms have to be picked out from among them. The mood AEE in the first figure, for example, would look like this:

	All Y's are X's,
	No Z's are Y's;
	Therefore no Z's are X's.

This would break the 4th rule and be an Illicit Major, because X is distributed in the conclusion, which is a negative proposition, but not in the major premise. In the second figure the same mood would be valid:

	All X's are Y's,
	No Z's are Y's;
	Therefore no Z's are X's.

In the third figure it becomes

	All Y's are X's,
	No Y's are Z's,
	No Z's are X's,

and once again it breaks the 4th rule with respect to the major term. Finally, in the 4th figure it is valid, as the reader can easily check for himself.

When all the valid moods have been picked out of the 44 possible ones, there turn out to be 24 altogether. Here they are:

	Valid Moods of the Syllogism.

	First Figure. | Second Figure. | Third Figure. | Fourth Figure.
	AAA | EAE | AAI | AAI
	EAE | AEE | IAI | AEE
	AII | EIO | AII | IAI
	EIO | AOO | EAO | EAO
	"" | "" | OAO | EIO
	[AAI] | [EAO] | EIO | ""
	[EAO] | [AEO] | "" | [AEO]

Five of these moods are set apart in brackets because, although they are valid, they are of little or no use. They are said to have a weakened conclusion, because the conclusion is particular when a general one could have been drawn. AAI in the first figure, for example, is represented by this:

	All material substances gravitate,
	All metals are material substances;
	Therefore some metals gravitate.

Obviously the conclusion states only part of the truth: in reality all metals gravitate. It is not actually a wrong conclusion. As we must carefully remember from Lesson 9, affirming a subaltern, or particular, proposition does not deny the general proposition that goes with it. It is perfectly true that some metals gravitate, and it must be true, because all of them do. But when we can just as easily prove that all of them gravitate, that is the conclusion we ought to draw.

If we follow most logicians and set aside the five syllogisms with weakened conclusions, nineteen remain that are both valid and useful. In the next lesson we will be given some ancient memory-verses, and those verses are about the only means by which most people could keep these 19 combinations in their heads. In the meantime, the reader can see from the table of valid moods above that the following remarks about the particular character of each figure of the syllogism are true.

The first figure is the only one that proves the proposition A, that is, the only one with A as a conclusion. It is also the only figure that can prove every one of the four propositions A, E, I and O. As for its premises, it is especially important to notice that the major premise is always universal (A or E) and the minor premise is always affirmative (A or I). We will look at this peculiarity further in the next lesson.

The second figure proves only negative conclusions (E or O), and the reason is easy to see. In this figure the middle term is the predicate of both premises. So if both premises were affirmative, the middle term would be undistributed in both of them, and we would commit the fallacy of undistributed middle that we saw illustrated in Lesson 15 (the Frenchmen and the Russians). It follows that one premise must be negative, and of course only one. So of the major and minor terms, one must be wholly included in the middle term or wholly excluded from it, and the other must at the same time be excluded from it or included in it, at least partly. To illustrate this, let X, Y and Z stand, as before, for the major, middle and minor terms of a syllogism. The four moods of this figure are then

	EAE | AEE
	no X's are Y's, | all X's are Y's,
	all Z's are Y's; | no Z's are Y's;
	∴ no Z's are X's. | ∴ no Z's are X's.

	EIO | AOO
	no X's are Y's, | all X's are Y's,
	some Z's are Y's; | some Z's are not Y's;
	∴ some Z's are not X's. | ∴ some Z's are not X's.

Notice also that in the second figure the minor premise can be any of the four, A, E, I or O.

The third figure proves only particulars (I or O), and its minor premise is always affirmative (A or I). It also has the largest number of moods, because none of its conclusions is a weakened one.

The fourth figure is usually considered unnatural and fairly useless, because the same arguments can be set out more clearly in the form of the first figure, which it resembles in some ways. It proves every proposition except A, that is, E, I and O, and its first mood, AAI, is really a weakened form of AAA in the first figure. Many logicians, including Sir William Hamilton in recent times, have rejected this figure altogether.

Clearly the different figures of the syllogism have different characters, and logicians have thought that each figure is best suited to certain special purposes. A German logician, Lambert, stated these purposes briefly as follows: "The first figure is suited to discovering or proving the properties of a thing; the second to discovering or proving the differences between things; the third to discovering or proving instances and exceptions; the fourth to discovering, or ruling out, the different species of a genus."

We may add that the moods Cesare and Camestres are often used to disprove a statement, because they give a universal negative conclusion, based on shutting one class out of another. Suppose someone still claimed that light consists of material particles. We could answer him with the following syllogism:

	"Material particles pass on impetus to whatever they strike.
	Light does not pass on impetus to whatever it strikes;
	Therefore light is not material particles."

The moods Baroko and Festino are used less, but they allow a particular conclusion to be established.

When we want to raise objections or exceptions to a general statement, though, which is in fact the natural way of answering one, we use the third figure. The statement that "all metals are solids" would be disproved at once by the exception mercury, like this:

	Mercury is not solid,
	Mercury is a metal;
	Therefore some metal is not solid.

Suppose someone claimed that what is incomprehensible cannot exist. We answer at once with this argument: infinity is incomprehensible; but infinity certainly exists, because without it we cannot explain the nature of a curved line, or of a quantity that varies continuously; therefore something that is incomprehensible exists. In this case even one exception is enough to overturn the claim completely, since the claim really means that because a thing is incomprehensible it cannot exist. And if one incomprehensible thing does exist, others may too, and the statement loses all its authority.

Under the Aristotelian system, the third figure must also be used whenever the middle term is a singular term, because in Aristotle's view of the subject a singular term could not stand as the predicate of a proposition.

Questions and Exercises

1. Name the rules of the syllogism broken by each of the following moods, paying no attention to figure: AIA, EEI, IEA, IOI, IIA, AEI.

2. Write out all 64 moods of the syllogism and strike out the 53 invalid ones.

3. Show in which figures the following premises give a valid conclusion: AA, AI, EA, OA.

4. In which figures are IEO and EIO valid?

5. To which moods do the following valid syllogisms belong? Arrange them in correct logical order.

(1) Some Y's are Z's. No X's are Y's. Some Z's are not X's.

(2) All Z's are Y's. No Y's are X's. No Z's are X's.

(3) No fish suckles its young; The whale suckles its young; Therefore the whale is no fish.

6. Draw conclusions from the following premises, and say which mood each syllogism belongs to.

(1) Some amphibious animals are mammalian. All mammalian animals are vertebrate.

(2) All planets are heavenly bodies. No planets are self-luminous.

(3) Mammalian animals are quadrupeds. No birds are quadrupeds.

(4) Ruminant animals are not predacious. The lion is predacious.

7. Invent examples to show that false premises can give true conclusions.

8. Supply premises for the following conclusions:

(1) Some logicians are not good reasoners.

(2) The rings of Saturn are material bodies.

(3) Party government exists in every democracy.

(4) All fixed stars obey the law of gravitation.
