William Stanley Jevons wrote this book in 1870 for Macmillan's series of elementary class-books, and for decades afterwards it was the logic that British and colonial schoolchildren, and a good many university students, learned first. It was reprinted again and again. Jevons revised it once, in 1876, the year he moved from Owens College in Manchester to University College London, and that revised text is the one retold here.

He was an unusual person to write a schoolbook on logic. He had left university at eighteen to spend five years as an assayer at the new mint in Sydney, came home to finish his degree, and made his name as an economist: The Coal Question (1865) asked what Britain would do when its coal ran out, and The Theory of Political Economy (1871) helped found modern economics. Logic was a second career pursued with the same energy. He had read George Boole's algebra of thought and decided it could be done without the algebra, and in 1869 he built a machine to do it, a wooden box with a keyboard of twenty-one keys that he showed to the Royal Society in January 1870. It sorted the possible combinations of four terms and struck out the ones the premises forbade, which is why it is sometimes counted among the ancestors of the computer. Lesson 23 teaches the same method by hand.

The book itself is the old logic of the schools, set out with unusual clarity: terms, propositions, the syllogism with its moods and figures and the Latin rhyme for remembering them, and the fallacies. Then, in its last third, it turns to induction, how scientists get from particular observations to general laws, and here Jevons is drawing on Mill, on Herschel and on his own work. One section has dated in an interesting way. The "recent logical views" of Lessons 22 and 23 set Sir William Hamilton beside Boole as rival reformers; Hamilton's system went nowhere, and Boole's became the foundation of the symbolic logic that eventually displaced most of this book. Jevons is left to say what he thought in 1876.

This edition changes his sentences and nothing else in the logic. His technical vocabulary is kept exactly, since it is the vocabulary every other book on traditional logic uses, and no modern notation has been added. The questions he printed at the back of the book now close the lessons they belong to, his index of terms has become a short glossary, and his references to his own pages now name lessons. Three examples have been reworded: a people he called by a name now taken as a slur is called by its own name, an exercise on the dignity of a Black man uses today's words for him, and a sentence offered only for its grammar, which happened to rank the races, has been replaced by another. Jevons gave no answers to his exercises, and none have been added.
