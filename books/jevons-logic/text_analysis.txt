TEXT ANALYSIS — W. STANLEY JEVONS, ELEMENTARY LESSONS IN LOGIC (1870)

THE BOOK
Jevons wrote this in 1870 as a schoolbook for Macmillan's "Elementary
Class-Books": thirty-three lessons, each a sitting's worth, each closing on
questions. It was the standard first logic in British and colonial schools
for sixty years. The text we translate is the revised one (preface dated
Hampstead, November 1876), from a late reprint of the same plates.

It covers the whole traditional syllabus — terms, propositions, the
syllogism, fallacies — then does two things no other primer of 1870 did:
it teaches Boole's new algebraic logic in a form a schoolboy can work by
hand (Lesson 23, Jevons's own method of indirect inference), and it gives
a third of its length to induction and scientific method, drawn from Mill,
Herschel and Jevons's own work as an economist and chemist.

STYLE AND VOICE
Plain, patient, Victorian-lecturer. Long periods with the operative point
in the middle; heavy use of "we may observe", "it will be seen", "it is
evident that". Examples are concrete and everyday for 1870: metals,
planets, London, railway trains, the Queen, Parliament, chemistry. He is
gently opinionated (the fourth figure "ill-arranged", Hamilton's
quantification over-rated, the "ologies" are really "logics") and fond of
an aphorism to close a lesson. He is not a comedian like Carroll; his
warmth is a teacher's confidence that the reader will get it.

Target: a fourteen-year-old should be able to follow a lesson start to
finish. That means short sentences, the claim first, the example straight
after it, and "we" kept as the friendly classroom "we".

THE DECISION THAT GOVERNS EVERYTHING: KEEP HIS TECHNICAL VOCABULARY
As with Carroll's Symbolic Logic, the words ARE the subject. A student who
reads this edition must be able to open any other logic book and find the
same names. So these stay exactly as Jevons writes them (spelling as he
has it, including "premise"/"premises"):

  term, singular/general/collective/abstract/concrete term, positive,
  negative, privative, relative, absolute, connotative, non-connotative;
  extension, intension, connotation, denotation; proposition, categorical,
  subject, predicate, copula, quality, quantity, universal, particular,
  affirmative, negative, A E I O, distributed/undistributed; opposition,
  contrary, contradictory, subcontrary, subaltern; conversion (simple, per
  accidens, by contraposition), immediate inference; predicable, genus,
  species, difference (differentia), property, accident, summum genus,
  infima species, division, definition; syllogism, major/minor/middle
  term, major/minor premise, conclusion, mood, figure, the mood names
  (Barbara, Celarent ... Fresison), reduction (ostensive, indirect),
  enthymeme, prosyllogism, episyllogism, epicheirema, sorites, conditional,
  hypothetical, disjunctive, dilemma (simple/complex, constructive/
  destructive); fallacy, paralogism, sophism, equivocation, amphiboly,
  composition, division, accident, ignoratio elenchi, petitio principii,
  argumentum ad hominem / ad populum / ad verecundiam / ad ignorantiam,
  non sequitur, false cause; quantification of the predicate; law of
  identity, of contradiction, of excluded middle; analysis, synthesis;
  perfect/imperfect induction, analogy; observation, experiment; method
  of agreement, of difference, joint method, of residues, of concomitant
  variations; hypothesis, theory, fact, tendency; classification,
  abstraction.

Gloss a term ONCE, at its defining lesson, inside the sentence — Jevons
usually defines his terms himself, so mostly no gloss is needed at all.
Latin tags that are names of fallacies stay Latin; a Latin phrase that is
NOT a name (e.g. "ceteris paribus" in passing) becomes English.

DO NOT MODERNISE THE LOGIC. No truth tables he did not print, no ∀/∃, no
"if-then" arrows, no Venn diagrams in place of his Euler circles, no
remarks that the syllogism has been superseded. Where he is out of date
(Hamilton, the "recent" views of Lesson 22) he stays out of date: the
editor's introduction says so, the lessons do not.

WHAT TO MODERNISE
- Sentence structure, above all. Unstack the periods, claim first.
- Everyday archaism: "shew" -> show, "whilst" -> while, "connexion" ->
  connection, "Metropolis" (meaning London) -> London where it is just a
  name, "vulgarly called" -> "casually called".
- Dated science as science: he says "carbonic acid" for carbon dioxide,
  "eight parts of oxygen to one of hydrogen" (equivalent weights). Keep
  the example; give the modern name in passing where a reader would be
  misled ("carbonic acid gas — carbon dioxide —"). Do not correct the
  example into a different one.
- Money, measures and institutions stay as his examples (the House of
  Commons, a shilling); a few words of context only where a modern reader
  cannot follow the logic without it.

EXAMPLES ARE THE ARGUMENT
Every example sentence inside a worked syllogism, conversion, or exercise
must keep its logical form exactly: quantity (all/some/no), quality, and
which term is subject and which predicate. "All metals are elements" must
not become "Metals are usually elements". Rephrase the wording of an
example only where the archaic wording would confuse; never change a word
that carries a quantifier or a negation. When an exercise asks the student
to find the fallacy, the fallacy must still be there — reproduce the
ambiguity (an equivocation must still equivocate in English).

TABLES, VERSE, FIGURES
- Tab-indented lines with " | " are tables: keep them, cell for cell.
  Mood lists (AAA | EAE ...) are data: copy them exactly.
- The mnemonic verse in Lesson 17 ("Barbara, Celarent, Darii, Ferioque,
  prioris; ...") is Latin doggerel whose syllables ARE the system. Copy it
  verbatim, line breaks and all, and explain it in his words as he does.
- [Figure id] lines stay exactly where they are, unchanged.
- Hamilton's aphorism closing Lesson 1 stays as a two-line verse.

CROSS-REFERENCES
The source cites its own pages ("see p. 140", "Lesson xiv."). Re-point
every page reference inside this book to the lesson it names ("see the
table of moods in Lesson 16"). The list is in running_notes.txt. References
to OTHER books' pages ("Mill's Logic, Book III, ch. 8") stay as he gives
them: they are reading suggestions, and the reading lists stay.

QUESTIONS AND EXERCISES
They are part of the book and are translated with the same care: modern
wording, same number, same order, same logical content. Do NOT add
answers (Jevons gave none). Numbered items keep their numbers.

FOOTNOTES
Lines beginning "Footnote: " stay as separate paragraphs starting
"Footnote: ", directly after their paragraph.

SENSITIVE CONTENT
This is scholarly modernization of a canonical Victorian textbook. The
examples reflect 1870s Britain: empire, religion, the Queen. Render them
as his, in a measured register, neither sensationalised nor cleaned up.
Specific rulings:
- Lesson 15 Q (3) "Hottentots are capable of education; for Hottentots
  are men, and all men are capable of education." The argument affirms
  the people's humanity and capacity, and the syllogism is the point.
  Use the people's own name: "The Khoikhoi are capable of education; for
  the Khoikhoi are human beings, and all human beings are capable of
  education." (The old name is a slur today and the reader loses nothing.)
- Lesson 21 Misc. Q71 "Honesty deserves reward; and a negro is a
  fellow-creature; therefore, an honest negro is a fellow-creature
  deserving of reward." An abolition-era example whose logic (combining
  attributes) is the exercise. Render: "Honesty deserves reward, and a
  Black man is a fellow human being; therefore an honest Black man is a
  fellow human being who deserves reward."
- "Mahomet" -> "Muhammad" throughout.
- "man"/"men" meaning people: use "people"/"human beings" where the
  logic does not depend on the word; where the example is the stock
  "All men are mortal" kind, "All humans are mortal" is fine, but keep
  the form.

RATIO
Jevons is already plain; unstacking adds a little. Expect 0.9–1.15.
Below 0.85 on any file means something was dropped; check the exercises
first, they are where a tired agent summarises.
