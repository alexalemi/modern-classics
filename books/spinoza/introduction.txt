Baruch Spinoza was born in Amsterdam in 1632, into a family of Portuguese Jews who had come there to escape the Inquisition. At twenty-three his own community expelled him, with a curse read out against him, and never took him back. He ground lenses for optical instruments for a living, turned down a professorship at Heidelberg because it came with a condition about not disturbing the established religion, and died in 1677 at forty-four. This book was published a few months later by his friends, some of them anonymously, because printing it while he was alive would have been dangerous. The States of Holland banned his entire works the following year, for containing profane, blasphemous and atheistic propositions.

The form is the first obstacle and it is best met head on. The Ethics is laid out like a geometry textbook. Each of the five Parts opens with definitions and axioms, and then comes a numbered sequence of propositions, each followed by a proof, and often by corollaries and notes. There are two hundred and fifty-nine propositions in all. Spinoza is not decorating an argument with the appearance of rigour; he means the reader to be able to check every step, and the notes — where he drops the geometry and talks — are where most people find the book.

Those steps are held together by more than a thousand cross-references, and in the seventeenth-century printing they are shorthand: a Roman numeral, a lower-case numeral, an abbreviation. In this edition every one of them has been resolved and written out in plain modern form, saying which Part, which proposition, and which piece of it. That is the main thing this edition does, and it is what makes it possible to follow a proof without counting on your fingers.

The claim the fuss was about is in Part One. There is only one substance, and God and Nature are two names for it. Not a person who made the world and watches it, but the world itself, taken as the single system that everything follows from by necessity. There are no miracles in it and no purposes. The Appendix to Part One traces the belief that Nature has goals to the fact that people know what they want and not why they want it, and it is the angriest and best-written thing in the book.

From there it turns to us. Mind and body are one thing described two ways. Free will is an illusion of the same kind as purposes in Nature. Forty-eight emotions are defined in order, jealousy and ambition and hatred among them, with no more moralising than a geometer gives a triangle. The flatness is deliberate: he says in the Preface to Part Three that he will treat human desires exactly as if he were dealing with lines, planes and solids, and then he does. Freedom, when it arrives in Part Five, turns out to be understanding, and nothing else.
