SPINOZA, ETHICS (1677) — TRANSLATION STRATEGY
=============================================
Source: R. H. M. Elwes's 1883 translation, Project Gutenberg #3800.
Standard Ebooks has no Spinoza (checked 2026-08-19), so Gutenberg is
the source rather than the fallback.
87,955 words of body text in five Parts.

WHY THIS BOOK NEEDS A RETELLING
-------------------------------
It is the hume/mill/burke case — class 3, technical calque — and by the
screening pass it is the last big one left:

    archaism   0.29 per 1,000 words   (below books struck as too clean)
    calque    38.6
    sentences 17.9 mean, only 16% over 35 words

Note what that third line says. Unlike Hume, Mill and Burke, Spinoza's
sentences are SHORT. Nothing here is a 200-word periodic construction.
The obstruction is not syntax at all, and a translator who arrives
expecting to unstack sentences will find nothing to do and will start
rewriting prose that was already clear — the hume failure mode.

WHAT ACTUALLY BLOCKS THE READER, in order of severity:

1. THE APPARATUS. The Ethics is not prose with headings; it is a
   machine of numbered parts that cite each other. 259 propositions,
   each trailing a Proof and often Corollaries, Notes and
   Explanations; 26 definitions, 15 axioms, 8 postulates, 48
   Definitions of the Emotions; and 422 parenthetical cross-references
   binding them together. A reader who cannot follow the citations
   cannot follow a single proof.

2. THE TECHNICAL VOCABULARY, used with total consistency for 88,000
   words and almost all of it false friends in modern English:
       substance     that which is in itself and conceived through
                     itself — NOT stuff or material
       mode          a modification of substance; what we would call
                     a particular thing
       attribute     what the intellect perceives as constituting the
                     essence of substance
       affection     a state or modification (Latin affectio) — NOT
                     fondness. Elwes footnotes this himself at I.Def.V
       emotion       affectus, a specific technical term
       idea          a conception the mind forms, not a notion or
                     opinion
       adequate      a technical property of an idea, not "sufficient"
       perfection    reality; Spinoza says outright they are the same
       virtue        power to act (virtus) — NOT moral goodness
       conatus       the striving each thing makes to persist in being
       blessedness   beatitudo, the book's last word and its goal
   These are locked in running_notes.txt. Getting "virtue" or
   "perfection" wrong does not make a sentence obscure; it makes it
   mean something Spinoza spent a Part disproving.

3. ELWES'S OWN VICTORIAN LAYER, which is thin but real: "whatsoever",
   "in so far as", "appertain", "owns God as its cause". This is the
   nights/ distinction — the costume the TRANSLATOR put on, not the
   author's age, and it goes.

THE REFERENCE RULING (Alex, 2026-08-19): RESOLVE AND NORMALISE
---------------------------------------------------------------
The source spells its 422 references in 137 distinct shapes:
    (II. 7. Coroll.)        (by Prop. iv.)        (by the last Prop.)
    (II. Def. 4)            (Ax. i.)              (by the foregoing Prop.)
    (Def. of the Emotions, xiii.)                 (See Pt. i., Prop. xxv.)
    (by Prop. iv. of this part)                   (I. 25. Coroll.)
Absolute, implicitly scoped, and RELATIVE, all mixed together.

All of them become one form: arabic numerals, scope always explicit,
relative references resolved to the number they actually point at.

    (II. 7. Coroll.)            -> (by Part 2, Proposition 7, Corollary)
    (by Prop. iv.)              -> (by Proposition 4 of this Part)
    (by the last Prop.)         -> (by Proposition 22 of this Part)
    (Def. of the Emotions, xiii.) -> (by Definition 13 of the Emotions)

This is the pillow-problems ruling, not the symbolic-logic one: there
Carroll's words WERE the machine and had to be kept, here the numerals
are notation and the machine is the reference STRUCTURE, which is
preserved exactly and in fact made visible for the first time.

AND IT IS INFERENCE, SO IT MUST BE VERIFIED, NOT TRUSTED. Resolution is
done once, mechanically, in prep.py — not 422 times by hand — and
check.py asserts that every resolved citation names an item that
exists, against the inventory below. A reference that silently points
at the wrong proposition is this book's version of the candle defect: it
reads perfectly and sends the reader somewhere Spinoza never sent them.

THE VERIFIED INVENTORY (spinoza/structure.py), which every reference is
checked against. Every count matches the received text:

FOUR SOURCE TRAPS, all found by surveying before writing a line of prep
-----------------------------------------------------------------------
1. PART II HAS NO "PART" LINE AT ALL. The five Parts are headed five
   different ways — "PART I. CONCERNING GOD.", then nothing whatever
   for Part II except its subtitle "ON THE NATURE AND ORIGIN OF THE
   MIND", then "PART III.", "PART IV:", "PART V:". A regex anchored on
   the word PART loses a fifth of the book, and loses it the way the
   grimm 151* tale was lost: prep never sees the section, so every
   check that compares prep's output against prep's own manifest
   agrees with itself and passes.
2. 233 OF THE 422 REFERENCES BREAK ACROSS A LINE — "(see\nAx. iv.)",
   "(by the last\nProp.)". Normalise whitespace before matching, or
   more than half of them are invisible to the resolver.
3. THE SAME ITEM IS LAID OUT TWO WAYS. Part I numbers a definition with
   a bare "I."; Part II writes "DEFINITION I." with the word in front.
   Written against either alone, the inventory reports that the other
   Part has no definitions — which is exactly what the first run said
   about Part II.
4. FOOTNOTE NUMBERING RESTARTS PER PART: 51 marks, 17 distinct numbers.
   "[1]" is five different notes. Scope the marks to their Part before
   inlining them (the candle pattern), or a note lands on the wrong
   sentence and reads perfectly.

VOICE
-----
Spinoza is the least rhetorical writer in this collection and the most
absolute. He is not persuading; he is demonstrating, and the flatness
is the argument — he says in the Preface to Part III that he will treat
human passions exactly as if he were dealing with lines, planes and
solids. That must survive. Do NOT warm him up, do not add connective
throat-clearing, and do not soften "hence it follows" into "so you can
see that".

But the Notes are a different voice, and this is the one place the book
opens up: the Appendix to Part I on final causes, the Preface to Part
III, the Notes on free will and on the man who believes he chooses. There
he is polemical, occasionally sardonic, and writing for a reader rather
than a proof. Keep the two registers apart.

SENSITIVE CONTENT — THE VERNE RULE, no note anywhere in the volume. The
Appendix to Part I is a sustained attack on providence, miracles and
scripture read as prophecy, and it is why the Ethics could not be
published in his lifetime and why he was expelled from his synagogue at
twenty-three. The identification of God with Nature stands as he wrote
it. So do the passages on women and on the weak-minded in Part IV and
the Political Treatise's terms where they surface. None of it is
softened; all of it is his subject.
