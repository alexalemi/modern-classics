Propositions 40 to 65

Proposition 40

Whatever conduces to men's social life, or makes them live together in harmony, is useful; and whatever brings discord into a state is bad.

Proof. For whatever makes men live together in harmony also makes them live according to reason (Proposition 35 of this Part), and is therefore (Proposition 26 of this Part; Proposition 27 of this Part) good; and for the same reason whatever brings about discord is bad. Q.E.D.

Proposition 41

Pleasure in itself is not bad but good; pain, on the contrary, is in itself bad.

Proof. Pleasure (Part 3, Proposition 11, Note) is an emotion by which the body's power of acting is increased or helped; pain is an emotion by which the body's power of acting is diminished or checked. Therefore (Proposition 38 of this Part) pleasure in itself is good, and so on. Q.E.D.

Proposition 42

Merriment cannot be excessive, but is always good; melancholy, on the contrary, is always bad.

Proof. Merriment (see Part 3, Proposition 11, Note) is a pleasure which, insofar as it is referred to the body, consists in all the parts of the body being affected equally; that is (Part 3, Proposition 11), the body's power of acting is increased or helped in such a way that its several parts keep their former proportion of motion and rest. So merriment is always good (Proposition 39 of this Part) and cannot be excessive. But melancholy (see Part 3, Proposition 11, Note) is a pain which, insofar as it is referred to the body, consists in the absolute decrease or checking of the body's power of acting; so (Proposition 38 of this Part) it is always bad. Q.E.D.

Proposition 43

Stimulation may be excessive and bad; and suffering may be good, insofar as stimulation, or pleasure, is bad.

Proof. Localised pleasure, or stimulation (titillatio), is a pleasure which, insofar as it is referred to the body, consists in one or some of its parts being affected more than the rest (see Part 3, Proposition 11, Note). The force of this emotion may be enough to overcome the body's other actions (Proposition 6 of this Part) and may stay obstinately fixed in it, so making it incapable of being affected in a variety of other ways; and so (Proposition 38 of this Part) it may be bad. Again, suffering, which is a pain, cannot as such be good (Proposition 41 of this Part). But since its force and increase are defined by the power of an external cause compared with our own (Proposition 5 of this Part), we can conceive infinite degrees and kinds of strength in this emotion (Proposition 3 of this Part); we can therefore conceive it as able to restrain stimulation and to prevent it becoming excessive and hindering the body's capabilities. To that extent, then, it will be good. Q.E.D.

Proposition 44

Love and desire may be excessive.

Proof. Love is pleasure accompanied by the idea of an external cause (Definition 6 of the Emotions); so stimulation accompanied by the idea of an external cause is love (Part 3, Proposition 11, Note), and love may therefore be excessive. Again, the strength of a desire varies with the emotion it arises from (Part 3, Proposition 37). Now an emotion may overcome all a man's other actions (Proposition 6 of this Part); so the desire arising from that emotion may likewise overcome all his other desires and become excessive, as we showed in the last proposition about stimulation.

Note. Merriment, which I have said is good, can be conceived more easily than it can be observed. For the emotions which assail us daily are generally referred to some part of the body which is affected more than the rest; and so the emotions are generally excessive, and fix the mind on one object so firmly that it cannot think of others. And although men are as a rule subject to many emotions, and very few are found who are always assailed by one and the same, there are cases where one and the same emotion stays obstinately fixed. We sometimes see men so absorbed in one object that, although it is not present, they think they have it before them; and when this happens to a man who is not asleep, we say he is delirious or mad. Nor are those thought any less mad who are inflamed with love, and dream night and day of nothing but their mistress, or some woman—they are made objects of ridicule. But when a miser thinks of nothing but gain, or money, or an ambitious man of nothing but glory, they are not reckoned mad, because they are generally harmful and are thought worthy of hatred. In fact, avarice, ambition, lust and the rest are kinds of madness, though they are not counted among the diseases.

Proposition 45

Hatred can never be good.

Proof. When we hate a man we endeavour to destroy him (Part 3, Proposition 39)—that is (Proposition 37 of this Part), we endeavour to do something bad. Therefore, and so on. Q.E.D.

Note. Here, and in what follows, by hatred I mean only hatred towards men.

Corollary 1. Envy, derision, contempt, anger, revenge and the other emotions belonging to hatred, or arising from it, are bad; this is evident from Part 3, Proposition 39 and Proposition 37 of this Part.

Corollary 2. Whatever we desire out of hatred is base, and in a state unjust. This too is evident from Part 3, Proposition 39, and from the definitions of baseness and injustice in Proposition 37 of this Part, Note.

Note. Between derision (which I have in Corollary 1 stated to be bad) and laughter I recognise a great difference. Laughter, like jesting, is merely pleasure, and therefore, so long as it is not excessive, is in itself good (Proposition 41 of this Part). Nothing but a grim and gloomy superstition forbids a man to enjoy himself. For why is it more proper to relieve one's hunger and thirst than to drive away one's melancholy? My reasoning, and my conviction, is this. No deity, and no one else except the envious, takes pleasure in my weakness and my discomfort, or counts as virtue in me the tears, the sobs and the fear which are signs of a weak spirit. On the contrary, the greater the pleasure we are affected with, the greater the perfection we pass to—in other words, the more we necessarily share in the divine nature. So it is the part of a wise man to make use of what comes his way and to enjoy it as much as he can, though not to the point of surfeit, for that would not be enjoyment. It is the part of a wise man, I say, to refresh and restore himself with moderate and pleasant food and drink, with perfumes, with the soft beauty of growing plants, with dress, with music, with games, with the theatre, and with everything else of the kind that a man can use without injury to his neighbour. For the human body is made up of a great many parts of different natures, which continually need fresh and varied nourishment, so that the whole body may be equally capable of everything that follows from the necessity of its own nature, and consequently so that the mind may be equally capable of understanding many things at once. This way of life, then, agrees best with our principles and with common practice; so if any other plan is in question, this one is the best and is in every way to be commended. There is no need for me to set the matter out more clearly or in more detail.

Proposition 46

He who lives under the guidance of reason endeavours, as far as he can, to repay other men's hatred, anger and contempt towards him with love, or kindness.

Proof. All emotions of hatred are bad (Proposition 45 of this Part, Corollary 1); so a man who lives under the guidance of reason will endeavour as far as he can to avoid being assailed by such emotions (Proposition 19 of this Part), and consequently will endeavour to prevent others being assailed by them too (Proposition 37 of this Part). But hatred is increased by being returned and can be quenched by love (Part 3, Proposition 43), so that hatred may pass into love (Part 3, Proposition 44). Therefore he who lives under the guidance of reason will endeavour to repay hatred with love, that is, with kindness. Q.E.D.

Note. He who chooses to avenge wrongs with hatred is certainly wretched. But he who strives to conquer hatred with love fights his battle in joy and confidence; he withstands many as easily as one, and has very little need of fortune's help. Those he defeats yield gladly, not because their strength failed but because it grew. All this follows so plainly from the mere definitions of love and of understanding that I need not prove it in detail.

Proposition 47

The emotions of hope and fear cannot be good in themselves.

Proof. The emotions of hope and fear cannot exist without pain; for fear is pain (Definition 13 of the Emotions), and hope (Definition 12 of the Emotions, Explanation; Definition 13 of the Emotions, Explanation) cannot exist without fear. Therefore (Proposition 41 of this Part) these emotions cannot be good in themselves, but only insofar as they can restrain excessive pleasure (Proposition 43 of this Part). Q.E.D.

Note. We may add that these emotions show a defect of knowledge and a lack of power in the mind; and for the same reason confidence, despair, joy and disappointment are signs of a want of mental power. For although confidence and joy are pleasurable emotions, they nevertheless imply a preceding pain, namely hope and fear. So the more we endeavour to be guided by reason, the less we depend on hope; we endeavour to free ourselves from fear and, as far as we can, to master fortune, directing our actions by the sure counsels of wisdom.

Proposition 48

The emotions of over-esteem and disparagement are always bad.

Proof. These emotions (see Definition 21 of the Emotions; Definition 22 of the Emotions) are contrary to reason, and are therefore (Proposition 26 of this Part; Proposition 27 of this Part) bad. Q.E.D.

Proposition 49

Over-esteem is apt to make its object proud.

Proof. If we see that someone rates us too highly out of love, we are apt to become elated (Part 3, Proposition 41), or to be affected with pleasure (Definition 30 of the Emotions); the good we hear of ourselves we readily believe (Part 3, Proposition 25); and so out of love we rate ourselves too highly—in other words, we are apt to become proud. Q.E.D.

Proposition 50

Pity, in a man who lives under the guidance of reason, is in itself bad and useless.

Proof. Pity (Definition 18 of the Emotions) is a pain, and therefore (Proposition 41 of this Part) in itself bad. The good effect which follows from it—our endeavour to free the object of our pity from misery—is an action we desire to do solely at the dictate of reason (Proposition 37 of this Part); and only at the dictate of reason can we perform any action we know for certain to be good (Proposition 27 of this Part). So in a man who lives under the guidance of reason, pity is in itself useless and bad. Q.E.D.

Note. A man who rightly grasps that all things follow from the necessity of the divine nature, and come to pass according to the eternal laws and rules of nature, will find nothing worth hating, deriding or despising, nor will he pity anyone; but so far as human virtue reaches, he will endeavour to do well, as the saying is, and to rejoice. We may add that a man who is easily touched with compassion, and moved by another's sorrow or tears, often does something he afterwards regrets—partly because we can never be sure that an action caused by an emotion is good, and partly because we are easily deceived by false tears. I am speaking here expressly of a man living under the guidance of reason. He who is moved to help others neither by reason nor by compassion is rightly called inhuman; for (Part 3, Proposition 27) he seems unlike a man.

Proposition 51

Approval is not contrary to reason, but can agree with it and arise from it.

Proof. Approval is love towards someone who has done good to another (Definition 19 of the Emotions); so it may be referred to the mind insofar as the mind is active (Part 3, Proposition 59)—that is (Part 3, Proposition 3), insofar as it understands. So it agrees with reason, and so on. Q.E.D.

Another Proof. He who lives under the guidance of reason desires for others the good he seeks for himself (Proposition 37 of this Part); so when he sees someone doing good to his fellow, his own endeavour to do good is helped—in other words, he will feel pleasure (Part 3, Proposition 11, Note) accompanied by the idea of the benefactor. Therefore he approves of him. Q.E.D.

Note. Indignation, as we defined it (Definition 20 of the Emotions), is necessarily bad (Proposition 45 of this Part). We may remark, however, that when the sovereign power punishes a citizen who has injured another, for the sake of keeping the peace, it should not be said to be indignant with the criminal; for it is not driven by hatred to ruin him, but led by a sense of duty to punish him.

Proposition 52

Self-approval may arise from reason, and the self-approval that arises from reason is the highest there can be.

Proof. Self-approval is pleasure arising from a man's contemplation of himself and of his own power of acting (Definition 25 of the Emotions). But a man's true power of acting, his virtue, is reason herself (Part 3, Proposition 3), which he contemplates clearly and distinctly (Part 2, Proposition 40; Part 2, Proposition 43); so self-approval arises from reason. Again, when a man contemplates himself, he perceives clearly and distinctly, that is adequately, only what follows from his power of acting (Part 3, Definition 2)—that is (Part 3, Proposition 3), from his power of understanding. So the highest self-approval there can be arises in that contemplation alone. Q.E.D.

Note. Self-approval is in reality the highest thing we can hope for. For (as we showed in Proposition 25 of this Part) no one endeavours to preserve his being for the sake of any further object; and since this approval is fed and strengthened more and more by praise (Part 3, Proposition 53, Corollary), and on the contrary (Part 3, Proposition 55, Corollary) is disturbed more and more by blame, fame becomes the most powerful of all incitements to action, and life under disgrace becomes almost unendurable.

Proposition 53

Humility is not a virtue, and does not arise from reason.

Proof. Humility is pain arising from a man's contemplation of his own weakness (Definition 26 of the Emotions). But insofar as a man knows himself by true reason, he is supposed to understand his own essence, that is, his power (Part 3, Proposition 7). So if in contemplating himself a man perceives some weakness in himself, that is not in virtue of his understanding himself, but (Part 3, Proposition 55) in virtue of his power of acting being checked. And if we suppose that a man perceives his own weakness in virtue of understanding something stronger than himself, by whose knowledge he measures his own power of acting, this is the same as saying that we conceive him to understand himself distinctly (Proposition 26 of this Part), because his power of acting is helped. So humility, the pain arising from a man's contemplation of his own weakness, does not arise from contemplation or from reason, and is not a virtue but a passion. Q.E.D.

Footnote: Land reads: "Quod ipsius agendi potentia juvatur"—which I have translated above. He suggests as alternative readings to 'quod', 'quo' (= whereby) and 'quodque' (= and that).

Proposition 54

Repentance is not a virtue, and does not arise from reason; a man who repents of an action is doubly wretched, or weak.

Proof. The first part of this proposition is proved like the one before it. The second part is proved from the mere definition of the emotion in question (Definition 27 of the Emotions); for the man lets himself be overcome, first by an evil desire and then by pain.

Note. Since men seldom live under the guidance of reason, these two emotions, humility and repentance, and hope and fear as well, bring more good than harm; so if we must sin, we had better sin in that direction. For if men who are subject to emotion were all equally proud, they would shrink from nothing and fear nothing, and how then could they be joined and bound together? The crowd plays the tyrant when it is not afraid; so we need not wonder that the prophets, who looked to the good not of a few but of all, so strenuously commended humility, repentance and reverence. Indeed those who are subject to these emotions may be led much more easily than others to live under the guidance of reason—that is, to become free and to enjoy the life of the blessed.

Proposition 55

Extreme pride, or extreme dejection, indicates extreme ignorance of oneself.

Proof. This is evident from Definition 28 of the Emotions and Definition 29 of the Emotions.

Proposition 56

Extreme pride, or extreme dejection, indicates extreme weakness of spirit.

Proof. The first foundation of virtue is self-preservation (Proposition 22 of this Part, Corollary) under the guidance of reason (Proposition 24 of this Part). So a man who is ignorant of himself is ignorant of the foundation of all the virtues, and consequently of all the virtues themselves. Again, to act from virtue is simply to act under the guidance of reason (Proposition 24 of this Part), and a man who acts under the guidance of reason must necessarily know that he does so (Part 2, Proposition 43). Therefore a man who is in extreme ignorance of himself, and consequently of all the virtues, acts least of all from virtue—in other words (Definition 8 of this Part), is most weak of spirit. So extreme pride, or extreme dejection, indicates extreme weakness of spirit. Q.E.D.

Corollary. It follows very clearly that the proud and the dejected are especially prey to the emotions.

Note. Yet dejection can be corrected more easily than pride; for pride is a pleasurable emotion and dejection a painful one, and the pleasurable is the stronger of the two (Proposition 18 of this Part).

Proposition 57

The proud man delights in the company of flatterers and hangers-on, but hates the company of the high-minded.

Proof. Pride is pleasure arising from a man's over-estimation of himself (Definition 28 of the Emotions; Definition 6 of the Emotions); and the proud man will endeavour to feed this estimation by every means in his power (Part 3, Proposition 13, Note). So he will delight in the company of flatterers and hangers-on, whose character is too well known to need definition here, and will avoid the company of the high-minded, who value him at his deserts. Q.E.D.

Note. It would take too long to list here all the evil results of pride, since the proud are prey to every emotion, though to none less than to love and pity. I cannot, however, pass over in silence the fact that a man may be called proud for underestimating other people; and in this sense pride may be defined as pleasure arising from the false opinion by which a man thinks himself superior to his fellows. And dejection, the quality opposite to this kind of pride, may be defined as pain arising from the false opinion by which a man thinks himself inferior to his fellows. This being so, we can easily see that a proud man is necessarily envious (Part 3, Proposition 41, Note), and takes pleasure only in the company of those who humour his weak mind to the top of its bent, and make him mad instead of merely foolish.

Though dejection is the emotion contrary to pride, the dejected man is very near akin to the proud one. For since his pain arises from comparing his own weakness with other men's power, or virtue, it will be removed—in other words, he will feel pleasure—if his imagination is occupied in contemplating other men's faults. Hence the proverb, "The unhappy are comforted by finding fellow-sufferers." And conversely, he will be the more pained the more he thinks himself inferior to others; so none are so prone to envy as the dejected. They are especially sharp in watching men's actions, with a view to finding fault rather than to correcting it, and they reserve their praise for dejection itself, and glory in it—though all the while with a dejected air. These effects follow as necessarily from that emotion as it follows from the nature of a triangle that its three angles are equal to two right angles. I have already said that I call these and similar emotions bad solely in respect of what is useful to man. The laws of nature have regard to nature's general order, of which man is only a part. I mention this in passing, in case anyone should think I have meant to set out men's faults and irrational deeds rather than the nature and properties of things. For, as I said in the Preface to Part Three, I regard human emotions and their properties as on the same footing with all other natural things. Human emotions certainly show the power and the ingenuity of nature, if not of human nature, quite as fully as the other things we admire and delight to contemplate. But I go on to note the qualities in the emotions which bring men advantage or do them harm.

Proposition 58

Honour (gloria) is not contrary to reason, but may arise from it.

Proof. This is evident from Definition 30 of the Emotions, and also from the definition of an honourable man (Proposition 37 of this Part, Note 1).

Note. Empty honour, as it is called, is self-approval fed only by the good opinion of the crowd; and when that good opinion ceases, so does the self-approval, which is the highest object of every man's love (Proposition 52 of this Part, Note). So a man whose honour is rooted in popular approval must strive and scheme and act anxiously, day after day, to keep his reputation; for the crowd is fickle and inconstant, and a reputation not kept up quickly withers away. Everyone wants to catch the applause of the crowd for himself, and readily suppresses the fame of others; and since the thing they are fighting over is reckoned the greatest of all goods, each combatant is seized with a fierce desire to put down his rivals by every possible means, until the one who comes out victorious at last is prouder of having harmed others than of having done himself good. This kind of honour, then, is really empty, being nothing at all.

What is to be noted about shame may easily be gathered from what was said about compassion and repentance. I will only add that shame, like compassion, though not a virtue, is nevertheless good insofar as it shows that the man who feels it really has a desire to live honourably—in the same way that pain is good, as showing that the injured part has not mortified. So although a man who feels shame is sorrowful, he is more perfect than a man who is shameless and has no desire to live honourably.

These are the points I undertook to make about the emotions of pleasure and pain. As for the desires, they are good or bad according as they spring from good or bad emotions. But all of them, insofar as they are engendered in us by emotions in which the mind is passive, are blind (Proposition 44 of this Part, Note), and would be useless if men could easily be brought to live under the guidance of reason alone, as I shall now briefly show.

Proposition 59

To every action to which we are determined by an emotion in which the mind is passive, we can be determined by reason without any emotion.

Proof. To act rationally is nothing but (Part 3, Proposition 3; Part 3, Definition 2) to perform the actions which follow from the necessity of our own nature considered in itself alone. But pain is bad insofar as it diminishes or checks the power of acting (Proposition 41 of this Part); so pain cannot determine us to any action we could not perform under the guidance of reason. Again, pleasure is bad only insofar as it hinders a man's capacity for action (Proposition 41 of this Part; Proposition 43 of this Part); so to that extent it could not determine us to any action we could not perform under the guidance of reason. And last, pleasure insofar as it is good agrees with reason, since it consists in a man's capacity for action being increased or helped; nor is the mind passive in it, except insofar as a man's power of acting is not increased far enough to give him an adequate conception of himself and his actions (Part 3, Proposition 3, Note).

So if a man affected with pleasure is brought to such a state of perfection that he gains an adequate conception of himself and his own actions, he will be equally capable—indeed more capable—of the actions to which he is determined by emotions in which the mind is passive. But all emotions belong to pleasure, to pain or to desire (Definition 4 of the Emotions, Explanation), and desire (Definition 1 of the Emotions) is nothing but the attempt to act. Therefore, to every action, and so on. Q.E.D.

Another Proof. An action is called bad insofar as it arises from someone's being affected by hatred or some other bad emotion. But no action considered in itself alone is either good or bad, as we pointed out in the Preface to Part 4, one and the same action being sometimes good and sometimes bad. So to the action which is sometimes bad, or arises from some bad emotion, we may also be led by reason (Proposition 19 of this Part). Q.E.D.

Note. An example will make this clearer. The act of striking, considered physically, insofar as we look only at the fact that a man raises his arm, clenches his fist and moves the whole arm violently downwards, is a virtue, or excellence, conceived as proper to the structure of the human body. If, then, a man moved by anger or hatred is led to clench his fist and move his arm, this happens (as we showed in Part 2) because one and the same action can be associated with various mental images of things; so we may be determined to one and the same action by confused ideas, or by clear and distinct ones. From which it is evident that every desire springing from an emotion in which the mind is passive would become useless if men could be guided by reason. Let us now see why we call a desire arising from an emotion in which the mind is passive blind.

Proposition 60

A desire arising from a pleasure or a pain which belongs not to the whole body but only to one or some of its parts is of no use in respect of a man as a whole.

Proof. Suppose, for instance, that A, a part of the body, is so strengthened by some external cause that it prevails over the remaining parts (Proposition 6 of this Part). This part will not endeavour to do away with its own powers in order that the other parts of the body may perform their office; for that it would need a force, or power, of doing away with its own powers, which (Part 3, Proposition 6) is absurd. That part, and consequently the mind too, will endeavour to preserve its condition. So a desire arising from a pleasure of that kind is of no use in respect of a man as a whole. And if we suppose instead that the part A is checked so that the remaining parts prevail, it may be proved in the same way that a desire arising from pain is of no use in respect of a man as a whole. Q.E.D.

Note. Since pleasure is generally (Proposition 44 of this Part, Note) referred to one part of the body, we generally desire to preserve our being without taking our health as a whole into account. To which we may add that the desires which have the strongest hold on us (Proposition 9 of this Part) take account of the present and not of the future.

Proposition 61

A desire which springs from reason cannot be excessive.

Proof. Desire (Definition 1 of the Emotions), considered absolutely, is the actual essence of a man insofar as it is conceived as in some way determined to a particular action by some given modification of itself. So a desire arising from reason—that is (Part 3, Proposition 3), engendered in us insofar as we are active—is the actual essence, or nature, of a man insofar as it is conceived as determined to those actions which are adequately conceived through a man's essence alone (Part 3, Definition 2). If such a desire could be excessive, human nature considered in itself alone would be able to exceed itself, or to do more than it can, which is a plain contradiction. Therefore such a desire cannot be excessive. Q.E.D.

Proposition 62

Insofar as the mind conceives a thing under the dictates of reason, it is affected equally whether the idea is of a thing future, past or present.

Proof. Whatever the mind conceives under the guidance of reason, it conceives under the form of eternity, or of necessity (Part 2, Proposition 44, Corollary 2), and is therefore affected with the same certainty (Part 2, Proposition 43, Note). So whether the thing is present, past or future, the mind conceives it under the same necessity and is affected with the same certainty; and whether the idea is of something present, past or future, it will in every case be equally true (Part 2, Proposition 41)—that is, it will always have the same properties of an adequate idea (Part 2, Definition 4). Therefore, insofar as the mind conceives things under the dictates of reason, it is affected in the same way whether the idea is of a thing future, past or present. Q.E.D.

Note. If we could have adequate knowledge of the duration of things, and could determine by reason how long they will exist, we should contemplate things future with the same emotion as things present; the mind would desire the good it conceived as future just as though it were present, and would consequently neglect a lesser present good for the sake of a greater future one, and would in no way desire what is good in the present but a source of evil in the future, as we shall shortly show. But we can have only a very inadequate knowledge of the duration of things (Part 2, Proposition 31), and we can determine how long they will exist (Part 2, Proposition 44, Note) only by imagination, which is not so powerfully affected by the future as by the present. So such true knowledge of good and evil as we have is merely abstract, or general, and the judgment we pass on the order of things and the connection of causes, with a view to determining what is good or bad for us in the present, is imaginary rather than real. It is therefore no wonder if the desire arising from such knowledge of good and evil, insofar as it looks into the future, is more readily checked than the desire for things which are agreeable at the present time (compare Proposition 16 of this Part).

Proposition 63

He who is led by fear, and does good in order to escape evil, is not led by reason.

Proof. All the emotions belonging to the mind as active—in other words, to reason—are emotions of pleasure and of desire (Part 3, Proposition 59). Therefore he who is led by fear, and does good in order to escape evil, is not led by reason.

Note. Superstitious people, who know better how to rail at vice than how to teach virtue, and who strive not to guide men by reason but so to restrain them that they would rather escape evil than love virtue, have no other aim than to make others as wretched as themselves. It is therefore no wonder that they are generally troublesome and odious to their fellow men.

Corollary. Under a desire which springs from reason we seek good directly and shun evil indirectly.

Proof. A desire which springs from reason can only spring from a pleasurable emotion in which the mind is not passive (Part 3, Proposition 59)—in other words, from a pleasure which cannot be excessive (Proposition 61 of this Part)—and not from pain. So this desire springs from the knowledge of good, not of evil (Proposition 8 of this Part); and hence under the guidance of reason we seek good directly and shun evil only by implication. Q.E.D.

Note. This corollary may be illustrated by the example of a sick man and a healthy one. The sick man eats what he naturally shrinks from, through fear of death; the healthy man takes pleasure in his food and so gets more enjoyment out of life than he would if he were in fear of death and directly desiring to avoid it. So too a judge who condemns a criminal to death, not out of hatred or anger but out of love of the public good, is guided solely by reason.

Proposition 64

The knowledge of evil is an inadequate knowledge.

Proof. The knowledge of evil (Proposition 8 of this Part) is pain, insofar as we are conscious of it. Now pain is the transition to a lesser perfection (Definition 3 of the Emotions) and therefore cannot be understood through a man's nature (Part 3, Proposition 6; Part 3, Proposition 7); so it is a passive state (Part 3, Definition 2) which (Part 3, Proposition 3) depends on inadequate ideas. Consequently the knowledge of it (Part 2, Proposition 29)—that is, the knowledge of evil—is inadequate. Q.E.D.

Corollary. It follows that if the human mind had only adequate ideas, it would form no conception of evil.

Proposition 65

Under the guidance of reason we should pursue the greater of two goods and the lesser of two evils.

Proof. A good which prevents our enjoying a greater good is in reality an evil; for we apply the terms good and bad to things insofar as we compare them with one another (see the Preface to this Part). So an evil is in reality a lesser good; and hence under the guidance of reason we seek, or pursue, only the greater good and the lesser evil. Q.E.D.

Corollary. Under the guidance of reason we may pursue the lesser evil as though it were the greater good, and may shun the lesser good which would be the cause of the greater evil. For the evil here called the lesser is really good, and the lesser good is really evil; so we may seek the one and shun the other. Q.E.D.
