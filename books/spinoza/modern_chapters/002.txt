Appendix

In what has gone before I have explained the nature and properties of God: that he necessarily exists; that he is one; that he is and acts solely by the necessity of his own nature; that he is the free cause of all things, and in what way; that all things are in God and depend on him so completely that without him they could neither exist nor be conceived; and lastly that all things are predetermined by God, not by his free will or absolute decree, but by the very nature of God, that is, by his infinite power. I have also taken care, where the occasion offered, to clear away the prejudices that might stand in the way of my demonstrations. But a good many misconceptions remain, and they might well prove serious obstacles to understanding the chain of things as I have set it out. I have therefore thought it worth while to bring them before the bar of reason.

All such opinions spring from one notion, commonly held: that everything in nature acts as men do, with an end in view. It is taken for certain that God himself directs all things to a definite goal—for it is said that God made all things for man, and man that he might worship God. So I shall consider this opinion, asking first why it is so widely believed, and why men are naturally so ready to adopt it; second, I shall show that it is false; and last, I shall show how it has given rise to prejudices about good and bad, right and wrong, praise and blame, order and confusion, beauty and ugliness, and the like. This is not the place to derive those misconceptions from the nature of the human mind. It will be enough here if I take as my starting point what everyone ought to admit: that all men are born ignorant of the causes of things; that all have a desire to seek what is useful to them; and that they are conscious of that desire. From this it follows, first, that men think themselves free, because they are conscious of their volitions and desires and never even dream, in their ignorance, of the causes that have disposed them to want and desire as they do. Second, that men do everything for an end, namely for what is useful to them and what they seek. So it happens that they look only for knowledge of the final causes of events, and when they have learned those they are satisfied, having no further ground for doubt. If they cannot learn such causes from outside, they are driven to turn to themselves and ask what end would have induced them personally to bring the event about; and so they necessarily judge other natures by their own. Further, since they find, in themselves and outside themselves, many things that help them considerably in the search for what is useful—eyes for seeing, teeth for chewing, plants and animals for food, the sun for light, the sea for breeding fish, and so on—they come to look on the whole of nature as a means for obtaining such conveniences. And since they are aware that they found these conveniences and did not make them, they think they have grounds for believing that some other being made them for their use. Looking on things as means, they cannot believe them to be self-created; and judging from the means they are used to preparing for themselves, they are bound to believe in some ruler or rulers of the universe, endowed with human freedom, who have arranged and adapted everything for human use. Having no information about such rulers, they are bound to estimate their nature by their own; and so they assert that the gods ordained everything for man's use in order to bind man to themselves and receive the highest honour from him. Hence it also follows that each man devised for himself, according to his abilities, a different way of worshipping God, so that God might love him more than the rest and direct the whole course of nature to satisfy his blind greed and insatiable avarice. Thus the prejudice grew into superstition and took deep root in the human mind; and this is why everyone laboured so hard to understand and explain the final causes of things. But in trying to show that nature does nothing in vain—that is, nothing useless to man—they seem only to have demonstrated that nature, the gods and men are all mad together. Consider, I beg you, the result. Among nature's many helps they were bound to find some hindrances too—storms, earthquakes, diseases and the like—so they declared that these things happen because the gods are angry at some wrong done them by men, or some fault in their worship. Experience protested day after day, and showed by countless examples that good and bad fortune fall alike to the pious and the impious; but they would not give up their inveterate prejudice. It was easier for them to file such contradictions among the other unknown things whose use they were ignorant of, and so keep their present and inborn ignorance intact, than to pull down the whole fabric of their reasoning and start again. So they laid it down as an axiom that God's judgments far surpass human understanding. That doctrine alone might well have kept the truth hidden from the human race for ever, if mathematics had not offered another standard of truth—concerned solely with the essences and properties of figures, and taking no account of final causes. There are other reasons besides mathematics, which I need not go into here, that might have turned men's minds towards these general prejudices and led them to a knowledge of the truth.

I have now said enough on my first point. There is no need to show at length that nature has no particular goal in view, and that final causes are mere human fictions. This is clear enough already, both from the causes and foundations on which I have shown the prejudice to rest, and from Proposition 16 of this Part and the Corollary of Proposition 32 of this Part, and in fact from all the propositions in which I have shown that everything in nature proceeds by a kind of necessity and with the utmost perfection. But I will add a few remarks to overthrow the doctrine of final causes altogether. What is really a cause it takes for an effect, and the other way round; it makes what is by nature first come last, and what is highest and most perfect the most imperfect. Passing over the questions of cause and priority as self-evident, it is plain from Proposition 21 of this Part, Proposition 22 of this Part and Proposition 23 of this Part that the effect produced immediately by God is the most perfect, and that an effect requiring several intermediate causes for its production is to that extent more imperfect. But if the things made immediately by God were made to enable him to reach his end, then the things that come after, for whose sake the first were made, are necessarily the most excellent of all.

Further, this doctrine does away with the perfection of God. For if God acts for an object, he necessarily desires something he lacks. Theologians and metaphysicians do, it is true, draw a distinction between an object of want and an object of assimilation; but they still confess that God made all things for his own sake and not for the sake of what he created. And since they can point to nothing before the creation, other than God himself, as an object for which God should act, they are driven to admit—as they plainly must—that God lacked the things for whose attainment he created the means, and that he desired them.

We should not fail to notice that the followers of this doctrine, eager to display their ingenuity in assigning final causes, have brought in a new method of proof: a reduction not to the impossible but to ignorance—which shows that they have no other way of exhibiting their doctrine. Take an example. If a stone falls from a roof onto someone's head and kills him, they will demonstrate by their new method that the stone fell in order to kill the man; for if it had not fallen with that object by God's will, how could so many circumstances have come together by chance, as they often do? Perhaps you will answer that it happened because the wind was blowing and the man was walking that way. "But why," they will press, "was the wind blowing, and why was the man walking that way at that very moment?" If you answer again that the wind had got up because the sea had begun to be rough the day before, the weather having been calm until then, and that the man had been invited out by a friend, they will press again: "But why was the sea rough, and why was the man invited just then?" And so they will pursue their questions from cause to cause until at last you take refuge in the will of God—the sanctuary of ignorance. In the same way, when they contemplate the structure of the human body they are amazed; and being ignorant of the causes of so great a work of art, they conclude that it was fashioned not mechanically but by divine and supernatural skill, and so put together that no one part injures another.

So anyone who looks for the true causes of miracles, and who tries to understand natural things as an intelligent being rather than gape at them like a fool, is set down and denounced as an impious heretic by those whom the masses worship as the interpreters of nature and of the gods. Such people know that if ignorance were removed, the wonder that is their only means of proving and preserving their authority would vanish with it. But I leave this subject and pass on to my third point.

Once men had persuaded themselves that everything created is created for their sake, they were bound to regard as the chief quality in everything whatever was most useful to themselves, and to reckon those things best that had the most beneficial effect on mankind. They were bound, too, to form abstract notions to explain the nature of things—goodness, badness, order, confusion, warmth, cold, beauty, deformity and so on. And from the belief that they are free agents came the further notions of praise and blame, sin and merit.

I shall speak of the second group later, when I treat of human nature; the first I will explain briefly here.

Whatever conduces to health and to the worship of God they have called good, and whatever hinders these they have called bad. And since people who do not understand the nature of things verify nothing, but merely imagine things after a fashion and mistake their imagination for understanding, they firmly believe that there is an order in things—being really ignorant both of things and of their own nature. When things are such that the impression they make on our senses costs the imagination little effort, and so can easily be remembered, we say they are well ordered; when the opposite, we say they are ill ordered or confused. And since what is easily imagined is more pleasing to us, men prefer order to confusion, as though there were any order in nature except in relation to our imagination; and they say that God has created all things in order, thus attributing imagination to God without knowing it—unless indeed they would have it that God foresaw human imagination and arranged everything so as to be most easily imagined. If that is their theory, they will perhaps not be troubled by the fact that we find countless things far surpassing our imagination, and a great many more that confound its weakness. But enough of this. The other abstract notions are nothing but ways of imagining, in which the imagination is affected differently; and yet the ignorant take them for the chief attributes of things, since they believe everything was created for their own sake, and call a thing good or bad, healthy or rotten and corrupt, according as it affects them. For instance, if the motion that visible objects communicate to our nerves is good for our health, the objects causing it are called beautiful; if the motion excited is the contrary, they are called ugly.

Things perceived by our sense of smell are called fragrant or foul; by taste, sweet or bitter, full-flavoured or insipid; by touch, hard or soft, rough or smooth, and so on.

Whatever affects our ears is said to produce noise, sound or harmony. In this last case there are men mad enough to believe that God himself takes pleasure in harmony, and philosophers are not wanting who have persuaded themselves that the motion of the heavenly bodies produces harmony. All of which sufficiently shows that everyone judges of things according to the state of his brain, or rather mistakes the forms of his imagination for the things themselves. We need no longer wonder that all the controversies we have seen have arisen, and scepticism at the end of them. For although human bodies agree in many respects, they differ in a great many others; so that what seems good to one seems bad to another, what seems well ordered to one seems confused to another, what pleases one displeases another, and so on. I need not go on, both because this is not the place to treat the subject at length and because the fact is well enough known. Everyone says: "So many men, so many minds; everyone is wise in his own way; brains differ as completely as palates." All of which proverbs show that men judge of things according to the disposition of their minds, and imagine rather than understand. For if they understood things, they would be convinced by what I have urged, even if they were not attracted by it—as mathematics bears witness.

We have now seen that all the explanations commonly given of nature are mere ways of imagining, and indicate not the true nature of anything but only the constitution of the imagination. And although they have names, as though they were entities existing outside the imagination, I call them imaginary entities rather than real ones. So all the arguments brought against us from such abstractions are easily rebutted.

Many argue like this. If all things follow from the necessity of God's absolutely perfect nature, why are there so many imperfections in nature—things corrupted to the point of stinking, loathsome deformity, confusion, evil, sin and the rest? But such reasoners are, as I have said, easily refuted. The perfection of things is to be reckoned from their own nature and power alone; things are not more or less perfect according as they please or offend human senses, or according as they serve mankind or go against it. And to those who ask why God did not create all men so that they should be governed by reason alone, I give no answer but this: because he did not lack the material for creating every degree of perfection from the highest to the lowest; or, to speak more strictly, because the laws of his nature are so vast that they suffice for the production of everything an infinite intelligence can conceive, as I have shown in Proposition 16 of this Part.

These are the misconceptions I undertook to note here. If there are any more of the same kind, anyone can easily dispel them for himself with a little reflection.
