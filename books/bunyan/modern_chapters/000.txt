The Author's Apology for His Book

	When I first took my pen in hand to write
	like this, I did not for a moment think
	that I should ever make a little book
	in such a style. In fact I had begun
	to make another; and when that was nearly done,
	before I knew it, I had started this.
	And this is how it was. I, writing of the way
	and race of saints, in this our gospel day,
	fell suddenly into an allegory
	about their journey, and the road to glory,
	in more than twenty things that I set down.
	That done, I had another twenty in my head;
	and then they started to multiply again,
	like sparks that fly up off the coals of a fire.
	Well then, I thought, if you breed as fast as that,
	I'll put you by yourselves, in case at last
	you go on for ever, and eat out the book
	that I am already busy with.
	So that is what I did. But even then
	I did not mean to show the world my pen and ink
	in such a style. I only meant to make
	I knew not what — nor did I set out
	to please my neighbour by it. No, not I.
	I did it to satisfy my own self.
	Nor did I spend on this scribble of mine
	anything but odd spare hours; nor did I mean
	anything by it but to divert myself
	from worse thoughts that make me go wrong.
	So I set pen to paper with delight,
	and quickly had my thoughts in black and white.
	Because, having got hold of my method by the end,
	it came as fast as I pulled; and so I wrote
	it down, until at last it came to be,
	for length and breadth, the size that you see here.
	Well, when I had put my ends together like this,
	I showed them to some others, to find out whether
	they would condemn them or approve of them.
	And some said, Let them live; and some, Let them die.
	Some said, John, print it. Others said, Not so.
	Some said, It might do good. Others said, No.
	So now I was in a difficulty, and could not see
	which was the best thing for me to do.
	At last I thought: since you are so divided,
	I will print it — and so the matter is settled.
	Because, I thought, some clearly want it done,
	although some others do not run that way.
	So to find out which side advised the best,
	I thought it right to put it to the test.
	I thought further that if I now refused
	the ones who wanted it, and would not oblige them,
	I did not know but I might be keeping them
	from something that would be a great delight.
	And to those who were against its coming out
	I said: I am unwilling to offend you;
	but since your brothers are pleased with it,
	hold off from judging till you have seen more.
	If you will not read it, then leave it alone.
	Some love the meat, and some love picking the bone.
	Yes, and to smooth them down a little further,
	I reasoned it out with them like this:
	May I not write in a style like this?
	And in this method too, and yet not miss
	my end, which is your good? Why should it not be done?
	Dark clouds bring water when the bright bring none.
	Yes — dark or bright, if they let their silver drops
	come down, the earth, by yielding crops,
	gives praise to both, and finds fault with neither,
	but stores up the fruit they yield together.
	Yes, it mixes them so thoroughly that in her fruit
	nobody can tell this from that. They suit
	her well when she is hungry; but if she is full,
	she spews them both out and makes their blessing nothing.
	You see the ways a fisherman will take
	to catch a fish; what devices he will make.
	See how he sets his whole wit to the job,
	and all his snares, his lines, his rods, his hooks and nets.
	And yet there are fish that neither hook nor line
	nor snare nor net nor device can make yours:
	they must be groped for, and tickled too,
	or they will not be caught, whatever you do.
	How the fowler goes about catching his game
	by all sorts of means, more than a man could name!
	His guns, his nets, his limed twigs, light and bell;
	he creeps, he walks, he stands; and who could tell
	the whole list of his postures? And yet not one of these
	will make him master of whatever birds he please.
	No, for this one he must pipe and whistle —
	and if he does, that other bird he'll miss.
	If a pearl can live inside a toad's head,
	and can be found inside an oyster shell too;
	if things that promise nothing hold inside them
	what is better than gold — then who will scorn,
	having some inkling of it, to look there,
	so that he may find it? Now, my little book,
	although it lacks all those decorations
	that would make it take with this man or that,
	is not without those things that far excel
	what lives in fine but empty notions.
	"Well, I am still not fully satisfied
	that this book of yours will stand up when it is properly tested."
	Why, what is the matter? "It is dark."
	And what of that? "But it is made up." What of that, I ask?
	Some men, with made-up words as dark as mine,
	make truth throw sparks and set its rays to shine.
	"But they lack solidity." Speak your mind, man.
	"They drown the weak; metaphors make us blind."
	Solidity is certainly what suits the pen
	of a man who writes of divine things to men.
	But must I necessarily lack solidity because
	I speak by metaphors? Were not God's laws,
	his gospel laws, in the old days set out
	by types and shadows and metaphors? And yet
	no sober man will be quick to find fault
	with them, in case he should be found assaulting
	the highest wisdom. No — he stoops instead,
	and tries to find out what, by pins and loops,
	by calves and sheep, by heifers and by rams,
	by birds and herbs, and by the blood of lambs,
	God is saying to him. And happy is the man
	who finds the light and grace that are in them.
	So do not be too quick to conclude
	that I lack solidity, or that I am crude.
	Not everything solid-looking is solid;
	nor should we despise everything in parables,
	in case we take up lightly what is most harmful
	and rob our souls of the things that are good.
	My dark and cloudy words only hold
	the truth, as cabinets enclose the gold.
	The prophets used metaphors a great deal
	to set out truth; and anybody who considers
	Christ and his apostles too will plainly see
	that truths are wrapped in such cloaks to this day.
	Am I afraid to say that holy scripture,
	which for its style and phrasing beats all wit,
	is everywhere so full of all these things —
	dark figures, allegories? And yet there springs
	from that same book that brightness, and those rays
	of light that turn our darkest nights to days.
	Come — let my critic look now at his own life
	and find darker lines there than any he finds
	in my book; yes, and let him know as well
	that in his best things there are worse lines too.
	If we may only stand before impartial men,
	I will lay ten against his one
	that they will take my meaning in these lines
	far better than they take his lies in silver shrines.
	Come — truth, even in swaddling clothes, I find,
	informs the judgment and puts right the mind;
	pleases the understanding, makes the will
	submit; and fills the memory as well
	with what our imaginations like;
	and it tends to quiet our troubles too.
	Sound words, I know, are what Timothy is to use,
	and old wives' fables are what he is to refuse;
	but nowhere did grave Paul forbid him
	the use of parables — in which lay hidden
	that gold, those pearls, those precious stones that were
	worth digging for, and digging with great care.
	Let me add one word more. O man of God,
	are you offended? Do you wish I had
	put my matter out in another dress?
	Or that I had been more direct in things?
	Let me put three things; then I submit
	to my betters, as is fitting.
	First. I do not find that I am denied the use
	of this method of mine, so long as I put no abuse
	on the words, the things or the readers, and am not crude
	in my handling of figure or comparison
	or in applying them; but rather seek in every way I can
	to advance the truth, one way or another.
	Denied, did I say? No — I have leave for it,
	and example too, and that from men who have
	pleased God better by their words or ways
	than any man alive today —
	leave to express my mind like this, to declare
	things to you that are the most excellent there are.
	Second. I find that men as high as trees will write
	in dialogue, and nobody slights them
	for writing so. Indeed, if they abuse
	the truth, then cursed be they and the craft they use
	to that end. But still, let truth go free
	to make her sallies upon you and me
	whichever way it pleases God. Because who knows better
	than the one who first taught us to plough
	how to guide our minds and pens for his design?
	And he makes lowly things usher in the divine.
	Third. I find that holy scripture in many places
	has a likeness to this method, where the case
	calls for one thing to be used to set out another.
	So I may use it, and yet smother
	none of truth's golden beams. In fact by this method I may
	make it throw out its rays as bright as day.
	And now, before I put my pen away,
	I will show what good my book may do; and then
	commit both you and it into that Hand
	which pulls the strong down and makes the weak stand.
	This book chalks out in front of your eyes
	the man who is looking for the everlasting prize.
	It shows you where he comes from, where he goes,
	what he leaves undone, and also what he does.
	It shows you too how he runs, and runs,
	until he comes to the gate of glory.
	It shows as well who set out for life at full speed,
	as though they meant to win the lasting crown;
	and here you may see the reason too
	why they lose their labour, and die like fools.
	This book will make a traveller of you,
	if you will be governed by its advice;
	it will direct you to the Holy Land,
	if you will understand its directions.
	Yes — it will make the lazy man active,
	and make the blind see delightful things as well.
	Are you after something rare and profitable?
	Would you like to see a truth inside a fable?
	Are you forgetful? Would you like to remember
	from New Year's Day to the last of December?
	Then read my fancies; they will stick like burrs,
	and to the helpless may be comforters.
	This book is written in the kind of language
	that may take hold of listless people's minds.
	It seems a novelty, and yet contains
	nothing but sound and honest gospel strains.
	Would you like to divert yourself from melancholy?
	Would you be cheerful, and yet be far from folly?
	Would you read riddles, and their explanation?
	Or else be drowned in your own contemplation?
	Do you love food you have to pick at? Or would you see
	a man up in the clouds, and hear him speak to you?
	Would you be in a dream, and yet not sleep?
	Or would you in a moment laugh and weep?
	Would you like to lose yourself and take no harm,
	and find yourself again without a charm?
	Would you read yourself, and read you know not what,
	and yet know whether you are blessed or not,
	by reading the very same lines? Oh, then come here,
	and lay my book, your head, and your heart together.

John Bunyan
