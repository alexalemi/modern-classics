The Wicket Gate

So he knocked, more than once or twice, saying:

	May I come in? Will the one inside
	open to sorry me, though I have been
	a rebel with no claim on him at all?
	Then I will sing his praise for ever after.

At last a solemn person came to the gate, whose name was Goodwill, and asked who was there, and where he had come from, and what he wanted.

Christian. Here is a poor burdened sinner. I have come from the City of Destruction, but I am going to Mount Zion, so that I may be delivered from the wrath to come. And since I am told that the road there runs through this gate, I would like to know, sir, whether you are willing to let me in.

Goodwill. I am willing with all my heart, he said — and with that he opened the gate.

But as Christian was stepping through, the other man gave him a sharp pull. Christian said: What was that for? And the other told him: a little way off from this gate there stands a strong castle, and Beelzebub is its captain. From there, he and the ones with him shoot arrows at people coming up to this gate, in the hope that they may die before they can get in.

Then Christian said: I am glad and I am shaking, both at once.

So when he was inside, the man at the gate asked him who had directed him there.

Christian. Evangelist told me to come here and knock, which I did; and he said that you, sir, would tell me what I had to do.

Goodwill. An open door is set in front of you, and no one can shut it.

Christian. Now I am beginning to get some good out of the risks I have run.

Goodwill. But how is it that you came alone?

Christian. Because none of my neighbours saw the danger they were in, the way I saw mine.

Goodwill. Did any of them know you were coming?

Christian. Yes. My wife and children saw me at the start, and called after me to turn back; and some of my neighbours stood shouting and calling after me to come back too. But I put my fingers in my ears, and came on my way.

Goodwill. But did none of them follow you, to persuade you to go back?

Christian. Yes — both Obstinate and Pliable. But when they saw they could not get anywhere with me, Obstinate went back cursing, and Pliable came a little way with me.

Goodwill. But why did he not come the whole way?

Christian. We did come along together, until we reached the Slough of Despond, which we both suddenly fell into. And then my neighbour Pliable lost heart and would not risk going any further. So he got out again on the side nearest his own house, and told me I could have the fine country to myself for all he cared. Off he went his way and I came mine — he after Obstinate, and I to this gate.

Goodwill. Then Goodwill said: The poor man! Does he think so little of the glory of heaven that he does not reckon it worth the risk of a few difficulties to get it?

Christian. Truly, said Christian, I have told you the truth about Pliable; and if I told you the whole truth about myself, it would turn out there is nothing to choose between us. It is true he went back to his own house — but I turned aside onto the road of death, talked into it by the worldly arguments of a certain Mr. Worldly-Wiseman.

Goodwill. Oh — did he get hold of you? What, and he would have had you go looking for relief from Mr. Legality! The pair of them are a complete fraud. But did you take his advice?

Christian. Yes, as far as I dared. I went off to find Mr. Legality, until I thought the mountain that stands beside his house was going to come down on my head — and there I had to stop.

Goodwill. That mountain has been the death of many, and it will be the death of many more. You did well to escape being smashed to pieces by it.

Christian. Truly, I do not know what would have become of me there if Evangelist had not happened to meet me again, while I was standing brooding in the depths of my misery. It was God's mercy that he came to me a second time; otherwise I should never have got here at all. And now here I am, such as I am — fitter, really, to have died under that mountain than to be standing here talking to my lord. But what a kindness it is to me, that even so I am let in.

Goodwill. We turn nobody away, whatever they have done before they get here. They are "by no means cast out." So, good Christian, come a little way with me and I will teach you about the road you have to take. Look ahead of you. Do you see this narrow road? That is the way you must go. It was laid out by the patriarchs and the prophets, by Christ and by his apostles, and it is as straight as a ruler can make it. That is the road you must go.

Christian. But, said Christian, are there no turnings or bends where a stranger might lose his way?

Goodwill. Yes, there are many roads that join onto this one, and they are crooked and wide. This is how you can tell the right from the wrong: only the right one is straight and narrow.

Then I saw in my dream that Christian asked him further whether he could not help him off with the burden on his back — because he still had not been rid of it, and there was no way he could get it off without help.

Goodwill told him: as for your burden, be content to carry it until you come to the place of deliverance; for there it will fall off your back by itself.

Then Christian began to tighten his belt and get himself ready for the road. So the other told him that when he had gone some distance on from the gate, he would come to the house of the Interpreter; and that he should knock at the door there, and the Interpreter would show him excellent things. Then Christian said goodbye to his friend, and his friend once more wished him godspeed.
