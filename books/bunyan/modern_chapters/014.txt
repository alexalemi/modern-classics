The Trial of Faithful

Then a suitable time was appointed, and they brought them out for trial with a view to condemning them. When the time came they were brought before their enemies and formally charged. The Judge's name was Lord Hate-good. Their indictment was one and the same in substance, though it varied a little in wording, and its contents were these:

"That they were enemies to their trade and disturbers of it; that they had stirred up disorder and division in the town, and had won a following over to their own most dangerous opinions, in contempt of the law of their prince."

Then Faithful began to answer, and said that he had only set himself against whatever had set itself against the one who is higher than the highest. And, said he, as for disorder, I make none, being a man of peace myself. Those who were won over to us were won by seeing our truth and innocence, and they have only turned from the worse to the better. And as for this king you speak of, since he is Beelzebub, the enemy of our Lord, I defy him and all his angels.

Then it was announced that anybody who had something to say on behalf of their lord the king against the prisoner at the bar should come forward at once and give their evidence. So three witnesses came in — Envy, Superstition and Pickthank. They were then asked whether they knew the prisoner at the bar, and what they had to say for their lord the king against him.

Then Envy stood forward and said, in effect: My Lord, I have known this man a long time, and I will testify on oath before this honourable bench that he is —

Judge. Stop. Swear him in first. (So they swore him in.) Then he said:

Envy. My Lord, this man, for all his respectable-sounding name, is one of the vilest men in our country. He has no regard for prince or people, law or custom, but does everything he can to fill everyone's head with certain disloyal notions of his, which he generally calls principles of faith and holiness. And in particular, I heard him say once myself that Christianity and the customs of our town of Vanity were flatly opposed to one another and could not be reconciled. By which statement, my Lord, he condemns at a single stroke not only everything we do that is worthy of praise, but us as well, for doing it.

Judge. Then the Judge said to him: Have you anything more to say?

Envy. My Lord, I could say a great deal more, only I would not want to weary the court. Still, if it is needed, once the other gentlemen have given their evidence — rather than let anything be missing that will finish him off — I will extend my testimony against him.

So he was told to stand aside.

Then they called Superstition and told him to look at the prisoner. They also asked him what he could say for their lord the king against him. Then they swore him in, and he began.

Superstition. My Lord, I have no great acquaintance with this man, nor do I want to know any more of him. However, this much I do know: that he is a thoroughly pestilent fellow, from a conversation I had with him in this town the other day. Because talking with him then, I heard him say that our religion was worthless, and of a kind by which a man could not possibly please God. And from these statements of his, my Lord, your Lordship knows perfectly well what necessarily follows: namely, that we still worship for nothing, that we are still in our sins, and that we shall be damned in the end. And that is what I have to say.

Then Pickthank was sworn in and told to say what he knew, on behalf of their lord the king, against the prisoner at the bar.

Pickthank. My Lord, and all you gentlemen — I have known this fellow a long time, and have heard him say things that ought not to be said. Because he has abused our noble prince Beelzebub, and has spoken contemptuously of his honourable friends, whose names are the Lord Old Man, the Lord Carnal Delight, the Lord Luxurious, the Lord Desire of Vain Glory, my old Lord Lechery, Sir Having Greedy, and all the rest of our nobility. And he has said, further, that if everybody were of his mind, then if it were possible not one of these noblemen would go on existing in this town any longer. Besides that, he has not been afraid to abuse you, my Lord, who are now appointed to be his judge, calling you an ungodly villain, along with a great many other such insulting terms, with which he has spattered most of the gentry of our town.

When Pickthank had told his story, the Judge turned his speech to the prisoner at the bar, saying: You renegade, heretic and traitor — have you heard what these honest gentlemen have testified against you?

Faithful. May I say a few words in my own defence?

Judge. Fellow! Fellow! You deserve to live no longer, but to be killed on the spot. Still, so that everybody may see how gentle we are with you, let us hear what you, vile renegade, have to say.

Faithful. First, then, in answer to what Mr. Envy has said. I never said anything but this: that whatever rule, or law, or custom, or people stands flat against the Word of God is directly opposed to Christianity. If I have spoken wrongly in this, show me my error, and I am ready here in front of you to take it back.

Second, as to Mr. Superstition and his charge against me. I said only this: that in the worship of God a divine faith is required; but there can be no divine faith without a divine revelation of the will of God. So whatever is forced into the worship of God that does not agree with divine revelation cannot be done by anything but a human faith, and that faith will be of no use towards eternal life.

Third, as to what Mr. Pickthank has said. I say — leaving aside his terms, such as that I am supposed to have abused people, and the like — that the prince of this town, together with the whole rabble of his attendants named by this gentleman, are fitter to exist in hell than in this town and country. And so, the Lord have mercy on me.

Then the Judge called to the jury, who had been standing by all this while to listen and observe: Gentlemen of the jury, you see this man, about whom so great an uproar has been made in this town. You have also heard what these worthy gentlemen have testified against him. And you have heard his reply and his confession. It now lies with you to hang him or spare his life; but I think it right to instruct you first in our law.

There was an Act made in the days of Pharaoh the Great, a servant of our prince, that to prevent those of a different religion from multiplying and growing too strong for him, their male children should be thrown into the river. There was also an Act made in the days of Nebuchadnezzar the Great, another of his servants, that whoever would not fall down and worship his golden image should be thrown into a fiery furnace. There was also an Act made in the days of Darius, that whoever, for a certain period, called on any god but him should be thrown into the lions' den.

Now this rebel has broken the substance of all these laws — not only in thought, which is not to be borne, but in word and deed as well; and that must necessarily be intolerable.

As for Pharaoh's law: that was made on a supposition, to prevent trouble, when no crime had yet appeared. But here is a crime that has appeared. As for the second and third, you see that he argues against our religion; and for the treason he has confessed, he deserves to die.

Then the jury went out. Their names were Mr. Blind-man, Mr. No-good, Mr. Malice, Mr. Love-lust, Mr. Live-loose, Mr. Heady, Mr. High-mind, Mr. Enmity, Mr. Liar, Mr. Cruelty, Mr. Hate-light and Mr. Implacable; and every one of them gave his private verdict against him among the rest, and afterwards they unanimously agreed to bring him in guilty before the Judge.

And first, among themselves, Mr. Blind-man the foreman said: I can see clearly that this man is a heretic. Then Mr. No-good said: Rid the earth of a fellow like that. Yes, said Mr. Malice, because I hate the very look of him. Then Mr. Love-lust said: I could never stand him. Nor could I, said Mr. Live-loose, because he was always condemning the way I live. Hang him, hang him, said Mr. Heady. A miserable little wretch, said Mr. High-mind. My heart rises against him, said Mr. Enmity. He is a rogue, said Mr. Liar. Hanging is too good for him, said Mr. Cruelty. Let us get him out of the way, said Mr. Hate-light. Then Mr. Implacable said: If I were given the whole world, I could not be reconciled to him; so let us bring him in guilty and sentence him to death at once.

And so they did. So he was immediately condemned — to be taken from the place where he was to the place he had come from, and there to be put to the cruellest death that could be devised.

So they brought him out, to deal with him according to their law. And first they whipped him, then they beat him with their fists, then they slashed his flesh with knives; after that they stoned him with stones, then jabbed him with their swords; and last of all they burned him to ashes at the stake. And so Faithful came to his end.

Now I saw that behind the crowd there stood a chariot and a pair of horses, waiting for Faithful. And as soon as his enemies had finished him off, he was taken up into it, and was carried straight up through the clouds with the sound of a trumpet, by the shortest way to the Celestial Gate.

But as for Christian, he had some respite, and was sent back to prison. So he stayed there for a while. But the one who overrules everything, holding the power of their rage in his own hand, so arranged things that Christian escaped them for the time being and went on his way. And as he went, he sang:

	Well, Faithful, you have faithfully professed
	before your Lord, and you shall be his guest
	and blessed with him, when faithless men, who prized
	their empty pleasures, cry out in the fires.
	Sing, Faithful, sing, and let your name survive;
	for though they killed you, you are still alive.
