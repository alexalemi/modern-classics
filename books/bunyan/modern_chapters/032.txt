The House of Gaius

Christiana then said she wished there were an inn for herself and her children, because they were tired. So Mr. Honest said: There is one a little way ahead of us, where a very honourable disciple lives, one Gaius.

So they all agreed to turn in there, and all the more readily because the old gentleman gave him such a good report. So when they came to the door they went in without knocking, because people do not usually knock at the door of an inn. Then they called for the master of the house, and he came to them. So they asked whether they might sleep there that night.

Gaius. Yes, gentlemen, if you are honest men — because my house is for nobody but pilgrims.

Then Christiana and Mercy and the boys were all the more pleased, because the innkeeper was a lover of pilgrims. So they asked for rooms, and he showed them one for Christiana and her children and Mercy, and another for Mr. Great-heart and the old gentleman.

Great-heart. Then Mr. Great-heart said: Good Gaius, what have you got for supper? Because these pilgrims have come a long way today and are tired.

Gaius. It is late, said Gaius, so we cannot conveniently go out for food. But you shall be welcome to whatever we have, if that will content you.

Great-heart. We shall be content with whatever you have in the house; because I have tested you before, and you are never without what is needed.

Then he went down and spoke to the cook, whose name was Taste-that-which-is-good, to get supper ready for so many pilgrims. When this was done he came up again, saying: Come, my good friends, you are welcome to me, and I am glad I have a house to entertain you in. And while supper is getting ready, if you like, let us entertain one another with some good conversation.

So they all said: Agreed.

Gaius. Then Gaius said: Whose wife is this older woman? And whose daughter is this young one?

Great-heart. The woman is the wife of one Christian, a Pilgrim of former days, and these are his four children. The young woman is an acquaintance of hers, whom she has persuaded to come with her on pilgrimage. The boys all take after their father and are eager to walk in his footsteps. In fact, if they only see a place where the old Pilgrim lay down, or the print of his foot, it gives their hearts joy, and they want to lie or tread in the same spot.

Gaius. Then Gaius said: Is this Christian's wife? And are these Christian's children? I knew your husband's father, yes, and his father's father as well. There have been a great many good men of this stock; their ancestors first lived at Antioch. Christian's forebears — I suppose you have heard your husband speak of them — were very worthy men. Beyond anybody I know, they showed themselves men of great virtue and courage for the Lord of the Pilgrims, for his ways, and for those who loved him.

I have heard of many of your husband's relations who stood up under every trial for the sake of the truth. Stephen, who was one of the first of the family your husband sprang from, was knocked on the head with stones. James, another of that line, was killed with the edge of the sword. To say nothing of Paul and Peter, men of the old family your husband came from, there was Ignatius, who was thrown to the lions; Romanus, whose flesh was cut piece by piece off his bones; and Polycarp, who played the man in the fire. There was the one who was hung up in a basket in the sun for the wasps to eat, and the one they put into a sack and threw into the sea to drown. It would be completely impossible to count up all of that family who have suffered injury and death for the love of a pilgrim's life.

And I cannot help being glad to see that your husband has left four such boys behind him. I hope they will keep up their father's name and walk in their father's footsteps and come to their father's end.

Great-heart. Indeed, sir, they are promising lads. They seem to choose their father's ways with all their hearts.

Gaius. That is exactly what I said. So Christian's family is likely to spread out over the face of the earth still, and to be numerous on it. So let Christiana look out some young women for her sons to be betrothed to, so that their father's name and the house of his forebears may never be forgotten in the world.

Honest. It would be a pity if this family were to die out and be extinct.

Gaius. Fall it cannot; but be diminished it may. Let Christiana take my advice, though, and that is the way to keep it up.

And, Christiana, said the innkeeper, I am glad to see you and your friend Mercy here together — a lovely pair. And if I may advise you, take Mercy into a nearer relationship with you. If she is willing, let her be given to Matthew, your eldest son. That is the way to keep you a posterity on the earth.

So this match was agreed on, and in due course they were married; but more of that later.

Gaius went on, and said: I will now speak on behalf of women, to take away their reproach. Because just as death and the curse came into the world by a woman, so did life and health: "God sent forth his Son, made of a woman." Yes, and to show how much those who came after detested the mother's act, the women of the Old Testament longed for children, in the hope that this or that woman might turn out to be the mother of the Saviour of the world.

I will say again that when the Saviour had come, women rejoiced in him before either man or angel did. I do not read that any man ever gave Christ so much as a coin; but the women followed him and supported him out of what they had. It was a woman who washed his feet with her tears, and a woman who anointed his body for burial. It was women who wept when he was going to the Cross, and women who followed him from the Cross, and who sat by his tomb when he was buried. It was women who were first with him on the morning of his resurrection, and women who first brought his disciples the news that he was risen from the dead. Women, then, are highly favoured, and show by all this that they share with us in the grace of life.

Now the cook sent up to say that supper was nearly ready, and sent somebody to lay the cloth and the plates, and to set the salt and the bread in order.

Then Matthew said: The sight of this cloth, and of this forerunner of the supper, gives me a keener appetite for my food than I had before.

Gaius. Then let everything in this life that ministers teaching to you give you a keener desire to sit at the supper of the great King in his kingdom. Because all the preaching and the books and the services here are only the laying of the plates and the setting of the salt on the table, compared with the feast our Lord will make for us when we come to his house.

So supper came up. And first a shoulder and a breast of meat were set on the table in front of them, to show that they must begin their meal with prayer and praise to God: the shoulder that David lifted his heart up to God with, and the breast where his heart lay, which he used to lean against his harp with when he played. These two dishes were very fresh and good, and they all ate heartily of them.

The next thing they brought up was a bottle of wine, red as blood. So Gaius said to them: Drink freely — this is the juice of the true vine, that gladdens the heart of God and of man. So they drank and were merry.

The next was a dish of milk with bread crumbled into it; but Gaius said: Let the boys have that, so that they may grow by it.

Then in due course they brought up a dish of butter and honey. Then Gaius said: Eat freely of this; because this is good for cheering up and strengthening your judgment and your understanding. This was our Lord's dish when he was a child: "Butter and honey shall he eat, that he may know to refuse the evil and choose the good."

Then they brought up a dish of apples, and they were very good-tasting fruit. Then Matthew said: May we eat apples, since apples were what the serpent tricked our first mother with?

Then Gaius said:

	Apples they were, with which we were beguiled;
	yet sin, not apples, has our souls defiled.
	Apples forbidden, eaten, taint the blood;
	to eat them when commanded does us good.
	Drink of his flagons then, you church, his dove,
	and eat his apples, you who are sick of love.

Then Matthew said: I raised the objection because a while ago I was made ill by eating fruit.

Gaius. Forbidden fruit will make you ill; but not what our Lord has allowed.

While they were talking like this, another dish was set in front of them, and it was a dish of nuts. Then some at the table said: Nuts spoil tender teeth, and especially children's teeth. Which when Gaius heard, he said:

	Hard texts are nuts — I will not call them cheats —
	whose shells keep back their kernels from the eaters.
	Open the shells, then, and you'll have the meat;
	they're brought here for you to crack and eat.

Then they were very merry, and sat at the table a long time talking about many things. Then the old gentleman said: My good landlord, while we are cracking your nuts, will you please open this riddle for us?

	There was a man, though some men called him mad:
	the more he threw away, the more he had.

Then they all listened carefully, wondering what good Gaius would say. So he sat still for a while, and then answered:

	The man who gives his goods away to the poor
	shall have as much again, and ten times more.

Then Joseph said: I must say, sir, I did not think you would find it out.

Oh, said Gaius, I have been trained up in this way a long while. Nothing teaches like experience. I have learned from my Lord to be kind, and I have found by experience that I have gained by it. "There is a man who scatters, and yet increases; and there is a man who withholds more than he ought, and it leads him to poverty." "There is a man who makes himself rich and yet has nothing; and there is a man who makes himself poor and yet has great riches."

Then Samuel whispered to Christiana his mother, and said: Mother, this is a very good man's house. Let us stay here a good while, and let my brother Matthew be married to Mercy here before we go any further.

Which Gaius the host overheard, and said: With a very good will, my child.

So they stayed there more than a month, and Mercy was given to Matthew as his wife.

While they stayed here, Mercy, as her custom was, would be making coats and clothes to give away to the poor, by which she brought a very good name on the Pilgrims.

But to come back to our story. After supper the lads asked for a bed, because they were worn out with travelling. So Gaius called for somebody to show them their room; but Mercy said: I will put them to bed. So she took them up to bed, and they slept well.

But the rest sat up all night, because Gaius and they were such congenial company that they could not tell how to part. Then after a great deal of talk about their Lord, and themselves, and their journey, old Mr. Honest — the one who had put the riddle to Gaius — began to nod off.

Then Great-heart said: What, sir, are you getting drowsy? Come, wake yourself up; here is a riddle for you.

Then Mr. Honest said: Let us hear it.

Then Mr. Great-heart said:

	He that will kill must first be overcome;
	who would live abroad must first die at home.

Ha, said Mr. Honest, that is a hard one — hard to explain and harder to practise. But come, landlord, said he, I will leave my part to you, if you like. You explain it, and I will hear what you say.

No, said Gaius; it was put to you, and you are expected to answer it.

Then the old gentleman said:

	He first by grace must conquered be
	who would his sin put by;
	and whoever lives and would convince me
	must to himself first die.

That is right, said Gaius. Good doctrine and experience both teach this. Because, first, until grace shows itself and overcomes the soul with its glory, the soul has no heart at all to oppose sin. Besides — if sin is Satan's rope, by which the soul lies tied up, how could it put up any resistance before it is loosed from that weakness?

Second, nobody who knows anything of either reason or grace will believe that a man can be a living monument of grace while he is a slave to his own corruptions.

And now that it comes into my mind, I will tell you a story worth hearing. There were two men who went on pilgrimage: the one began when he was young, the other when he was old. The young man had strong corruptions to grapple with; the old man's had decayed along with the decay of his nature. The young man walked his road as steadily as the old one did, and was every bit as bright as he was. Now which of the two had his graces shining clearest, given that both seemed alike?

Honest. The young man's, without a doubt. Because whatever holds out against the greatest opposition gives the best proof that it is the strongest — especially when it also keeps pace with something that meets nothing like half as much opposition, as old age certainly does not.

Besides, I have noticed that old men have congratulated themselves on this mistake: taking the decay of their nature for a gracious victory over their corruptions, and so being apt to fool themselves. Old men who have grace really are best able to give advice to the young, because they have seen most of the emptiness of things. But even so, when an old man and a young one set out together, the young one has the advantage in showing the clearest evidence of a work of grace in him — even though the old man's corruptions are naturally the weaker.

So they sat talking until daybreak. Now when the household was up, Christiana asked her son James to read a chapter; so he read the fifty-third of Isaiah. When he had finished, Mr. Honest asked why the Saviour is said to come "out of a dry ground," and also that "he had no form or comeliness in him."

Great-heart. Then Mr. Great-heart said: To the first I answer that it is because the church of the Jews, which Christ came from, had by then lost nearly all the sap and spirit of religion. To the second I say that the words are spoken in the character of unbelievers — who, because they lack the eye that can see into our Prince's heart, therefore judge him by the plainness of his outside. Just like people who do not know that precious stones are covered over with a rough crust, and who, when they have found one, throw it away again because they have no idea what they have found, as men throw away a common stone.
