THE PILGRIM'S PROGRESS — TRANSLATION STRATEGY
John Bunyan, Part One 1678, Part Two 1684
Source: Standard Ebooks, after George Offor's 1855 edition


1. WHAT THIS BOOK IS, AND WHY IT NEEDS A MODERNIZATION AT ALL

Bunyan was a tinker with almost no formal schooling who wrote Part One in
Bedford jail, where he spent twelve years for preaching without a licence.
He wrote for people like himself, and it shows: the sentences are short,
the vocabulary is concrete, the images are all roads, weather, mud, packs,
inns, dogs and stiles. Standard Ebooks scores the text at a reading ease of
74 — "fairly easy," roughly seventh grade, which is already this project's
target level.

So the usual justification does not apply. We are NOT here to unstack
periodic sentences, as with Hobbes or Gibbon. Read the opening and you will
see there is nothing to unstack.

We are here for a subtler and more interesting reason: THIS BOOK MISLEADS A
MODERN READER WITHOUT THEIR NOTICING. Its difficulty is not visible. A
reader who trips on "hath" knows they have tripped and can look it up. A
reader who meets

    "your conversation gives this your mouth-profession the lie"

reads it smoothly, understands "conversation" as talking, and takes away
the exact opposite of what Bunyan wrote — because in 1678 "conversation"
means CONDUCT, one's whole way of life, and the sentence says Talkative's
BEHAVIOUR contradicts what he professes. Nothing signals the error. That is
the case for this volume, and it should govern every decision below: our
first duty is to the reader who does not know what they are missing.

The secondary duty is the archaic forms — about 655 of them in Part One
alone (thou 231, thee 129, thy 99, hath 78, art 43, doth 35, hast 31),
roughly one per ninety words. These are ordinary friction and they go.


2. THE TWO PARTS ARE DIFFERENT BOOKS AND MUST SOUND DIFFERENT

PART ONE (54,800 words, chapters 001–020) is Christian's journey, and it is
a book about being ALONE. He leaves his wife and children with his fingers
in his ears so he cannot hear them calling him back. He fights Apollyon by
himself. His terror in the Valley of the Shadow of Death is the terror of a
man who cannot tell whether the blasphemies he hears are coming from
outside him or from his own mind. The register is urgent, frightened,
and driving.

PART TWO (50,600 words, chapters 022–038) is Christiana's, and Bunyan
wrote it as a deliberate answer. The same road, the same landmarks — and
almost none of the same fear, because this time nobody walks it alone.
There is a guide (Great-heart), there are children, there are inns and
meals and a wedding, and the weak are carried rather than left: Mr.
Feeble-mind, Mr. Ready-to-halt on his crutches, Mr. Despondency and his
daughter Much-afraid all finish the journey. The register is warmer,
slower, more domestic, and frequently funny. DO NOT flatten Part Two into
Part One's key. Its whole point is that the road is survivable in company.


3. THE ALLEGORICAL NAMES ARE LOCKED — DO NOT TRANSLATE THEM

These are the book's contribution to English and several are now ordinary
idiom. They pass through untouched, capitalised as Bunyan has them:

  Places   the City of Destruction, the Slough of Despond, the Wicket
           Gate, the House of the Interpreter, the Hill Difficulty, the
           House Beautiful, the Valley of Humiliation, the Valley of the
           Shadow of Death, Vanity Fair, By-path Meadow, Doubting Castle,
           the Delectable Mountains, the Enchanted Ground, the Land of
           Beulah, the Celestial City, the River of Death
  People   Christian, Christiana, Evangelist, Obstinate, Pliable,
           Mr. Worldly-Wiseman, Goodwill, the Interpreter, Faithful,
           Hopeful, Talkative, Mr. By-ends, Demas, Giant Despair,
           Diffidence, Apollyon, Ignorance, Great-heart, Mercy, Old
           Honest, Mr. Feeble-mind, Mr. Ready-to-halt, Mr. Despondency,
           Much-afraid, Mr. Valiant-for-truth, Mr. Stand-fast, Madam
           Bubble, Mr. Fearing, Gaius, Mnason, Gonwit-thurland

Every one of them is a pun or a description, and every one still lands in
modern English. "Mr. Worldly-Wiseman" does not become "Mr. Conventional-
Wisdom." "Slough of Despond" does not become "Swamp of Despair" — the
phrase is in the dictionary. If a name's meaning has genuinely gone dark
(Diffidence, Giant Despair's wife, means DISTRUST rather than shyness),
let the surrounding prose carry the sense; do not rename her.

Bunyan's minor allegorical crowds — Mr. Blind-man, Mr. No-good, Mr.
Love-lust, Mr. Live-loose, Mr. Hate-light, Mr. Implacable on the Vanity
Fair jury — likewise stand. They are a joke that still works.


4. SPEAKER TAGS: THE ONE TRAP THAT WILL RUIN A CHAPTER SILENTLY

The source marks dialogue with abbreviated tags — "Chr.", "Hope.",
"Great-heart." — set at the head of the paragraph. EXPAND EVERY ONE to the
full name and a period:

    Chr.  -> Christian.        Hope.   -> Hopeful.
    Faith.-> Faithful.         Inter.  -> Interpreter.
    Evan. -> Evangelist.       Pli.    -> Pliable.
    Obst. -> Obstinate.        World.  -> Worldly-Wiseman.
    Apol. -> Apollyon.         Talk.   -> Talkative.
    Ignor.-> Ignorance.        Prud.   -> Prudence.
    Char. -> Charity.          Hon.    -> Honest.
    Tim.  -> Timorous.         Shep.   -> Shepherd.
    Por.  -> Porter.           Form.   -> Formalist.
    Sag.  -> Sagacity.         Feeble. -> Feeble-mind.
    Men.  -> The men.          Skill.  -> Mr. Skill.

*** "Christ." IS CHRISTIANA. IT IS NOT JESUS CHRIST. ***

Bunyan abbreviates Christiana as "Christ." fifty-six times in Part Two.
Read it as Christ and you will write fluent, confident, completely wrong
dialogue that no mechanical check in this project can catch. The
distribution is clean and you can rely on it:

    "Chr."    appears ONLY in Part One  (files 001-019) = CHRISTIAN
    "Christ." appears ONLY in Part Two  (files 023-031) = CHRISTIANA

Likewise "Matt.", "James.", "Sam." and "Joseph." are Christiana's four
sons — Matthew, James, Samuel and Joseph — not books of the Bible.

Keep the expanded tags Title-Case with a period. NEVER set a speaker tag
in capitals: assemble.py reads an all-caps line as a section heading.


5. FALSE FRIENDS — THE HEART OF THE WORK

Every one of these reads naturally to a modern eye and means something
else. Counts are for Part One; Part Two uses them the same way.

  conversation (11x)  conduct, way of life, how one behaves — NOT talking
  want (13x)          lack, be without
  prevent (7x)        go before, come ahead of (never "stop")
  conceit (4x)        opinion, notion, idea of a thing
  suffer              allow, permit
  meat                food generally, a meal
  closet              a private room for prayer
  carriage            bearing, behaviour, how one conducts oneself
  let                 hinder, obstruct (the older sense)
  presently           at once, immediately
  by and by           immediately (NOT "eventually")
  admire              wonder at, be astonished by
  amuse               bewilder, distract
  nonage              legal minority, being under age
  sensible of         keenly aware of
  discover            reveal, uncover, show
  several             separate, distinct, various
  still               continually, always
  brave / bravely     fine, splendid, splendidly
  entertain           receive as a guest; also to hold an idea
  naughty             worthless, wicked (much stronger than modern)
  ere                 before

Render the MEANING. Do not preserve the word and hope. Where the modern
reader would be helped by knowing the old sense — rarely — the prose can
carry it, but never a footnote.


6. THE DOCTRINE

Bunyan is a seventeenth-century Nonconformist Baptist, and long stretches
of the book are theological argument in allegorical costume: Faithful
against Talkative, Christian and Hopeful against Ignorance, By-ends and
his friends on the ethics of a profitable religion. The vocabulary is
technical and it is load-bearing: justification, imputed righteousness,
the Law and the Gospel, effectual calling, election, the new birth,
saving faith versus historical faith.

  - Keep the technical terms and make the argument follow. Do not
    dissolve "imputed righteousness" into a vague gesture at grace; the
    whole quarrel with Ignorance turns on it. Where a term is opaque,
    let the surrounding sentence unpack it once, in Bunyan's own voice,
    and then use it plainly.
  - Bunyan's scripture proof-texts run continuously through the dialogue.
    RULE: a scripture QUOTED AND MARKED with quotation marks in the source
    keeps its familiar Authorised Version shape, lightly touched — drop
    "thee"/"thou"/"-eth", change nothing else. Scripture woven UNMARKED
    into Bunyan's own sentence is modernized with everything around it.
  - THE VERNE RULE APPLIES IN FULL. This is a devotional book and it makes
    hard claims: the City of Destruction will burn; the wrong road ends in
    damnation; Ignorance, having walked the whole way, is bound hand and
    foot at the very gate of the Celestial City and carried to hell.
    Render every one of these as BUNYAN'S claim, unhedged, with no
    softening and no editorial wink — exactly as this project keeps Ball's
    canals on Mars and Fleming's aether. We are not here to agree with him
    or to correct him. Bowdlerizing the ending would gut the book: "Then I
    saw that there was a way to hell, even from the gates of heaven" is
    the last sentence of Part One's vision and it must land.


7. WHAT TO DO ABOUT OFFOR'S 1,010 ENDNOTES

They are NOT translated. They are nineteenth-century devotional commentary
by Burder, Scott, Mason and others — not Bunyan, a different century from
the text, and at 500 KB larger than either part of the book. They sit in
reference/notes.txt as a crib.

Consult them freely for sense. Import from them only where a modern reader
genuinely cannot follow, and then as a short bracketed note, sparingly —
the way soap-bubbles/ annotates obsolete chemistry. The model is note 1:
"the Den" of the first sentence is Bedford jail. A reader who does not know
that misses what the book's opening image IS. Expect to want fewer than a
dozen such notes in the whole volume.


8. VOICE

Bunyan's own voice — the frame narrator, "I saw in my dream" — is plain,
propulsive, and slightly breathless, a man recounting something he watched.
Keep the dream frame absolutely intact; it is the book's structure and its
excuse. Keep "Now I saw in my dream that..." as a real sentence, not a tic
to be smoothed away.

The dialogue is where the book lives, and it is far more colloquial than
its reputation. People interrupt, sulk, boast and get cross. Pliable is a
whiner. Worldly-Wiseman is a smooth, patronising, plausible advisor of the
kind everyone has met. Talkative is unbearable. Giant Despair is a
domestic bully who takes his wife's advice. By-ends is a snob. Play all of
this straight — it is comedy, and Bunyan meant it to be.

The book is also, constantly, about FEAR, and its most famous images are
psychological: sinking in a bog under the weight of your own guilt; a
burden that falls off your back by itself; a valley so dark you cannot
tell your own thoughts from the devil's; a castle whose owner is Despair
and whose dungeon opens to a key called Promise that the prisoner has had
in his pocket the whole time. Do not modernize the terror out of these.
They are exact.

Register target: eighth grade to high school, contemporary, unfussy.
Never arch, never jokey-modern, no anachronistic slang. Bunyan's plainness
is the model — we are matching him, not improving him.


9. VERSE

810 lines of it, and Bunyan drops into verse constantly — at chapter ends,
as songs, as inscriptions, and for the whole of "The Author's Apology for
His Book" (file 000). It arrives tab-indented and must stay tab-indented,
one line per line: assemble.py renders an indented run as a <pre> block.

Modernize the verse as verse. Keep the line breaks and the sense; keep
rhyme where it comes without strain and drop it rather than pad. Bunyan's
verse is rough and knows it — the Apology jokes about its own metre — so do
not smooth it into something more accomplished than he wrote.


10. RATIO AND MECHANICAL CHECKS

Expect close to 1:1. Modernizing thee/thou is one-for-one; unpacking a
compressed doctrinal phrase expands; cutting seventeenth-century throat-
clearing contracts. Verify with --min-ratio 0.85 --max-ratio 1.3 as for the
other English-to-English volumes, and treat anything near a bound as a
signal to reread rather than a number to satisfy.

Run the numeric-token check from fleming/running_notes.txt. Bunyan has few
figures, but the ones he has are load-bearing — "almost 5,000 years agone"
at Vanity Fair, the twelve years, the counts of the jury.


11. A NOTE ON THE SOURCE

One transcription slip is visible in chapter 006: "the springs that were in
his head sent the waters down his checks" — read cheeks. Correct it
silently. Watch for others; Offor's text is good but not perfect, and this
is the class of defect no mechanical check in this project can see.
