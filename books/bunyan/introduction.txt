John Bunyan was a tinker's son from a village outside Bedford, born in 1628, with a year or two of schooling and no other education at all. He served in the Parliamentary army as a teenager, came home, took up his father's trade, and started preaching. After the monarchy was restored he was arrested for preaching without a licence and spent twelve years in Bedford jail, having refused several offers of release on condition that he stop. Part of this book was written there. The man sleeping in a den at the first sentence is Bunyan in prison, and that is the only thing in the volume a note explains.

The first part came out in 1678 and the second in 1684. For the next two centuries it was the book an English-speaking Protestant household owned if it owned two books, the other being the Bible, and a great deal of English owes its shape to it — a slough of despond, a vanity fair, a hill called Difficulty, a giant named Despair.

Most editions print only the first part. Both are here. Part One is a man alone: Christian leaves his city with his fingers in his ears so he cannot hear his wife and children calling him back, and everything that happens to him afterwards happens to him by himself, in terror. Part Two sends Christiana down the same road with four sons, a friend, a hired guide and a good deal of company, and its argument is the opposite one — that the weak all get across. Mr. Feeble-mind and Mr. Ready-to-halt on his crutches arrive. It is the warmer book and much the funnier, and it should not be read in the first part's register.

The difficulty for a modern reader is not the old words, because there are almost none. Bunyan wrote deliberately plain English for people like himself and his sentences are short. The difficulty is the ordinary-looking words that have moved. Conversation means conduct, not talk, and it occurs eleven times in Part One. Want means lack. Let means hinder. Prevent means go before, conceit means opinion, and a professor is someone who professes a faith. A reader trips on hath and knows something is wrong; the same reader takes conversation for chat and comes away confident and mistaken.

Bunyan's opinions are his own and are printed as he had them, including what happens to Ignorance, who walks the whole road, and the jokes at the expense of the Roman church.
