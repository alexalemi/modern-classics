Antigone
(Part 3 of 3)

Creon.
Do you not realise that nobody would ever stop singing and wailing
before they died, if wailing did any good? Take her away. Now. Wall her
up in the roofed tomb the way I ordered, and leave her there alone,
whether she wants to die or to go on living in a house like that. Our
hands are clean as far as this girl is concerned. But one way or the
other, she loses her home up here.

Antigone.
Tomb. Bridal room. Deep-dug house that will keep me for ever. I am on my
way to my own people, and Persephone has already taken in so many of
them, dead among the dead. I go down last of all, and by far the worst,
before my share of life has run out. And yet I go with a strong hope in
me: that I will be welcome to my father when I come, and dear to you,
Mother, and dear to you, my brother. Because when each of you died it
was my own hands that washed you and laid you out and poured the
offerings at your graves. And now, Polyneices, this is what I get for
tending your body.

And yet I was right to honour you. Anyone with sense would say so. I
would never have taken this on against the will of the city for
children, if I had been a mother, or for a husband, if he had lain dead
and rotting. What law am I appealing to when I say that? If my husband
died, I could have had another. If I lost a child, I could have had
another, by another man. But with my mother and my father both hidden in
Hades, no brother could ever grow for me again. That was the law I
honoured you by, above all the others, and for it Creon decided that I
was a criminal and had dared something terrible, my brother.

And now he has hold of me and is taking me away like this: no marriage
bed, no wedding song, no share of marriage, no bringing up of children.
Stripped of everyone who owes me anything, in misery, I go alive into
the hollow places of the dead. What justice of the gods have I broken?
Why should I look to the gods any more, wretched as I am? Who is there
to call on for help? I did what was holy, and holiness has got me
convicted of the opposite. Well, if this is what the gods approve of,
then once I have suffered I will admit that I was at fault. But if the
fault is these men's, I wish them nothing worse than what they are
doing, unjustly, to me.

Chorus.
	The same winds still,
	the same gusts of the spirit, have hold of her.

Creon.
	Then the men taking her
	will have reason to cry over their slowness.

Antigone.
	Oh. That word
	has come as close to death as a word can come.

Chorus.
	I cannot tell you to take heart.
	It is going to end the way he says.

Antigone.
	City of my fathers, land of Thebes,
	and you gods who were here before us,
	they are taking me, and there is no more waiting.
	Look at me, you lords of Thebes:
	the last daughter left of the house of your kings.
	Look what I suffer, and at whose hands,
	for holding holy what is holy.

(Antigone is led away.)

Chorus.
	Danae too had to give up the light of the sky
	for a room with walls of bronze.
	Her father shut her in it, because a prophecy
	had said her son would kill him,
	and she was hidden in a bedroom that was a tomb,
	and put under the yoke.
	And yet she came of an honoured line, my child, my child,
	and she kept in her body the seed of Zeus,
	who came to her as a shower of gold.
	But whatever the power is that hands out our portion,
	it is terrible.
	Not wealth escapes it, not war,
	not a walled tower, not the black ships
	that beat against the sea.

Chorus.
	And the son of Dryas, quick to anger,
	king of the Edonians, was put under the yoke too,
	for his mocking rages:
	Dionysus shut him up in a prison of rock.
	There the terrible, flowering force of his madness
	drained out of him drop by drop,
	and he came to know the god
	he had struck at in his frenzy with a mocking tongue.
	He had tried to stop the women the god possessed,
	and the fire of the god's cry,
	and he had angered the Muses who love the flute.

Chorus.
	And beside the Dark Rocks, where two seas meet,
	are the shores of the Bosporus
	and Salmydessus in Thrace, that welcomes no stranger,
	where Ares, who lives close to that city,
	saw the cursed wound
	dealt to the two sons of Phineus by his savage second wife:
	darkness struck into the circles of their eyes,
	crying out for vengeance,
	eyes put out not by a blade
	but by bloody hands and the sharp point of a weaver's shuttle.

Chorus.
	They wasted away, poor boys, and wept
	for their miserable suffering,
	born of a mother whose marriage had been torn from her.
	And she was of the ancient blood of the Erechtheids,
	the kings of Athens,
	and in far-off caves she was reared
	among her father's storms:
	the daughter of Boreas, the North Wind,
	running with the horses over the steep hills,
	a child of gods. And yet on her too
	the long-lived Fates laid their hands, my child.

(Teiresias comes in, blind, led by a boy.)

Teiresias.
Lords of Thebes, we have come here by one road, two people seeing with
one pair of eyes. That is how the blind travel: somebody goes in front.

Creon.
What is it, old Teiresias? What news?

Teiresias.
I will tell you. And you, do what the prophet says.

Creon.
I have never yet stood apart from your judgement.

Teiresias.
That is why you have steered this city on a straight course.

Creon.
I can testify to it. I have had the benefit.

Teiresias.
Then understand this: you are standing on a razor's edge again.

Creon.
What do you mean? Your mouth makes me shiver.

Teiresias.
You will know, when you hear what my art has shown me.

I was sitting in my old place for reading the birds, where every kind of
bird comes in to me as into a harbour, and I heard a sound out of them I
had never heard: they were screaming in some ugly frenzy, and it had
turned to gibberish. And I could tell they were tearing at each other
with their claws, murderously. The beating of their wings was clear
enough. I was afraid at once, and I tried the burnt offering on an altar
with the fire well lit. But the god of fire would not blaze up out of
the meat. Instead a wet ooze sweated out of the thigh-bones onto the
embers and smoked and spat; the gall burst and scattered into the air;
and the thighs lay there streaming, bare of the fat that had been
wrapped round them. That is what this boy told me: the rites failing,
the signs giving nothing. He is my guide, as I am the guide of others.

And the city is sick with this because of you, because of your decision.
Every altar in our city, every hearth, is fouled with what the birds and
the dogs have carried there off the poor fallen son of Oedipus. And so
the gods will no longer take our prayers at the sacrifice, or the flame
from the thigh-meat; and no bird screams out a sign we can read, because
they have eaten the fat of a slaughtered man's blood.

Think about it, my son. Everybody makes mistakes. But when a man has
made one, he is not stupid, and not doomed either, if he sets about
mending the harm he has fallen into instead of standing there immovable.
Stubbornness gets a man convicted of stupidity. Give way to the dead. Do
not keep stabbing at a man who is finished. What kind of strength is it,
to kill the dead a second time? I mean you well, and what I am telling
you is good for you. And the sweetest thing there is, is to learn from
good advice, when the advice is to your profit.

Creon.
Old man, every one of you shoots at me like archers at a target, and now
I am not even safe from your prophet's trade. Your tribe has been buying
and selling me for years; I have been loaded and shipped like cargo. Go
on, make your profit; trade in the white gold of Sardis and the gold of
India, if you like. But you will not put that man in a grave. Not even
if the eagles of Zeus want to snatch him up for food and carry him to
the throne of Zeus himself. Not even then will I be frightened by that
pollution into allowing him a burial. I know very well that no human
being has the strength to pollute the gods. And even the cleverest of
men, old Teiresias, take a shameful fall when they dress shameful words
up handsomely for the sake of money.

Teiresias.
Ah. Does any man know, does any man stop to think,

Creon.
What? What is this great truth for everyone that you are announcing?

Teiresias.
How much the best thing anyone can own is good judgement?

Creon.
As much, I suppose, as having no sense is the worst thing.

Teiresias.
And that is the sickness you are full of.

Creon.
I do not want to answer a prophet with insults.

Teiresias.
You are doing it, when you say my prophecies are lies.

Creon.
The whole tribe of prophets loves money.

Teiresias.
And the tribe of tyrants loves dirty profit.

Creon.
Do you know that you are saying that about your king?

Teiresias.
I know. It was through me that you saved this city.

Creon.
You are a skilled prophet. You just like doing wrong.

Teiresias.
You will make me say what should stay unspoken in my mind.

Creon.
Say it. Only not for money.

Teiresias.
Is that what you think I have been doing, where you are concerned?

Creon.
Understand this: you are not going to buy and sell my judgement.

Teiresias.
Then understand this, and understand it well. The sun's chariot will not
run many more laps before you pay back, out of your own body, one corpse
for the corpses: a dead child for the dead. Because you have taken one
who belonged up here and thrown her down below; you have lodged a living
soul in a tomb, with no honour. And you keep up here what belongs to the
gods below: a dead body, with no share of the rites, no burial, unholy.
None of that is any business of yours, or of the gods above; you are
forcing it on them. And for that the destroyers who come late are lying
in wait for you, the Furies of Hades and of the gods, and you will be
caught in these same evils yourself.

Now look and see whether I say this because somebody has silvered my
palm. Not much time will pass before this house of yours is loud with
the wailing of men and of women. And every city is stirring up in hatred
against you: every city whose dead have had their funeral rites
performed by dogs, or wild beasts, or some bird of the air, carrying the
unholy stink home to the hearths of the city.

You provoked me, so I have shot these at you like an archer, in anger,
straight from the heart, and they fly true. You will not outrun their
sting.

Boy, take me home. Let him spend his temper on younger men, and learn to
keep a quieter tongue in his head, and a better mind than the one he is
carrying now.

(Teiresias is led away.)

Chorus.
The man is gone, my king, and he has left terrible prophecies behind
him. And we know one thing: in all the time since this hair of ours
turned from black to white, he has never once told this city a lie.

Creon.
I know it myself. My mind is in confusion. To give way is terrible. To
stand and resist, and have my will smashed by ruin, that is terrible
too.

Chorus.
You need to take good advice, son of Menoeceus.

Creon.
Then what should I do? Tell me. I will do it.

Chorus.
Go and let the girl out of her underground room. And build a tomb for
the man who is lying there.

Creon.
That is your advice? You think I should give in?

Chorus.
Yes, my king, and as fast as you can. The harm the gods send has swift
feet, and it cuts off men whose thinking has gone wrong.

Creon.
Oh, it is hard. I am going against my heart, but I will do it. There is
no use fighting necessity.

Chorus.
Then go and do it yourself. Do not leave it to other people.

Creon.
I will go as I am. Come, come, servants, all of you, those here and
those not here. Get axes in your hands and run to that place over there,
the one in view. My judgement has turned this way, and I tied her up, so
I will be there to untie her myself. I am afraid, now, that the best
thing is to keep the laws that have been laid down, right to the end of
your life.

(Creon goes out with his men.)

Chorus.
	God of many names, glory of Semele, the bride of Cadmus,
	and child of Zeus who thunders deep,
	you who watch over famous Italy
	and rule in the folds of the valley of Eleusis,
	Demeter's, where everyone is welcome:
	Bacchus, who lives in Thebes,
	the mother-city of your Bacchants,
	beside the wet streams of the Ismenus,
	on the ground where the savage dragon's teeth were sown.

Chorus.
	You are seen through the flaring smoke of torches
	above the twin-peaked rock of Parnassus,
	where the nymphs of the Corycian cave
	walk in your train,
	and Castalia's spring sees you too.
	The ivy-covered slopes of the hills of Nysa
	and the green shore heavy with grapes
	send you here,
	while immortal voices cry your cry,
	to watch over the streets of Thebes.

Chorus.
	Thebes you honour above every city,
	you and your mother whom the lightning struck.
	And now, when the whole city and all its people
	are held in the grip of a violent sickness,
	come with cleansing feet over the slope of Parnassus,
	or across the groaning strait.

Chorus.
	You who lead the dance of the stars that breathe fire,
	who watch over the voices in the night,
	child of Zeus, show yourself,
	my lord, with the women who go round you,
	the Thyiads, who dance for you all night in their madness
	and call you Iacchus, the giver.

(A Messenger comes in.)

Messenger.
You who live beside the house of Cadmus and Amphion: there is no human
life, however it stands, that I would ever praise or blame as settled.
Luck sets a man on his feet and luck knocks him down, lucky and unlucky
alike, every day; and nobody can prophesy to men how things will stand.
Creon was a man to envy once, in my opinion. He had saved this land of
Cadmus from its enemies, taken sole and complete command of the country,
and steered it straight, and he was flourishing in a noble crop of
children. And now all of it is gone. When a man has thrown away what
gave him pleasure, I do not count him as living. He is a breathing
corpse. Pile up wealth in your house if you like; live with a king's
pomp. If the joy is missing from it, I would not give the shadow of
smoke for the rest, set against joy.

Chorus.
What is this new weight of grief for the royal house that you have
brought us?

Messenger.
They are dead. And the living are to blame for their dying.

Chorus.
Who killed? Who is lying dead? Tell us.

Messenger.
Haemon is dead. And the hand that shed his blood was his own.

Chorus.
His own? Or his father's?

Messenger.
His own, in rage at his father over the killing.

Chorus.
Prophet, how true your word has come.

Messenger.
That is how things are. The rest is for you to decide.

Chorus.
Wait. Here is Eurydice, poor woman, Creon's wife. Either she has heard
about her son, or it is chance that brings her out of the house.

(Eurydice comes out.)

Eurydice.
People of Thebes, all of you: I heard what you were saying as I was
going out to the gate to pray to the goddess Pallas. I was just drawing
back the bolts of the door to open it, when the sound of a disaster in
my own house struck through my ears. I fell back terrified into my
slaves' arms, and everything went dark. But tell me again, whatever the
news was. Tell it again. I am no stranger to bad news. I can hear it.

Messenger.
Dear lady, I was there, and I will tell you, and I will leave out not
one word of the truth. Why should I soften it for you, and be shown up
afterwards as a liar? The truth is always the straight road.

I went with your husband as his guide to the far edge of the plain,
where the body of Polyneices was still lying, unpitied, torn by dogs. We
prayed to the goddess of the crossroads and to Pluto to hold back their
anger and be kind. We washed him with the holy washing, and with
fresh-cut branches we burned what was left of him, and heaped up a high
mound of his own earth over it. Then we turned to go into the girl's
hollow bridal room of Hades, with its floor of stone.

While we were still some way off, one of the men heard the sound of
loud, high wailing from the direction of that bridal room where no rites
had been paid, and came and told his master Creon. As Creon crept closer
the confused sounds of a wretched cry came round him, and he groaned,
and let out a word of misery. "Oh. Am I a prophet? Is this the
unluckiest road I have ever walked? It is my son's voice calling me.
Servants, go closer, quickly. Stand by the tomb, go in through the gap
where the stones have been torn out of the mound, right up to the mouth
of it, and look. Is it Haemon's voice I hear, or are the gods deceiving
me?"

Messenger.
We looked, as our despairing master ordered. And in the furthest part of
the tomb we saw her, hanging by the neck, in a noose of fine linen, and
him, with his arms thrown round her waist, holding her, crying out for
his bride lost to the world below, and for what his father had done, and
for his wretched marriage.

When his father saw him he gave a terrible groan and went in to him and
called to him, wailing: "Poor boy, what have you done? What was in your
mind? What disaster has destroyed your reason? Come out, my son. I am
begging you on my knees." But the boy glared at him with wild eyes, spat
in his face, said nothing in reply, and drew his two-edged sword. His
father ran out and he missed. Then, poor wretch, he turned his anger on
himself, and just as he stood, leaned on the sword and drove it half its
length into his side. Still conscious, he took the girl in the loose
curve of his arm and held her, and gasping, sent a sharp jet of blood
down her white cheek, drop by drop.

Dead, he lies holding the dead. He got his marriage in the end, poor
boy, in the house of Hades. And he has shown the world how badly a man
is served by bad judgement, the greatest evil that can lie on him.

(Eurydice goes back into the house.)

Chorus.
What do you make of that? The woman has gone back in without a word,
good or bad.

Messenger.
It startled me too. But I live on the hope that she has heard her son's
fate and does not think it proper to cry out in front of the city: that
she will go inside and set her slaves to mourn the household's grief.
She has too much judgement to do anything wrong.

Chorus.
We do not know. To us, too much silence is as heavy a sign as a lot of
useless shouting.

Messenger.
Well, we will find out whether she is hiding some purpose in the dark of
a heart worked up to fury. I will go into the house. You are right. Too
much silence can weigh as much.

(He goes in. Creon comes in carrying the body of Haemon.)

Chorus.
	And here is the king himself,
	with a memorial in his arms that speaks for itself:
	if it is right to say it, the ruin of no stranger's making,
	but his own mistake.

Creon.
	Oh,
	the mistakes of a mind that would not think,
	stubborn, deadly.
	Look at us: the killer and the killed,
	one blood.
	Oh, the misery of my decisions.
	My son. So young, and dead so young,
	aiai, aiai,
	you are dead, you are gone,
	by my folly, not yours.

Chorus.
	Oh. You seem to have seen justice too late.

Creon.
	Oh, misery.
	I have learned. I am wretched, and I have learned.
	A god, then, it was then, with a great weight in his hand
	struck me on the head and shook me down wild roads,
	oh, and trampled my joy underfoot.
	Ah. Ah. The pains men take, and the pain of them.

Messenger.
	Master, you have your hands full, and more in store.
	One grief you carry in your arms;
	the other is in the house, and you will see it soon.

Creon.
	What? What can be worse, after this?

Messenger.
	Your wife is dead. The mother of this dead boy,
	all mother to him, poor woman, by wounds cut a moment ago.

Creon.
	Oh.
	Oh harbour of Hades, that no washing can clean,
	why are you killing me? Why?
	You who bring me the worst news,
	what is it you are saying?
	Aiai. You have killed a dead man over again.
	What are you telling me, boy? What new thing,
	aiai, aiai,
	a woman's death, slaughtered,
	heaped on the death that was here already?

Chorus.
	You can see for yourself. It is not hidden inside any more.

(The doors are opened. Eurydice's body lies inside.)

Creon.
	Oh,
	a second evil. I see it, wretched as I am.
	What fate is still waiting for me? What more?
	I have just had my son in my arms,
	and now, in front of me, another corpse.
	Ah, ah, poor mother. Ah, my son.

Messenger.
	She stood at the altar with a sharp-whetted sword
	and let the dark come down over her eyes.
	First she cried for the glorious death of Megareus, who died before,
	then for this boy, and last of all
	she called down evil on you, the killer of her sons.

Creon.
	Aiai, aiai.
	I am shaking with fear. Why does nobody
	strike me full in the chest with a two-edged sword?
	I am wretched, aiai,
	and I am drowning in wretched misery.

Messenger.
	Yes. She laid the blame for both deaths, this one and the other,
	on you, this dead woman, before she died.

Creon.
	And how did she die? How did the blood come?

Messenger.
	By her own hand. A blow under the heart,
	when she heard what had happened to her son, and the crying.

Creon.
	Oh, oh. This will never fit another man.
	This is mine. I did it.
	I killed you, I did, it was me, I say it,
	and it is true. Servants,
	take me away. Quickly. Take me out of the way.
	I am nothing now. I am less than nobody.

Chorus.
	What you ask is a gain, if there is any gain in evil.
	When trouble lies at your feet, the shortest way is best.

Creon.
	Let it come, let it come,
	the best death of all, the one that brings me
	my last day. Let it come, let it come,
	so that I never see another.

Chorus.
	That is for later. There is work here in front of us.
	Those things are in the hands of the ones whose business they are.

Creon.
	Everything I want, I put into that prayer.

Chorus.
	Then pray for nothing more. There is no escape for a human being
	from the suffering that is allotted.

Creon.
	Take me away, out of the way, a useless man,
	who killed you, my son, without meaning to,
	and you too, my wife. Oh, misery. There is nowhere I can look,
	nothing I can lean on. Everything in my hands
	has gone crooked, and on my head
	a fate has jumped that I cannot carry.

(Creon is led into the house.)

Chorus.
	Good sense is the first part of happiness,
	by a long way.
	And do nothing the gods would count as unholy.
	Great words from boasting men
	are paid for with great blows,
	and in old age they teach good sense.
