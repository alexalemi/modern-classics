Ajax
(Part 2 of 3)

Chorus.
Ajax, I wish pity would touch you the way it touches me. Then you would
think well of what she says.

Ajax.
She will have my good opinion, if she can bring herself to do what she
is told and do it properly.

Tecmessa.
Dear Ajax, I will do anything you say.

Ajax.
Then bring me my son. I want to see him.

Tecmessa.
I was frightened. I sent him away.

Ajax.
Because of this trouble of mine? Is that what you mean?

Tecmessa.
In case the poor child came across you and died of it.

Ajax.
That would have suited my luck exactly.

Tecmessa.
Well, I took care that it should not happen.

Ajax.
I approve. That was a good thought and you were right to have it.

Tecmessa.
Then how can I help you now, as things are?

Ajax.
Let me speak to him. Let me see his face.

Tecmessa.
He is close by. The servants are watching him.

Ajax.
Then why is he not here?

Tecmessa.
Child, your father is calling you. Bring him here — whichever of you
has him by the hand.

Ajax.
Is the man coming, or did your voice not reach him?

Tecmessa.
Here he is now, one of the servants, bringing him.

(A servant brings in Eurysaces, a small boy.)

Ajax.
Lift him. Lift him up here. He will not flinch at the sight of this
fresh blood — not if he is really mine, his father's son. He has to be
broken in early to his father's raw ways, and shaped to the same
nature.

Boy, I hope you have better luck than your father, and are like him in
everything else. Then you will not turn out badly. Even now there is
one thing about you I could envy: you have no idea of any of this. Life
is sweetest when you cannot think yet. Not being able to think is an
affliction with no pain in it — until you learn what joy is, and what
hurt. When you get that far, you will have to show my enemies what you
are made of, and whose son you are.

Meanwhile feed on light air. Grow, and be your mother's happiness. No
Greek is going to lay a hand on you, I am certain of that, or insult
you, even with me gone. I will leave Teucer at your gate to keep watch,
and he will not slacken in looking after you, though at the moment he
is far off, hunting enemies.

My shield-men, my companions at sea — I lay this on you as I lay it on
him, a service you all share. Tell Teucer my orders. He is to take this
child home and show him to Telamon, and to my mother — Eriboea, I
mean — so that he can be the comfort of their old age from now on,
until they reach the deep places of the god below. And my arms are not
to be set up as a prize for the Greeks by any master of the games, nor
by the man who destroyed me.

That much you take for me, son — the broad shield you are named for.
Hold it. Swing it by the strong grip. Seven layers of hide, and no
spear has gone through it. The rest of my gear will be buried with me.

Now take the child, quickly. Shut the doors. And no mourning outside
the house. God, women are a weepy business. Shut it, and be quick. It
is no clever doctor who chants spells over a wound that wants the
knife.

Chorus.
I am afraid, hearing you in such a hurry. I do not like the edge on
your tongue.

Tecmessa.
Ajax, my lord — what is in your mind?

Ajax.
Do not judge me. Do not question me. Keeping yourself in hand is a fine
thing.

Tecmessa.
I have no heart left. By your child, by the gods, I beg you — do not
betray us.

Ajax.
You are irritating me. Do you not understand that I owe the gods
nothing any more?

Tecmessa.
Say better words than that.

Ajax.
Say them to somebody who is listening.

Tecmessa.
Will you not hear me?

Ajax.
You have said too much already.

Tecmessa.
Because I am afraid, my king.

Ajax.
Shut the doors. Now.

Tecmessa.
In the gods' name, soften.

Ajax.
You are a fool, I think, if you have only now decided to start training
my temper.

Chorus.
	Famous Salamis, you sit
	out among the beating waves, and you are happy,
	and every man can see you where you are.
	And I, wretched, have been here a long time,
	making my bed month after month
	on the fields of Ida and not counting them,
	worn down by the time,
	with the bad expectation still in front of me
	that I shall get through at last
	to Hades, whom nobody wants and nobody sees.

Chorus.
	And now here is Ajax to sit down beside me,
	past curing, and no relief in it,
	sharing his tent with a madness sent by a god.
	Once you sent him out from home
	and he was master in the rush of war.
	Now he grazes his mind in country of his own
	and he has been found a great grief to his friends.
	What his hands did before, the highest courage there is,
	has fallen unloved before the unloving,
	fallen, fallen, before the miserable sons of Atreus.

Chorus.
	And his mother, who has lived with the old days,
	who is white with age — when she hears
	that his mind has been destroyed,
	she will not cry the way the nightingale cries,
	that pitiful bird.
	She will sing sharp songs over him,
	and the beat of hands will fall on her breast,
	and grey hair will come out in her fingers.

Chorus.
	Better hidden in the world below, the man sick with a madness
	that came to nothing, who by his father's line
	was the best of the Greeks who have endured so much,
	and is no longer steady in the temper he was born with
	but has gone outside himself and lives out there.
	Unhappy father — what news is waiting for you,
	the ruin that has fallen on your son,
	which no life in the house of Aeacus
	has carried before him.

(Ajax comes out with the sword in his hand. Tecmessa follows.)

Ajax.
Everything is brought out of the dark by the long, uncountable years,
and everything in the light is buried by them. There is nothing a man
should not expect. The most terrible oath is beaten. So is the will
that would not bend.

Even I — who was so hard, then, so like iron off the anvil — I have
been softened at the mouth by this woman's words. I feel the pity of
leaving her a widow and the boy an orphan among my enemies.

I am going to the washing places, to the meadows at the water's edge,
so that I can clean off what is on me and get out from under the
goddess's heavy anger. I will find some untrodden spot, and bury this
sword of mine, the most hateful of weapons, dig it into the ground
where nobody will see it. Let Night and the world below keep it down
there.

From the day I took it into my hand, a present from Hector, my greatest
enemy, I have had nothing good from the Greeks. The proverb men have is
true: an enemy's gifts are no gifts, and no good comes of them.

So from now on I will know how to give way to the gods, and I will
learn to respect the sons of Atreus. They are in command. One gives
way. Of course one does. The most fearsome and unbreakable things give
way to office. Winter, with its paths under snow, stands aside for
summer and the fruit. Night's long circle steps back and lets day light
her fire with her white horses in front. The blast of terrible winds
lets the groaning sea lie down. And Sleep, who is stronger than
anything, ties a man up and then unties him, and does not keep him for
ever.

And are we not going to learn to keep ourselves in hand? I am. I have
just found out that an enemy is to be hated only so far as suits a man
who is going to be his friend later on; and that with a friend I will
want to do just as much good and no more, since he is not going to stay
one. Most people find the harbour of comradeship untrustworthy.

But all of that will be well enough. You, wife, go inside and pray to
the gods that what my heart wants may be finished, right to the end.

(Tecmessa goes in.)

And you, my friends, honour what I have asked, exactly as she does. Tell
Teucer, when he comes, to take care of us, and to be good to you at the
same time. I am going where I have to go. Do as I say, and before long,
perhaps — badly off as I am now — you will hear that I have found rest.

(He goes out.)

Chorus.
	I shudder with longing. I am up and flying with joy.
	Pan, Pan,
	Pan the sea-wanderer, come to us
	from the stony ridge of Cyllene where the snow is beaten flat.
	King, dance-maker to the gods, appear,
	and join us, and set us going in the Nysian steps,
	the Cnosian steps, the ones you taught yourself.
	Now I want to dance.
	And let Apollo come over the Icarian water,
	the lord of Delos, in a shape I will know,
	and be with me, kindly, all the way through.

Chorus.
	Ares has lifted the terrible ache off our eyes.
	Now, now, Zeus,
	now the white light of a good day can come near
	our fast ships that run on the sea,
	because Ajax has forgotten his pain again,
	and has carried out every offering the gods are owed,
	keeping the rule, with the greatest respect.
	Great time makes everything fade.
	And I would not call anything unsayable,
	now that Ajax, against all our hope,
	has been talked round out of his anger
	and out of his great quarrel with the sons of Atreus.

(A messenger comes in from the camp.)

Messenger.
Friends, here is my news first. Teucer is back, just now, from the
heights of Mysia. He came to the generals' quarters in the middle of
the camp and the whole Greek army is shouting at him. They recognised
him a long way off and closed round him, and then they pelted him with
insults from every side — not one of them held off — calling him the
brother of the madman, the man who plotted against the army, saying he
was not going to get out of it without being torn to pieces under
their stones. It went so far that swords were pulled out of scabbards
and drawn. Then the quarrel, having run nearly the whole way, was
stopped by the older men talking them round.

But where do I find Ajax? I have to tell him. Everything has to be
reported to the man in charge.

Chorus.
He is not inside. He went out a moment ago. He has yoked a new purpose
to a new mood.

Messenger.
No. No. Then the man who sent me on this errand sent me too late — or I
came too slowly.

Chorus.
What is urgent about it? What part of it has been neglected?

Messenger.
Teucer said flatly that Ajax was not to be let out of the hut until he
got here himself.

Chorus.
Well, he is gone. I tell you again. He turned his mind to the best
thing he could do: making his peace with the gods over his anger.

Messenger.
Those words are full of foolishness, if there is anything in what
Calchas prophesies.

Chorus.
What does he prophesy? What do you know about this?

Messenger.
This much. I was there and saw it. Calchas got up out of the circle of
chiefs sitting in council, went off by himself away from the sons of
Atreus, put his right hand into Teucer's hand in a friendly way, and
told him — laid it on him — to use every method there is to keep Ajax
inside his hut for the length of this one day that is shining on us
now, and not to let him move, if he ever wanted to see him alive.

Because it is this day alone, he said, that the anger of the goddess
Athena will drive at him. Lives that have grown outsized and stopped
being any use, the prophet went on, come down under heavy trouble from
the gods — especially when a man born to a man's nature stops thinking
like one.

And Ajax, from the moment he first set out from home, showed himself a
fool when his father was giving him good advice. Telamon said to him,
"Son, want to win with the spear — but always want to win with a god
beside you." And he answered, high and stupid: "Father, with the gods
even a nobody could win. I mean to drag that glory in without them."

That is the size of the boast he made. Then a second time, when the
goddess Athena was urging him on and telling him to turn a killing hand
on the enemy, he answered her with something terrible, a thing that
should not be said: "Queen, go and stand by the other Greeks. Where we
are, the line will never break."

Words like that are how he earned the goddess's anger, which does not
soften — because he was thinking thoughts too big for a man. But if he
is still alive today, we may find some way, with a god's help, to save
him. That is what the prophet said.

Teucer got up from his seat at once and sent me to you with these
orders. But if we have missed our chance, the man is dead — unless
Calchas knows nothing.

Chorus.
Tecmessa, born to suffer — come out and hear what this man is saying.
The razor is against the skin, and it will take all our happiness off.

(Tecmessa comes out, with Eurysaces.)

Tecmessa.
Why do you get me up again, when I had only just found some rest from
troubles that never let go?

Chorus.
Listen to this man. He has come with news of Ajax that hurt me to hear.

Tecmessa.
Oh — what are you saying? Are we destroyed?

Messenger.
I know nothing about your position. About Ajax I know this: if he is
out of doors, I have no confidence.

Tecmessa.
He is out of doors. I am in agony to know what you mean.

Messenger.
Teucer gives strict orders to keep him under the roof of his hut, and
not to let him out alone.

Tecmessa.
Where is Teucer? Why does he say this?

Messenger.
He has only just come back. And he thinks this going out is going to
kill Ajax.

Tecmessa.
Oh, this is misery. Who did he learn it from?

Messenger.
From the son of Thestor, the prophet. It is about today: today decides
whether the man lives or dies.

Tecmessa.
Friends — stand in front of me. This is a fate I cannot get round.
Hurry. Some of you go and make Teucer come faster. Some of you take the
western bays and some the eastern, and go looking for the way the man
went out, which is a bad way.

I know now that my husband has deceived me and that I am thrown out of
the favour I used to have with him.

Oh, child — what am I to do? I must not sit here. I will go too, as far
as I have strength for it. Move. Quickly. This is no time to be
sitting, if we want to save a man who is in a hurry to die.

Chorus.
I am ready to go, and I will show it in more than words. Speed of foot
and speed of doing will come together.

(A lonely place by the shore. Ajax is alone, with the sword fixed in
the ground.)

Ajax.
The killer is standing where it will cut deepest — if a man has leisure
even to work that out. It is Hector's gift, and of all the guests I
ever had he was the one I hated most and least wanted to look at. It is
planted in enemy ground, in Troy, and freshly sharpened on the stone
that eats iron. And I have set it firmly, and packed the earth round it
myself, so that it will be as kind to me as it can and kill me quickly.

So we are properly equipped.

Now, Zeus, you first — as is right — do this much for me. It is not a
large prize I am asking. Send somebody with the bad news to Teucer, so
that he is the first to lift me when I have fallen on this sword and
made it wet, and I am not spotted by one of my enemies first and thrown
out for the dogs and the birds to pick at.

That is all I ask of you, Zeus. And I call on Hermes, who takes men
below, to put me to sleep properly, with one quick jump and no
struggling, when I have broken my side open on this blade.

And I call for help on the maidens who are always there and always
watching everything that men suffer, the dread Furies with their long
stride, to see how I am being destroyed, wretched, by the sons of
Atreus. And let them take those bad men in the worst possible way and
finish them off entirely. As they see me here, fallen and killed by my
own hand, so let those two be killed by their own, at the hands of
their own dearest children.

Go, Furies. You are quick and you do not forgive. Feed. Spare nobody in
the whole army.

And you, Sun, driving your chariot up the steep sky — when you see the
country my fathers came from, pull in the gold-backed rein and tell my
ruin and the way I died to my old father, and to the unhappy woman who
nursed me. When she hears it, poor woman, I think she will send up a
great cry through the whole city.

But there is no use weeping about it like this. The thing has to be
started, and quickly.

Death — Death, come and look at me now. Though I will speak to you
again where I am going, and be with you there.

And you, light of the day that is shining now — you, and the Sun in his
chariot — I say goodbye to you. This is the last time, and there will
be no other. Light. Sacred ground of my own Salamis, the firm floor of
my father's hearth. Famous Athens, and the people who are my own kin.
Springs and rivers here — and I say goodbye to the plains of Troy as
well — all of you who fed me, goodbye. This is the last word Ajax says
to you. The rest I will say to the dead, down below.

(He throws himself on the sword. The Chorus comes back in two groups,
from opposite sides.)

First Half-Chorus.
	Work brings work and work again.
	Where — where
	have I not tramped?
	And no place will say it knows anything.
	Listen.
	I hear something. A thud.

Second Half-Chorus.
	Us. Your shipmates, off the same voyage.

First Half-Chorus.
	Well then?

Second Half-Chorus.
	The whole western side of the ships has been gone over.

First Half-Chorus.
	And?

Second Half-Chorus.
	A great deal of work, and nothing more to see.

First Half-Chorus.
	And on the road that faces the morning sun
	the man has not shown himself either.

Chorus.
	Who can point me the way — some hard-working
	fisherman out at his sleepless catch,
	or one of the goddesses of Olympus,
	or of the streams that run down to the Bosporus —
	if anywhere she has seen
	the fierce-hearted man wandering,
	and could call out? It is cruel
	that I have gone so far and worked so hard
	and cannot come near him with a fair wind,
	cannot see where the failing man is.

Tecmessa.
	Oh. Oh no.

Chorus.
	Whose cry came out of the trees just there?

Tecmessa.
	Oh, this is misery.

Chorus.
	It is the poor woman his spear won,
	Tecmessa. I see her, and she is dissolved in it.

Tecmessa.
	I am gone. Destroyed. Levelled to the ground, friends.

Chorus.
	What is it?

Tecmessa.
	Ajax. Here. Newly killed,
	lying folded round the sword, with the sword inside him.

Chorus.
	Then there is no going home.
	My king, you have killed me,
	the man who sailed with you.
	And her. Poor broken woman.

Tecmessa.
	The state he is in, there is nothing to say but aiai.

Chorus.
	By whose hand did he do it, the unlucky man?

Tecmessa.
	His own. It is obvious. The sword he planted in the earth
	and fell on gives the evidence against him.

Chorus.
	What blindness in me. You bled alone,
	then, and none of your friends was guarding you.
	And I took no care of it, deaf as I was, knowing nothing.
	Where — where is he lying,
	the man who would not bend, whose name is grief?

Tecmessa.
	He is not to be looked at. I will cover him
	with this cloak, right over, wrapping him,
	because no one who loved him could bear to see
	the black blood driven up through his nostrils
	out of the wound he made himself.
	What am I to do? Which of his friends will lift him?
	Where is Teucer? He would come at the right moment,
	if he came now, to lay his brother out.
	Ajax, unlucky man — such as you were, and lying like this.
	Even your enemies owe you mourning.

Chorus.
	You were bound to, poor man. With that stiff heart
	you were bound in the end to work your way through
	to a hard fate, and to troubles without number.
	Such things you groaned out at me
	all night and into the daylight,
	savage-hearted, hating the sons of Atreus,
	with a deadly passion in it.
	That was the great beginning of these sorrows,
	the hour when the arms were put up
	as the prize for a contest in courage.

Tecmessa.
	Oh. Oh no.

Chorus.
	It goes to the liver, I know. That grief is a true one.

Tecmessa.
	Oh. Oh no.

Chorus.
	I do not wonder that you cry out twice over, lady,
	having just now lost a man you loved so much.

Tecmessa.
	You can work my troubles out. I have to feel them.

Chorus.
	That is true.

Tecmessa.
	Oh, my son — what a yoke of slavery
	we are walking into. What overseers are standing over us.

Chorus.
	The things you name, in the middle of this grief —
	they would be unspeakable, from those two
	hard men, the sons of Atreus.
	May a god hold them off.

Tecmessa.
	None of what you see would have turned out this way
	if the gods had not been in it.

Chorus.
	Yes. They have loaded on more weight than can be carried.

Tecmessa.
	And this is the kind of suffering the terrible goddess,
	Pallas, daughter of Zeus, grows for Odysseus' sake.

Chorus.
	No doubt the man who endures everything
	is exulting in his dark heart, laughing loud
	at these mad sorrows — shame on it —
	and the two royal brothers laughing with him
	when they hear.

Tecmessa.
Then let them laugh, and enjoy this man's ruin. Perhaps, though they
did not want him while he was alive, they will mourn him dead, when the
fighting goes badly for them. Men with crooked judgement do not know
what good thing they are holding until they have thrown it away.

His death is more bitter to me than it is sweet to them, and to himself
it is a pleasure, because he got hold of everything he wanted: the
death he wanted. So what have they got to exult over? He died before
the gods. Not before them. No.

Let Odysseus go on being insulting to no purpose. For them Ajax is no
longer there. For me he is gone, and he has left me my grief and my
mourning, and gone.

Teucer.
Oh. Oh no.

Chorus.
Be quiet. I think I hear Teucer's voice, striking the note that fits
this disaster.

(Teucer comes in.)

Teucer.
Ajax, dearest of men, brother, the face I loved to look at — is it true
what you have done, as the rumour says it is?

Chorus.
He is dead, Teucer. Take it as a fact.

Teucer.
Then my luck is too heavy to carry.

Chorus.
Things being as they are —
