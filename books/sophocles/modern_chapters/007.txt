Oedipus the King
(Part 2 of 3)

Chorus.
But that reproach may well have been forced out of him by anger, not
by any judgement of his mind.

Creon.
And was it said out loud — that the prophet was put up to his lies by
me, on my advice?

Chorus.
That was said. What he meant by it, I do not know.

Creon.
And when he laid this charge against me — were his eyes steady? Was
his mind?

Chorus.
I do not know. What the men in power do, I do not see. But here he
comes now, out of the house himself.

(Oedipus comes out of the palace.)

Oedipus.
You. How did you get here? Have you really got the face for it — the
nerve to come to my house, when you are plainly the murderer of the man
who owns it, and the barefaced thief of my throne? Come on, tell me,
in the gods' name: was it cowardice you saw in me, or stupidity, that
made you plan this? Did you think I would not notice this work of yours
creeping up on me in the dark, or that once I had seen it I would not
fight it off? Is the attempt itself not stupid — to go hunting a throne
without a following and without friends, when a throne is a thing you
take with a following and with money?

Creon.
Do you know what you should do? Hear as much back as you have said,
and then judge for yourself, once you know.

Oedipus.
You are a clever talker, and I am a poor learner from you. I have
found you my enemy, and a heavy weight on me.

Creon.
On that very point, hear first how I will put it.

Oedipus.
On that very point, do not tell me that you are not a bad man.

Creon.
If you think stubbornness with no sense in it is something worth
having, you are not thinking straight.

Oedipus.
If you think you can do a kinsman wrong and not answer for it in
justice, you are not thinking well.

Creon.
I agree — that is justly said. Now teach me what this injury is that
you say you have suffered from me.

Oedipus.
Did you or did you not persuade me that I ought to send someone for
that holy prophet?

Creon.
I did. And I am still of the same mind.

Oedipus.
How long ago is it now, then, that Laius —

Creon.
Did what? I do not follow you.

Oedipus.
— vanished from sight, by a deadly stroke?

Creon.
You would be measuring long years, and old ones.

Oedipus.
And was this prophet in his trade back then?

Creon.
As skilled as he is now, and honoured just the same.

Oedipus.
Did he say anything about me, at that time?

Creon.
Never — not while I was standing anywhere near.

Oedipus.
But did you not hold an inquiry into the killer?

Creon.
We did, of course we did. And we heard nothing.

Oedipus.
Then how is it this clever man did not say all this then?

Creon.
I do not know. Where I do not understand, I like to keep my mouth
shut.

Oedipus.
This much you do know, and could say — if you were in your right
mind.

Creon.
What? If I know it, I will not deny it.

Oedipus.
That if he had not put his head together with yours, he would never
have said this killing of Laius was mine.

Creon.
If that is what he says, you know best. But I think it only fair that
I learn from you what you have just learned from me.

Oedipus.
Learn all you like. I will not be caught as a murderer.

Creon.
Well then. You are married to my sister?

Oedipus.
There is no denying what you ask.

Creon.
And you rule this land as she does, sharing it equally?

Oedipus.
Whatever she wants, she gets from me.

Creon.
And am I not the third of you, equal to the two of you?

Oedipus.
That is exactly where you show yourself a false friend.

Creon.
Not if you reasoned with yourself the way I do. Consider this first:
do you think anyone would choose to rule with fear at his back, rather
than sleep untroubled — if he was going to have the same power either
way? I was never born wanting to be king, rather than to do what a
king does; nor was any other man who knows how to keep himself in
hand. As it is, I have everything from you and nothing to fear. If I
ruled myself, there would be a great deal I had to do against my will.
So how could a throne be sweeter to me than power without pain, and
influence? I am not yet so far deceived that I want anything beyond
what is good and profitable too. As it is, everyone greets me;
everyone welcomes me; everyone who wants something from you calls me
out to talk, because their whole chance of getting it lies with me.
How would I give up all this and take the other? A mind that thinks
well would not turn bad. No — I am no lover of that idea, and I could
never bear to act with anyone else who was.

And here is your proof. First, go to Delphi and ask whether I reported
the oracle to you accurately. Then, if you catch me having plotted
anything at all with the seer, take me and kill me — not by one vote
but by two, yours and my own. But do not accuse me on your own, on a
suspicion you cannot prove. It is not just to think bad men good at
random, or good men bad. To throw away a good friend, I tell you, is
the same as throwing away the life in your own body, the thing you
love most. In time you will know all this for certain. Time alone
shows a man to be just. A bad one you can know in a single day.

Chorus.
He has spoken well, my lord, for a man who is careful not to fall.
Those who are quick to judge are not safe.

Oedipus.
When a man plotting in secret moves fast, I have to be fast in
planning against him. If I sit quiet and wait, his business will be
done and mine will have failed.

Creon.
What do you want, then? To throw me out of the country?

Oedipus.
Far from it. I want you dead, not exiled — so that you can show the
world what envy comes to.

Creon.
So you will not give way, and you will not believe me?

Creon.
Because I can see you are not in your right mind.

Oedipus.
In my own interest, I am.

Creon.
You should be in mine just as much.

Oedipus.
But you were born bad.

Creon.
And if you understand nothing?

Oedipus.
I must rule all the same.

Creon.
Not if you rule badly.

Oedipus.
City! Oh, my city!

Creon.
I have a share in the city too. It is not yours alone.

Chorus.
Stop, my lords. I see Jocasta coming out of the house, and in good
time. With her help you must settle this quarrel that stands between
you.

(Jocasta comes out.)

Jocasta.
You poor fools, why have you started up this senseless brawl of
tongues? Are you not ashamed — with the land as sick as this — to be
stirring up troubles of your own? Go into the house, you; and you,
Creon, go to yours. And stop making so much of a grief that is
nothing.

Creon.
Sister, your husband Oedipus thinks it right to do a terrible thing to
me. He has picked one of two evils: either to drive me from my
fathers' land, or to take me and kill me.

Oedipus.
I do. I have caught him, wife, working evil against my person with an
evil craft.

Creon.
May I get no good of my life — may I die under a curse — if I have
done any of the things you charge me with.

Jocasta.
In the gods' name, Oedipus, believe him. Believe this oath before the
gods most of all, and then believe me, and these men standing here
before you.

Chorus.
	Give way. Be willing. Think, my lord. I beg you.

Oedipus.
	What is it you want me to give you?

Chorus.
	Respect a man who was never a fool before,
	and who now is bound by a great oath.

Oedipus.
	Do you know what you are asking?

Chorus.
	I know.

Oedipus.
	Then say what you mean.

Chorus.
	A friend who has put himself under a curse —
	never cast him out in dishonour
	on a charge from a story nobody can prove.

Oedipus.
	Then understand this. When you ask that,
	you are asking for my death, or my exile from this land.

Chorus.
	No — by the Sun, the first of all the gods.
	Godless, friendless, by the worst death there is,
	let me die, if that thought is in me.
	But the land is dying, and it wears my heart out,
	unlucky as I am —
	if now, on the old troubles,
	new troubles are to be piled from the two of you.

Oedipus.
	Then let him go. Even if it means I must die,
	or be driven out of this land by force, dishonoured.
	It is your mouth I pity, not his —
	your pleading. Him, wherever he goes, I will hate.

Creon.
	You are sullen in giving way, that much is plain,
	as you are heavy when your anger runs past the mark.
	Natures like that are hardest, and rightly,
	on themselves.

Oedipus.
	Will you not leave me alone and get out?

Creon.
	I am going.
	You have not known me. To these men I am what I was.

(Creon goes.)

Chorus.
	Lady, why do you wait to take this man inside?

Jocasta.
	When I have learned what happened.

Chorus.
	Blind suspicion came, out of talk.
	And even an unjust word bites.

Jocasta.
	From both of them?

Chorus.
	Yes.

Jocasta.
	And what was the story?

Chorus.
	Enough, for me — enough, with the land already in pain.
	Let it stay where it stopped.

Oedipus.
	Do you see where you have got to, good judge that you are,
	letting my cause go slack and blunting my heart?

Chorus.
	My lord, I have said it more than once.
	Know this: I would be shown a madman,
	lost to all sense, if I turned away from you —
	you who set my beloved country on a straight course
	when it was wandering in trouble,
	and who now, if you can, will be its good pilot again.

Jocasta.
	In the gods' name, my lord, tell me too:
	what is this thing that has set such anger in you?

Oedipus.
I will, wife, for I honour you above these men. It is Creon, and the
plot he has laid against me.

Jocasta.
Tell me, then — if you can say clearly what the quarrel is that you
charge him with.

Oedipus.
He says I am the murderer of Laius.

Jocasta.
On his own knowledge, or on hearsay from somebody else?

Oedipus.
Oh, he sent in a villain of a prophet to say it for him. As for his
own mouth, he keeps that entirely clean.

Jocasta.
Then let go of the things you are talking about. Listen to me, and
take this to heart: no human being has any share in the prophet's
art. I will show you the proof, and briefly.

An oracle came to Laius once — I will not say from Apollo himself, but
from his servants — that his fate would be to die at the hands of a
child born to him and to me. And Laius, so the story goes, was killed
one day by foreign robbers, at a place where three roads meet. As for
the child, three days had not passed since its birth when Laius pinned
its ankles together and had it thrown away, by other hands, on a
mountain where no one goes.

So there Apollo did not bring it about that the child became his
father's killer, or that Laius suffered the terrible thing he was
afraid of and died at his child's hands. That is how the voices of
prophecy had marked out the future. Pay them no attention. Whatever
the god needs, he will easily bring to light himself.

Oedipus.
What a wandering of the soul, wife, what an upheaval of the mind has
taken hold of me, just now, hearing you.

Jocasta.
What worry has turned you round, that you say this?

Oedipus.
I thought I heard you say that Laius was cut down at a place where
three roads meet.

Jocasta.
That was what was said, and the story has never stopped.

Oedipus.
And where is this place, where the thing happened?

Jocasta.
The country is called Phocis. The road forks there, and one branch
comes to that spot from Delphi and the other from Daulia.

Oedipus.
And how much time has gone by since this happened?

Jocasta.
It was announced to the city a little before you appeared and took
power over this land.

Oedipus.
Zeus. What have you decided to do with me?

Jocasta.
What is it, Oedipus? Why does this weigh on your mind?

Oedipus.
Do not ask me yet. Laius — tell me what he looked like, what build he
had, and how far on he was in life.

Jocasta.
He was tall. His hair was just beginning to go white. And in shape he
was not so very unlike you.

Oedipus.
Oh, this is wretched. I think I have just laid myself under terrible
curses, without knowing it.

Jocasta.
What do you mean? I am frightened to look at you, my lord.

Oedipus.
I am terribly afraid the prophet can see. But you will show me better,
if you tell me one thing more.

Jocasta.
I am frightened too. But ask, and what I know I will tell you.

Oedipus.
Was he travelling with a small party, or with a lot of armed men
around him, the way a ruler does?

Jocasta.
There were five of them in all, and one of them was a herald. And one
carriage, carrying Laius.

Oedipus.
Oh — now it is all coming clear. Who was it that told you this story,
wife?

Jocasta.
A servant of the house, the only one who came back alive.

Oedipus.
And does he happen to be in the house now?

Jocasta.
No. When he got back from there and saw you holding the power and
Laius dead, he took my hand and begged me to send him out to the
fields, to the pastures with the flocks, so that he could be as far
out of sight of this city as possible. And I sent him. For a slave, he
deserved that favour and a bigger one.

Oedipus.
Can he be brought back to us quickly?

Jocasta.
He can. But why do you want that?

Oedipus.
I am afraid for myself, wife — afraid I have said far too much. That
is why I want to see him.

Jocasta.
Well, he will come. But I think I have a right, too, to learn what
weighs so heavily on you, my lord.

Oedipus.
You will not be kept from it, now that my fears have come this far.
Who has a better claim than you to hear it, when I am walking through
luck like this?

My father was Polybus of Corinth; my mother was Merope, a Dorian. I
was counted the greatest man in that city until a thing happened to
me — a thing worth wondering at, though not worth the weight I gave
it. At a dinner a man who had drunk too much, in his cups, threw it at
me that I was not my father's true son but a changeling. That day I
held myself in, though it was hard. The next day I went to my mother
and father and questioned them, and they were furious at the man who
had let the insult fly. So on their side I was comforted; and still
the thing kept scratching at me, because it had spread a long way.

So without telling my mother or my father I went to Delphi. And Apollo
sent me away without an answer to what I had come for, but he said
other things, and showed me miseries and terrors and griefs: that I
was to sleep with my mother, and show the world a breed of children
that men could not bear to look at, and be the killer of the father
who made me. When I heard that, I fled the land of Corinth, and from
then on I measured my distance from it by the stars, running for some
place where I would never see the shame of those evil oracles come
true. And on my way I came to this very country, where you say this
king of yours was killed.

Oedipus.
And I will tell you the truth, wife. When I was on the road and close
to this place where the three ways meet, there a herald came towards
me, and a man riding in a carriage drawn by young horses, just as you
describe. And the man in front, and the old man himself, tried to
force me off the road. I was angry, and I struck the one who was
shoving me aside, the driver. And when the old man saw that, he
watched for the moment I was passing the carriage, and brought his
double goad down on the middle of my head.

He did not pay an equal price. He paid more. In one stroke from the
staff in this hand he went over backwards and rolled straight out of
the middle of the carriage. And I killed them. Every one of them.

But if this stranger had any tie of blood with Laius, then who is more
wretched than the man in front of you? What man could the gods hate
more? No stranger and no citizen may take him into their house; no one
may speak to him; everyone must drive him from their doors. And it was
nobody else who laid these curses on me — I did it, myself. And I
defile the dead man's bed with the very hands that killed him. Was I
born bad? Am I not wholly unclean? — if I must go into exile, and in
exile never see my own people or set foot in my own country, or else
be yoked in marriage to my mother and kill my father Polybus, who
fathered me and brought me up. If a man judged that all this came from
some savage power, would he not be right about Oedipus?

No. No — by the holy majesty of the gods, let me never see that day.
Let me go out of the sight of men, vanished, before I see a stain like
that come down on me.

Chorus.
To us, my lord, this is frightening. But until you have learned it all
from the man who was there, keep hoping.

Oedipus.
That is exactly how much hope I have left: to wait for the man, the
herdsman, and nothing else.

Jocasta.
And when he has appeared, what is it you want from him?

Oedipus.
I will tell you. If he is found to say the same as you, then I have
escaped this disaster.

Jocasta.
And what did you hear from me that matters so much?

Oedipus.
You said he told of robbers — that it was men, plural, who killed him.
If he still says the same number, then I did not kill him: one man
cannot be the same as many. But if he speaks of one man travelling
alone, then the deed is plain, and it tips onto me.

Jocasta.
Well, be sure that is how the story came out, and he cannot take it
back again. The city heard it, not I alone. And even if he does turn
aside from what he said before, he can never, my lord, show that the
killing of Laius squares with the prophecy — for Apollo said outright
that he was to die at the hand of a child of mine. And that poor
creature never killed him; it died first itself. So as far as
prophecy goes, from now on I would not look this way for it or that.

Oedipus.
You are right. But all the same, send somebody to fetch the farm hand.
Do not let it drop.

Jocasta.
I will send at once. But let us go into the house. I will do nothing
that is not what you want.

(Oedipus and Jocasta go inside.)

Chorus.
	May it be my portion to carry
	a holy purity in every word and act,
	under the laws that are set on high,
	walking the heights, born through the bright air of heaven,
	whose only father is Olympus.
	No mortal nature of men gave them birth,
	and forgetting will never lay them asleep.
	A great god is in them,
	and he does not grow old.

Chorus.
	Outrage breeds the tyrant.
	Outrage, once it has gorged itself for nothing
	on all that is out of season and does no good,
	climbs to the topmost crag
	and plunges down a sheer drop into necessity,
	where no foot can be of any use.
	But the wrestling that is good for the city —
	I pray the god never to end it.
	The god is my protector, and I will not let him go.

Chorus.
	If a man walks in arrogance, in what he does or says,
	with no fear of Justice, no reverence for the seats of the gods,
	let an evil portion take him
	for his ill-starred pride of living —
	if he will not win his gain justly
	and keep his hands from what is unholy,
	but reaches out, in his folly, for what must not be touched.
	If that goes on, what man will ever again
	pray to keep the gods' arrows off his soul?
	If deeds like these are honoured,
	what is my dancing for?

Chorus.
	I will go no more in reverence to the untouched navel of the earth,
	nor to the temple at Abae, nor to Olympia,
	unless these things are made to fit together
	so that every mortal can point to them.
	But you who rule — if it is right to call you that —
	Zeus, lord of all: let this not slip past you,
	or past your deathless, everlasting power.
	The old prophecies about Laius are fading;
	already men are setting them aside,
	and nowhere is Apollo plain in his honours.
	The things of the gods are going.

(Jocasta comes out, carrying a wreathed branch and incense.)

Jocasta.
Lords of this land, it has come into my mind to visit the temples of
the gods, with these garlands in my hands and this incense. Oedipus is
letting his spirit run too high, on every kind of grief. He does not
judge the new by the old, the way a man in his senses would; he
belongs to whoever is talking, if the talk is of terrors. So, since I
do no good by advising him, I have come to you, Apollo — Lycean
Apollo, whose altar is nearest — as a suppliant with these offerings
of prayer, that you may find us some clean way out of this. For now we
are all afraid, seeing him struck out of his wits, as if he were the
helmsman of our ship.

(A Messenger enters, an old man.)

Messenger.
Could you tell me, strangers, where the house of King Oedipus is? Or
better still, tell me where he is himself, if you know.

Chorus.
This is the house, stranger, and he is inside. And this lady is his
wife, the mother of his children.

Messenger.
Then may she be blessed, and always among the blessed, since she is
his wife in every sense.

Jocasta.
And the same to you, stranger. You deserve it, for your kind words.
But tell me what you have come wanting, and what you wish to say.

Messenger.
Good news, lady, for your house and for your husband.

Jocasta.
What good news? And who has sent you?

Messenger.
I come from Corinth. And the word I am about to say — you will be glad
of it, how could you not be? — but perhaps you will grieve too.

Jocasta.
What is it? What is this double power it has?

Messenger.
The people of the Isthmus are going to set him up as their king. That
is what was being said there.

Jocasta.
What? Is old Polybus no longer in power?

Messenger.
No. Death holds him in his tomb.

Jocasta.
What did you say? Polybus is dead, old man?

Messenger.
If I am not telling the truth, I deserve to die.

Jocasta.
You, girl — go in and tell your master this, quickly.

(A servant goes in.)

Oracles of the gods, where are you now? This is the man Oedipus was
afraid of all these years; this is the man he fled, so as not to kill
him. And now he is dead — by chance, and not by Oedipus.

(Oedipus comes out.)

Oedipus.
Jocasta, dearest wife, why have you sent for me out here from the
house?

Jocasta.
Listen to this man. And as you listen, think where the god's solemn
oracles have got to.

Oedipus.
Who is he, and what has he got to tell me?

Jocasta.
He is from Corinth, with news of your father Polybus: that he is no
more. He is dead.

Oedipus.
What are you saying, stranger? Tell it to me yourself.

Messenger.
If that is what I must make plain first — then know it for certain: he
has gone down to his death.

Oedipus.
By treachery, or by a visit of sickness?

Messenger.
A small tilt of the scale puts an old body to sleep.

Oedipus.
Sickness, then. Poor man — that is what finished him, it seems.

Messenger.
That, and the long years he had lived, measured out in full.

Oedipus.
Ah. Ah, wife — why would anyone look to the prophet's hearth at Delphi
now, or to the birds screaming over our heads, who guided me to this:
that I was going to kill my father? He is dead. He is hidden under the
earth, and here I am, and I never laid a hand on a spear — unless he
died of longing for me. That way, yes, he would have died because of
me. But as it stands, Polybus has gathered up the oracles we had and
taken them with him, and lies in Hades. They are worth nothing.

Jocasta.
Did I not tell you this long ago?

Oedipus.
You did. But I was led astray by my fear.

Jocasta.
Then from now on, do not take any of it to heart.

Oedipus.
And how am I not to fear my mother's bed?

Jocasta.
What should a man be afraid of, when chance rules his life and there
is no clear foresight of anything? Best to live at random, however you
can. And as for marrying your mother — do not be afraid of that. Many
men before now have slept with their mothers, in their dreams. The man
who counts such things as nothing carries his life most easily.

Oedipus.
All that would be well said, if the woman who bore me were not alive.
As it is, since she is alive, I have to be afraid, however well you
argue.

Jocasta.
And yet your father's grave is a great light to see by.

Oedipus.
Great — I know it. But the fear is of the woman who is living.

Messenger.
And who is this woman you are so afraid about?

Oedipus.
Merope, old man. The woman Polybus lived with.

Messenger.
And what is there about her that frightens you?

Oedipus.
A terrible oracle, stranger, sent by the gods.

Messenger.
Can it be spoken? Or is it not lawful for another man to know?

Oedipus.
It can. Apollo said once that I must sleep with my own mother, and
take my father's blood with my own hands. That is why I have kept
Corinth far away from me all these years. Happily enough — and yet
there is nothing sweeter than to look into the eyes of your parents.

Messenger.
And was it really for fear of this that you kept away from the city?

Oedipus.
And because I did not want to be my father's killer, old man.

Messenger.
Then why, my lord, since I came here meaning well, have I not set you
free of this fear?

Oedipus.
Do that, and you would have a reward from me worth having.

Messenger.
And that is mainly why I came — so that when you came home, I would do
well out of it.

Oedipus.
But I will never go where my parents are.

Messenger.
My boy, it is plain as day you do not know what you are doing.

Oedipus.
How, old man? In the gods' name, teach me.

Messenger.
If it is because of all this that you will not go home.

Oedipus.
I am afraid that Apollo will come out true for me.

Messenger.
Afraid of taking some pollution from your parents?

Oedipus.
That very thing, old man. That is what frightens me, always.

Messenger.
Then do you know that you are trembling at nothing, and rightly
nothing?

Oedipus.
How can it be nothing, if I was born the child of those two?

Messenger.
Because Polybus was nothing to you by blood.

Oedipus.
What did you say? Polybus did not father me?

Messenger.
No more than the man talking to you now. Exactly as much.
