Electra
(Part 3 of 3)

Chrysothemis.
Oh, if only you had thought like this on the day our father died. You
would have carried the whole thing off.

Electra.
I was the same by nature. My mind was younger, that is all.

Chrysothemis.
Then work at keeping your mind that young for the rest of your life.

Electra.
You lecture me like somebody who is not going to help.

Chrysothemis.
No. Because the one who puts her hand to a thing like this is likely to
come to grief by it.

Electra.
I envy you your good sense. Your cowardice I loathe.

Chrysothemis.
I will listen just as calmly the day you have something good to say
about me.

Electra.
You will never have to put up with that from me.

Chrysothemis.
There is a long time still to come to decide that in.

Electra.
Go away. There is no help in you.

Chrysothemis.
There is. It is you who cannot be taught.

Electra.
Go and tell your mother all of this.

Chrysothemis.
No. I do not hate you as much as that.

Electra.
Then at least understand what dishonour you are dragging me down into.

Chrysothemis.
Not dishonour. I am thinking ahead for you.

Electra.
So I have to follow your idea of what is right?

Chrysothemis.
Yes. When you have some sense, you can lead the two of us.

Electra.
Terrible, that somebody can talk so well and be so wrong.

Chrysothemis.
You have just described exactly the fault you cling to.

Electra.
What? You do not think justice is on my side in this?

Chrysothemis.
There are times when even justice does harm.

Electra.
I do not want to live by those laws.

Chrysothemis.
Well, if you go through with it, you will end up agreeing with me.

Electra.
I will go through with it. You have not frightened me at all.

Chrysothemis.
Is that really true? You will not think it over again?

Electra.
No. There is nothing I hate more than bad advice.

Chrysothemis.
You seem to take in nothing of what I am saying.

Electra.
I made up my mind about this long ago, not today.

Chrysothemis.
Then I am going. You cannot bring yourself to approve of what I say,
and I cannot approve of the way you behave.

Electra.
Go on inside. I will never come after you, however much you may wish I
would. It is a great foolishness even to go chasing what is empty.

Chrysothemis.
Well, if you think you are being sensible, go on thinking it. When you
are already standing in the wreckage, you will agree with what I said.

(Chrysothemis goes into the house.)

Chorus.
	We look up at the birds,
	the wisest of all creatures,
	and see how they take care to feed
	the ones who gave them life and the good of it.
	Why do we not pay the same debt?
	No. By the lightning of Zeus,
	by Themis who sits in the sky,
	the punishment is not long in coming.
	Voice that goes down under the earth to the dead,
	carry a pitiful cry for me
	to the sons of Atreus below,
	a shame no dancing goes with.

Chorus.
	Tell them that the house is sick now, sick to the root;
	that between the children
	a double quarrel has broken out
	and nothing loving levels it any more.
	Betrayed, Electra rides the storm alone,
	miserable, mourning her father without end,
	like the nightingale that cries for everything.
	She takes no thought for dying.
	She is ready to go into the dark
	if she could only bring down the pair
	who are the Fury of this house.
	Who was ever so much her father's child?

Chorus.
	No one born good is willing
	to live badly and shame a fair name,
	and go into the dark without one, my child, my child.
	So you too chose a life
	of nothing but weeping, shared with the dead,
	and armed yourself against dishonour,
	so as to carry two names in a single breath:
	the clever one, and the best of daughters.

Chorus.
	May I see you live as far above your enemies,
	in strength and in wealth,
	as you now live under their hand.
	Because I have found you walking in no good fortune,
	and yet, of the greatest laws there are,
	you carry off the prize,
	by your reverence for Zeus.

(Orestes comes in with Pylades and two attendants. One of them carries
a small bronze urn.)

Orestes.
Ladies, were we told the way correctly? Is this the right road to where
we want to be?

Chorus.
What are you looking for? What brings you here?

Orestes.
I have been asking for a while where Aegisthus lives.

Chorus.
Then you have found it, and whoever told you did nothing wrong.

Orestes.
Which of you, then, would let the people inside know that we have
arrived — a party they have been waiting for?

Chorus.
She will, if it is the nearest of kin who should announce it.

Orestes.
Then go in, lady, and tell them that some men from Phocis are asking
for Aegisthus.

Electra.
Oh, no. No. You are not bringing plain proof of the story we heard?

Orestes.
I know nothing of your story. Old Strophius sent me with news about
Orestes.

Electra.
What news, stranger? The fear is creeping up on me.

Orestes.
He is dead. We are carrying what little is left of him, in a small
urn, as you see.

Electra.
Oh. Then this is it. It is certain now, it seems, and I am looking at
it: the weight in your hand.

Orestes.
If you are weeping for anything that happened to Orestes, know that
this vessel holds his body.

Electra.
Stranger, if this urn really holds him, then in the gods' name give it
to me, let me take it in my hands, so that I can weep over these ashes
— for myself, and for our whole family with me.

Orestes.
Bring it and give it to her, whoever she is. She is not asking for it
out of any ill will. She is one of his own, or of the same blood.

(The urn is put into Electra's hands.)

Electra.
The one thing left to remember him by — the man I loved best in the
world — all that is left of Orestes. How far this is from the hopes I
had when I sent you away. Now I am lifting you in my hands and you are
nothing; and you were shining, child, when I sent you out of this
house. I wish I had left this life first, before I stole you away with
these two hands and sent you into a strange country and saved you from
being murdered — because then you would have been dead that same day,
and had your share of our father's tomb beside him. Instead, an exile,
far from home, on somebody else's soil, you died miserably, with your
sister nowhere near you. These hands that loved you never washed you or
laid you out; I never lifted your poor weight, as I should have, out of
the fire that burns everything. Strangers tended you, poor boy, and
that is how you come back to us: a little heap in a little jar.

Oh, all the care I gave you long ago, and it was for nothing — the
work I did for you so often, and it was sweet work. You were never your
mother's darling. You were mine. Nobody else in the house nursed you.
I did, and you always called me sister. And now in a single day all of
it is gone, because you are dead. You have gone like a whirlwind and
swept everything away with you. Our father is gone. I am dead, because
of you. You yourself are dead and gone. Our enemies are laughing. And
our mother, who is no mother, is out of her mind with joy — the woman
you used to send me secret messages about, so many times, saying that
you would come yourself to punish her. But the bad luck that is yours
and mine has snatched all of that away, and sent you back to me like
this: instead of the face I loved best, ash and a useless shadow.

Electra.
Oh, oh.
Poor body. Oh, oh.
You were sent on the most terrible road of all, my dearest, and it has
destroyed me. It has destroyed me, brother. So take me in with you,
into this house of yours — me, nothing, into your nothing — and let me
live down there with you from now on. When you were up here I had an
equal share in everything with you. Now I want to die and not be shut
out of your grave. The dead, as far as I can see, are not in pain.

Chorus.
Electra, remember: you are a mortal father's daughter, and Orestes was
mortal. So do not grieve too much. Every one of us has this debt to
pay.

Orestes.
Oh — what am I to say? I am lost. Where do I go for words? I have no
strength left to hold my tongue.

Electra.
What is hurting you? Why do you say that?

Orestes.
Is this the famous Electra? Is this her?

Electra.
This is her. And in a very wretched state.

Orestes.
Then this is a miserable thing to have happened.

Electra.
Stranger, surely it is not me you are groaning over?

Orestes.
A body wasted away — dishonoured, godlessly.

Electra.
Those ugly words fit nobody but me, stranger.

Orestes.
Oh — no husband, and a life as unlucky as this.

Electra.
Stranger, why are you looking at me like that, and groaning?

Orestes.
Because it seems I knew nothing of my own sorrows.

Electra.
What was said that made you see that?

Orestes.
The sight of you, marked out by so much pain.

Electra.
And you are seeing only a little of what I suffer.

Orestes.
How could there be anything uglier to look at than this?

Electra.
Because I live in the same house as the murderers.

Orestes.
Whose murderers? Where does this evil you are pointing at come from?

Electra.
My father's. And on top of that I am their slave, by force.

Orestes.
Who is it that forces this on you?

Electra.
She is called my mother. She is nothing like a mother.

Orestes.
What does she do? Use her hands, or make your life a misery?

Electra.
Her hands, and misery, and every kind of evil.

Orestes.
And there is nobody to help you, nobody to stop it?

Electra.
No. There was one. You have set his ashes in front of me.

Orestes.
Poor girl. I have pitied you for a while now, looking at you.

Electra.
Then you are the only human being who ever has.

Orestes.
Yes, because I am the only one who came here and feels the same pain.

Electra.
You are not — you cannot be some kinsman of ours, from somewhere?

Orestes.
I would tell you, if these women are on your side.

Electra.
They are. You can speak in front of them; they can be trusted.

Orestes.
Then put down that urn, and you will learn everything.

Electra.
No. In the gods' name, stranger, do not do that to me.

Orestes.
Do as I say, and you will not go wrong.

Electra.
No, please, by your beard — do not take from me the dearest thing I
have.

Orestes.
I will not let you keep it.

Electra.
Oh, Orestes — this is misery, if I am robbed even of burying you.

Orestes.
No ill-omened words. You have no right to grieve.

Electra.
No right to grieve for my dead brother?

Orestes.
It is not for you to talk about him that way.

Electra.
Have I so little claim on the dead?

Orestes.
You have every claim. But this is not your business.

Electra.
It is, if this is Orestes' body that I am holding.

Orestes.
It is not Orestes'. Only a story has dressed it up as his.

(He takes the urn out of her hands.)

Electra.
Then where is that poor man's grave?

Orestes.
There is none. The living have no grave.

Electra.
What did you say, boy?

Orestes.
Nothing I am saying is a lie.

Electra.
The man is alive?

Orestes.
If I am breathing.

Electra.
You — are you him?

Orestes.
Look at this. Our father's ring. Then tell me whether I speak the
truth.

Electra.
Oh, light. Oh, dearest light.

Orestes.
Dearest. I will bear witness to that.

Electra.
Your voice — you have come?

Orestes.
Do not ask anyone else about it again.

Electra.
I am holding you? In my arms?

Orestes.
And may you hold me so for the rest of our lives.

Electra.
Dearest women, women of my own city, look — this is Orestes. He was
dead by a trick, and now by a trick he is safe and home.

Chorus.
We see him, child, and at this turn of your fortune a tear of joy is
running out of my eyes.

Electra.
	Oh, child of the man I loved best,
	child of his body, born of him,
	you have come at last,
	you have found, you have reached, you have seen
	the ones you longed for.

Orestes.
	I am here. But keep quiet, and wait.

Electra.
	Why?

Orestes.
	Better to be silent. Someone inside may hear.

Electra.
	No. By Artemis, who is a virgin for ever,
	I will never stoop to fear that —
	the useless weight of women
	shut up in that house all their lives.

Orestes.
	Take care, though. There is a war god in women too.
	You know that well enough, I think. You have felt it.

Electra.
	Oh — oh, oh, oh.
	You have laid it open again,
	the evil that no cloud can cover,
	that can never be undone,
	that can never be forgotten. It is ours.

Orestes.
	I know it too. But when the moment tells us to,
	then is the time to remember what was done.

Electra.
	All of it, all the time there is,
	every hour of it, would be right
	for me to say these things in, and justly.
	It was hard enough to get my mouth free now.

Orestes.
	I agree. So keep it free.

Electra.
	By doing what?

Orestes.
	When it is not the moment, do not want to talk at length.

Electra.
	But who could fairly change
	speech for silence like this,
	when you have appeared?
	I have seen you now,
	past all thought, past all hope.

Orestes.
	You saw me when the gods urged me to come.

Electra.
	Then you have told me of a grace
	higher still than the first,
	if a god brought you to our house.
	I count it the work of heaven.

Orestes.
	I hate to hold back your gladness,
	and I am afraid of too much joy getting the better of you.

Electra.
	Oh — after so long a time
	you thought this road the dearest one to take,
	and came to me. Do not, having seen me in all this pain —

Orestes.
	Do not do what?

Electra.
	Do not rob me of this,
	do not make me give up the joy of your face.

Orestes.
	I would be very angry if I saw anyone else try it.

Electra.
	You agree?

Orestes.
	How could I not?

Electra.
	My friends, I heard a voice
	I could never have hoped to hear,
	and hearing it I could not hold in
	what rose in me — not silent, not without a cry,
	wretch that I am. But now I have you.
	You have come into the light, with the dearest face,
	the face that even in my misery I could never forget.

Orestes.
Leave out everything that does not need saying. Do not teach me how
wicked our mother is, or how Aegisthus drains our father's property out
of this house — pouring some of it away and scattering the rest for
nothing. The story would only eat up the time we have. Tell me what
fits the moment we are in: where we can show ourselves, or stay hidden,
and stop our enemies laughing by this journey of ours. And see to it
that our mother does not read your face, all lit up, when the two of us
have gone into the house. Keep on mourning, for the disaster in the
story that was never true. When we have won, then there will be time to
be glad, and to laugh out loud, free.

Electra.
Brother, whatever pleases you will please me. The joy I have I took
from you; none of it is mine. And I would not accept the smallest hurt
to you to win a great gain for myself. That would be no way to serve
the power that is with us now.

You know how things stand here — how could you not? You will have
heard that Aegisthus is away from the house and our mother is inside.
And you need never be afraid that she will see my face light up with
laughter. My old hatred of her has melted into me, and now that I have
seen you I will never stop weeping — for joy. How could I stop? On one
road, on one day, I have seen you dead and seen you alive. What you
have done to me is past all reckoning. If my father came walking back
to me alive I would no longer call it a miracle; I would believe that
I was seeing him. So, since you have come to us by such a road, take
the lead yourself, as your heart tells you. Left on my own, I would not
have failed at one of two things: I would have saved myself well, or
died well.

Orestes.
Quiet. I hear someone inside, coming towards the door.

Electra.
Go in, strangers — all the more because what you are carrying is a
thing nobody could turn away from this door, and nobody could be glad
to receive.

(The Tutor comes out of the house.)

Tutor.
You utter fools, with no sense left in your heads. Have you stopped
caring whether you live at all, or were you born without any wits, that
you cannot see you are not near the worst danger but right in the
middle of it? If I had not been standing at these doors this long
while, keeping watch, what you are planning would have been inside the
house before you were. As it is, I took care of that myself. Now stop
these long speeches and this shouting for joy that never has enough of
itself, and get inside. In business like this, delay is the danger; the
moment is to have it finished.

Orestes.
Then how do things stand for me inside, when I go in?

Tutor.
Well. It helps that nobody will know you.

Orestes.
You told them I was dead, I take it.

Tutor.
Understand that in this house you are one of the men in Hades.

Orestes.
And are they glad about it? What are they saying?

Tutor.
I will tell you when it is all over. As things stand now, everything on
their side is going well for us — even what is not going well.

Electra.
Who is this man, brother? In the gods' name, tell me.

Orestes.
You do not understand?

Electra.
No. I cannot even guess.

Orestes.
You do not know the man you once put me into the hands of?

Electra.
What man? What are you saying?

Orestes.
The one whose hands carried me out in secret to Phocis, because you
thought ahead.

Electra.
This is him? The one man out of so many I found faithful, when our
father was murdered?

Orestes.
This is him. Do not put me through any more questions.

Electra.
Oh, dearest light. Only saviour of Agamemnon's house — how did you come
here? Are you really the man who saved him, and me, out of so much
suffering? Dearest hands — and feet that ran the sweetest errand — how
could you be beside me so long and not know you, and give no sign, but
kill me with stories while you had the sweetest truth in your keeping?
Welcome, father — because it is a father I seem to be looking at.
Welcome. And know this: in one day I have hated you and loved you more
than any man alive.

Tutor.
That is enough, I think. As for the story of everything in between,
there are many nights circling round and as many days, and they will
make it all clear to you, Electra. But I say this to you two, standing
there: now is the moment to act. Now Clytemnestra is alone. Now there
is not a man inside. If you hold back, think what you will be fighting
then — the people in there, and others cleverer than they are, and more
of them.

Orestes.
Pylades, this work no longer needs any long words from us. It needs us
inside, as fast as we can go — after we have bowed to the seats of my
father's gods, all of them that live at these gates.

(Orestes and Pylades go into the house, and the Tutor after them.
Electra stays outside.)

Electra.
Lord Apollo, hear them kindly, and hear me with them — I who have stood
before you so often, with my hands full of what I could find to give
you. Now, Apollo of the wolves, with what I have, I ask you, I fall
before you, I beg you: be our willing helper in these plans, and show
mankind what the gods give back to people for their irreverence.

(She goes into the house.)

Chorus.
	Look where the war god is stalking forward,
	breathing the blood that no one can fight.
	They have just gone under the roof of the house,
	the hounds no one escapes,
	hunting down the crimes and the wickedness.
	So the dream in my mind
	will not hang there in the air much longer.

Chorus.
	The champion of the dead
	is led inside on stealthy feet,
	into the old rich seat of his father,
	with new-whetted death in his hands.
	And Hermes, the son of Maia,
	who hid the trick in darkness,
	leads him straight to the finishing line,
	and waits no longer.

(Electra comes back out of the house.)

Electra.
	Dearest women, the men are about to finish
	the work now. Wait, and keep silent.

Chorus.
	How? What are they doing now?

Electra.
	She is dressing the urn for burial.
	The two of them are standing close beside her.

Chorus.
	And why did you rush out?

Electra.
	To keep watch. So Aegisthus
	does not get in past us without our seeing.

Clytemnestra.
	Aiai. Oh, house —
	empty of friends, and full of the ones who kill.

Electra.
	Somebody is shouting inside. Can you not hear it, friends?

Chorus.
	I heard — what should never be heard.
	It makes me shudder.

Clytemnestra.
	Oh, no. Aegisthus, where are you? Where?

Electra.
	Listen. Someone is crying out again.

Clytemnestra.
	My son, my son — pity the one who bore you.

Electra.
	But you had none for him,
	or for the father who fathered him.

Chorus.
	City, wretched family, now
	the fate that held you day by day
	is dying, dying.

Clytemnestra.
	Oh — I am struck.

Electra.
	Strike her again, if you have the strength.

Clytemnestra.
	Oh — again.

Electra.
	If only Aegisthus were under it too.

Chorus.
	The curses are being carried out.
	The ones who lie under the ground are alive.
	The blood is flowing back the other way:
	the men killed long ago
	are draining it out of their killers.

(Orestes and Pylades come out of the house.)

Chorus.
	And here they are. The red hand
	drips with the war god's sacrifice,
	and I cannot find it in me to blame.

Electra.
	Orestes, how is it with you?

Orestes.
	Inside the house it is well —
	if Apollo's oracle spoke well.

Electra.
	Is the wretched woman dead?

Orestes.
	Never be afraid again
	that your mother's pride will trample you.

Chorus.
	Stop. I can see Aegisthus, out in the open.

Electra.
	Boys — back inside, now.

Orestes.
	Where do you see the man?

Electra.
	There, coming up from the edge of town.
	He is ours. He is full of joy.

Chorus.
	Go, as fast as you can, to the doorway,
	so that having done the first thing well
	you do this one well again.

Orestes.
	Do not worry. We will finish it.

Electra.
	Then hurry, wherever you mean to go.

Orestes.
	I am gone.

Electra.
	What happens here I will see to.

(Orestes and Pylades go back into the house.)

Chorus.
	It would be as well to say a few words
	into this man's ear, softly,
	so that he rushes blind
	into the trial where justice is waiting.

(Aegisthus comes in.)

Aegisthus.
Which of you knows where those strangers from Phocis are? I am told
they have brought us news that Orestes lost his life in a wreck of
chariots. You — yes, you, I am asking you — the one who used to be so
bold. I should think it concerns you most, and you know most about it,
and can tell me best.

Electra.
I know. How could I not? Otherwise I would be a stranger to what
happened to the nearest of my own.

Aegisthus.
Then where might the strangers be? Tell me.

Electra.
Inside. They have found their way to the heart of the lady of the
house.

Aegisthus.
And did they report him dead — really dead?

Electra.
Not just reported it. They have shown him.

Aegisthus.
Then I can see him for myself, in plain view?

Electra.
You can. It is not a sight anybody would envy.

Aegisthus.
You have given me a great deal of joy — which is not like you.

Electra.
Enjoy it, if there is anything in this for you to enjoy.

Aegisthus.
Silence, I say. And open the gates wide, for every man of Mycenae and
Argos to see — so that if any of them was buoyed up before on empty
hopes of this man, he can look at the corpse now, and take my bit in
his mouth, instead of waiting until I punish him and beat sense into
him by force.

Electra.
It is being done, on my side. Time has taught me sense: to go along
with the stronger.

(The doors are opened. A body lies under a covering. Orestes and
Pylades stand beside it.)

Aegisthus.
Zeus — what I see is a thing that could not have fallen without the
gods' envy. If there is a nemesis in saying so, I unsay it.

(He turns to Orestes and speaks to him.)

Take all the covering off the face, so that his kinsman, at least, can
give him the mourning he is owed from me as well.

Orestes.
Lift it yourself. It is not my place but yours to look at this, and to
greet it with love.

Aegisthus.
Good advice. I will take it.

(He turns to Electra and speaks to her.)

You — call Clytemnestra for me, if she is anywhere in the house.

Orestes.
She is close by you. Do not look anywhere else.

(He lifts the covering.)

Aegisthus.
Oh — what am I looking at?

Orestes.
Who are you afraid of? Who is it you do not know?

Aegisthus.
Whose nets have I walked into, wretch that I am? Who are these men?

Orestes.
Have you not noticed yet that for some time now you have been talking
to the living as if they were dead?

Aegisthus.
Oh — I understand you. There is no way that this can be anyone but
Orestes speaking to me.

Orestes.
And such a fine prophet, and wrong for so long.

Aegisthus.
Then I am finished, wretch that I am. But let me say a little. Only a
little.

Electra.
In the gods' name, brother, do not let him say another word, do not let
him spin out speeches. When a man is tangled in trouble and is going to
die, what does he gain by a little more time? Kill him as fast as you
can, and when you have killed him, throw him out to the buriers he
deserves — the birds and the dogs — far out of our sight. That is the
only thing that could ever set me free from all these years of evil.

Orestes.
Get inside, quickly. This is not a contest of words now. It is your
life.

Aegisthus.
Why take me into the house? If what you are doing is right, why does it
need the dark? Why is your hand not ready to strike now?

Orestes.
Do not give orders. Go to the place where you killed my father, so that
you die on the same spot.

Aegisthus.
Must this house see everything, then — all the evils of the sons of
Pelops, now and still to come?

Orestes.
Yours, at any rate. In that I am a prophet of the first rank.

Aegisthus.
The skill you are boasting of was not your father's.

Orestes.
You answer back and back, and the walk gets no shorter. Move.

Aegisthus.
You lead the way.

Orestes.
You go first.

Aegisthus.
In case I escape you?

Orestes.
No. In case you die the way you would like. I have to make sure this is
bitter for you. This justice ought to come at once to everyone who
wants to go beyond the laws: death. Then there would not be so much
wickedness.

(He drives Aegisthus into the house. Electra follows.)

Chorus.
	Seed of Atreus, after so much suffering,
	you have come out at last, into freedom,
	made whole by what was done today.
