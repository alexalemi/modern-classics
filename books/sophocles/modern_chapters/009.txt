The Women of Trachis
(Part 1 of 3)

(Trachis, in front of the house where Deianeira and her children live
as guests. Deianeira comes out alone.)

Deianeira.
There is an old saying among people: you cannot know whether a
person's life has been good or bad until they are dead. But I know
mine, before I ever go down to Hades. It is unlucky, and it is heavy.

I was still living in my father Oeneus's house at Pleuron when I came
to dread marriage worse than any girl in Aetolia. Because my suitor
was a river — Achelous, I mean — and he kept coming to my father to
ask for me in three shapes. Sometimes he came as a bull, plain to see;
sometimes as a snake, glittering and coiled; sometimes with the body
of a man and the face of an ox, with springs of fresh water spraying
out of his thick, shadowy beard. With a suitor like that waiting for
me, I was wretched, and I prayed all the time to die before I ever
came near that bed.

Then, late, but to my joy, the famous son of Zeus and Alcmena came,
and went into a fight with him for me, and set me free. How the fight
went I could not tell you. I do not know. Anyone who sat and watched
it without shaking could describe it; I sat there stunned with fear
that my beauty was going to find me pain in the end. But Zeus, who
presides over contests, made it come out well — if it was well.
Because since I was joined to Heracles as his chosen bride, I have
been feeding one fear after another, always worrying over him. One
night brings a trouble in, and the next night takes it away and hands
on a new one.

We had children, and he saw them the way a farmer sees a field he has
taken on somewhere far off: once at sowing, and once at harvest. That
was the kind of life that kept sending the man out of the house and
back to it, in service to one master or another.

And now, when he has come through those labours, now is when I am
most afraid. Ever since he killed the strong man Iphitus, we have been
living here in Trachis, uprooted, in the house of a foreign host; and
where he has gone, nobody knows. All I know is that he went away and
left me a sharp pain on his account. I am nearly certain some harm has
come to him. It is not a short time — it is ten months now, and five
more on top of that, and no word. And there is some terrible trouble
in it: he left me a tablet when he went, and I keep praying to the
gods that I did not take it to my sorrow.

(An old woman, a slave of the house, has come out and heard her.)

Servant.
Deianeira, my mistress, I have watched you many times already, crying
your eyes out and mourning Heracles going away. But now — if it is
right for a slave's judgement to put a free woman straight, then I
have to say what is best for you. How is it that you have so many
sons, and you do not send one of them to look for your husband?
Hyllus above all: he is the obvious one, if he cares at all whether
his father is thought to be doing well. And here he is, coming up to
the house at a run, on his own two feet. So if you think what I say
is timely, you have the man and my advice both, ready to use.

(Hyllus arrives.)

Deianeira.
My child, my son — so good words can fall from people with no birth
to speak of. This woman is a slave, but what she has just said is a
free woman's speech.

Hyllus.
What did she say? Tell me, mother, if it is something I can be told.

Deianeira.
That it is a disgrace to you: your father gone so long, out of the
country, and you have not tried to find out where he is.

Hyllus.
But I know where he is, if you can trust what people say.

Deianeira.
And where in the world, my child, do they say he has settled?

Hyllus.
All last year, they say, through the whole length of it, he was
working as a slave for a Lydian woman.

Deianeira.
If he put up with that, then you could believe anything.

Hyllus.
But he is free of that now, from what I hear.

Deianeira.
Then where do they report him now? Alive, or dead?

Hyllus.
They say he is making war on Euboea, on the city of Eurytus — or is
about to.

Deianeira.
Do you know, my son, that he left me oracles I can trust about that
very country?

Hyllus.
What oracles, mother? I do not know what you mean.

Deianeira.
That either he will meet the end of his life there, or, if he takes
on that contest and comes through it, he will live happily for all
the rest of his days. So when he stands on the edge like that, my
child, will you not go and help him? Because either we are saved, if
he saves his life, or we fall with your father when he is destroyed,
and are lost together.

Hyllus.
I will go, mother. If I had known what these prophecies said, I would
have been at his side long ago. As it was, my father's usual luck did
not let me be frightened for him ahead of time, or worry too much.
Now that I understand, I will do everything, and leave nothing out,
to learn the whole truth about this.

Deianeira.
Then go, my son. Good news is worth having even for someone who hears
it late, once he has heard it.

(Hyllus goes. The Servant goes back into the house. The Chorus comes
in: young women of Trachis.)

Chorus.
	You whom the shimmering Night brings to birth
	as she is stripped and put to death,
	and lays to sleep again when you are all on fire —
	Sun, Sun, I ask you:
	tell me this. Where is Alcmena's son,
	where, where is he living now,
	you who blaze with the bright lightning?
	Is he down some channel of the sea,
	or leaning on one of the two continents?
	Tell me, you whose eye is stronger than any.

Chorus.
	Because I hear that Deianeira,
	the bride two suitors fought over,
	is always longing in her heart
	like some poor bird,
	and never puts the ache in her eyes to sleep
	long enough for them to be dry.
	She feeds a fear that will not let her forget
	her husband's road,
	and wears herself out on a bed
	that has a wife in it and no man,
	expecting, in her misery,
	a bad end.

Chorus.
	You could watch the waves go by
	on the wide sea, wave after wave,
	coming on and passing under
	the south wind that never tires, or the north;
	and that is how the trouble of his life,
	heavy as the Cretan sea,
	turns the Theban-born man over
	and lifts him up again.
	But some god keeps him always
	from going wrong, and away
	from the house of Hades.

Chorus.
	I blame you for this, with respect,
	but I will say the other side.
	I say you must not wear away
	the good hope in you.
	The king who accomplishes everything,
	the son of Cronus,
	has not laid on mortals a life without pain.
	Sorrow and joy come round to everyone,
	like the wheeling paths of the Bear.

Chorus.
	Nothing stays. Not the shimmering night
	for mortals, not the powers of death,
	not wealth. In a moment it is gone,
	and it comes to the next one in turn
	to be glad, and then to lose.
	So I tell you, our queen, to hold on
	to this in your hopes, always.
	Because who has ever seen Zeus
	so careless of his children?

Deianeira.
You have heard about my trouble, I suppose, and that is why you are
here. But how I am eating my heart out — may you never learn that by
suffering it. You know nothing of it now. A young thing grows in its
own sheltered places, and the god's heat does not shake it, nor rain,
nor any wind; it lifts its life up in pleasure, with no labour, until
the day a girl is called a wife instead of a girl, and takes on her
share of thinking in the night, afraid for a husband or for children.
Then, looking at her own situation, a woman might see the troubles
that weigh me down.

I have wept over a great many things. But there is one like nothing
before it, and I will tell you now. When my lord Heracles set out from
home on this last journey of his, he left an old tablet in the house,
with signs written on it that he had never before brought himself to
explain to me, though he had gone out to many contests. He always went
as a man going to do something, not a man going to die. This time, as
if he were already no longer alive, he told me what I was to take as
my widow's portion, and what share of their father's land he wanted
divided among the children. And he fixed a time for it: when he had
been out of the country a year and three months, then either he would
have to die in that time, or he would get past the end of it and live
the rest of his life untroubled. That, he said, was what the gods had
settled as the end of the labours of Heracles — the old oak at Dodona
had said so once, through the two doves who speak for it. And the
truth of it is falling due now, at this present time, just as it has
to be fulfilled. So I leap up out of sweet sleep in terror, my
friends, afraid that I will be left behind, widowed of the best man of
all.

Chorus.
No more of that now. Keep quiet — I see a man coming, with a garland
on his head as though he had good news.

(A man of Trachis arrives, garlanded.)

Messenger.
Deianeira, my mistress, I will be the first messenger to set you free
of your fear. Know this: Alcmena's son is alive, and he has won, and
he is bringing the first-fruits of the battle to the gods of this
country.

Deianeira.
What is this you are telling me, old man?

Messenger.
That soon your much-envied husband will come to your house, showing
himself in the strength of his victory.

Deianeira.
And who did you learn this from — a townsman, or a stranger?

Messenger.
Out in the meadow where the oxen graze in summer, Lichas the herald is
telling it to a crowd. I heard him and I ran off, so that I might be
the first to bring it to you, and get something out of it from you,
and win your thanks.

Deianeira.
And why is he not here himself, if he has done so well?

Messenger.
He is not finding it easy to move, lady. The whole people of Malis are
standing round him in a ring asking questions, and he cannot get a
step further. Every one of them wants to learn what he is longing to
hear, and will not let the man go until he has heard it to his
satisfaction. So there he is, not by his choice but by theirs. But you
will see him yourself, any moment.

Deianeira.
Zeus, who holds the meadow on Oeta where no scythe is allowed — you
have given us joy at last, late as it is. Raise your voices, women,
those of you inside the house and those outside the gate. This message
has risen on me like a light I never hoped to see, and we are
gathering the fruit of it now.

Chorus.
	Let the house give the cry,
	the girl who is soon to be a bride,
	with shouts of triumph at the hearth.
	And with her let the men's voice go up together,
	a shout for Apollo of the fine quiver,
	our protector.
	And at the same time, girls,
	raise the paean, the paean —
	cry out to his sister,
	Artemis of Ortygia, the shooter of deer,
	with a torch in each hand,
	and to the Nymphs, her neighbours.
	I am lifted up. I will not push away the flute —
	you master of my mind.
	Look, the ivy is whirling me,
	euoi! —
	turning me round now
	in the race of Bacchus.
	Oh, oh, Paean!
	Look, dear lady:
	this is coming straight at you,
	and you can see it, plain.

Deianeira.
	I see it, my friends. My eyes were keeping watch
	and they have not missed this procession.
	Welcome to the herald, I say, appearing after so long —
	if what you bring is anything to be glad of.

(Lichas the herald comes in with a group of captive women. One of them
is young, and says nothing.)

Lichas.
	We are glad to be back, and glad of your greeting,
	lady, and it matches what we have done.
	When a man has done well
	he is bound to earn good words.

Deianeira.
	Dearest of men, first tell me the first thing I want:
	will I have Heracles back alive?

Lichas.
	I left him alive and strong,
	in full health, and no sickness on him.

Deianeira.
	Where? On his own soil, or in a foreign country? Tell me.

Lichas.
	There is a headland on Euboea, where he is marking out
	altars and the fruits of the ground for Zeus of Cenaeum.

Deianeira.
	To pay a vow, or because some oracle told him to?

Lichas.
	For a vow, made when he was taking by the spear
	the country of these women you see in front of you.

Deianeira.
	And these — in the gods' name, whose are they, and who are they?
	They are to be pitied, unless their trouble deceives me.

Lichas.
	When he sacked the city of Eurytus, he picked these out
	as a prize, for himself and for the gods.

Deianeira.
	And was it that city that kept him away
	all that time beyond reckoning, more days than I can count?

Lichas.
	No. Most of that time he was held in Lydia —
	not a free man, as he says himself, but sold.
	And there should be no resentment at the story, lady,
	when it turns out that Zeus was behind it.
	He was sold to Omphale, the foreign woman,
	and served out a full year, as he tells it himself.
	And he was so stung by the disgrace of it
	that he swore an oath, and bound himself with it,
	that one day he would make a slave of the man
	who had brought that suffering on him — the man, his wife and his child.
	He did not say it for nothing. Once he had been purified,
	he raised an army of hired men and went against
	the city of Eurytus. That man alone among mortals, he said,
	was to blame for what he had suffered.
	Heracles had come to his house and his hearth
	as a guest of long standing, and Eurytus
	shouted at him again and again, with a mind bent on ruin,
	saying that he had arrows in his hands that never miss,
	and still fell short of Eurytus's own sons in a contest of the bow;
	and that he was a slave, and a free man's slave, and broken;
	and at dinner, when his guest was full of wine,
	he threw him out of the house. Heracles was angry at that,

Lichas.
	and when Iphitus came afterwards to the hill of Tiryns,
	tracking horses that had strayed,
	he caught him with his eyes on one thing and his mind on another
	and hurled him from the top of the tower-high rock.
	For that deed the king, Olympian Zeus, father of all,
	was furious, and sent him away to be sold.
	He would not stand for it, because this once, and only this once,
	Heracles had killed a man by treachery.
	If he had taken his revenge in the open,
	Zeus would have forgiven him, since he would have won it with justice.
	The gods have no love for outrage either.
	So those men, who ran wild with their bad tongues,
	are all of them living in Hades now,
	and their city is a slave. And these women you are looking at,
	who have found a life nobody envies after a happy one,
	are coming to you. That was your husband's order,
	and I am his faithful man, and I carry it out.
	As for Heracles himself: once he has made
	clean sacrifice to Zeus of his fathers for taking the city,
	expect him to come. Of all this good news,
	well told and at length, that is the sweetest thing to hear.

Chorus.
	Queen, now your joy is plain to see —
	part of it in front of you, the rest in what you have heard.

Deianeira.
	How could I not be glad, with all my heart,
	hearing that my husband has done so well?
	My joy has to run alongside his success.
	And yet a person who looks carefully
	can feel fear for the one who is doing well, in case he stumbles.
	Because a terrible pity came over me, my friends,
	looking at these unlucky women,
	wandering in a strange country, with no home and no father.
	They were the daughters of free men, perhaps, before;
	now they live the life of slaves.
	Zeus, who turns the tide of battle,
	may I never see you come against my children like this —
	or if you are going to, not while I am still alive.
	That is how afraid I am, looking at them.
	You poor girl. Which of these young women are you?
	Are you unmarried, or a mother? By the look of you
	you know nothing of all that yet, but you are somebody well born.
	Lichas, whose is she, this stranger?
	Who is her mother? Who is the father who begot her?
	Tell me. She is the one I pitied most of all when I looked at them,
	because she is the only one who knows how to carry herself.

Lichas.
	How would I know? Why ask me? Maybe
	she is a child of one of the better families out there.

Deianeira.
	Not of the ruling house? Was she some child of Eurytus?

Lichas.
	I do not know. I did not ask many questions.

Deianeira.
	You did not even get her name from one of the others she travelled with?

Lichas.
	No. I did my job in silence.

Deianeira.
	Then tell us yourself, poor girl, out of your own mouth.
	It is a misfortune too, not to know who you are.

Lichas.
	If she opens her lips now it will be nothing like
	the time before. She has not brought out a word,
	big or small. She has been in labour
	with the weight of her misfortune the whole time, weeping,
	poor thing, since she left her windy country.
	Her luck is bad — for her — but it earns her some understanding.

Deianeira.
	Then let her be, and let her go in under the roof
	in whatever way is easiest for her. Let her not take
	another grief from me on top of the ones she has;
	what she has is enough. Now let us all go in,
	so you can hurry off wherever you want to go,
	and I can set the house in order.

(Lichas goes into the house with the captives. The Messenger stops
Deianeira before she can follow.)

Messenger.
	Wait here first, a little, so that you can learn,
	with those people out of the way, who it is you are taking indoors,
	and find out what you need to know and have not heard a word of.
	I know the whole of it.

Deianeira.
	What is it? Why are you stopping me on my way?

Messenger.
	Stand there and listen. The last thing you heard from me
	was not a waste of your time, and I do not think this will be.

Deianeira.
	Should we call them back out here, then,
	or do you want to speak to me and these women?

Messenger.
	To you and these women there is nothing to stop me. Leave the others.

Deianeira.
	Well, they are gone. Let your story say what it means.

Messenger.
	Nothing this man told you just now
	was spoken straight by the rule of justice. Either he is a liar now,
	or he was no honest messenger before.

Deianeira.
	What are you saying? Tell me clearly everything you mean.
	What you have said so far I do not understand.

Messenger.
	I heard this man say, with many witnesses standing by,
	that it was for this girl's sake
	that Heracles brought down Eurytus and Oechalia with its high towers,
	and that Eros, the god of desire, and no other god,
	bewitched him into that fighting —
	not the labours in Lydia, not slaving under Omphale,
	not Iphitus hurled to his death.
	Now he has shoved Eros aside and tells the opposite story.
	When he could not persuade her father
	to give him the girl to keep in his bed in secret,
	he got up some small complaint as a pretext
	and marched on her country — the country where, as this man said,
	Eurytus held the throne — and killed her father the king,
	and sacked her city. And now, as you see, he has come home,
	and sends her to this house not carelessly, lady,
	and not to be a slave. Do not expect that.
	It is not likely, if he is burning with desire.
	So I decided to tell you the whole of what I happened
	to learn from this man, my mistress.
	And many people in the middle of the Trachinian market
	heard it exactly as I did, so they can prove him wrong.
	If what I say is not welcome, I am sorry.
	But it is the truth, and I have told it.

Deianeira.
	Oh no. Oh, I am wretched. Where do I stand now?
	What trouble have I taken in under my roof
	without knowing it? Oh, the misery of it. So she has no name —
	as the man who brought her swore?

Messenger.
	She is very bright, in her name and in her blood.
	She is the daughter of Eurytus by birth, and she was called
	Iole once. And he could tell you nothing of where she came from —
	because, of course, he never asked.

Chorus.
	Damnation — not on all bad men, but on the one
	who practises hidden wrongs that do not become him.

Deianeira.
	What am I to do, women? What I have just heard
	has knocked me stupid.

Chorus.
	Go and question the man. He might say something clear,
	if you were willing to press him hard and force it out of him.

Deianeira.
	I will go. What you say is not without sense.

Messenger.
	And we — should we wait here? Or what do you want done?

Deianeira.
	Stay. Here is the man coming out of the house —
	no messenger of mine fetched him; he comes of his own accord.

(Lichas comes out.)

Lichas.
	Lady, what am I to say to Heracles when I get there?
	Tell me. As you see, I am on my way.

Deianeira.
	You took your time coming, and how you rush off now —
	before we have even taken up the conversation again.

Lichas.
	Well, if there is anything you want to ask, I am here.

Deianeira.
	And will you give me the truth, and honestly?

Lichas.
	Great Zeus be my witness — in anything I know.

Deianeira.
	Then who is the woman you came here bringing?

Lichas.
	She is from Euboea. Who her parents are I cannot say.

Messenger.
	You there. Look at me. Who do you think you are talking to?

Lichas.
	And you — what are you asking me that for?

Messenger.
	Have the nerve to answer what I ask you, if you have any sense.

Lichas.
	To the lady in charge here, Deianeira, daughter of Oeneus,
	wife of Heracles, and — unless my eyes are playing tricks on me —
	my mistress.

Messenger.
	That is exactly what I wanted, exactly what I wanted to hear from you.
	You say this woman is your mistress?

Lichas.
	It is only right.

Messenger.
	Then what? What punishment do you think you deserve
	if you are found to have done her wrong?

Lichas.
	Wrong? What is this you have been weaving?

Messenger.
	Nothing. Though you are doing a great deal of it yourself.
