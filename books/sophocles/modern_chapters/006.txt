Oedipus the King
(Part 1 of 3)

(Before the palace at Thebes. People of every age sit on the altar
steps holding suppliants' branches. Oedipus comes out.)

Oedipus.
My children — the newest growth on old Cadmus' tree — why are you
sitting here in front of me like this, crowded round the altars with
your branches wound in wool? The whole city is full of incense, and
full at the same time of hymns for healing and of groaning. I did not
think it right, children, to hear about it from other people's
messengers, so I have come myself: Oedipus, the man whose name
everybody knows. Now, old man — you speak, since it is your place to
speak for the rest of them. What has you sitting here? Is it something
you are afraid of, or something you want? You can count on me to help
in every way I can. I would be a hard man indeed if I could look at a
gathering like this and feel no pity.

Priest.
Oedipus, ruler of my country, you can see for yourself the ages of
those of us sitting at your altars: some not yet strong enough to fly
far, some heavy with years — priests, I am the priest of Zeus — and
these here, picked from the young men. The rest of the people sit
wreathed in the market-place, at the two temples of Pallas, and beside
the ash of Ismenus, where the fire gives its answers. Because the
city, as you see yourself, is pitching in a heavy sea now, and can no
longer lift her head up out of the trough of the bloody swell. She is
dying in the fruit-buds of her soil; dying in the herds at pasture;
dying in the labour of her women, who bring nothing to birth. And on
top of it all the fire-bearing god has swooped down and is driving the
city before him — the plague, the most hated thing there is. By him
the house of Cadmus is emptied, and black Hades grows rich in groans
and weeping.

It is not that we rank you with the gods, I and these children, that
we are sitting at your hearth. It is that we judge you the first of
men, both in the ordinary chances of life and in dealings with the
powers above. It was you who came to the city of Cadmus and freed us
from the tax we were paying to the cruel singer, the Sphinx — and you
did it knowing no more than the rest of us, with nobody to teach you.
A god was at your side, people say, and believe it; and it was you who
set our lives straight. So now, Oedipus, mightiest of all in our eyes,
every one of us here is begging you: find us some defence. Whether you
have heard a voice from one of the gods or know something from a man —
because I notice that with men of experience, even the outcome of
their plans tends to come out alive. Come, best of mortals, set the
city on its feet again. Come, take care: this land calls you its
saviour now, for the good will you showed us before. Do not let us
remember your reign as the time we were stood up straight and then
fell down. Set the city upright, and make it safe. The luck you
brought us then came under a good omen; be the same man now. If you
are going to rule this land, as you do now, it is better to rule it
with men in it than empty. A wall is nothing, and a ship is nothing,
with no men living inside them together.

Oedipus.
My poor children. I know what you have come wanting; it is no mystery
to me. I know very well that you are all sick — and sick as you are,
there is not one of you as sick as I am. Your pain comes to each of
you singly, to himself and to nobody else; but my heart groans for the
city, and for myself, and for you, all at once. So you are not waking
a man who was asleep. Understand that I have wept, a great deal, and
have gone down many roads in the wanderings of my thought. And the one
cure I could find, looking hard, I have already acted on: I sent
Creon, the son of Menoeceus, my wife's brother, to Apollo's house at
Pytho, to learn what I must do or say to save this city. And now that
I count the days against the time, it worries me what he is doing. He
has been gone longer than is reasonable — longer than the journey
takes. But when he comes, I would be a poor sort of man if I did not
do everything the god makes plain.

Priest.
You have timed that well. These men are signalling to me that Creon
is coming up now.

Oedipus.
Lord Apollo — let him come with some saving luck, as bright as his
face is.

Priest.
To guess from the look of him, it is good news. He would not be coming
with his head wreathed so thick in bay, all in berry.

Oedipus.
We will know in a moment; he is close enough to hear. My lord, my
kinsman, son of Menoeceus — what word from the god have you brought
us?

Creon.
Good. I say that even hard things, if they happen to come out
straight, can all turn out well.

Oedipus.
But what is the word itself? What you have said so far leaves me
neither bold nor frightened.

Creon.
If you want to hear it with these people standing close, I am ready to
speak. Or we can go inside.

Oedipus.
Speak to all of them. I carry the grief for these people more than for
my own life.

Creon.
Then I will tell you what I heard from the god. Lord Apollo commands
us, in plain terms, to drive out a pollution of this country — one
that has been nursed in this soil — and not to go on feeding it until
it cannot be healed.

Oedipus.
Drive it out with what kind of cleansing? What sort of trouble is
this?

Creon.
By banishing a man, or by paying blood back with blood. It is that
blood which is storming the city.

Oedipus.
And what man's fate is he pointing to?

Creon.
We had a leader once in this country, my lord — Laius — before you
took the steering of the city.

Oedipus.
I know it, from hearing. I never saw him.

Creon.
He was killed. And now the god tells us clearly to punish his killers,
whoever they are, by force.

Oedipus.
And where on earth are they? Where is anyone going to find the track
of an old crime, so hard to read?

Creon.
In this country, he said. What is looked for can be caught. What is
neglected gets away.

Oedipus.
Was it at home, or in the fields, or on some other soil that Laius met
this death?

Creon.
He left the country, he told us, to consult the oracle. And once he
had set out he never came home again.

Oedipus.
And no messenger, no companion on the road, saw it — nobody whose
account could have been learned from and used?

Creon.
They died — all but one, who ran away in terror, and of what he saw he
could say for certain only one thing.

Oedipus.
What thing? One thing can lead to the discovery of many, if we could
get hold of even a short beginning for hope.

Creon.
He said robbers fell in with them and killed him — not one man's
strength, but a crowd of hands.

Oedipus.
Then how would a robber have come to that pitch of daring, unless
something was being arranged from here, with silver?

Creon.
That was thought at the time. But with Laius dead, nobody came forward
to help, in the troubles we were in.

Oedipus.
What trouble stood in the way? A throne had fallen. What stopped you
finding out?

Creon.
The Sphinx, with her shifting riddle-song. She made us drop what was
out of sight and look at what was under our feet.

Oedipus.
Then I will go back to the beginning and bring it to light again
myself. Apollo has done right, and you have done right, to take up
this care for the dead man. So you will see me too, and justly,
standing with you as an ally, taking vengeance for this country and
for the god together. Not for some far-off friend's sake — for my own
sake I will scatter this pollution. Whoever killed that man might well
want to take the same hand to me. So in helping him I am helping
myself. Now quickly, children — up from the steps, and take your
suppliants' branches with you. And let somebody gather the people of
Cadmus here, since I mean to do everything there is to do. With the
god's help we will be shown lucky — or fallen.

Priest.
Children, let us stand up. What we came here for, this man has
promised us of his own accord. And may Apollo, who sent these oracles,
come himself — to save us, and to stop the sickness.

(They go. The Chorus, elders of Thebes, comes in.)

Chorus.
	Sweet-spoken word of Zeus,
	what are you, that have come from Pytho, rich in gold,
	to shining Thebes?
	I am stretched on the rack; my frightened heart is shaking.
	Healer of Delos, to whom we cry —
	I stand in awe of you. What will you bring to pass for me?
	Something new, or a debt come round again with the turning seasons?
	Tell me, deathless Voice, child of golden Hope.

Chorus.
	First I call on you, daughter of Zeus, deathless Athena,
	and on your sister, who holds the land,
	Artemis, seated on her famous throne at the circle of the market,
	and on Apollo, who shoots from far away —
	come, you three who turn death aside, show yourselves to me.
	If ever before, when ruin rose up over the city,
	you drove the flame of the plague out of our borders, come now too.

Chorus.
	Oh, the pain — the sorrows I carry have no number.
	The whole people is sick, and thought has no spear
	that anyone can fight it off with.
	Nothing grows from the famous soil,
	and the women do not come up out of the screaming labour of birth.
	One after another you can watch them go,
	like a bird on strong wings,
	faster than fire that nobody can fight,
	to the shore of the god of evening.

Chorus.
	And of those deaths the city dies without number.
	Her children lie on the ground, unpitied, carrying death,
	with no one to weep for them;
	and among them wives, and grey-haired mothers with them,
	at the altar's edge, from here and from there,
	cry out begging for an end to their bitter pains.
	The hymn to the Healer rings out, and with it the sound of groaning.
	For all this, golden daughter of Zeus,
	send us the bright face of your help.

Chorus.
	And the raging god — Ares — who now with no bronze shield
	burns me up, coming at me with a shout —
	I beg him to turn his back and run out of my country
	with a fair wind behind him,
	either into the great bedchamber of Amphitrite
	or into the Thracian surf where there is no harbour.
	Because whatever the night leaves undone
	the day comes and finishes.
	Him — father Zeus, you who hold the power
	of the fire-bearing lightning —
	destroy him under your thunderbolt.

Chorus.
	Lycian lord, Apollo — I would have your arrows too,
	from the gold-twisted string of your bow,
	dealt out unconquerable, set to guard us,
	and the fire-bearing torches of Artemis
	with which she races over the hills of Lycia.
	And I call on the god with the golden headband,
	who carries this land's name,
	wine-faced Bacchus, whom the Bacchants cry to,
	who goes with the Maenads —
	come near with your blazing pine-torch
	against the god who has no honour among the gods.

(Oedipus comes out of the palace.)

Oedipus.
You pray. And what you pray for — if you are willing to hear what I
say and take it in, and do the sickness's own work against it — you
may get: help, and relief from your troubles. I say this as a stranger
to the story, and a stranger to the deed. I could not have tracked it
far on my own, without some clue to go on. But as it is — since I was
made a citizen among citizens only after the event — I proclaim this
to all of you, Cadmus' people:

Whoever among you knows at whose hand Laius the son of Labdacus died,
I order him to tell me everything. And if he is afraid, let him take
the charge out of his own way by laying it against himself. He will
suffer nothing unpleasant beyond this: he will leave the country,
unharmed. If instead someone knows the killer to be another man, from
another land, let him not keep silent. I will pay the reward, and my
thanks will go with it.

But if you keep silent — if any man, out of fear for a friend or for
himself, pushes this order of mine aside — then hear what I will do
about that. I forbid anyone in this land, where I hold the power and
the throne, to take that man in, whoever he is, or to speak to him, or
to let him share in prayers to the gods or in sacrifices, or to give
him a share of the holy water. Drive him from your houses, all of you.
He is our pollution — the Pythian oracle of the god has just shown me
this. That is the kind of ally I am to the god and to the dead man.

And I curse the one who did it, whether he is one man who has kept
hidden or one of several: may he wear out his miserable life, badly,
as the bad man he is. And I pray one thing more. If he should turn out
to be a member of my own household, at my own hearth, with my
knowledge — may I suffer everything I have just called down on them.
All this I charge you to carry out, for my sake, and for the god's,
and for this country's, wasted as it is, fruitless and godless.

Oedipus.
Even if the god had not pushed this matter on us, it was not right to
leave it uncleansed as you did. The best of men had died, and your
king. You should have hunted it down. As it is, I hold the power that
he held before me; I hold his bed, and his wife, who had borne his
children; and if his hope of a family had not failed him, we would
have had children in common, born of one mother. Instead, fate came
down on his head. For all these reasons I will fight for him as I
would for my own father, and I will go to every length to catch the
man whose hand did the murder — for the son of Labdacus, of Polydorus
before him, of Cadmus before him, and of Agenor of old.

And on those who do not do this, I pray the gods send them no harvest
from the earth and no children from their women, but let them die of
the doom that is on us now, or of one worse. As for the rest of you,
Cadmus' people, who are content with this — may Justice, our ally,
and all the gods be with you for ever.

Chorus.
Since you have bound me under your curse, my lord, I will speak on
those terms. I did not kill him, and I cannot show you the man who
did. As for the search — it was for Apollo, who sent the order, to
tell us who did it.

Oedipus.
That is fairly said. But no man alive can force the gods to do what
they do not want to.

Chorus.
Then I will say what seems to me the second-best thing after that.

Oedipus.
If there is a third, do not leave that out either.

Chorus.
Lord Teiresias — I know he sees the same things that Lord Apollo sees.
From him, my king, a man searching into this could learn about it most
clearly.

Oedipus.
That is not something I have let sit idle either. On Creon's advice I
have sent two messengers already. And I have been wondering for a
while why he is not here.

Chorus.
Apart from him, the other talk is faint and old.

Oedipus.
What talk? I am going into every story.

Chorus.
He was said to have been killed by some travellers.

Oedipus.
I heard that too. But nobody sees the one who saw it.

Chorus.
Well, if he has a scrap of fear in him, he will not stand still once
he hears the curses you have laid.

Oedipus.
A man who was not afraid of doing it will not be afraid of a word.

Chorus.
But here is the one who will convict him. They are bringing the
prophet of the god at last — the only man in whom the truth is born.

(Teiresias is led in by a boy.)

Oedipus.
Teiresias, you who take everything in — what can be taught and what
cannot be spoken, what is in heaven and what walks on the earth — you
cannot see, but you understand all the same what sickness the city
lives with. And against it, lord, we find you our one protector and
saviour. Apollo — in case you have not already heard it from the
messengers — sent back this answer to our question: the only release
from this sickness would come if we found out for certain who killed
Laius, and killed them, or sent them out of the land in exile. So do
not grudge us anything — a voice from the birds, or any other road of
prophecy you have. Save yourself, save the city, save me, save
everything the dead man's pollution has touched. We are in your hands.
And there is no finer work for a man than to help others with what he
has and what he can do.

Teiresias.
Ah. How terrible it is to understand, when understanding does the man
who has it no good. I knew that well, and I let it slip — or I would
not have come here.

Oedipus.
What is it? You have come in with no heart at all.

Teiresias.
Let me go home. You will bear what is yours most easily, and I what is
mine, if you take my advice.

Oedipus.
That is no lawful thing you are saying, and no loving one, towards the
city that reared you — to refuse her this answer.

Teiresias.
Because I see that your words are not going where they should either.
So, in case the same thing happens to me —

Oedipus.
In the gods' name, if you know something, do not turn away. All of us
here are bowing to you, begging.

Teiresias.
Because none of you understands. I will never bring out what is mine —
I will not call it yours — my troubles.

Oedipus.
What are you saying? You know, and you will not tell? You mean to
betray us and destroy the city?

Teiresias.
I will not hurt myself, and I will not hurt you. Why cross-examine me
for nothing? You will not get it from me.

Oedipus.
Nothing? You worst of the worst — you would put a stone into a rage —
will you never speak out? Are you going to stand there, unmoved, and
never come to the end of it?

Teiresias.
You find fault with my temper. You have not looked at your own, living
in the same house with you. You blame me instead.

Oedipus.
Who would not lose his temper, hearing words like that — the way you
are treating this city, with contempt?

Teiresias.
It will come of itself, whether I cover it in silence or not.

Oedipus.
Then what is going to come, you must tell me.

Teiresias.
I will say nothing more. Rage at that, if you like, with the wildest
anger you have in you.

Oedipus.
I will, and I will leave nothing out, angry as I am, of what I have
understood. Know this: it looks to me as if you helped to plant this
deed, and did it — everything short of killing with your own hands. If
you happened to have eyes, I would say the deed was yours alone.

Teiresias.
Would you. Then I tell you: stand by the proclamation you made. From
this day forward do not speak to these men, or to me. You are the
unholy polluter of this land.

Oedipus.
You have the gall to fling that out? And where do you think you are
going to get away from it?

Teiresias.
I have got away already. The truth I carry is strong.

Oedipus.
Taught by whom? Not by your art, at any rate.

Teiresias.
By you. You pushed me into saying it, against my will.

Oedipus.
Saying what? Say it again, so I can learn it better.

Teiresias.
Did you not understand it the first time? Or are you testing me,
making me talk?

Oedipus.
Not so well that I could call it known. Say it again.

Teiresias.
I say you are the murderer of the man whose murderer you are looking
for.

Oedipus.
You will not be glad you said that twice — that piece of misery.

Teiresias.
Shall I tell you something else, then, to make you angrier?

Oedipus.
As much as you want. It will be said for nothing.

Teiresias.
I say you have been living, without knowing it, in the most shameful
closeness with those nearest to you — and you do not see how deep in
evil you stand.

Oedipus.
And you think you can go on saying things like that and enjoy it?

Teiresias.
Yes — if there is any strength in the truth.

Oedipus.
There is. But not for you. You have none of it, because you are blind
— in your ears, in your mind, in your eyes.

Teiresias.
And you are a wretched man, throwing those insults — which every man
here will soon be throwing at you.

Oedipus.
You are fed by one long night. You could never hurt me, or any man who
sees the light.

Teiresias.
No. It is not your fate to fall by me. Apollo is enough for that, and
it is his business to bring it about.

Oedipus.
Is this Creon's invention, or yours?

Teiresias.
Creon is no harm to you. You are your own harm.

Oedipus.
Wealth, and kingship, and the skill that outdoes every other skill in
this life that everybody envies — how much jealousy you keep stored up
against yourselves, if for the sake of this power, which the city put
into my hands as a gift — I never asked for it — Creon the faithful,
Creon my friend from the beginning, has crept up on me in secret and
wants to throw me out, and has put up this scheming wizard, this
tricky beggar-priest, who has sharp eyes for profit only and was born
blind in his art.

Come, tell me: where have you ever shown yourself a true prophet? When
the singing bitch was here, why did you say nothing to set these
citizens free? And that was a riddle not for the first man who came
along to read; it needed prophecy. And you were shown to have none —
nothing from the birds, nothing learned from any god. I came along
instead, Oedipus who knew nothing, and I stopped her. I hit the answer
by my own wits, with nothing learned from birds. And this is the man
you are trying to throw out, hoping to stand close beside Creon's
throne. I think you will be sorry, you and the man who put this
together, that you tried to purge the land. If you did not look like
an old man, you would have learned by suffering what your ideas were
worth.

Chorus.
To our way of judging, Oedipus, his words were spoken in anger, and so
were yours. And we do not need that. What we need is to think how best
we can carry out the god's oracles.

Teiresias.
You may be king. But the right to answer, at least, has to be held
equal, word for word — I am master of that much. I do not live as your
slave. I live as Apollo's. So I will not be put on the roll as Creon's
client. And since you have thrown my blindness in my face, I tell you
this: you have your eyes and you do not see the evil you stand in —
not where you live, not whom you live with. Do you know where you come
from? You have been, without knowing it, the enemy of your own people,
under the earth and on it. And the curse of your mother and your
father — a double lash, a terrible-footed thing — will one day drive
you out of this land, seeing straight now, and after that seeing
darkness. Where will there be a harbour for your cries? What corner of
Cithaeron will not echo them, soon, when you understand the marriage
you sailed into in that house — no harbour there — after such a fair
voyage? And there is a crowd of other evils you have no sense of, that
will bring you level with what you really are, and level with your own
children. Now — spit on Creon, and on this mouth of mine. There is no
mortal alive who will be ground down more miserably than you.

Oedipus.
Am I to stand here and listen to that from him? Damn you — out, and
quickly. Turn round and get away from this house, back where you came
from.

Teiresias.
I would not have come if you had not called me.

Oedipus.
I did not know you would talk like a fool. Otherwise I would have
taken my time about summoning you to my house.

Teiresias.
That is what I am — a fool, as you think. But to the parents who bred
you, I had sense.

Oedipus.
What parents? Wait. Who among mortals bred me?

Teiresias.
This day will breed you, and destroy you.

Oedipus.
Everything you say is riddles and darkness.

Teiresias.
And are you not the best man born at solving them?

Oedipus.
Go on, throw that at me. It is where you will find I am great.

Teiresias.
And yet that is the luck that has destroyed you.

Oedipus.
If I saved this city, I do not care.

Teiresias.
Then I am going. Boy — take me away.

Oedipus.
Yes, let him take you. Standing here you are in the way; you are a
nuisance. Once you are gone you cannot hurt me any more.

Teiresias.
I am going — after I have said what I came to say. I am not afraid of
your face. There is no way you can destroy me. I tell you this: the
man you have been looking for all this time, with your threats and
your proclamations about the murder of Laius — that man is here. A
stranger, supposedly, living among us; but he will be found a Theban,
born here, and he will take no joy in the discovery. Blind, who now
sees; a beggar, who now is rich; he will go into a foreign land
feeling the ground in front of him with a stick. And he will be found
to be, to his own children, both brother and father; to the woman he
came from, both son and husband; and to his father, the sharer of one
wife, and the killer. Go inside and work that out. And if you catch me
out in a lie, then say I know nothing about prophecy.

(The boy leads him away. Oedipus goes into the palace.)

Chorus.
	Who is he? The god-speaking rock of Delphi
	has spoken of a man who has done, with bloody hands,
	unspeakable things past all speaking.
	Time for him to run —
	to set his feet moving faster than storm-horses.
	Because the son of Zeus is leaping on him, armed
	with fire and lightning,
	and behind him come the terrible ones,
	the death-spirits that never miss.

Chorus.
	It has flashed out, just now, from snowy Parnassus:
	the voice that says track down the unknown man, everyone.
	He is wandering under the wild wood,
	through the caves and over the rocks,
	like a bull, wretched, on wretched feet, alone,
	trying to get clear of the oracles
	from the navel of the earth.
	And they are alive for ever, and they fly round him.

Chorus.
	Terrible — the clever reader of birds
	has stirred up terrible things in me,
	and I can neither believe him nor deny him. I do not know what to say.
	I hover on hopes, and see nothing clearly, here or behind me.
	What quarrel there was between the house of Labdacus
	and the son of Polybus, I never learned, then or now —
	nothing I could use as a test
	to go against the public name of Oedipus
	and take the side of Labdacus' house in unsolved deaths.

Chorus.
	Zeus and Apollo understand; they know what men do.
	But that a human prophet sees more than I do —
	there is no true way to judge that.
	One man may pass another in skill.
	But never — not until I saw the words come true —
	would I side with the men who blame him.
	Because the winged girl came at him once, in plain sight,
	and he was seen to be wise, and passed the test,
	and the city loved him.
	So in my heart he will never be found guilty of evil.

(Creon comes in.)

Creon.
Men of this city — I have heard that Oedipus the king is making
terrible charges against me, and I am here because I will not put up
with it. If, in the trouble we are in now, he thinks he has suffered
anything at my hands, in word or in action, that was meant to harm
him, then I have no wish for a long life, carrying a name like that.
The damage this story does me is not a small thing. It is the biggest
there is — if I am to be called a bad man in the city, and a bad man
by you and by my friends.
