Electra
(Part 1 of 3)

(Mycenae. Before the palace of the house of Pelops, at first light.
The Tutor, an old man, comes in with Orestes and Pylades.)

Tutor.
Son of Agamemnon, who once commanded the army at Troy — now you can
look with your own eyes at what you have always longed for. This is
the ancient Argos you were homesick for, the sacred ground of Io,
Inachus' daughter, whom the gadfly stung and drove across the world.
And this, Orestes, is the Lycean marketplace, named for Apollo the
wolf-killer. There on the left is Hera's famous temple. And the place
we have come to — call it Mycenae, rich in gold, that you are looking
at, and this the house of Pelops' line, where so many have been
killed. It was from here, when your father was murdered, that I took
you from your sister, your own blood, and carried you off and saved
you and raised you to the age you are now, to avenge your father's
murder.

So now, Orestes — and you, Pylades, dearest of friends from abroad —
we have to decide quickly what to do. Already the sun's bright light
is stirring the birds into their clear morning songs, and the black
night of stars is gone. Before any man comes out of that house, put
your heads together. We are at the point where there is no more time
for hesitating. It is the moment to act.

Orestes.
Dearest of all the men who serve me — how clearly you show what a
loyal man you are to our house. A thoroughbred horse, even when he is
old, does not lose heart in danger; he pricks up his ears. That is
you. You drive us on, and you follow in the front rank yourself. So I
will tell you what I have decided. Listen sharply, and if I miss the
mark anywhere, put me right.

When I went to Apollo's oracle at Delphi to learn how I should take
justice for my father from the men who killed him, Apollo gave me this
answer — you will hear it now: that I myself, with no armour and no
army, should use cunning to steal the just killing that my hand owes.
So, since that is the oracle we heard — you go into that house, when
the moment lets you in, and find out everything that is going on in
there, so that you can come back and tell us for certain. They will
never know you, after so many years and at your age; nobody will
suspect a man with hair as white as yours. Your story is this: you are
a stranger from Phocis, sent by Phanoteus — he is the greatest ally
they have. Tell them, and swear to it, that Orestes is dead — an
accident he could not escape, thrown from a racing chariot at the
Pythian games. Let that be the story, and let it stand.

Orestes.
Meanwhile the two of us will go first to my father's tomb, as the god
ordered, and crown it with poured wine and a cut lock of hair. Then we
will come back here, carrying in our hands the bronze urn — you know
the one; it is hidden in the bushes — so that we can bring them, by a
lie, the sweet news that this body of mine is gone, burned already and
turned to ash. What does it cost me? Dead in words, and in fact alive,
and carrying off the glory. I think no word is a bad omen if it brings
gain. I have seen it often enough: clever men who died in rumour, and
then came home, and were honoured more than before. So I am confident
that this report will see me alive as well, and blazing down on my
enemies like a star.

Land of my fathers, and gods of this country: receive me, and let this
journey go well. And you, house of my fathers — I come to cleanse you,
in justice, sent on my way by the gods. Do not send me out of this
land stripped of honour. Let me hold the wealth that was mine and set
my house in order.

That is all I have to say. Old man, go now, and see to the task you
have been given. The two of us will be off. It is the moment — and the
moment is the greatest master of any work that men undertake.

(From inside the house, a cry.)

Electra.
Oh — oh, misery.

Tutor.
Listen, my boy. I think I heard one of the servant women moaning
inside, behind the doors.

Orestes.
Could it be Electra? Poor Electra. Do you want us to stay here and
listen to her crying?

Tutor.
No. Nothing before Apollo's orders. Let us set out to do those and
make our beginning from them — pouring the offerings at your father's
grave. That is what brings victory into our hands, and mastery over
everything we do.

(The three go out, the Tutor one way and Orestes and Pylades the
other. Electra comes out of the house.)

Electra.
	Pure light,
	and air, that shares the earth with the light —
	how many songs of grief you have heard from me,
	how many blows struck full on this breast
	until it bled,
	whenever the dark night lets go.
	And my hateful bed in that house of suffering
	knows my night-long vigils by now:
	how much I mourn my wretched father.
	In a foreign land the bloody god of war
	did not take him in as a guest.
	No — my mother, and the man who shares her bed,
	Aegisthus, split his head with a murdering axe,
	the way woodcutters split an oak.
	And for that no one grieves but me.
	No cry of pity from any lips but mine,
	for you, father, dead
	so shamefully, so pitifully.

Electra.
	But I will not stop —
	not the grieving, not the bitter crying —
	as long as I see the stars flash,
	as long as I see this daylight.
	Like the nightingale, who killed her own child,
	I will cry out in front of my father's doors
	so that everyone hears.
	House of Hades and Persephone,
	Hermes of the underworld, and lady Curse,
	and you dread Furies, daughters of the gods,
	who see the people unjustly killed,
	who see the marriage-beds stolen in secret —
	come. Help me. Take vengeance
	for my father's murder,
	and send me my brother.
	Alone I have no strength left
	to carry the weight of grief that drags me down.

(The Chorus comes in: women of Mycenae.)

Chorus.
	Electra — child of the most wretched of mothers —
	why are you always wasting away like this
	in a mourning that never has enough,
	for Agamemnon, caught long ago,
	godlessly, in your treacherous mother's snare,
	and betrayed to a wicked hand?
	May whoever did it die —
	if I may say such a thing without offending the gods.

Electra.
	Daughters of noble houses,
	you have come to comfort me in my trouble.
	I know it. I understand it. None of it escapes me.
	But I will not give this up.
	I will not stop mourning my poor father.
	You who return my love with every kind of love —
	let me go on raving like this.
	Aiai. I beg you.

Chorus.
	But you will never raise your father
	out of the lake of Hades, where everyone goes,
	not by crying and not by prayers.
	You go past what can be borne into a grief
	that has no cure, and you groan yourself to death,
	and in all of it there is no release from evil.
	Why do you reach for what cannot be carried?

Electra.
	Only a fool forgets a parent
	who died pitifully.
	No — my heart is set on the bird of grief,
	who cries Itys, always Itys,
	frantic with mourning, Zeus's messenger.
	And you, Niobe, who suffered everything,
	I count you a god,
	weeping for ever in your tomb of rock.

Chorus.
	Not to you alone, child,
	of all people has grief come,
	yet you take it harder than the others in the house,
	your own family, born of the same blood.
	Chrysothemis lives, and Iphianassa,
	and he lives too, hidden away from grief
	in his youth, a fortunate boy,
	whom the famous land of Mycenae
	will one day welcome home as a nobleman's son,
	when Zeus guides his step back to this land —
	Orestes.

Electra.
	Yes, and I wait for him without tiring —
	childless, wretched, unmarried, always walking my road
	soaked in tears, carrying a fate of evils
	that never ends. And he forgets
	what he suffered and what he learned.
	What message comes to me
	that does not turn out a lie?
	He is always longing to come,
	and for all his longing he never thinks it worth appearing.

Chorus.
	Courage, child. Take courage.
	Zeus is still great in the sky;
	he watches everything and rules it.
	Hand your too-painful anger over to him.
	Do not hate the people you hate too much,
	and do not forget them either.
	Time is a god who makes things easy.
	Agamemnon's son, on the cattle-grazed
	shore at Crisa, has not turned his back on you,
	and neither has the god who rules beside Acheron.

Electra.
	But most of my life has gone already
	without hope, and I have no strength left.
	I am wasting away without children.
	No man who loves me stands up for me.
	Like some worthless foreigner in the house
	I keep my father's rooms,
	dressed like this, in rags,
	standing at tables with nothing on them.

Chorus.
	Pitiful was the cry at his homecoming,
	pitiful the cry in your father's banquet hall
	when the blow of the all-bronze axe
	swung straight at him.
	Cunning planned it. Lust did the killing.
	Between them they bred a terrible shape, terribly —
	whether it was a god or a mortal
	who did the thing.

Electra.
	That day — of all days
	the one I hate most.
	That night, the horror of it,
	the feast no one can speak of,
	where my father saw shameful death
	come from two hands —
	the hands that took my life and betrayed it,
	the hands that destroyed me.
	May the great god of Olympus
	give them suffering to pay for it,
	and may they never enjoy their splendour,
	the pair who did such things.

Chorus.
	Take care. Say no more.
	Do you not understand how you are falling,
	so shamefully, from where you stand
	into ruin of your own making?
	You have earned yourself far more trouble than you needed,
	breeding wars, always, in your sullen soul.
	And you cannot fight the powerful
	and expect to win.

Electra.
	I was forced to it by terrible things — terrible things.
	I know. My anger does not escape me.
	But in the middle of terrors I will not hold back
	this ruin, as long as life is in me.
	Who, dear women, who could ever say
	a fitting word to me — who, with any sense of what is right?
	Let me be. Let me be, you comforters.
	This will be called a thing without a cure,
	and I will never rest from my labours,
	my cries beyond counting.

Chorus.
	I say it out of goodwill,
	the way a mother you could trust would say it:
	do not breed ruin on ruin.

Electra.
	And what is the measure of evil? Tell me —
	how can it be right to forget the dead?
	Where in the human race did that grow?
	May I never be honoured by such people,
	and if I ever live with anything good
	may I not live at ease,
	holding back the wings of my shrill grief
	and cheating my father of his honours.
	Because if the dead man lies there, poor wretch,
	dust and nothing,
	and they do not pay back in return
	blood for blood, in justice,
	then shame is gone, and reverence for the gods
	is gone, from the whole human race.

Chorus.
Child, I came here as much for your sake as for my own. But if what I
say is wrong, you win. We will follow you.

Electra.
I am ashamed, women, if you think I go too far with all this grieving.
But it is force that makes me do it — forgive me. How could any woman
of good birth not do it, seeing what has happened to her father, when
I see it night and day, and not fading but growing?

First, my own mother, who gave birth to me, has become my worst enemy.
Then, in my own house, I live with my father's murderers. They rule
me. It is from them that I get what I get, and it is from them that I
go without.

And then what kind of days do you think I have — when I see Aegisthus
sitting on my father's throne, when I see him wearing the same clothes
my father wore, pouring wine at the hearth where he killed him; and
when I see the crowning outrage of it all, the murderer in my father's
bed beside my wretched mother — if I must call her mother, when she
sleeps with that man. She is so shameless that she lives with the man
who stained her, with no fear of any Fury. No — as if she were
laughing at what she did, she found the day on which she killed my
father by treachery, and on that day she sets up dances, and every
month she slaughters sheep to the gods who saved her.

Electra.
And I watch it — I, the ill-fated one — and I weep in the house, and
waste away, and wail over the accursed feast that is named after my
father, alone with myself. I cannot even cry as much as my heart would
like. Because this lady — so noble, in words — shouts abuse at me like
this: "You godless, hateful thing. Is your father the only one who
ever died? Is no one else in the world in mourning? Damn you — and may
the gods below never release you from your crying." That is her
outrage. Except when she hears from someone that Orestes is coming.
Then she goes wild, and stands over me and screams: "Is this not your
doing? Is this not your work — you who stole Orestes out of my hands
and smuggled him away? You will pay for it, and you will pay in full."
She barks like that, and right beside her, egging her on, is her
glorious bridegroom, the same words in his mouth — that complete
coward, that walking disaster, the man who fights his battles with
women at his side.

And I wait, always, for Orestes to come and put an end to it, and I am
dying while I wait. He is always about to do something, and it has
destroyed every hope I have, the ones I had and the ones I might have
had. In a life like this, friends, there is no room for self-control
or for reverence. In evil times there is a great need to do evil too.

Chorus.
Tell us — is Aegisthus near, while you say this to us, or has he left
the house?

Electra.
He is gone. Do not imagine I would be out of doors if he were near. He
is in the country now.

Chorus.
Then I could speak to you more boldly, if that is how things stand?

Electra.
He is not here. Ask what you like.

Chorus.
Then I ask you: what of your brother? Is he coming, or is he putting
it off? I want to know.

Electra.
He says he is coming. He says it, and does none of what he says.

Chorus.
A man will hesitate, when the thing he is doing is a great one.

Electra.
I did not hesitate when I saved him.

Chorus.
Take heart. He is of good blood. He will stand by his own.

Electra.
I believe it. Otherwise I would not have lived this long.

Chorus.
Say no more now. I see your sister coming out of the house —
Chrysothemis, your father's daughter and your mother's — with
offerings for the grave in her hands, the kind that custom sends down
to the dead.

(Chrysothemis comes out of the house.)

Chrysothemis.
What is it this time, sister? What speech have you come out to the
doors to make in public? Will you never learn, in all this time, to
stop feeding a useless temper with empty words? I know this much about
myself: I hate what is happening. If I had the strength, I would show
them what I think of them. But as things are, in bad times, I think it
best to sail with the sail lowered, and not to look as if I am doing
something when I cannot hurt them. I wish you would do the same. And
yet — the right is on your side, not on mine. But if I am to live as a
free woman, then the people in power have to be obeyed in everything.

Electra.
It is a terrible thing that you, the daughter of the father you were
born to, can forget him and care about your mother. Every lecture you
give me was taught to you by her. Not a word of it is your own. Then
choose one or the other: be a fool, or be sensible and forget the
people you owe. You said just now that if you had the strength you
would show your hatred of them — and when I do everything I can to
avenge our father, you do not help me. You try to turn me aside from
doing it. Is that not cowardice on top of everything else?

Explain to me — or learn it from me — what I would gain by stopping
this grieving. Am I not alive? Badly, I know, but well enough for me.
And I hurt them, and that is an honour paid to the dead man, if there
is any pleasure where he is. But you, who hate them — you hate in
words, while in fact you live with our father's murderers. I would
never give in to them, not if someone were to bring me the gifts you
preen yourself on now. Keep your rich table and your overflowing life.
My food is this: not to hurt myself. I have no wish for the honour you
have — and neither would you, if you had any self-respect. As it is,
when you could be called the daughter of the best father there ever
was, be called your mother's. That way most people will see you for
what you are — a traitor to your dead father and your own people.

Chorus.
Nothing in anger, for the gods' sake. There is gain for both of you in
what has been said, if you would learn to use her words, and she
yours.

Chrysothemis.
Women, I am used to what she says, more or less. I would never have
mentioned this at all if I had not heard that the greatest evil of all
is coming for her — the thing that will put an end to her long
grieving.

Electra.
Then say it — this terror. If you can tell me anything worse than what
I have now, I will not argue with you any more.

Chrysothemis.
I will tell you everything I know. If you do not stop this grieving,
they mean to send you where you will never see the light of the sun
again — alive, in a covered cell, outside this country, singing your
own miseries. Think about that, and do not blame me afterwards when it
happens to you. Now is the time to be sensible.

Electra.
They have really decided to do that to me?

Chrysothemis.
They have. As soon as Aegisthus comes home.

Electra.
Then let him come quickly, if that is what he is bringing.

Chrysothemis.
Poor girl. What curse are you calling down on yourself?

Electra.
That he should come — if he means to do any of that.

Chrysothemis.
So that what will happen to you? Where are your wits?

Electra.
So that I can get as far away from all of you as I can.

Chrysothemis.
Do you give no thought to the life you have?

Electra.
My life is a beautiful thing. A wonder to look at.

Chrysothemis.
It would be, if you knew how to be sensible.

Electra.
Do not teach me to betray my own people.

Chrysothemis.
I am not. I am teaching you to give way to the people in power.

Electra.
You do the flattering. That is not my way.

Chrysothemis.
It is a fine thing, all the same, not to fall through stupidity.

Electra.
We will fall, if we must, avenging our father.

Chrysothemis.
Our father forgives me for this. I know he does.

Electra.
Those are words only cowards would praise.

Chrysothemis.
So you will not be persuaded? You will not agree with me?

Electra.
No. I hope I am never that empty of sense.

Chrysothemis.
Then I will go where I was sent.

Electra.
Where are you going? Who are these offerings for?

Chrysothemis.
Mother is sending me to pour them on our father's grave.

Electra.
What did you say? For the man she hated most in the world?

Chrysothemis.
The man she killed herself. That is what you want to say.

Electra.
Which of her friends talked her into it? Whose idea was it?

Chrysothemis.
Something frightened her in the night, I think.

Electra.
Gods of my father — stand with me now, at last.

Chrysothemis.
Do you find something to hope for in her fright?

Electra.
Tell me the dream, and then I can answer you.

Chrysothemis.
I only know a little of it.

Electra.
Then tell me that. A few words have often brought people down before
now, and set them on their feet.

Chrysothemis.
The story is that she saw your father and mine come back to the light,
and stand beside her a second time. Then he took the sceptre he used
to carry himself — the one Aegisthus carries now — and planted it at
the hearth; and from it a branch grew up, thick with leaves, and its
shade covered the whole land of Mycenae. That is what I heard from
someone who was there when she told her dream to the Sun. More than
that I do not know — only that she is sending me because of this fear
of hers. So I beg you, by the gods of our family, listen to me, and do
not fall through stupidity. If you push me away now, you will come
looking for me later, in trouble.

Electra.
Dear sister — do not let one thing you are holding touch that tomb. It
is not lawful, it is not holy, for you to set grave-gifts or pour
offerings to our father from the woman who hated him. Throw them to
the winds, or dig a deep hole and bury them in the dust, where none of
it can ever come near our father's resting place. Let it be kept as
treasure for her underground, when she dies.

If she were not the most shameless woman ever born, she would never
have thought of pouring these hateful offerings to the man she killed.
Ask yourself whether the dead man in his grave is likely to receive
her gifts with any love — she, by whose hand he died dishonoured,
hacked about like an enemy, his hands and feet cut off and strung
under his armpits, and who wiped the bloodstains off on his head, to
wash herself clean. Do you really think what you are carrying will
absolve her of the murder? It cannot.

No. Put those things away. Cut a lock from the ends of your hair, and
one from mine — I am wretched, and this is little, but it is what I
have — and give him this hair, which has no oil on it, and this belt
of mine, with no ornament on it. Then kneel and pray that he himself
will come up from the ground, kind to us, to help us against our
enemies; and that his son Orestes lives, and will set his foot on our
enemies with the upper hand, so that from now on we can crown his tomb
with richer hands than we bring him now. I think — yes, I think he too
had some part in sending her these dreams she did not want to see.
Still, sister, do this. Help yourself, and help me, and help the man
we love most of all, who lies in Hades — the father we share.

Chorus.
The girl speaks with reverence. And you, dear friend, will do as she
says, if you have any sense.

Chrysothemis.
I will do it. When a thing is right there is no reason for two people
to argue about it; the only sense is to hurry and do it. But while I
am trying to do this, friends — by the gods, keep silent about it. If
my mother hears of it, I think I will pay a bitter price for daring
this.

(She goes out towards the tomb.)

Chorus.
	If I am not a prophet gone wrong,
	if my judgement has not lost its wisdom,
	then Justice is coming, who sent this sign ahead of her,
	carrying just victory in her hands.
	She will come after them, child, and not long from now.
	There is courage in me
	since I heard, just now,
	of that dream, and the sweet breath of it.
	Your father never forgets, the lord of the Greeks;
	and the old axe never forgets,
	bronze-struck, two-edged,
	that cut him down in the most shameful outrage.

Chorus.
	She is coming — many feet, many hands,
	hidden in terrible ambush,
	the Fury with feet of bronze.
	Because they went at it, that pair,
	a marriage that was no marriage, no wedding,
	a contest of lust polluted with murder,
	which the gods' law forbade them.
	And so I am sure
	that never, never will this portent come near
	the doers and their helpers
	without blame. For there is no prophecy for mortals
	in terrible dreams or in oracles
	if this vision of the night does not come safely to shore.

Chorus.
	Pelops, that chariot-race of yours long ago,
	so full of pain —
	how it has come down, unending,
	on this land.
	Ever since Myrtilus, the charioteer who let you win,
	was put to sleep in the sea,
	thrown out root and branch
	from the golden chariot,
	a wretched outrage —
	never once since then
	has outrage, full of pain,
	left this house.

(Clytemnestra comes out of the house, with servants.)

Clytemnestra.
Loose again, it seems, and roaming about — because Aegisthus is not
here, and he at least always kept you from going out of doors and
shaming your family. But now that he is away, you take no notice of
me. And yet you have told a great many people, often, that I am
reckless and rule beyond what justice allows, and that I outrage you
and everything that is yours. I do no outrage. I speak ill of you
because I hear ill from you, constantly.

Your father — that and nothing else is your excuse, always — died at
my hands. At my hands. I know it perfectly well. I do not deny it.
Because it was Justice that took him, not I alone — and you should
have been on Justice's side, if you had any sense. This father of
yours, whom you never stop mourning, was the one man of all the Greeks
who could bring himself to sacrifice your sister, your own blood, to
the gods — he who had none of the pain in sowing her that I had in
giving birth.

Well then. Tell me: for whose sake did he sacrifice her? For the
Argives, you will say? They had no right to kill a daughter of mine.
Or was it for his brother Menelaus that he killed my child — and was
he not going to pay me for that? Did Menelaus not have two children of
his own, who ought to have died before mine, seeing that their father
and mother were the reason for that voyage in the first place? Did
Hades have some special appetite to feast on my children rather than
hers? Or had that accursed father lost all love for the children I
bore him, and kept it for the children of Menelaus? Is that not the
mark of a thoughtless father, and a bad one? I think so, even if you
judge it differently. And the dead girl would say so too, if she could
find a voice.

So I feel no regret over what was done. And if you think I am wrong,
get a just judgement of your own before you blame the people around
you.
