The Women of Trachis
(Part 3 of 3)

(A cry from inside the house.)

First Half-Chorus.
Am I imagining it, or did I just hear a cry of grief start up
somewhere inside the house? What was that?

Second Half-Chorus.
That is no vague sound. It is a wail, and it is a wail of disaster,
from indoors. Something new has happened in that house.

Half-Chorus.
And look. The old woman is coming out to us with her head down and
her brows drawn together. She has something to tell.

(The Nurse comes out.)

Nurse.
Children — so this is what it came to. The present she sent off to
Heracles has started no small run of evils for us.

Chorus.
What has happened, old woman? What is new?

Nurse.
Deianeira has gone on the last journey of all her journeys, and never
moved a foot.

Chorus.
You cannot mean she is dead?

Nurse.
You have heard it all.

Chorus.
She is dead? The poor woman?

Nurse.
That is the second time you have heard it.

Chorus.
Poor lost woman. How did she die? Tell us how.

Nurse.
In the cruellest way anyone could do it.

Chorus.
	Tell us, woman, what death
	she ran to meet.

Nurse.
	She destroyed herself.

Chorus.
	What passion, what sickness of the mind
	brought her down on the point of that evil weapon?
	How did she plan it, alone —
	death on top of death, and carried it through by herself?

Nurse.
	With the cut of the iron that makes people groan.

Chorus.
	You saw it, you poor helpless thing — this outrage?

Nurse.
	I saw it. I was standing right beside her.

Chorus.
	Who did it? How? Come, tell us.

Nurse.
	Herself. With her own hand. She made it happen.

Chorus.
	What are you saying?

Nurse.
	The plain truth.

Chorus.
	She has borne, she has borne a great Fury
	for this house — the new bride,
	the girl who only just arrived.

Nurse.
Too great a one. And if you had been there beside her and seen what
she did, you would have pitied her far more.

Chorus.
And a woman's hand brought itself to do that?

Nurse.
Yes, and terribly. You will hear it, and then you can bear me out.

When she came into the house alone and saw her son out in the
courtyard spreading a hollow litter, to go back and meet his father
with it, she hid herself where nobody could see her. She threw herself
down at the altars and cried aloud that they were going to be left
empty. She wept over every household thing she touched — the things
she used to use, poor woman, in the old days. She went from one room
to another, and if she caught sight of any of the servants she was
fond of, she wept at the sight of them, the unhappy woman, calling out
on her own fate and on a life that would have no children in it from
now on.

When she had done with that, all at once I saw her rush into Heracles'
bedroom. I kept my eye on her in secret, from the shadows, watching.
And I saw the woman throwing coverings onto Heracles' bed and
spreading them out. When she had finished that she jumped up onto it
and sat down in the middle of the bed, and hot streams of tears broke
out of her, and she said: "My bed, and my bridal room — goodbye now,
for good. You will never take me in to lie in these sheets again."

She said that much and no more. Then with a hard, quick hand she undid
her dress where the brooch of beaten gold sat over her breasts, and
bared her whole left side and her arm. I ran, as fast as I had the
strength to, and told her son what she was contriving. And in the time
it took to go there and come back, we found her with a two-edged sword
driven into her side, under the liver, into the heart.

The boy saw it and cried out. Because he understood, poor boy, that
his anger had driven her to it. He had learned too late, from the
people in the house, that she had done it without knowing what she
did, because of the beast — the Centaur. And then there was no lament
the poor boy left unmade, groaning over her, falling on her to kiss
her mouth; he lay down side by side with her and groaned and groaned
that he had thrown a false charge at her, and wept that now he was
orphaned of both of them at once, his father and her.

So that is how things stand in there. And anyone who counts on two
days, or on more than that, is a fool. There is no tomorrow until you
have got safely through today.

(She goes back inside.)

Chorus.
	Which do I groan for first?
	Which sorrow is the heavier?
	I am wretched, and I cannot judge.

Chorus.
	One of them we can see, here in the house.
	The other we wait for, and dread.
	To have it and to wait for it come to the same.

Chorus.
	If only some wind would rise
	and blow fair, away from this hearth,
	and carry me off out of this place,
	so that I need not die of terror
	at one sight of the strong son of Zeus.
	They say he is coming to the house
	in pains that nothing will loosen,
	a horror past all telling.

Chorus.
	And he was near, not far —
	the thing I cried out for beforehand, like the shrill nightingale.
	Here is a procession of strangers.
	And how are they carrying him? Like someone they love
	and are caring for — a heavy, soundless step.
	Aiai. He is carried in, and makes no sound.
	Am I to think him dead,
	or only sleeping?

(An Old Man comes in with attendants, carrying Heracles on a litter.
Hyllus comes out of the house.)

Hyllus.
	Oh, father — oh, this is misery,
	father, this is misery for me.
	What is to become of me? What am I to do? Oh.

Old Man.
	Quiet, child. Do not wake
	your father's savage pain. He is at the edge of it —
	alive, and barely. Bite your lip and hold it.

Hyllus.
	What are you saying, old man? He is alive?

Old Man.
	You must not wake him. Sleep has hold of him.
	You must not stir it, must not bring it back —
	the sickness that comes and goes, that terrible thing, child.

Hyllus.
	But the weight on me is more than I can carry.
	My mind is out of my hands.

Heracles.
	Zeus —
	what country have I come to? Who are these people
	I lie among, worn down by pains
	that never stop? Oh, I am wretched.
	It is biting again, the filthy thing. Ah.

Old Man.
	Did I not know how much we gained
	by keeping quiet, by not scattering
	the sleep off his head and off his eyes?

Hyllus.
	I cannot. I do not know how
	to look at this and bear it.

Heracles.
	Cenaeum, foundation of my altars,
	what thanks you have got me
	for my offerings there — Zeus.
	What ruin you have made of me. What ruin.
	I wish these eyes had never seen it,
	never looked on this flower of madness
	that nothing can charm away.
	What singer, what healer's hand
	will charm this ruin quiet, except Zeus?
	Show me one. I would be amazed to see him, even far off.

Heracles.
	Ah —
	let me be, let me be, I am done for,
	let me sleep my last sleep.

Heracles.
	Where — where are you touching me? Where are you laying me down?
	You are killing me. You are killing me.
	You have woken whatever had gone quiet.
	It has me — oh — here it comes again, crawling. Where are you from,
	you men, the most unjust in all Greece? I wore myself out
	clearing your seas and every forest of their monsters,
	I was destroyed doing it — and now, when I am sick,
	will not one of you bring fire, or a sword, and do me that kindness?

Heracles.
	Ah —
	will nobody come and hack the head
	off this hateful body? Ah. Ah.

Old Man.
	Son of this man, the work has grown beyond
	my strength. You take him. You have more in your hands
	to save him than I have.

Hyllus.
	I have hold of him.
	But I have nothing, in myself or from outside,
	that will make his life forget its pain. That is what Zeus deals out.

Heracles.
	Son, where are you? Here — take me here,
	lift me, take the weight. Ah. Oh, what has come to me.

Heracles.
	It leaps again, it leaps, the wretched thing,
	it is finishing me,
	the wild sickness nobody can go near.
	Pallas, Pallas — it is torturing me again. Son —
	pity your father. Draw the sword — nobody will blame you —
	and strike here, under my collarbone. Cure the pain
	your godless mother filled me with. Let me see her fall
	like this, exactly like this, the way she has destroyed me. Sweet Hades —

Heracles.
	brother of Zeus — put me to sleep, to sleep,
	finish me with a fast death. I am wretched.

Chorus.
I shudder to hear it, friends — what our king suffers. A man like
that, driven by pains like these.

Heracles.
Oh — many a hot, hard thing, and not just in the telling, these hands
have laboured through, and this back. But nothing like this was ever
set me, not by Zeus's wife, not by hateful Eurystheus — nothing like
what the daughter of Oeneus, with her lying face, has fastened on my
shoulders: this woven net of the Furies I am dying in. It has glued
itself to my sides and eaten the flesh right through to the bone; it
lives inside me and sucks at the passages of my lungs; it has already
drunk my fresh blood, and my whole body is destroyed, mastered by this
chain that no one can describe.

No spear on a battlefield ever did this to me. Nor the earth-born army
of the Giants, nor any wild beast's strength, nor Greece, nor any land
with no Greek in it, nor any country I came to and cleared of its
monsters. A woman did it. A female, born with nothing of a man's
nature in her — she, alone, has brought me down without a sword.

Son — be my true son now, born of me, and do not set your mother's
name above mine. Go into the house yourself and bring her out with your
own two hands, and put the woman who bore you into my hand, so that I
can know for certain which sight hurts you more: mine, or hers, when
her body has been mutilated as justice demands.

Go, child. Have the stomach for it. And pity me, whom so many would
pity — bellowing and crying like a girl. Nobody could say he had ever
seen this man do that before. I followed my troubles without a groan,
always. And now, from that, I have been found out a woman. I am
wretched.

Heracles.
Now come here and stand beside your father, and see what kind of
misfortune has done this to me. I will show it to you. I will take off
the coverings. Look — all of you, look at this miserable body. Look at
the wretch I am. Look at the pitiful state I am in.

Aiai — oh, I am wretched. It has flared up again, just now, this spasm
of ruin; it has shot through my sides. It will not leave me alone, it
seems, this wretched, devouring sickness.

Lord Hades, take me. Fire of Zeus, strike. Hurl it, lord — send the
bolt of your thunder down on me, father. It is feeding again; it has
burst into flower; it has broken out.

Hands — my hands, my back, my chest, my own dear arms. Are you the same
ones that once broke by force the thing that lived in Nemea, the
herdsmen's curse, the lion, a creature nobody could approach or speak
to? And the Hydra of Lerna; and that double-natured army of beasts
that would mix with no one, that went on horses' legs — the Centaurs,
violent, lawless, overbearing in their strength; and the boar of
Erymanthus; and, under the earth, the three-headed dog of Hades, a
monster nothing could fight, the whelp of the terrible Echidna; and the
snake that guarded the golden apples at the ends of the earth. I
tasted ten thousand other labours, and no man ever set up a trophy
over these hands.

And now look at me — my joints unstrung, torn to rags, sacked and
ruined by a ruin I could not see coming. I, who am called the son of
the noblest of mothers. I, who am named the child of Zeus among the
stars.

But know this one thing for certain: even if I am nothing, even if I
cannot crawl a step, the woman who did this I will get into my hands,
even from here. Only let her come near me, so that she can learn to
tell the whole world that living or dead I punished the bad.

Chorus.
Poor Greece — what mourning I see coming for her, if she loses this
man.

Hyllus.
Father, since you have paused and given me a chance to answer, be
quiet a while and listen to me, sick as you are. I am going to ask you
for what I have a right to. Give yourself to me — not as you are now,
savage with the pain that is biting you. Otherwise you cannot know
where you are wrong to want to take pleasure, and where you are wrong
to hurt.

Heracles.
Say what you want, and then stop. I am sick; I cannot follow any of
these riddles you have been spinning.

Hyllus.
I have come to tell you about my mother — the state she is in now, and
how she went wrong without meaning to.

Heracles.
You vile — you name her again, in my hearing? Your mother, who killed
your father?

Hyllus.
Things are such that silence would not be right.

Heracles.
Not right — after what she did before?

Hyllus.
You will not say so. Not after what she has done today.

Heracles.
Speak. But take care you do not turn out a bad son.

Hyllus.
I will speak. She is dead, just now, newly killed.

Heracles.
By whose hand? That is a monstrous thing to prophesy, out of all this
evil.

Hyllus.
Her own. No one else's — no outsider's.

Heracles.
Oh — before she could die as she should have, by my hand?

Hyllus.
Even your anger would turn, if you knew the whole of it.

Heracles.
That is a strange way to begin. Tell me what you mean.

Hyllus.
The whole thing is this: she went wrong, meaning to do good.

Heracles.
Good, you vile creature? She kills your father, and that is good?

Hyllus.
She thought she was putting a love-charm on you, when she saw the
marriage inside the house. She missed.

Heracles.
And which of the Trachinians is that great a poisoner?

Hyllus.
Nessus the Centaur. Long ago he persuaded her to madden your desire
with a love-charm like that.

Heracles.
Oh — oh, I am wretched. I am gone. Finished, finished — there is no
light left for me. Oh. Now I understand where we stand.

Go, child. You have no father any more. Call all your brothers, the
whole seed of my blood, and call poor Alcmena, Zeus's wife for nothing,
so that you can hear my last word on the oracles — everything I know.

Hyllus.
But your mother is not here. It happens that she has settled at Tiryns
on the coast. Some of your children she has taken and is raising
herself; others, you should know, are living in the city of Thebes.
Those of us who are here, father — if anything needs doing, tell us,
and we will do it.

Heracles.
Then listen. Here is the work. You have come to where you will show
what kind of man you are, who are called my son.

It was foretold me by my father long ago that I would die by nothing
that draws breath, but by someone already dead, a dweller in Hades. So
this beast, the Centaur, exactly as the god foretold it, has killed me
alive, being dead.

And I will show you fresh oracles that match those, and agree with the
old ones. I went into the grove of the Selli, the men who live on the
mountain and sleep on the ground, and wrote them down from my father's
oak, the oak that speaks with many tongues. It told me that at the
time which is alive and here now, my release from the labours laid on
me would be completed. And I thought that meant I would do well. It
meant nothing but this: that I would die. The dead have no more
labours.

So since all this is coming out clearly, child, you must be this man's
ally again, and not wait until my tongue gets sharp; give way of your
own accord and work with me. Find the finest law there is: obey your
father.

Hyllus.
Father — I am frightened to have come to this point in what we are
saying. But I will do what you decide.

Heracles.
First of all, put your right hand in mine.

Hyllus.
Why do you press this pledge on me so hard?

Heracles.
Give it. Now. Do not distrust me.

Hyllus.
There. I hold it out. I will not refuse you anything.

Heracles.
Now swear, by the head of Zeus who fathered me —

Hyllus.
Swear to do what? Will you tell me that too?

Heracles.
— to carry out for me the task I name.

Hyllus.
I swear it. Zeus is my witness.

Heracles.
And pray that if you break it, you suffer for it.

Hyllus.
I will not suffer for it, because I will do it. But I pray it all the
same.

Heracles.
Do you know the peak of Oeta, the mountain of Zeus?

Hyllus.
I know it. I have stood up there many times to sacrifice.

Heracles.
Then you must lift my body and carry it there — with your own hands,
and with whatever friends you want. Cut a great pile of wood from the
deep-rooted oak, and cut down a great deal of the male wild olive with
it, and lay my body on top, and take a flaming pine torch and set it
alight. And let no tear of mourning come into it. Do it without a
groan and without a tear, if you are this man's son. If you do not, I
will be waiting for you, even from under the earth — a curse on you
for ever, and a heavy one.

Hyllus.
Oh, father — what have you said? What have you done to me?

Heracles.
What has to be done. Otherwise, get yourself some other father, and
never be called my son again.

Hyllus.
Oh — again, oh. What are you calling on me to be, father? Your
murderer, with your blood on my hands?

Heracles.
Not that. The healer of what I have — the one doctor for my pain.

Hyllus.
And how do I heal your body by setting fire to it?

Heracles.
If you are afraid of that part, do the rest.

Hyllus.
You will not be refused the carrying, at any rate.

Heracles.
And building the pyre I have described?

Hyllus.
So long as I do not touch it with my own hands. The rest I will do; my
part will not fall short.

Heracles.
That will be enough. Then add one small favour to the large ones.

Hyllus.
Even if it is very large, it will be done.

Heracles.
You know the girl, Eurytus's daughter?

Hyllus.
You mean Iole, I would guess.

Heracles.
You know her. This is what I lay on you, child. When I am dead — if
you want to do what is right by the gods, remembering the oath you
swore your father — take her as your wife. Do not refuse your father.
Let no other man ever have her, who has lain at my side — no one but
you. Make that marriage yours, my son. Do as I say. You have trusted
me in great things; to distrust me in small ones wipes out the thanks
you earned before.

Hyllus.
Oh. It is wrong to be angry with a sick man. But who could bear to see
him think like this?

Heracles.
You talk as if you meant to do nothing I say.

Hyllus.
Who would? She is the one to blame — the one cause of my mother's
death, and of your being as you are. Who would choose that, unless he
was sick with avenging spirits? Better that I die too, father, than
live in the same house with the people I hate most.

Heracles.
This man, it seems, will not give me what is due to a dying man. Well
— the curse of the gods will wait for you, if you disobey what I say.

Hyllus.
Oh — you will soon show how sick you are, it seems.

Heracles.
Yes, because you are stirring up the evil that had gone to sleep.

Hyllus.
I am wretched. There is no way out, anywhere I turn.

Heracles.
Because you do not think it right to listen to the man who fathered
you.

Hyllus.
Then am I to be taught to offend the gods, father?

Heracles.
It does not offend the gods, if it gladdens my heart.

Hyllus.
So you order me to do this, as wholly just?

Heracles.
I do. I call the gods to witness it.

Hyllus.
Then I will do it. I will not refuse — and I show the gods that the
deed is yours. I could never turn out bad by trusting you, father.

Heracles.
You finish well. And add the favour quickly, my son: put me on the
pyre before some spasm or sting comes over me again. Come — hurry —
lift me. This is the rest from evils. This is the last end of this
man.

Hyllus.
Nothing stops it being done for you, since you command it and force
it on me, father.

Heracles.
Come, then — before you wake this sickness again, my hard soul, set a
bit of steel in my mouth, stone fitted to stone, and stop my crying.
You are finishing a task you did not want, and it is a joy.

Hyllus.
Lift him, men. Grant me great forgiveness for this, and know the great
unfeelingness of the gods in what is being done — they who beget
children and are called fathers, and look on at suffering like this.
Nobody sees what is to come. What is here now is pitiful for us, and
shameful for them, and hardest of all men for the one who is bearing
this ruin.

And you, girl — do not stay behind at the house either. You have seen
great deaths, fresh ones, and many sufferings never suffered before,
and there is nothing in any of it that is not Zeus.
