Oedipus at Colonus
(Part 1 of 4)

(The edge of a grove at Colonus, a mile or so outside Athens. Oedipus,
blind and old, comes in led by Antigone.)

Oedipus.
Antigone, child of a blind old man — where have we come to? Whose city
is this? Who is going to take in the wanderer Oedipus today, with a
little something to spare for him? I ask for little, and I get less
than that, and it is enough for me. Suffering has taught me to be
content, and the long years I have lived with it, and thirdly the good
blood in me. Now, child, if you see a place to sit, on open ground or
in some grove of the gods, stop me and settle me there, so we can find
out where we are. We are strangers here. We have come to learn from the
townspeople, and to do whatever we are told.

Antigone.
Father — poor worn-out Oedipus — the towers that shut in the city are a
long way off, as far as I can see. And this place is holy, by the look
of it: it is thick with laurel and olive and vine, and inside it the
nightingales are singing, a whole crowd of them. Bend your legs and sit
here, on this rough stone. It has been a long road for an old man.

Oedipus.
Then sit me down, and keep watch over the blind man.

Antigone.
I have been doing that long enough not to need telling.

Oedipus.
Can you tell me where we have stopped?

Antigone.
I know it is Athens. I do not know this place.

Oedipus.
Yes — every traveller on the road told us that much.

Antigone.
Shall I go somewhere and find out what the place is called?

Oedipus.
Yes, child, if anyone lives here.

Antigone.
Someone lives here. But there is no need, I think. I can see a man,
close by.

Oedipus.
Coming this way? Heading for us?

(A man of Colonus has come up to them.)

Antigone.
He is here already. Whatever seems right to you to say, say it. The man
is in front of you.

Oedipus.
Stranger — this girl sees for both of us, and she tells me you have
come at a lucky moment, a scout to explain what we do not know —

Stranger.
Before you ask me anything else, get up off that seat. You are on
ground it is not holy to walk on.

Oedipus.
What ground is it? Which god does it belong to?

Stranger.
Nobody touches it and nobody lives on it. The goddesses of fear hold
it — the daughters of Earth and Darkness.

Oedipus.
What holy name do they have? If I hear it I can pray to them.

Stranger.
The people here would call them the Eumenides, the Kindly Ones, who see
everything. Other places like other names.

Oedipus.
Then may they be kind, and take in the man who kneels to them. I will
not leave this seat, in this land, ever again.

Stranger.
What is that supposed to mean?

Oedipus.
It is the sign I was told to wait for. The password of my fate.

Stranger.
Well, I would not dare move you myself without the city behind me,
before I report what I am doing.

Oedipus.
Then in the gods' name, stranger, do not refuse me — a wanderer, look
at me — the answers I am begging you for.

Stranger.
Ask. You will get no refusal from me.

Oedipus.
What is this place, then, that we have come into?

Stranger.
Everything I know, you will hear. This whole place is holy. Poseidon
the revered holds it, and the fire-carrying god is in it too, the Titan
Prometheus. And the spot you are standing on is called the bronze
threshold of this land, the thing that props Athens up. The fields
round about claim the horseman Colonus as their founder — that is his
statue there — and all the people take his name together and call
themselves by it. That is what this place is, stranger. It is not
honoured in stories. It is honoured by living with it.

Oedipus.
So people do live in these parts?

Stranger.
Certainly. They are named for that god there, Colonus.

Oedipus.
Does someone rule them? Or does the decision lie with the people?

Stranger.
The king in the city rules here.

Oedipus.
And who is he, this man with the power of word and of force?

Stranger.
His name is Theseus, son of Aegeus who was king before him.

Oedipus.
Could one of you go to him with a message?

Stranger.
To say what? To get him to come?

Oedipus.
To tell him that a small service will win him a great gain.

Stranger.
And what help can there be from a man who cannot see?

Oedipus.
Everything I say will have eyes in it.

Stranger.
Listen, stranger — I do not want you coming to harm. You have good
blood in you, anyone can see it, whatever your luck has been. Stay
where I found you, right here, while I go and tell the people of this
district — here, not in the city. They will decide for you whether you
stay or go back the way you came.

(The stranger goes off the way he came.)

Oedipus.
Child, has the stranger gone?

Antigone.
He has gone. You can say anything you like now, father, in peace.
There is nobody near you but me.

Oedipus.
Ladies with the terrible eyes — since yours is the first seat in this
land where I have bent my knees, do not be hard on Apollo, or on me.
When he foretold all those evils for me, he told me this too: that
after a long time I would find rest here. I would come to a land at
the end of my journey, where I would find a seat of the Revered
Goddesses and a place to shelter a stranger, and there I would round
off my miserable life — a profit to the people who took me in, if I
settled among them, and a ruin to the ones who sent me away, who drove
me out. And he promised that signs would come to warn me: an
earthquake, or thunder, or the lightning of Zeus.

I know now that it must have been some trustworthy sign from you that
guided me on this road into this grove. Otherwise I would never have
come across you first of all in my wandering — I who drink no wine,
meeting you who take none — or sat down on this holy seat that no axe
has shaped. So, goddesses, according to the word of Apollo, give me
now some way to reach the end and close my life — unless you think me
beneath your notice, a slave for ever to the worst troubles any man on
earth has known. Come, sweet daughters of ancient Darkness. Come,
Athens, called the city of great Athena, honoured above every city:
pity this wretched ghost of the man Oedipus. It is not the body he
once had.

Antigone.
Quiet. Some men are coming — old men, by their years — to see who is
sitting here.

Oedipus.
I will be quiet. Hide me in the grove, off the road, until I hear what
they say. Knowing is the safe way to act.

(They hide. The Chorus comes in, old men of Colonus, searching.)

Chorus.
	Look. Who was he? Where is he lodged?
	Where has he run to, out of the place,
	the most shameless man alive,
	of all men the most shameless?
	Search. Look for him. Hunt for him everywhere.
	A wanderer, that old man, a wanderer,
	not one of ours. He would never have walked
	into the untrodden grove
	of these girls no one can fight,
	whose name we tremble to speak,
	whom we pass without a look,
	without a sound, without a word,
	only the lips moving in silent prayer.
	And now they say someone has come
	with no fear of them at all,
	and I look across the whole holy ground
	and still I cannot find
	where he is hiding.

Oedipus.
	Here is the man. I am the one.
	I see by the voice, as people say.

Chorus.
	Oh. Oh.
	Terrible to look at. Terrible to hear.

Oedipus.
	I beg you — do not look at me as a lawbreaker.

Chorus.
	Zeus, keep us safe. Who is this old man?

Oedipus.
	Not one whose lot you would call happy,
	not the best of luck, guardians of this land.
	That is plain. Or I would not creep along
	on somebody else's eyes,
	a big man moored to a small one.

Chorus.
	Ah. Those blind eyes —
	were you born with them? A hard life,
	and a long one, by the look of you.
	But not with my help
	will you add this curse to it.
	You are trespassing. Trespassing.
	Do not stumble on into that silent glade
	deep in grass, where the bowl of water
	runs together with the honeyed drink.
	Stranger, cursed in everything, take care.
	Move. Come away. Come out.
	There is a long way between us.
	Do you hear, worn-out wanderer?
	If you have anything to say to us,
	come off the forbidden ground
	to where everyone may go, and speak there.
	Until then, keep off.

Oedipus.
	Daughter, what are we to think?

Antigone.
	Father, we must do as the townspeople do,
	giving way where we must, and listening.

Oedipus.
	Then take hold of me.

Antigone.
	I have you.

Oedipus.
	Strangers, do not let me be wronged
	for trusting you and moving from my place.

Chorus.
	Never, old man. Nobody will take you
	from this resting place against your will.

Oedipus.
	Further?

Chorus.
	Come further still.

Oedipus.
	Further?

Chorus.
	Lead him on, girl, further.
	You can hear what we say.

Antigone.
	Come, father, come this way
	with your dark steps, where I am leading you.

Chorus.
	Stranger in a strange land,
	poor man, bring yourself to hate
	what the city has learned to hate,
	and to honour what it loves.

Oedipus.
	Then lead me, child,
	to where we can stand on holy ground
	and speak, and listen,
	and stop fighting what has to be.

Chorus.
	There. Do not lean your foot
	past that ledge of natural rock.

Oedipus.
	Like this?

Chorus.
	Enough. You heard me.

Oedipus.
	Shall I sit?

Chorus.
	Yes — sideways, on the edge of the stone,
	crouch down low.

Antigone.
	Father, this is my job. Gently —

Oedipus.
	Oh. Oh no.

Antigone.
	— one step fitted to the next,
	and lean your old body
	on my arm, that loves you.

Oedipus.
	Oh, the ruin, the madness of it.

Chorus.
	Poor man, now that you are at ease,
	tell us: who are you, what man?
	Who is led here in such pain?
	What country can you name as yours?

Oedipus.
	Strangers, I have no city. But do not —

Chorus.
	Do not what? What are you forbidding, old man?

Oedipus.
	Do not, do not, do not ask me who I am.
	Do not question me. Do not dig further.

Chorus.
	Why?

Oedipus.
	A terrible birth.

Chorus.
	Speak.

Oedipus.
	Child — oh — what am I to say?

Chorus.
	What seed are you from, stranger? Speak. Who was your father?

Oedipus.
	Oh no. What is going to happen to me, my child?

Antigone.
	Say it. You are at the very edge now.

Oedipus.
	Then I will say it. There is no hiding it.

Chorus.
	You are both taking too long. Quickly.

Oedipus.
	You know of a son of Laius?

Chorus.
	Oh. Oh.

Oedipus.
	And the house of Labdacus?

Chorus.
	Oh, Zeus.

Oedipus.
	The wretched Oedipus?

Chorus.
	You are that man?

Oedipus.
	Do not be afraid of anything I say —

Chorus.
	Oh. Oh, no.

Oedipus.
	I am cursed.

Chorus.
	Oh. Oh.

Oedipus.
	Daughter, what happens now?

Chorus.
	Out. Get out of this country, far away.

Oedipus.
	And your promise? What will you do with that?

Chorus.
	No fate punishes a man
	who pays back what was first done to him.
	Trick set against trick
	is paid in pain, not thanks.
	You — up from this seat, out again,
	off my land, away with you, fast,
	before you fasten some heavier debt
	on my city.

Antigone.
	Strangers, you have respect in you.
	You will not put up with my old father,
	because you have heard the story
	of things he did without meaning to.
	Then pity me, I beg you, strangers,
	pity a wretched girl, who begs you
	for her father alone,
	who looks at you with eyes not blind,
	eyes meeting eyes, as if I were
	one of your own blood,
	and asks respect for a broken man.
	We lie in your hands as in a god's,
	we poor sufferers. Come, grant us
	the kindness we hardly dared to hope for.
	I beg you by whatever is dear to you at home,
	by a child, by a wife, by property, by a god.
	Look where you like, you will not find
	the man alive who could escape
	if a god were leading him.

Chorus.
Daughter of Oedipus, be sure of this: we pity you, and we pity him as
much, for what has happened to you. But we are afraid of what comes
from the gods, and we cannot say anything beyond what we have said to
you already.

Oedipus.
Then what is the good of a reputation? What use is a fine name that
runs away like water, if Athens, as they say, is the most god-fearing
city there is, and the only one able to save a stranger who has been
badly treated, and the only one able to help him? Where is any of that
for me, when you have raised me up off this seat only to drive me out
of the country — and all for fear of a name? Not of my body, surely,
and not of my acts. My acts were things done to me, far more than
things I did — if I have to speak of my mother and my father, which is
what makes you afraid of me. I know that perfectly well. And yet how am
I evil by nature? I was struck, and I struck back. Even if I had known
what I was doing, I could not have been called evil for that. As it
was, I knew nothing, and I went where I went — while the ones who
wronged me knew exactly what they were doing, and were out to destroy
me.

So I beg you by the gods, strangers: you made me stand up; now keep me
safe. Do not honour the gods and then, in the same breath, treat the
gods as if they were fools. Reckon instead that they look at the man
who respects them, and they look at the man who does not, and that no
godless man has ever yet escaped them. With those gods behind you, do
not cloud over the good fortune of Athens by making yourselves the
servants of an unholy act. You took the suppliant in on your word; now
rescue me and guard me to the end. Do not look at this face — I know
it is hard to look at — and dishonour me. I come here holy, a man who
respects the gods, and I bring a benefit to the people of this city.
When your master comes, whoever is your leader, you will hear all of
it and understand. Until then, do not be bad to me.

Chorus.
Old man, what you have laid before us has to be respected. You have
not put it in a few words. But I am content that the lords of this
land should judge the matter.

Oedipus.
And where is the ruler of this country, strangers?

Chorus.
He is in his father's city, in our land. The man who spotted you and
sent us here has gone to fetch him.

Oedipus.
And do you think he will have any regard for a blind man, or care
enough to come himself?

Chorus.
Certainly — the moment he hears your name.

Oedipus.
And who is going to carry him that word?

Chorus.
It is a long road, and travellers' talk gets around. He will hear it,
and he will come; do not worry. Your name, old man, has reached
everyone, and even if he is slow and half asleep, once he hears it is
you he will come quickly enough.

Oedipus.
Then let him come, and good fortune with him, for his city and for me.
What good man is not a friend to himself?

Antigone.
Zeus — what am I to say? What am I to think, father?

Oedipus.
What is it, Antigone, child?

Antigone.
I see a woman coming towards us, riding a pony from Sicily, and a wide
Thessalian hat on her head shading her face from the sun. What can I
say? Is it her? Is it not? Is my mind playing tricks? Yes — no — I
cannot tell. Oh, I am wretched. It is no one else. Her eyes are
bright, she is greeting me as she comes close. She is waving to me.
Clearly it is Ismene, and nobody else.

Oedipus.
What did you say, child?

Antigone.
That I see your daughter, my own sister. In a moment you can know her
by her voice.

(Ismene comes in, with one attendant.)

Ismene.
Father — sister — the two sweetest words I have to say. It was hard to
find you, and now I have found you, it is hard to see you through my
tears.

Oedipus.
Child, you have come?

Ismene.
Father — what a sight you are.

Oedipus.
Child, you are really here?

Ismene.
It was not easy getting here.

Oedipus.
Touch me, child.

Ismene.
I have you both — one hand for each.

Oedipus.
Oh, my own blood.

Ismene.
Oh, what a wretched way to live.

Oedipus.
Her life and mine?

Ismene.
And mine, wretched as I am, makes three.

Oedipus.
Child, why have you come?

Ismene.
Out of concern for you, father.

Oedipus.
Because you missed me?

Ismene.
Yes, and to bring you news with my own mouth. I had one servant I could
trust, and I brought him.

Oedipus.
And the young men, your brothers — where are they, when there is work
to be done?

Ismene.
They are where they are. It is terrible between them now.

Oedipus.
Those two! In their nature and the way they live they are copies of the
Egyptians. Over there the men sit at home at the loom, and the wives go
out and earn the daily bread. And you two, children — the ones who
should be doing this work sit at home keeping house like girls, while
you, in their place, carry your wretched father's troubles for him.
This one, from the time she stopped being a child and her body grew
strong, has wandered with me always, poor girl, an old man's guide,
often going hungry and barefoot through wild woodland, often drenched
by rain and burned by sun — hard going, and she puts the comforts of
home second, so long as her father is looked after. And you, child —
before this you came out to your father, without the Thebans knowing,
bringing every oracle that had been given about this body of mine, and
you stood guard for me faithfully when I was being driven from the
land. And now what story have you brought your father, Ismene? What
errand has sent you from home? You have not come empty-handed; I know
that much for certain. You are bringing me something to fear.

Ismene.
What I went through, father, hunting for the place where you were
living — I will let that go. I do not want to suffer it twice, once in
the doing and again in the telling. It is the trouble that is on your
two unlucky sons now that I have come to tell you about.

At first they wanted to leave the throne to Creon and not stain the
city, when they looked with cool heads at the old blight on our family
and how it has clung to your wretched house. But now some god, and
their own guilty minds, have put a wicked rivalry into the two of them
— three times wretched — to seize the rule and the power of a tyrant.
The younger one, the one born second, has stripped Polyneices, the
elder, of the throne and driven him out of the country. And Polyneices
— this is the story everywhere among us — has gone as an exile down to
the valley of Argos, and there he is taking a new marriage and new
shield-companions for allies, meaning that Argos will soon either hold
the land of Thebes in honour, or lift Thebes' name to the sky. These
are not just words piled up, father. They are terrible deeds. And
where the gods will take pity on your sufferings, I cannot tell.

Oedipus.
So you actually had hope that the gods would ever give me a thought,
and that I would be saved one day?

Ismene.
I do, father. From the new oracles.

Oedipus.
What oracles? What has been prophesied, child?

Ismene.
That the people there will want you one day, dead and alive, for their
own safety's sake.

Oedipus.
And who could be helped by a man like me?

Ismene.
Their power, they say, comes to rest in you.

Oedipus.
When I no longer exist, then I am a man?

Ismene.
Yes. The gods are raising you up now. Before, they were destroying you.

Oedipus.
It is a poor thing, to prop up an old man who fell when he was young.

Ismene.
Well, know this: Creon is coming to you because of it — and soon, not
in some far-off time.

Oedipus.
To do what, daughter? Explain it to me.

Ismene.
To settle you near the Theban border, so they have control of you, but
you never set foot inside the land.

Oedipus.
And what good am I to them, lying outside their gates?

Ismene.
Your tomb, if it is neglected, weighs heavily on them.

Oedipus.
A man could work that out without a god's help.

Ismene.
That is why they want you close to the country — and not somewhere you
would be your own master.

Oedipus.
Will they at least cover me with Theban earth?

Ismene.
No, father. The blood of your own family forbids it.

Oedipus.
Then they will never get their hands on me.

Ismene.
One day that will be a heavy thing for Thebes.

Oedipus.
How? What will bring it about, child?

Ismene.
Your anger, when they stand at your tomb.

Oedipus.
And who told you what you are telling me?

Ismene.
The sacred envoys, coming back from the hearth at Delphi.

Oedipus.
And Apollo really said this about me?

Ismene.
So say the men who came back to Thebes.

Oedipus.
And has either of my sons heard it?

Ismene.
Both of them, and they understand it well.

Oedipus.
And then those two — the worst of men — heard this, and still put the
throne before wanting me back?

Ismene.
It hurts to hear it, but I bear it.

Oedipus.
Then may the gods never put out the quarrel that is fated between
them, and may the end of the fight they are set on — with spears
already lifted — rest with me. Then the one who holds the sceptre and
the throne now would not keep it, and the one who has gone out would
never come back. When I, their father, was being shoved out of my own
country in that disgrace, they did not stop it, they did not defend
me; I was uprooted and sent out by them, and proclaimed an exile.

You might say the city gave me what I wanted then, and gave it
reasonably. No. On that first day, when my heart was boiling and the
sweetest thing would have been to die, to be stoned to death — nobody
came forward to help me to what I wanted. Later, when the whole pain
had softened and I had learned that my anger had run out ahead of me, a
bigger punishment than my old mistakes deserved — then, after all that
time, the city drove me out of the land by force, and they, the sons
of their father, who could have helped their father, would not lift a
finger. For the sake of one small word from them I was thrown out, a
beggar, and I have wandered ever since.

From these two girls, as far as their nature allows, I get the food
that keeps me alive, and safety on the road, and the support of my own
family. Those two chose the throne over the father who made them — the
sceptre, and ruling the land as tyrants. Well, they will not get me for
an ally, and they will never have any joy of ruling Thebes. I know
that, hearing this girl's oracles and thinking over the old prophecies
Apollo made to me long ago, which he has now brought to pass. So let
them send Creon to hunt me, and anyone else with power in the city.
Strangers, if you are willing — together with the Revered Goddesses
who stand guard over your people — to make a defence of me, you will
win a great saviour for this city, and pains for my enemies.
