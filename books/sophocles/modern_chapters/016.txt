Philoctetes
(Part 2 of 3)

Chorus.
	Pity him, my lord. He has told of many struggles,
	of pains hard to bear. May no one I love
	ever meet the like.
	And if you hate the sons of Atreus, my lord,
	if you hate them bitterly, then I would turn
	their wrong into this man's gain
	and carry him where he longs to go,
	home, in your fast, well-fitted ship,
	and get clear of the anger of the gods.

Neoptolemus.
Take care. You are easy about it now, standing here looking on. When you
have had your fill of the sickness, living alongside it, you may find
you are no longer the man who said these things.

Chorus.
Never. You will never have a fair reason to throw that in my face.

Neoptolemus.
Well then, it would be a disgrace for me to be found less ready than you
to work for a stranger when the moment calls for it. If you all agree,
let us sail. Let him set out, and quickly. The ship will carry him, and
will not say no. Only let the gods bring us safe out of this land, and
on from here to wherever we choose to sail.

Philoctetes.
Oh, dearest of days. Oh, sweetest of men — and you, sailors, my friends.
How can I show you in deeds what a friend you have made of me? Let us
go, my boy. But first the two of us will go in and say goodbye to my
home that is no home, so you can learn what I lived on and how strong a
heart I was born with. I think nobody else could have stood so much as
the sight of it, let alone lived with it. Necessity was my teacher, and
it taught me to put up with what is bad.

Chorus.
Wait, both of you. Let us hear this. Two men are coming: one of them a
sailor off your ship, the other a foreigner. Hear what they have to say,
and then go in.

(A merchant comes in, with one of Neoptolemus's sailors.)

Merchant.
Son of Achilles, this man here, my fellow traveller, was one of three
keeping guard on your ship, and I asked him to tell me where you were to
be found, since I had run into you — not by design, but by some chance
that brought me to anchor on this same shore. I am a shipmaster, sailing
home from Troy with no great company, to Peparethus, where the grapes
grow thick; and when I heard that the sailors were all men who had
sailed out with you, I decided not to go on my way in silence without
first telling you what I know, and getting the same in return. You know
nothing, I suspect, about your own affairs — what new plans the Greeks
have about you. And not plans only: things being done, no longer put
off.

Neoptolemus.
I will remember the kindness of your thinking ahead, stranger, unless I
was born bad. Tell me what you spoke of, so I can know what new plan of
the Greeks you are carrying to me.

Merchant.
They have set out after you with ships — old Phoenix, and the sons
of Theseus.

Neoptolemus.
To bring me back by force, or by talking?

Merchant.
I do not know. I heard it, and I have come to tell you.

Neoptolemus.
Phoenix and his shipmates are that keen to do this for the sons of
Atreus?

Merchant.
Take it as being done. Not about to be done. Done.

Neoptolemus.
Then why was Odysseus not ready to sail and bring the message himself?
Was some fear holding him back?

Merchant.
He and the son of Tydeus were fitting out to go after another man, when
I put to sea.

Neoptolemus.
Who was this other man, that Odysseus himself was sailing after?

Merchant.
There was a man — but first tell me who that is over there. And whatever
you say, keep your voice down.

Neoptolemus.
This, stranger, is the famous Philoctetes.

Merchant.
Then ask me nothing more. Gather yourself up and get out of this land,
as fast as you can, and sail.

Philoctetes.
What is he saying, boy? What is this sailor selling of me to you, in
whispers, in the dark?

Neoptolemus.
I do not know yet what he means. Whatever he has to say, he must say it
in the light — to you and me and these men.

Merchant.
Seed of Achilles, do not get me in trouble with the army for saying what
I should not. I do a good deal for them, and I get a good deal back, as
much as a poor man can.

Neoptolemus.
I am the enemy of the sons of Atreus, and this man is my greatest
friend, because he hates them. You came to me as a friend. Then you must
hide from us nothing of what you have heard.

Merchant.
Watch what you are doing, boy.

Neoptolemus.
I have been watching it a long time.

Merchant.
I will hold you responsible.

Neoptolemus.
Do. But talk.

Merchant.
I am talking. It is this man that those two are sailing after — the two
you heard me name, the son of Tydeus and the brute strength of Odysseus.
They have sworn to bring him, either by talking him round or by main
force. And all the Greeks heard it plainly, from Odysseus's own mouth;
he had more confidence than the other that he could do it.

Neoptolemus.
And what for? Why should the sons of Atreus, after so long a time, turn
their minds so hard to a man they threw out years ago? What longing came
over them? Or was it the force of the gods, and their anger — the gods
who pay back evil deeds?

Merchant.
I will teach you the whole of it, since it seems you have not heard.
There was a seer of good family, a son of Priam, Helenus by name. That
man went out alone one night — the man everybody calls foul and
disgraceful names, Odysseus the trickster — and caught him, and led him
in chains into the middle of the Greeks and showed him off: a fine
catch. And Helenus prophesied everything else they asked him, and this
about Troy: that they would never take its towers unless they talked
this man round and brought him from the island where he lives now. And
as soon as the son of Laertes heard the seer say it, he promised on the
spot to bring the man and show him to the Greeks. He thought he would
most likely get him willing; and if he would not come, then unwilling;
and if he failed, anybody who liked could cut his head off. You have
heard it all, boy. I advise speed — for you, and for anyone you care
about.

Philoctetes.
Oh, misery. He — that man, that walking harm — has sworn to talk me
round and bring me to the Greeks? Then I will be talked round into
coming up out of Hades, dead, into the light — the way his father did,
Sisyphus, who talked his way back up once.

Merchant.
I know nothing about that. I am going back to my ship. May a god bring
the two of you whatever is best.

(He goes.)

Philoctetes.
Is it not astonishing, my boy, that the son of Laertes should ever
expect to lead me off his ship with soft words and show me in the middle
of the Greeks? No. I would sooner listen to the thing I hate most in the
world — the viper that made me the cripple I am. But there is nothing he
would not say, nothing he would not dare. And I know now that he is
coming here. Come, son, let us move, so that a wide sea can lie between
us and Odysseus's ship. Let us go. Hurry at the right moment, and when
the work is done there is sleep, and rest.

Neoptolemus.
Then as soon as the wind off the bow drops, we sail. It is dead against
us now.

Philoctetes.
The sailing is always good when you are running from trouble.

Neoptolemus.
No. It is against them too.

Philoctetes.
No wind is ever against pirates, when there is a chance to steal
something and grab it by force.

Neoptolemus.
Well, if that is what you want, let us go — once you have taken from
inside whatever you most need and most want.

Philoctetes.
There are things I need. Not that there is much to choose from.

Neoptolemus.
What is there that is not on board my ship?

Philoctetes.
I have a leaf, a herb, that I use more than anything to put this wound
to sleep, till it is quite tamed.

Neoptolemus.
Bring it out, then. What else do you want to take?

Philoctetes.
Any of these arrows that may have been forgotten and slipped out of my
sight — so I do not leave one for someone else to pick up.

Neoptolemus.
Is that really the famous bow — the one in your hands now?

Philoctetes.
This one, and no other. The one I carry in my two hands.

Neoptolemus.
Could I look at it closer? Hold it — and bow to it, as if it were a god?

Philoctetes.
For you, my boy, that and anything else of mine that would do you good.

Neoptolemus.
I do want to. But my wanting goes like this: if it is allowed, I would
like it. If not, let it go.

Philoctetes.
What you say is holy, son, and it is allowed. You alone have given me
this light of the sun to look at, and the land of Oeta to see again, and
my old father, and my own people; you alone, when I was down under my
enemies' feet, have lifted me clear of them. Take heart. You may touch
it, and give it back to the hand that gave it, and you can boast to the
world that for your goodness you alone among men have laid a hand on it.
It was by doing good that I got it myself.

Neoptolemus.
I am not sorry I found you, and made a friend of you. A man who knows
how to do good when good has been done to him is a friend worth more
than any possession. Go in, please.

Philoctetes.
And I will bring you in with me. The sickness in me wants you standing
by.

(They go into the cave.)

Chorus.
	I have heard the story, though I never saw it:
	the man who once went near the bed of Zeus —
	Ixion — and how the all-powerful son of Cronus
	bound him to the running wheel.
	But of no other man, by hearsay or by sight,
	do I know one who met a fate more hateful than this man's.
	He did no one wrong. He robbed no one.
	He dealt fairly with fair men,
	and he was left to die like this, for nothing.
	It is a wonder to me,
	how — how in the world — alone,
	hearing the surf beat on both sides of him,
	he kept hold of a life so full of tears.

Chorus.
	Here he was his own only neighbour,
	with no way to walk,
	no one in the country to be near him in his misery,
	no one to answer his groans
	when he cried out over the heavy gnawing bloody thing,
	no one to gather soft leaves from the feeding earth
	and put to sleep the hot blood oozing
	from the sores of his poisoned foot,
	whenever the fit came on.
	He would crawl, this way and that,
	dragging himself like a child without its nurse,
	to wherever something could be had,
	when the ruin that bites the heart let go.

Chorus.
	Not the seed of the holy earth for food,
	nor anything else we eat,
	we men who work for our bread —
	only what his fast-shooting bow
	and its winged arrows won for his belly.
	Poor wretched soul.
	Ten years, and not one taste of poured wine;
	looking round for standing water, wherever he could find it,
	and always making his way towards it.

Chorus.
	But now he has met the son of good men,
	and out of all that he will come to happiness, and to greatness.
	After the fullness of many months
	the boy carries him on the sea-going timbers home,
	to his father's hall,
	the haunt of the Malian nymphs, the banks of the Spercheius,
	where the man of the bronze shield, Heracles,
	went up to the gods, all in a blaze
	of his father's divine fire, above the heights of Oeta.

(Neoptolemus comes out of the cave, carrying the bow. Philoctetes
follows, and stops.)

Neoptolemus.
Come on, if you are willing. Why are you silent like this, for no
reason? Why are you standing there struck dumb?

Philoctetes.
Aah. Aah.

Neoptolemus.
What is it?

Philoctetes.
Nothing terrible. Go on, son.

Neoptolemus.
Is it the pain of the sickness? Has it come on you?

Philoctetes.
No, no. I think it is easing just now. Oh, gods.

Neoptolemus.
Why do you groan like that and call on the gods?

Philoctetes.
For them to come to us, to save and to soothe. Aah. Aah.

Neoptolemus.
What has happened to you? Will you not say, but stay silent like this?
It is plain that you are in some trouble.

Philoctetes.
I am finished, son. I cannot hide it from you. Attatai. It goes through
me — it goes right through. Oh, I am wretched. I am finished, son. It is
eating me, son. Papai. Apappapai. Papappapappapappapai. By the gods, if
you have a sword ready to your hand, son, strike at the end of my foot.
Cut it off, quickly. Do not spare my life. Go on, boy.

Neoptolemus.
What is this new thing that has come on you so suddenly, that makes you
howl and groan over yourself like this?

Philoctetes.
You know, son.

Neoptolemus.
What is it?

Philoctetes.
You know, boy.

Neoptolemus.
What is wrong with you? I do not know.

Philoctetes.
How can you not know? Papappapappapai.

Neoptolemus.
It is terrible, the weight of this sickness.

Philoctetes.
Terrible past telling. Pity me.

Neoptolemus.
What am I to do?

Philoctetes.
Do not take fright at me and betray me. She comes now and then, this
thing — perhaps when she has had her fill of wandering somewhere else.

Neoptolemus.
Oh, oh — poor man. Poor, poor man, as all this pain has shown you to be.
Do you want me to take hold of you? To touch you somewhere?

Philoctetes.
No, not that. But take this bow of mine, as you asked me to just now,
and keep it, and guard it, until this present fit of the sickness lets
go. Sleep takes me as soon as the evil passes off, and it cannot stop
before then. You must let me sleep in peace. And if those men come in
the meantime, by the gods I charge you: not willingly, not unwillingly,
not by any trick, let this bow go to them — or you will be the death of
yourself and of me together, me, who am your suppliant.

Neoptolemus.
Do not worry about my care. It will go into no hands but yours and mine.
Give it to me, and good luck go with it.

Philoctetes.
There. Take it, boy. And bow to the jealousy of the gods, so that it
does not bring you pain on pain, as it did me, and the man who had it
before me.

Neoptolemus.
Gods, grant us that, both of us. And grant us a voyage with a fair wind,
and a quick one, to wherever the god thinks right and our errand is
bound.

Philoctetes.
I am afraid your prayers are empty, son. Look — again the dark blood is
dripping from deep down, oozing, and I expect something new. Papai. Oh.
Papai — again — foot, what you do to me. It is creeping up. It is coming
— here, close. Oh, I am wretched. Now you have the whole thing. Do not
run away, whatever you do. Attatai. Odysseus — you stranger from
Cephallenia — I wish this pain could go right through your chest and
stay there. Oh. Papai. Papai again. You two generals, Agamemnon,
Menelaus — if only you could feed this sickness in my place, for as long
as I have. Oh, oh. Death. Death. I call you every day, day after day —
why can you never come? Son. My noble boy. Take me up and burn me in
this fire they call Lemnian, the fire of this island. Burn me, noble
boy. I did that once myself, for the son of Zeus, and it was for that I
got these weapons, that you are keeping safe now. What do you say, boy?
What do you say? Why are you silent? Where are you, son?

Neoptolemus.
I have been in pain a long while, groaning over your suffering.

Philoctetes.
Then take heart too, son. She comes sharp and she goes quickly. Only I
beg you — do not leave me alone.

Neoptolemus.
Take heart. We will stay.

Philoctetes.
You will stay?

Neoptolemus.
Be sure of it.

Philoctetes.
I do not think it right to put you on oath, son.

Neoptolemus.
It is not allowed me to leave without you.

Philoctetes.
Give me your hand on it.

Neoptolemus.
I give it. To stay.

Philoctetes.
Now take me there — over there —

Neoptolemus.
Where do you mean?

Philoctetes.
Up —

Neoptolemus.
What is this? Are you raving again? Why are you staring at the sky's
circle above us?

Philoctetes.
Let me go. Let me go.

Neoptolemus.
Let you go where?

Philoctetes.
Let me go, will you.

Neoptolemus.
I will not.

Philoctetes.
You will kill me if you touch me.

Neoptolemus.
There. I am letting you go, since you seem to know better.

Philoctetes.
Earth, take me, dying, as I am. This evil will not let me stand up any
more.

(He sinks down.)

Neoptolemus.
Sleep will have the man before long, I think. His head is falling back.
Yes — sweat is running down his whole body, and a dark vein has burst at
the end of his foot, and the blood is pouring out. Let us leave him
quiet, friends, so he can fall asleep.

Chorus.
	Sleep, who knows nothing of anguish, Sleep, who knows nothing of pain,
	come to us with a kind breath,
	come happy, come happy, and bring happiness, lord.
	Hold over his eyes
	this light that is spread across them now.
	Come, come to me, healer.
	My son, look where you are going to stand,
	where you will step from here,
	in your thinking. You see already.
	What are we waiting for?
	The right moment is the judge of everything,
	and wins a great, great victory
	at a single step.

Neoptolemus.
	No. He hears nothing. But I see this: we have hunted this bow for nothing
	if we sail without him.
	His is the crown. He is the one the god said to bring.
	To boast of a thing half done, and done with lies, is a foul disgrace.

Chorus.
	That, my son, a god will see to.
	But when you answer me again,
	softly, my son, softly,
	send me your words.
	In sickness every man's sleep is sleepless;
	it sees sharply.
	But as far as you can, as far as ever you can,
	that — that is what to look to, secretly:
	how you will bring it off.
	You know whom I mean.
	If you keep the mind you have now towards him,
	a shrewd man can see troubles ahead with no way out.

Chorus.
	A wind, my son, a fair wind.
	The man is eyeless, helpless,
	stretched out in his night —
	sleep in the sun is a good sleep —
	master of no hand, no foot, of nothing,
	like a man lying beside Hades.
	Look. See if what you say
	is timely. What my mind can take hold of, boy,
	is this: the work that frightens no one
	is the best work.

Neoptolemus.
Quiet, I tell you, and keep your wits. The man is moving his eyes and
lifting his head.

Philoctetes.
Light, after sleep. And you — watchers, keeping guard by me, past
anything I could have hoped for from strangers. I could never have
boasted of this, boy: that you would bear to stay by my sufferings,
pitying, standing by me and helping. The sons of Atreus certainly never
had the stomach to bear it so lightly, those fine generals. But your
nature is good, my son, and comes from good men, and so you made light
of all of it, though you were full of my screaming and my stench. And
now, since the evil seems to be giving me some forgetting and rest, son,
you lift me up yourself, you set me on my feet, so that when the
weakness lets me go we can start for the ship and not hold back from
sailing.

Neoptolemus.
I am glad to see you, past what I hoped, looking about you with no pain
and still breathing. From your symptoms, set against the trouble you
were in, you looked like a man already gone. Now lift yourself up. Or if
you would rather, these men will carry you. They will not shirk the
labour, since you and I have decided to do this.

Philoctetes.
I thank you for that, boy. Lift me yourself, as you meant to. Leave them
alone, so they are not weighed down with the foul smell before they have
to be. It will be trouble enough for them, living on board with me.

Neoptolemus.
So be it. Stand up, then, and hold on to me.

Philoctetes.
Do not worry. My old habit will get me upright.

Neoptolemus.
Papai. What am I to do now, from here?

Philoctetes.
What is it, boy? Where has your talk gone wandering?

Neoptolemus.
I do not know where to turn. There is no way through what I have to say.

Philoctetes.
No way through — for you? Do not say that, son.

Neoptolemus.
But that is where I am now. This is what it has come to.

Philoctetes.
It is not the foulness of my sickness that has talked you out of taking
me on board?

Neoptolemus.
Everything is foul, when a man leaves his own nature and does what does
not fit him.

Philoctetes.
But you are doing nothing, saying nothing, that goes beyond the man who
fathered you, when you help a good man.

Neoptolemus.
I will be shown up for a disgrace. That is what has been eating me a
long while.

Philoctetes.
Not for anything you do. But what you say — that is starting to frighten
me.

Neoptolemus.
Zeus, what am I to do? Am I to be caught out twice over as bad — hiding
what I should not hide, and saying the foulest words there are?

Philoctetes.
This man, unless I am a poor judge, means to betray me: leave me here,
and sail.

Neoptolemus.
Leave you? Not I. Take you — and hurt you by it. That is what has been
eating me.

Philoctetes.
What are you saying, son? I do not understand.

Neoptolemus.
I will hide nothing. You must sail to Troy. To the Greeks, and the sons
of Atreus and their fleet.

Philoctetes.
Oh. What did you say?

Neoptolemus.
Do not groan before you understand.

Philoctetes.
Understand what? What do you mean to do to me?

Neoptolemus.
Save you from this misery first. Then go with you and lay waste the
plains of Troy.

Philoctetes.
And you truly mean to do this?

Neoptolemus.
A great necessity governs it. Do not be angry to hear it.

Philoctetes.
I am finished. Wretched — betrayed. What have you done to me, stranger?
Give me back my bow. Now.

Neoptolemus.
It cannot be done. Justice and my own interest, both of them, make me
obey the men in command.

Philoctetes.
You fire — you thing of pure terror — you hateful masterpiece of
terrible cunning. What you have done to me. How you have cheated me. Are
you not ashamed to look at me — the suppliant who turned to you, the man
who begged you, you brute? You took the bow, and you took my life with
it. Give it back, I beg you. Give it back, I implore you, son. By the
gods of your fathers, do not take my life from me. Oh, I am wretched. He
does not even speak to me any more. He looks away, like a man who will
never let go.

Harbours. Headlands. You mountain beasts who lived here with me. You
broken rocks. To you — I know no one else to say it to — to you who are
here, my old companions, I cry out what the son of Achilles has done to
me. He swore to take me home, and he takes me to Troy. He gave me his
right hand on it, and he has taken my bow, the holy bow of Heracles, son
of Zeus, and keeps it, and wants to show it to the Greeks. He drags me
off by force as if he had caught a strong man, and does not see that he
is killing a corpse, a shadow of smoke, a phantom and nothing more. He
could never have taken me with my strength in me. Not even as I am,
except by a trick. Now I have been cheated, damned as I am. What am I to
do?

Give it back. Even now — come back to yourself. What do you say?
Silence. I am nothing, I am damned. Cave, you rock with two doors, back
I come to you, back again, stripped, with nothing to live on. I will
wither in that den alone, bringing down no winged bird with this bow, no
beast that walks the hills; I will die there myself, wretched, and make
a meal for the creatures I fed on, and the ones I hunted will hunt me
now. Blood for blood — I will pay it, wretched, at the hands of a man
who seemed to know nothing of evil. Damn you — no. Not yet. Not till I
learn whether you will change your mind again. If you will not, die a
bad death.

Chorus.
What do we do? It is with you, my lord, whether we sail now, or give way
to what this man says.

Neoptolemus.
A terrible pity for this man has fallen on me — not now for the first
time, but long since.

Philoctetes.
Have mercy, boy, by the gods. Do not let men call you a disgrace for
cheating me.

Neoptolemus.
Oh, what am I to do? I wish I had never left Scyros. That is how sick I
am at what is happening.

Philoctetes.
You are not bad. You came here, it seems, having learned bad things from
bad men. Leave that to the others — the ones it suits — and sail. Give
me my weapons, and go.
