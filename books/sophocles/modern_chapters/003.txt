Antigone
(Part 1 of 3)

(Thebes, in front of the royal house. It is just before dawn. Antigone
has brought Ismene out through the courtyard gates.)

Antigone.
Ismene — my sister, my own blood, the same mother and the same father.
Is there one evil, out of everything Oedipus left behind him, that Zeus
is not going to bring down on the two of us while we are still alive?
There is nothing — no pain, no ruin, no shame, no dishonour — that I
have not already seen among your troubles and mine. And now this. What
is this order they say the general has just given to the whole city? Do
you know anything? Have you heard? Or has it slipped past you that what
our enemies do to their enemies is on its way to the people we love?

Ismene.
Not one word about the people we love has reached me, Antigone, good or
bad, since the day we lost our two brothers — the two of them, on one
day, each by the other's hand. And since the Argive army ran during the
night just gone, I know nothing beyond that. I am no better off than I
was, and no worse.

Antigone.
I knew it. That is why I brought you out here past the courtyard gates:
so that you could hear it alone.

Ismene.
Hear what? Something is turning you dark. I can see it.

Antigone.
Yes. Creon, and the burial of our two brothers: one he has honoured and
one he has thrown away. Eteocles, they say, he has laid in the ground as
justice and custom require, so that he has his honour among the dead
below. But Polyneices — the body of Polyneices, who died so miserably —
the word has gone out to the citizens that nobody is to cover him with a
grave, or so much as cry over him. He is to be left unwept and unburied,
a sweet find for the birds, who are watching him already and looking
forward to their meal. That, they say, is what the good Creon has
proclaimed for you and for me — yes, for me too — and he is coming here
now to say it out loud to anyone who has not heard, and he does not
treat it as a small matter. Whoever does any of these things is to die
for it: stoned to death by the people, in the middle of the city. So
that is how things stand for you. And very soon you will show what you
are — whether you were born of good blood, or whether you are the bad
daughter of a good line.

Ismene.
Poor sister. If that is how things are, what use could I be, either way
— pulling the knot tighter, or picking it loose?

Antigone.
Decide whether you will share the work and the risk with me.

Ismene.
What risk? What are you thinking of?

Antigone.
Whether you will help these hands of mine lift his body.

Ismene.
You mean to bury him? When the city has forbidden it?

Antigone.
He is my brother. And yours, whether you like it or not. Nobody will
ever catch me betraying him.

Ismene.
You reckless thing — with Creon standing against it?

Antigone.
Creon has no right to keep me from my own.

Ismene.
Oh, sister. Think. Think how our father died, hated and disgraced, after
he found out his own crimes for himself and struck out both his eyes
with his own hand. Then his mother, who was also his wife — two names
for one woman — twisted a rope and ended her life. And third, our two
brothers, on a single day, killed each other, the poor fools, each with
a hand raised against the other, and made one death between them. Now
look at us, the two who are left, alone. Think how much worse we will
die if we break the law and go against the ruler's decree and the
ruler's power. We have to remember what we are. We were born women; we
are not made for fighting men. And we are ruled by people stronger than
we are, so we must obey in this, and in things that hurt more than this.
So I will ask the dead below to forgive me, because I am forced into it,
and I will obey the men in charge. To do more than you can is madness —
it makes no sense at all.

Antigone.
I would not ask you now. And if you changed your mind later and wanted
to do it, I would not be glad to have you with me. Be whatever you have
decided to be. I will bury him. If I die doing it, that is a fine way to
die. I will lie beside him, his own beside her own — a criminal, and
holy. There is more time that I have to please the dead than the living:
I will lie there forever. But you, if that is what you choose, go on
dishonouring what the gods hold in honour.

Ismene.
I dishonour nothing. But to act against the whole city — I was not made
for it. I cannot.

Antigone.
Use that as your excuse. I am going to heap up a grave over the brother
I love best.

Ismene.
Oh, you poor thing. I am so afraid for you.

Antigone.
Do not be afraid for me. Get your own life straight.

Ismene.
Then at least tell nobody what you are doing. Keep it hidden. I will do
the same.

Antigone.
No — shout it. I will hate you far more if you keep quiet and do not
announce it to everyone.

Ismene.
You have a hot heart for cold work.

Antigone.
I know I am pleasing the people I most need to please.

Ismene.
If you can. But you are in love with what cannot be done.

Antigone.
Then when my strength gives out, I will stop.

Ismene.
You should not go hunting the impossible in the first place.

Antigone.
If you go on like this you will have my hatred, and the dead man will
hate you too, and he will be right to. Leave me alone — me and my bad
judgment — to suffer this terrible thing. Nothing I suffer will be so
terrible that it stops me dying well.

Ismene.
Go, then, if you have decided. And know this: you are going out of your
mind, but to the people you love, you are truly loving.

(Antigone goes out towards the plain. Ismene goes back into the house.
The Chorus comes in: the old men of Thebes.)

Chorus.
	Ray of the sun, the finest light
	that ever shone on seven-gated Thebes,
	you appeared at last, eye of the golden day,
	coming up over the streams of Dirce,
	and the man with the white shield, who came out of Argos
	in full armour — you set him running,
	headlong, with a sharper bit in his mouth.

Chorus.
	He rose up against our land
	because of Polyneices and his quarrelsome claims,
	screaming shrilly,
	and flew over into our country like an eagle,
	roofed with a wing of white snow,
	with a mass of weapons
	and helmets with horsehair crests.

Chorus.
	He stood over our houses, his mouth gaping wide
	around our seven gates, with spears that wanted blood,
	and he went away again
	before his jaws could fill with our blood,
	before the pine-fed fire of Hephaestus took our crown of towers.
	That is how loud the crash of War rose about his back —
	too hard a hold for a man wrestling the dragon's own children.

Chorus.
	For Zeus hates the boasts of a big tongue
	above everything, and when he saw them coming
	in a great flood, arrogant
	with the clang of their gold,
	he threw his fire at the man
	on the top of our walls, at the finish line,
	just as he was starting to shout victory.

Chorus.
	He swung and fell on the hard ground,
	the torch still in his hand — the man who a moment ago
	was raging at us in a frenzy, like a drunk god,
	with the blasts of the most hateful winds.
	But it went otherwise for him,
	and to the others great War dealt out other things, smashing them down,
	the strong horse on the right of our team.

Chorus.
	Seven captains at seven gates,
	posted equal against equal,
	left their bronze arms behind for Zeus who turns the battle —
	all but the two hated ones, who, born of one father
	and one mother, set their two
	conquering spears against each other
	and took an equal share of one death.

Chorus.
	But Victory with the great name has come
	and smiled back at Thebes of the many chariots.
	So after this war let us
	make ourselves forget,
	and go to all the temples of the gods
	with dancing all night long, and let Bacchus,
	who shakes the ground of Thebes, lead the way.

Chorus.
	But here is the king of the country,
	Creon, son of Menoeceus, our new ruler
	by the new turn the gods have given things.
	What plan is he rowing towards,
	that he has called this special council of old men
	and summoned us
	with one proclamation for all?

(Creon comes in, with attendants.)

Creon.
Men. As for the city: the gods shook it with a heavy sea, and they have
set it upright again, safe. I sent messengers to bring you here, you
apart from everyone else, because I know that you always respected the
power of the throne of Laius; and again, when Oedipus set the city
straight; and when he was destroyed, you stayed loyal in your hearts to
his sons. So now that those two have died on one day by a double fate —
each striking and each struck, with the pollution of a brother's blood
on their own hands — I hold all the power and the throne, as the nearest
kin of the dead.

Now, there is no way to know a man fully — his spirit, his thinking, his
judgment — until he has shown himself in office, with the laws in his
hands. For my part, a man who steers the whole city and does not hold to
the best advice, but locks his tongue out of some fear — that man I
think the worst of men, and I have always thought so. And a man who
rates one of his own people above his own country — I count him nowhere.
I myself — let Zeus, who sees everything, always, be my witness — would
never keep silent if I saw ruin marching on the citizens instead of
safety; and I would never make a friend of a man who was an enemy of
this land. Because I know this: it is the country that keeps us safe,
and only while she sails upright can we make any friends at all.

Those are the laws I use to make this city great. And in keeping with
them I have now proclaimed this to the citizens, about the sons of
Oedipus. Eteocles, who died fighting for this city and was the best of
them all with a spear — he is to be buried, with every rite that goes
down to the noblest of the dead. But his brother — Polyneices, I mean —
who came back from exile wanting to burn his fathers' land and his
family's gods to the ground, wanting to drink the blood of his own
people and lead the rest away as slaves — the city has been told: nobody
is to give him a grave, nobody is to mourn him. He is to be left
unburied, his body a thing for the birds and the dogs to eat, mangled
where anyone can see it. That is my thinking. Bad men will never,
through me, stand ahead of the just in honour. But whoever means well by
this city — I will honour him dead and alive alike.

Chorus.
That is your decision, Creon, son of Menoeceus, for the man who hates
this city and the man who loves it. And you have the power, we suppose,
to use whatever law you like — over the dead, and over all of us who are
alive.

Creon.
Then see to it that you keep watch over what I have said.

Chorus.
Put that on some younger man's shoulders.

Creon.
That is not what I meant. There are men watching the body already.

Chorus.
Then what else are you ordering us to do?

Creon.
Not to take the side of anyone who disobeys.

Chorus.
Nobody is fool enough to want to die.

Creon.
And that is the price, exactly. But hope of profit has destroyed many
men before now.

(A Guard comes in from the plain.)

Guard.
My lord, I am not going to tell you that I have run here, or that I am
out of breath from getting my feet up. I stopped a lot of times to
think, and I turned round in the road more than once to go back. My mind
kept talking to me. "You poor fool, why are you going somewhere you will
be punished the moment you arrive?" And then: "You idiot, are you
standing still again? If Creon hears this from somebody else, how do you
think you will feel?" Turning it over like that, I got here slowly, in
my own time, and that is how a short road turns into a long one. In the
end, though, coming to you won. And even if what I have to say is
nothing, I will say it anyway, because I have come holding onto one
hope: that nothing can happen to me except what was going to happen
anyway.

Creon.
What is it that has you so downhearted?

Guard.
I want to tell you about myself first. I did not do it, and I did not
see who did it, and it would not be fair for me to come to any harm.

Creon.
You aim carefully, and you fence the thing off all round you. Plainly
you have some news.

Guard.
Bad news makes a man hang back a long time.

Creon.
Will you say it, and then get out of here?

Guard.
All right, I am telling you. Somebody has just buried the body and gone.
They sprinkled dry dust over the flesh and did the rites that have to be
done.

Creon.
What are you saying? What man dared do this?

Guard.
I do not know. There was no mark of a pickaxe there, no earth thrown up
by a spade. The ground was hard and dry, unbroken, no wheel-tracks
across it. Whoever did the work left no sign. When the first man on the
day watch showed us, everybody was amazed, and none of us liked it. The
body had vanished — not buried, no, but there was a thin layer of dust
over it, the way someone does it who is trying to get clear of a curse.
And there was no trace of any animal, any dog, coming near it or tearing
at it. Then the ugly words started flying, guard accusing guard, and it
would have ended in blows, and there was nobody there to stop it. Every
one of us was the man who did it, and none of us was caught doing it;
everybody said he knew nothing about it. We were ready to pick up
red-hot iron in our hands, to walk through fire, to swear by the gods
that we had not done it and did not know who had planned it or done it.
In the end, when searching got us nowhere, one man said something that
made every one of us bow his head to the ground in fear, because we
could not argue with him, and we could not see how it would go well for
us if we did what he said. What he said was that this had to be reported
to you, not hidden. That won. And the lot fell on me — lucky me — to win
this prize. So here I am. I did not want to come, and you do not want me
here, I know that. Nobody loves the man who brings bad news.

Chorus.
My lord, something in us has been asking for a while now whether this
might be the gods' doing.

Creon.
Stop, before what you are saying fills me up with anger too, and you
turn out to be a fool as well as an old man. What you are saying is
unbearable — that the gods take thought for that corpse. What, were they
burying him as a benefactor, out of the honour they hold him in? A man
who came to burn their pillared temples and the offerings in them, and
their land, and to scatter their laws? Or do you see the gods honouring
bad men? It does not happen. No. From the beginning there have been men
in this city who could hardly bear this order and were muttering against
me, shaking their heads where I could not see, and would not keep their
necks under the yoke properly, as they should if they were content with
me. It was those men — I am certain of it — who paid somebody, and led
them on, to do this.

Money. Nothing that ever grew among human beings is as evil as money. It
sacks cities. It drives men out of their homes. It teaches honest minds
and twists them round to stand for shameful things. It showed men how to
be villains, and how to know every kind of impiety there is. But the men
who did this for hire have made sure that sooner or later they pay for
it. And if Zeus still has any respect from me, know this for certain,
and I say it on my oath: if you do not find the very hand that did this
burial and bring him before my eyes, then a plain death will not be
enough for you. Not until you have been hung up alive, and shown
everyone what this outrage is — so that from now on you will know where
to get your profit when you go thieving, and learn that you should not
love money from every source there is. You will find that dirty earnings
have brought more men to ruin than to safety.

Guard.
Will you let me say something? Or do I just turn round and go?

Creon.
Do you not know, even now, how much your voice annoys me?

Guard.
Does it bite you in the ears, or in the heart?

Creon.
Why are you working out where my pain is?

Guard.
The man who did it hurts your heart. I only hurt your ears.

Creon.
God. What a born chatterer you are.

Guard.
Maybe. But not the man who did this. Never.

Creon.
Yes, and you sold your life for silver to do it.

Guard.
Oh. It is a terrible thing when a judge judges, and judges wrong.

Creon.
Be as clever about judging as you like. But if you do not show me the
men who did this, you will find out that dirty profits bring misery.

(Creon goes into the house.)

Guard.
Well, I hope he is found. That would be best. But caught or not — and
luck will decide that — there is no chance that you will see me coming
here again. I have got out of this alive, when I never hoped to or
expected to, and I owe the gods a great deal of thanks.

(The Guard goes out.)

Chorus.
	There are many wonders, and none more wonderful than man.
	He crosses the grey sea in the winter south wind,
	going through under swells
	that roar all round him.
	And the oldest of the gods, Earth,
	who never dies and never tires, he wears away,
	his ploughs going round year after year,
	turning her over with the horse's breed.

Chorus.
	The light-headed tribe of birds he snares and carries off,
	and the nations of wild beasts, and the salt brood of the sea,
	in the coils of his woven nets —
	the man who thinks of everything.
	With his devices he masters the beast
	that lives in the open and walks the hills; he puts the yoke
	on the neck of the shaggy-maned horse
	and on the tireless mountain bull.

Chorus.
	Speech, and thought quick as the wind, and the temper
	that builds a city — he taught himself all of these, and how to escape
	the arrows of frost under the open sky, and the arrows of the rain.
	He has a way through everything. Nothing that is coming
	finds him without a way. From Hades alone he will get no escape,
	but from diseases that had no cure he has thought out ways out.

Chorus.
	Clever beyond all hope, this inventing skill of his,
	and it carries him now to evil, now to good.
	When he honours the laws of the land and the justice of the gods he swore to,
	his city stands high. But the man who lets what is not right
	live with him for daring's sake has no city at all. May he never share my hearth,
	never think my thoughts — the man who does such things.

(The Guard comes back from the plain, leading Antigone.)

Chorus.
	This is a sign from the gods, and I cannot make sense of it.
	I know her. How can I argue
	that this is not the girl Antigone?
	Unhappy child
	of an unhappy father, Oedipus —
	what is this? Surely they are not bringing you in
	for breaking the king's laws,
	caught doing something mad?

Guard.
This is her. This is the one who did it. We caught her burying him.
Where is Creon?

Chorus.
Here he comes, back out of the house, just when he is needed.

(Creon comes out.)

Creon.
What is it? What have I come out in time for?

Guard.
My lord, there is nothing a man should swear he will never do. Second
thoughts make a liar of the first. I could have sworn I would not be
back here in a hurry, after the way your threats had me shaking. But a
joy you never hoped for is bigger than any other pleasure, and so I have
come, oath or no oath, bringing this girl. She was caught arranging the
grave. No lots were drawn this time. This piece of luck is mine, and
nobody else's. And now, my lord, take her yourself, as you see fit —
question her, convict her. And I am free of it. I have a right to be,
and clear of this whole trouble.

Creon.
This girl you are bringing — how did you take her, and where?

Guard.
She was burying the man. You know all of it now.

Creon.
Do you understand what you are saying? Are you telling it straight?

Guard.
I saw her burying the body you gave orders about. Is that clear and
plain enough?

Creon.
And how was she seen? How was she caught in the act?

Guard.
It went like this. When we got back, with those terrible threats of
yours hanging over us, we swept away all the dust that had been covering
the body and stripped the corpse properly bare — it was going soft by
then. Then we sat down on the top of the ridge, upwind, to keep the
smell of him from hitting us, every man keeping his neighbour awake with
a stream of abuse if he looked like slacking off. That went on until the
bright disc of the sun stood in the middle of the sky and the heat was
burning. Then, all at once, a whirlwind lifted a pillar of dust off the
ground — a trouble that reached the sky — and filled the plain, and tore
the leaves off every tree on the flat, and the whole great air was
choked with it. We shut our eyes and put up with it, this sickness from
the gods.

And when it had passed, a long while later, there was the girl. She was
crying out, the sharp cry of a bird in bitter grief, the way a bird
cries when she looks into her nest and finds it empty and the young ones
gone. That is how she was when she saw the body bare: she wailed out
loud, and she called down evil curses on the men who had done it. Then
straight away she brought dry dust in her hands, and from a fine bronze
jug held up high she crowned the dead man with a triple pouring of
drink-offerings. We saw it and went for her, and closed round her at
once, and she was not frightened at all. We charged her with what she
had done before and what she had done just now, and she denied none of
it — which was sweet to me, and painful at the same time. Getting out of
trouble yourself is the sweetest thing there is. Bringing trouble on
people you like is painful. But all of that counts for less with me than
my own skin.
