Oedipus at Colonus
(Part 2 of 4)

Chorus.
You deserve pity, Oedipus, you and these two girls with you. And since
you add to that plea the claim that you will be this country's saviour,
we want to give you some advice that is in your interest.

Oedipus.
Dearest friends, be my sponsors here, and I will carry out all of it.

Chorus.
Then make an act of cleansing to these goddesses, whose ground you
walked on when you first came in.

Oedipus.
What is the way of doing it? Teach me, strangers.

Chorus.
First bring holy water for the offering from a spring that never runs
dry, and touch it only with hands that are clean.

Oedipus.
And when I have got this pure water?

Chorus.
There are bowls, the work of a skilled hand. Wreathe the rims of them,
and the handles on both sides.

Oedipus.
With leafy twigs, or strips of wool, or in what way?

Chorus.
With the new-shorn fleece of a young ewe.

Oedipus.
Very well. And after that, how do I finish it?

Chorus.
Pour the offering out, standing with your face to the first light of
dawn.

Oedipus.
Do I pour it from those jars you spoke of?

Chorus.
Yes, three streams; and empty the last jar to the bottom.

Oedipus.
And what do I fill that one with before I set it down? Teach me this
too.

Chorus.
With water and with honey. Bring no wine near it.

Oedipus.
And when the earth under its dark leaves has taken all this?

Chorus.
Lay three times nine sprigs of olive on it with both your hands, and
say this prayer over them.

Oedipus.
I want to hear the prayer. That is the most important part.

Chorus.
We call them the Kindly Ones. So ask them, out of hearts that are
kindly, to receive the man who begs their protection and to keep him
safe. Ask it yourself, or let someone else ask it for you, speaking
so low that no one can hear and never raising your voice. Then walk
away, and do not turn round. Do all that and we would stand beside you
with a good heart. Otherwise, stranger, we would be afraid for you.

Oedipus.
Daughters, do you hear what these men of the place are saying?

Antigone.
We heard. Tell us what has to be done.

Oedipus.
I cannot make the walk. Two things are against me: I have no strength
and I have no eyes. One of you must go and do it in my place. I think
one soul can pay this debt for ten thousand, if it comes with a good
will. But do it quickly. Only do not leave me alone. This body of mine
has not the strength to crawl about with no one there and no hand to
guide it.

Ismene.
I will go and do it. But I want to be told where I am to find the
place.

Chorus.
On the far side of this grove, young woman. And if you find you need
anything, there is a keeper of the place who will show you.

Ismene.
Then I will go. Antigone, you stay here and watch over our father.
When it is for a parent, whatever the labour is, it is not to be
counted as labour.

(Ismene goes into the grove.)

Chorus.
	It is a terrible thing, stranger, to wake an old evil
	that has lain quiet so long. And still I long to ask.

Oedipus.
	Ask what?

Chorus.
	About that misery with no way out of it,
	the pain you have wrestled with.

Oedipus.
	By the welcome you have given me, do not open up
	the shameful things that were done to me.

Chorus.
	The story is everywhere and it never dies down.
	I want to hear it, friend, told straight.

Oedipus.
	Oh.

Chorus.
	Give way. I beg you.

Oedipus.
	Oh. Oh.

Chorus.
	Do as I ask. I did all that you asked of me.

Oedipus.
	Then I have borne the worst of evils, strangers.
	I bore it, but not by my own will.
	Let a god be witness.
	Not one part of it was chosen.

Chorus.
	But what was it?

Oedipus.
	A wicked marriage. The city tied me,
	knowing nothing, to a bed that was my ruin.

Chorus.
	Is it true, as I hear, that you filled
	your mother's bed, a bed with an evil name?

Oedipus.
	Oh. It is death to hear it said, stranger.
	And these two girls of mine —

Chorus.
	What are you saying?

Oedipus.
	Two children. Two curses —

Chorus.
	Oh, Zeus.

Oedipus.
	— born from the same womb that bore me.

Chorus.
	Then they are your children, and also —

Oedipus.
	Yes. Their own father's sisters.

Chorus.
	Oh.

Oedipus.
	Oh, yes. Ten thousand evils, wheeling back on me.

Chorus.
	You suffered —

Oedipus.
	I suffered what can never be forgotten.

Chorus.
	You did it —

Oedipus.
	I did not do it.

Chorus.
	Then what?

Oedipus.
	I was given a gift.
	I wish to my aching heart I had never
	earned that gift from the city I had served.

Chorus.
	Wretched man. And then? The killing —

Oedipus.
	What is this? What do you want to know?

Chorus.
	— of your father?

Oedipus.
	Ah. You strike a second time. Wound on top of wound.

Chorus.
	You killed —

Oedipus.
	I killed. But there is something in it for me —

Chorus.
	What?

Oedipus.
	— on the side of justice.

Chorus.
	How?

Oedipus.
	I will tell you.
	The men I killed would have killed me.
	Before the law I am clean. I came to it not knowing.

Chorus.
Look, here is our king, Theseus, son of Aegeus. Your message reached
him, and he has come to do what it asked.

(Theseus comes in.)

Theseus.
I have heard from so many people over the years about the bloody
ruin of your eyes that I knew who you were, son of Laius; and what I
have been told on the road just now makes me more certain still. Your
clothes and that unhappy face tell us plainly who you are. I pity you,
and I want to ask you, ill-starred Oedipus, what it is you have come
to beg from my city and from me, you and the poor girl who stands
beside you. Tell me. It would have to be some terrible business you
described before I would turn my back on it. I know what it is to be
brought up in exile, as you were, and in foreign lands I have wrestled
with more dangers to my own life than any man alive. So I would never
turn away from a stranger like you and refuse to help him to safety.
I know very well that I am a man, and that I have no larger share in
tomorrow than you have.

Oedipus.
Theseus, your generosity in a few words has left me needing only a few
in reply. You have said yourself who I am, who my father was and what
land I come from. So nothing is left for me but to say what I want,
and then the talking is over.

Theseus.
Then tell me exactly that, so that I understand it.

Oedipus.
I have come to give you my wretched body as a gift. It is not much to
look at. But what comes of it is worth more than a handsome shape.

Theseus.
And what is this gain you claim to be bringing?

Oedipus.
In time you will learn it. Not now, I think.

Theseus.
Then when will your offering show itself?

Oedipus.
When I am dead, and you have buried me.

Theseus.
You are asking for the last service of a life. What lies in between,
you have either forgotten or you count as nothing.

Oedipus.
Yes. Because in that one thing all the rest is gathered up for me.

Theseus.
Well, then, this favour you ask of me is a small one.

Oedipus.
Be careful, though. This is not a small struggle. No, it is not.

Theseus.
Do you mean between your sons and me?

Oedipus.
They will want to carry me back there, my lord. To Thebes.

Theseus.
But if you are willing to go, exile is no fit thing even for you.

Oedipus.
No. When I was willing, they would not allow it.

Theseus.
Fool. Anger is no use to a man in trouble.

Oedipus.
When you have heard me out, lecture me. Until then, let it be.

Theseus.
Speak. I should not say anything without understanding.

Oedipus.
I have suffered, Theseus. Terrible things, evil on top of evil.

Theseus.
Do you mean the old disaster of your family?

Oedipus.
No. Every Greek talks about that.

Theseus.
Then what is this sickness of yours that is bigger than a man's?

Oedipus.
It is this. I was driven out of my own country by my own sons. And I
can never go back, because I killed my father.

Theseus.
Then why would they send for you, if you must live apart from them?

Oedipus.
The voice of the god will force them to it.

Theseus.
What do the oracles say they should be afraid of?

Oedipus.
That they must be struck down in this land.

Theseus.
And how could there be bitterness between them and me?

Oedipus.
Dearest son of Aegeus, only to the gods does old age never come, and
death never. Everything else all-mastering time throws into
confusion. The strength of the earth decays, and the strength of the
body; trust dies, and distrust grows up in its place; and the same
wind never blows for long between one man and his friends, or between
city and city. For some now, for others later, what was sweet turns
bitter, and then turns back to love. If it is all fine weather now
between Thebes and you, time in its endless going gives birth to
endless nights and days, and in one of them the pledges that join
hands today will be scattered by the spear over some small word. And
then, one day, my corpse, sleeping and hidden and cold, will drink
their warm blood — if Zeus is still Zeus, and Apollo, the son of Zeus,
speaks true.

But there is no pleasure in saying words that should stay unmoved.
Let me stop where I began. Only keep your own word good, and you will
never say that you took Oedipus in as a useless resident of this place
— unless the gods are lying to me.

Chorus.
My lord, from the first this man has shown that he means to carry out
these promises, or promises like them, for our land.

Theseus.
Who would throw away the goodwill of a man like this? First, the
hearth of an ally in war is always open to him with us, by a right
that goes both ways. Then, he has come here to beg protection from
our gods, and he pays this land and me no small tribute for it. I
respect those claims. I will never throw away what he offers, and I
will settle him in this country as one of our own. If it pleases the
stranger to stay here, I will order you to guard him. Or, if he
prefers, he can come with me. Choose whichever you like, Oedipus, and
take it. I will go along with you either way.

Oedipus.
Zeus, be good to men like this one.

Theseus.
Then what do you want? To come to my house?

Oedipus.
If it were allowed me. But this is the place —

Theseus.
Where you will do what? I will not stand in your way.

Oedipus.
— where I will get the better of the men who threw me out.

Theseus.
Then the gift of your staying here would be a great one.

Oedipus.
Yes, if you hold to what you have said and do it for me.

Theseus.
Have no fear on that man's account. I will not betray you.

Oedipus.
I will not bind you with an oath, as if you were a bad man.

Theseus.
You would get nothing more by it than you have by my word.

Oedipus.
Then how will you manage it?

Theseus.
What is it that frightens you most?

Oedipus.
Men will come —

Theseus.
These men here will see to that.

Oedipus.
Take care, if you leave me —

Theseus.
Do not tell me what I have to do.

Oedipus.
Fear makes me —

Theseus.
My heart feels no fear.

Oedipus.
You do not know the threats —

Theseus.
I know that no man will take you out of here by force against my
will. Threats have blustered many times, many loud and empty words
out of a man's temper; but when the mind comes back to itself, the
threats are gone. And as for those people — even if they have talked
themselves up to some bold plan of carrying you off, I know that the
sea between here and there will turn out long, and hard to sail.
Whatever I may decide, I tell you to take heart, if it really was
Apollo who sent you here. And even when I am not with you, I know my
name will guard you from harm.

(Theseus goes.)

Chorus.
	Stranger, in this land of fine horses
	you have come to the fairest farmstead on earth,
	white Colonus, where the nightingale,
	clear-voiced, sings and sings,
	a constant guest in the green hollows,
	keeping to the wine-dark ivy
	and the god's untrodden leaves
	heavy with ten thousand berries,
	where no sun comes
	and no wind out of any storm;
	where Dionysus, drunk with his own joy,
	walks always on the ground,
	going about with the goddesses who nursed him.

Chorus.
	And under the dew of heaven
	the narcissus with its lovely clusters
	blooms new every day,
	the ancient crown of the two great goddesses,
	Demeter and her daughter;
	and the crocus, with its gleam of gold.
	The springs never sleep and never dwindle,
	the springs that feed the wandering streams of Cephisus,
	but every day it comes down over the plain
	with its clean water, quick to bring the crops,
	over the broad breast of the land.
	The dancing Muses have never turned away from this place,
	nor has

Chorus.
	Aphrodite with her golden reins.
	And there is a thing here I have not heard of
	in the land of Asia, nor ever yet
	in the great Dorian island of Pelops:
	a tree no hand has tamed, that plants itself,
	a terror to the spears of the enemy,
	and it grows best of all in this land —
	the grey-leaved olive, the nurse of children.
	No man young or old
	will lay a hand on it and wreck it and get away,
	for the eye of Zeus who guards the olives
	looks on it always and never closes,
	and so does grey-eyed Athena.

Chorus.
	And I have another praise for this mother city,
	the strongest of all, the gift of a great god,
	the land's proudest boast:
	good horses, good foals, and a good sea.
	Son of Cronus, Lord Poseidon,
	it was you who set her on that height of pride,
	when on these roads, the first of any,
	you made the bit that cures the rage of horses.
	And the oar, well shaped for the hand,
	that goes flashing past the land on the salt water,
	leaps on the sea to follow
	the Nereids with their hundred feet.

Antigone.
Land that is praised above all lands, now is the time to show these
bright words in what you do.

Oedipus.
What is it, child? What has happened?

Antigone.
Creon is coming towards us, father. He is close, and he has men with
him.

Oedipus.
Dearest old men, now let me have from you the last proof that I am
safe.

Chorus.
Take heart. You will have it. I may be old, but the strength of this
country has not grown old.

(Creon comes in, with armed men behind him.)

Creon.
Gentlemen, well-born people of this land, I can see in your eyes that
my coming has put a sudden fear into you. Do not shrink from me, and
do not let a hard word slip out. I have not come here meaning to do
anything. I am an old man, and I know that the city I have come to is
strong — as strong as any in Greece. No. I have been sent, old as I
am, to persuade this man to come with me back to the land of Cadmus.
Not sent by any one man: the whole people gave me the order, because
it falls to me, as his kinsman, more than to anyone in Thebes, to
grieve for what has happened to him.

Poor Oedipus, listen to me and come home. All the people of Cadmus
call you, and they have the right to; and I most of all, because —
unless I am the worst man ever born — I hurt most for your troubles,
old man, when I see you, wretched as you are, a stranger and a
wanderer for ever, going about with nothing to live on and one girl
to lean on. I never thought, poor fool that I am, that she could sink
as low as she has sunk, this unlucky child, looking after you and
that head of yours for ever on a beggar's diet, at her age, with no
husband, and there for the first passer-by to seize. Is that not a
miserable reproach — oh, it is misery to say it — that I have
thrown at you and at me and at our whole family? But what is out in
the open cannot be hidden. So, Oedipus, by the gods of your fathers,
listen to me and hide it: agree to come to the city and the house of
your fathers. Say a kind goodbye to this city, which has deserved it.
But the city at home has the older claim on your respect, because it
was Thebes that raised you, long ago.

Oedipus.
You who will dare anything, who can spin a cunning device out of any
honest argument — why are you trying this on me? Why do you want to
catch me a second time, in the trap where being caught would hurt me
most? Before, when I was sick with the evils of my own house and it
would have been a joy to me to be thrown out of the country, you
would not do me that favour, though I wanted it. But when my anger
had had its fill at last and living quietly in the house had become
sweet to me — that was when you shoved me out and threw me away. And
this kinship you talk about was nothing to you then. And now, again,
when you see that this city and its whole people have taken me in and
wish me well, you try to drag me away, saying hard things in a soft
voice.

And what pleasure is there in being loved against your will? It is as
if a man begged you and begged you for something and you gave him
nothing and would not help him, and then, when his heart was full of
all he had wanted, you handed it over — when the favour was no favour
at all. Would that not be an empty kind of pleasure? Yet that is
exactly what you are offering me: good in the words, evil in the
deeds. And I will say it in front of these men too, to show them what
you are. You have come to fetch me — not to take me home, but to plant
me on your border, so that your city may get clear of this land
without harm. That is not what you will get. What you will get is
this: my avenging spirit living in your country for ever. And my sons
will get just so much of my land as will serve them to die in.

Do I not understand the affairs of Thebes better than you do? Far
better — because the voices I hear them from are truer: Apollo's, and
Zeus's himself, who is Apollo's father. But your mouth has come here
with a lie in it, and a very sharp edge; and with all your talking you
are more likely to reap harm than safety. Still, I know I will not
persuade you of that. Go. And let us live here. Even as we are, our
life would not be a bad one, if we were content with it.

Creon.
Which of us do you think comes off worse in this exchange — me, by
what you have said, or you, by your own words?

Oedipus.
The sweetest thing for me is that you cannot persuade me, and cannot
persuade these men beside me either.

Creon.
You poor wretch. Will you show everyone that not even time has grown
you a mind, and that you feed your old age on what disgraces it?

Oedipus.
You have a clever tongue. But I never knew an honest man who could
make a fine speech out of any case at all.

Creon.
Saying a great deal and saying what fits the moment are two different
things.

Oedipus.
As if what you say were short, and fitted the moment.

Creon.
Not to anyone with a mind like yours, it does not.

Oedipus.
Get out. I say it for these men too. And stop standing guard over me,
watching the place where I am meant to stay.

Creon.
I call these men to witness, not you, how you answer your own people.
And if I ever get my hands on you —

Oedipus.
And who is going to lay hands on me, against all these allies of mine?

Creon.
I promise you, you will be hurt soon enough without that.

Oedipus.
What have you done, that lets you make that threat?

Creon.
Of your two daughters, one I have just now seized and sent away. The
other I will take with me shortly.

Oedipus.
Oh.

Creon.
You will soon have more to cry about than that.

Oedipus.
You have my child?

Creon.
And this one too, before long.

Oedipus.
Oh, strangers, what will you do? Will you betray me? Will you not
drive this godless man out of your land?

Chorus.
Out, stranger. Quickly. There is no justice in what you are doing now,
and none in what you did before.

Creon.
(to his men) It is time you took this girl away, whether she likes it
or not, since she will not come willingly.

Antigone.
Oh, no. Where can I run to? What help can I find, from gods or from
men?

Chorus.
What are you doing, stranger?

Creon.
I will not touch this man. Only what is mine.

Oedipus.
Lords of this land.

Chorus.
Stranger, what you are doing is not just.

Creon.
It is just.

Chorus.
How is it just?

Creon.
I am taking my own.

Oedipus.
Oh, city.

Chorus.
What are you doing, stranger? Let her go. It will come to a trial of
strength between us soon.

Creon.
Stand off.

Chorus.
Not from you. Not while this is what you mean to do.

Creon.
You will be at war with Thebes if you do me any harm.

Oedipus.
Did I not say so?

Chorus.
Take your hands off the girl. Now.

Creon.
Do not give orders where you are not the master.

Chorus.
Let her go, I tell you.

Creon.
And I tell you: be on your way.

Chorus.
Here, men of Colonus — come here, come here. The city is being taken
by force. My city. Come here to me.

Antigone.
They are dragging me away. Strangers. Strangers.

Oedipus.
Child, where are you?

Antigone.
They are taking me by force.

Oedipus.
Reach out your hands to me, child.

Antigone.
I cannot. I have no strength.

Creon.
(to his men) Take her. Go.

Oedipus.
Oh. I am wretched, wretched.

(The men go off with Antigone.)

Creon.
So. You will never go walking on those two sticks again. And since you
are set on beating your own country and your own people — whose
orders I am carrying out, though I am the ruler there too — go on and
beat them. In time, I know, you will understand this: that you are
doing yourself no good now, and did none before, when you went against
your own people and gave your anger its way. Your anger has always
been the ruin of you.

(He moves towards Oedipus. The Chorus closes in on him.)

Chorus.
Stop where you are, stranger.

Creon.
Keep your hands off me, I tell you.

Chorus.
I will not let you go until you give back those girls.

Creon.
Then you will soon be paying the city a bigger ransom. I will lay hold
of more than those two.

Chorus.
What will you turn to next?

Creon.
I will take this man, and carry him off.

Chorus.
That is a terrible thing to say.

Creon.
It will be done, now.

Chorus.
Not unless the ruler of this land stops you.

Oedipus.
Voice without shame. You are going to lay hands on me?

Creon.
Be quiet, I tell you.

Oedipus.
No. May the goddesses of this place not take my voice from me before
I have said this one curse more. You, worst of men, when these eyes
of mine were already dark, you tore from me the helpless girl who was
my eyes, and you are making off with her by force. And so may the
Sun, the god who sees everything, give you and your family a life
like mine — an old age exactly like this.

Creon.
Do you see this, people of the land?

Oedipus.
They see you and they see me. And they understand that what was done
to me was done with hands, and I fight back with words.

Creon.
I will not hold my temper any longer. I will take this man by force,
alone as I am and slow with age.

Oedipus.
Oh. I am wretched.

Chorus.
What arrogance you brought here with you, stranger, if you think you
can carry this off.

Creon.
I think so.

Chorus.
Then I no longer count this a city at all.

Creon.
When a man has justice on his side, the small man beats the big one.
