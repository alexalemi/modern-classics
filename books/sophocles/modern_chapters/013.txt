Electra
(Part 2 of 3)

Electra.
You will not say, this time at least, that I started it — that I said
something to hurt you first and then got this from you in return. But
if you will let me, I would like to say what is right on behalf of the
dead man, and of my sister with him.

Clytemnestra.
I will let you. And if you always opened with me in that tone, you
would not be so painful to listen to.

Electra.
Then here it is. You say you killed my father. Is there any admission
more shameful than that, whether you were right to do it or not? But I
will show you that you had no right. It was persuasion that dragged you
into it — the persuasion of a bad man, the one you live with now.

Ask Artemis the huntress what she was punishing when she held all those
winds at Aulis. Or I will tell you, since we are not allowed to learn it
from her. My father once, as I hear it, was out hunting in the
goddess's grove, and his footfall startled a dappled stag with antlers,
and he killed it, and over the kill he let some boast fall. Leto's
daughter was angry at that, and she held the Greeks there until my
father should sacrifice his own daughter to balance the beast. That is
how she came to be sacrificed. There was no other release for the
army, not homeward and not to Troy. So he was forced to it, and fought
against it, and at last, barely, he killed her — and not for the sake
of Menelaus.

But suppose — I will make your case for you — suppose he did it because
he wanted to help his brother. Did he have to die for that, at your
hands? Under what law? Be careful. If you lay that law down for people,
you may be laying down pain and regret for yourself. Because if we are
going to kill one person for another, you would be the first to die, if
you got justice.

So look at whether this excuse of yours is any excuse at all. Tell me,
if you will, what you are paying back now by doing the most shameful
things of all: sleeping with the man whose hands are bloody, the man
you first destroyed my father with, and having children by him — while
the children you had before, born in decency from a decent marriage,
you have thrown out. How am I supposed to approve of that? Or will you
say this too is payment for your daughter? A shameful thing to say, if
you do say it. There is nothing fine about marrying an enemy for a
daughter's sake.

But there is no arguing with you. You let your whole tongue loose about
how I abuse my mother. And I count you less a mother than a master over
me, given the miserable life I lead, always in the troubles that come
from you and from the man you keep. And the other one, out there, who
barely escaped your hand — poor Orestes — grinds out an unhappy life.
You have accused me many times of raising him as the avenger against
you. And I would have, believe me, if I had had the strength. So on
that account, proclaim me to everyone, if you like: bad, loud-mouthed,
full of shamelessness. If I was born with a talent for that kind of
thing, then I am not far from doing credit to your nature.

Chorus.
I see her breathing rage. But whether she has justice on her side — I
no longer see her caring about that.

Clytemnestra.
And what kind of care do I owe to her, when she has done such outrage
to the mother who bore her — and at her age? Do you not think she would
go to any length, with no shame at all?

Electra.
Understand this: I am ashamed of it, whatever you think. I know that
what I do is unfit for my age and unlike me. But your hatred and your
actions force me to it, against my will. Shameful behaviour is taught
by shameful behaviour.

Clytemnestra.
You shameless creature. I and my words and my actions give you far too
much to say.

Electra.
It is you who say it, not me. You do the deed, and the deeds find their
own words.

Clytemnestra.
Well, by our lady Artemis, you will not get away with this boldness
once Aegisthus comes home.

Electra.
You see? You are carried off into a rage. You let me say what I liked,
and you have no idea how to listen.

Clytemnestra.
Will you not even let me make my sacrifice without your ill-omened
shouting, now that I have let you say everything?

Electra.
I let you. I urge you. Sacrifice. And do not blame my mouth, because I
will not say another word.

Clytemnestra.
(to the attendant) You there — lift up the offerings, the fruits of
every kind, so that I can raise my prayers to this lord whose statue
stands at the door, prayers to be released from the fears I have now.

Apollo, guardian of this house, hear me now, though my words are
hidden. I am not among friends when I speak, and it is not fitting to
unfold all of it to the light with her standing at my elbow, or she
will take her spite and her chattering tongue and sow an idle story
through the whole city. So hear me like this. This is how I will say
it.

The visions I saw last night, in dreams that could be read two ways —
if they came to me for good, Lord Apollo, grant that they are
fulfilled; if for harm, throw them back on the people who wish me harm.
And if anyone is scheming to throw me out, by trickery, of the wealth I
have now, do not allow it. Let me go on living as I do, unharmed,
holding the house of the sons of Atreus and this sceptre; living with
the people I live with now, in good days, and with those of my children
who bear me no ill will and no bitter grudge. Lord Apollo, hear this
kindly and give it to us all, just as we ask. And everything else,
though I say nothing of it — you are a god, and I take it you know. The
children of Zeus, I suppose, see everything.

(The Tutor comes in, as a traveller from Phocis.)

Tutor.
Ladies — I am a stranger here — how can I be sure that this is the
house of the king, Aegisthus?

Chorus.
This is it, stranger. You have guessed right.

Tutor.
And am I right again in guessing that this lady is his wife? She has
the look of a queen.

Chorus.
Right in every way. That is she, in front of you.

Tutor.
Greetings, my queen. I come bringing you sweet news, and Aegisthus too,
from a friend.

Clytemnestra.
I accept the greeting. But first I want to know from you who it was who
sent you.

Tutor.
Phanoteus of Phocis, on a matter of weight.

Clytemnestra.
What matter, stranger? Tell me. You come from a friend, so I know the
news you bring will be friendly.

Tutor.
Orestes is dead. That is the short of it.

Electra.
Oh — no. I am finished. This is the day.

Clytemnestra.
What did you say? What did you say, stranger? Do not listen to her.

Tutor.
I said it and I say it again. Orestes is dead.

Electra.
I am destroyed. I am nothing any more.

Clytemnestra.
You — mind your own business. You, stranger, tell me the truth. How
did he die?

Tutor.
That is what I was sent for, and I will tell you all of it.

He went to the great showplace of Greece, to compete for the prizes at
Delphi. When he heard the herald call out in his high voice for the
foot race, which is the first event to be judged, he came in shining,
and every man there was in awe of him. He ran the course from the start
to the finish and came out with the prize of victory, and all the
honour that goes with it. To tell you a little where there is a great
deal to tell — I never knew a man to do what that man did, or win as he
won. But know this one thing: in every contest the judges announced,
the sprints, the double course, the pentathlon with its five events, he
carried off all the victories and was called a happy man, proclaimed
each time as an Argive, Orestes by name, son of Agamemnon, who once
gathered the famous army of Greece.

So far, so good. But when a god does harm, not even a strong man can
escape it. Another day, at sunrise, came the fast race of the horses,
and he entered it with many charioteers. One was an Achaean; one was
from Sparta; two were Libyans, masters of yoked cars; and he among
them, driving Thessalian mares, was the fifth. The sixth was from
Aetolia, with chestnut colts; the seventh was a man from Magnesia; the
eighth, with white horses, was of the Aenian people; the ninth was from
Athens, the city the gods built; and a Boeotian was another, filling
the tenth chariot.

They took their places where the appointed judges had set them by lot
and ranged the cars, and at the bronze trumpet they were off. All of
them shouted at their horses together and shook the reins in their
hands; the whole course filled with the crash of rattling chariots; the
dust went up. They were all packed together, none of them sparing the
goad, each trying to get past the others' wheel-hubs and their snorting
horses; for the breath of the horses was foaming all round their backs
and round the rolling wheels, and breaking over them.

Orestes, keeping right under the pillar at the end, was grazing it with
his wheel-hub every time, giving rein to the trace-horse on the right
and holding in the one on the inside. And up to then every chariot was
still upright. But then the Aenian's hard-mouthed colts got away with
him by force, and coming out of the turn, finishing the sixth lap and
beginning the seventh, they dashed their foreheads against the chariot
from Barca. And from there, out of that one accident, one car smashed
into another and went over, and the whole plain of Crisa filled up with
the wreckage of chariots.

Tutor.
The clever driver from Athens saw it and pulled his horses out wide and
held back, letting the wave of horses go by that was churning in the
middle. Orestes was driving last, holding his colts back, trusting the
finish. But when he sees that only one man is left in against him, he
sends a sharp cry through the ears of his fast colts and goes after
him. They brought their yokes level and drove, first one man, then the
other, showing his head in front of the other's car.

And all the other laps the poor man got through safely, upright in an
upright chariot. Then, letting out the left rein while the horse was
turning, he struck the edge of the pillar without knowing it. He broke
the axle-box in the middle and slid over the rail and was tangled in
the cut reins; and as he fell to the ground his colts scattered into
the middle of the course.

When the crowd saw him thrown from his chariot, they cried out for the
young man — what deeds he had done, and what he had drawn instead —
dragged along the ground, now with his legs showing against the sky,
until the charioteers, with difficulty, got the running horses under
control and cut him loose, so bloody that not one of his friends,
seeing him, would have known the pitiful body. They burned him on a
pyre at once, and chosen men of Phocis are bringing that mighty frame,
now a wretched handful of ash, in a small bronze urn, so that he can
have his share of a tomb in his father's country.

That is my story. It is painful even as a story; to those who saw it,
as we saw it, it was the worst thing my eyes have ever looked on.

Chorus.
Oh, oh. So the whole line of our old masters is destroyed, it seems,
root and all.

Clytemnestra.
Zeus, what is this? Do I call it good luck? Or terrible, but a gain? It
is a painful thing, when I save my own life by my own losses.

Tutor.
Why so downcast, lady, at what I have told you?

Clytemnestra.
There is a terrible power in giving birth. Even when they treat you
badly, you cannot hate the ones you bore.

Tutor.
Then it seems we came for nothing.

Clytemnestra.
Not for nothing. How can you say for nothing, when you came bringing me
sure proof of his death? He came out of my own life, and left my breast
and my care, and made himself a stranger, an exile. Once he left this
country he never saw me again; he charged me with his father's murder
and swore he would do terrible things to me. So that neither by night
nor by day would sweet sleep cover me; every hour standing over me led
me on as a woman about to die. But now — because today I am free of the
fear of him, and of her; she was the greater harm, living in my house,
drinking my life's blood undiluted, always — now, I think, for all her
threats, I will pass my days in peace.

Electra.
Oh, no. Now, Orestes, I can cry for what has happened to you, when this
is what you have come to and your mother, this woman, outrages you for
it. Is it not fine?

Clytemnestra.
Not for you. He is fine, as he is.

Electra.
Hear her, Nemesis of the newly dead.

Clytemnestra.
She heard whom she should, and settled it well.

Electra.
Outrage us. Today is your lucky day.

Clytemnestra.
Then you and Orestes will not be putting a stop to it.

Electra.
We are the ones stopped. We will not be stopping you.

Clytemnestra.
Stranger, you would deserve a great reward for your coming, if you have
stopped that chattering tongue.

Tutor.
Then I will take my leave, if all is well.

Clytemnestra.
Certainly not. That would be no welcome worthy of me, or of the friend
who sent you. Come inside. Leave her out here to howl her own troubles
and the troubles of her friends.

(Clytemnestra and the Tutor go into the house.)

Electra.
Did she look to you like a woman in pain and grief, weeping terribly
and crying out over her son, poor woman, dead like that? She left
laughing. Oh, I am wretched. Orestes, my dearest, your death has
destroyed me. You have gone, and torn out of my heart the only hopes I
had left — that you would come back one day alive, to avenge our
father, and me in my misery. Now where am I to turn? I am alone. Robbed
of you, and of my father. Now I must be a slave again among the people
I hate most in the world, my father's murderers. Is it not fine for me?

But no. Not for what time is left me. I will not go inside and live
under one roof with them. I will lie down here at this gate, with no
friend, and let my life wither. And if that weighs on anyone in that
house, let them come out and kill me. Killing me is a kindness; living
is the pain. I have no wish for life.

Chorus.
	Where are the thunderbolts of Zeus,
	where is the blazing Sun,
	if they look down on this
	and calmly cover it over?

Electra.
	Ah. Aiai.

Chorus.
	Child, why do you weep?

Electra.
	Oh.

Chorus.
	No loud cry. It is ill-omened.

Electra.
	You will kill me.

Chorus.
	How?

Electra.
	If you hold out hope
	for the ones who have plainly gone
	down to Hades, you tread
	the harder on me as I waste away.

Chorus.
	I know of a king, Amphiaraus,
	whom a woman's chain of gold,
	a bribe, trapped and hid underground.
	And now, under the earth —

Electra.
	Ah. Oh.

Chorus.
	he rules, with all his wits alive.

Electra.
	Oh.

Chorus.
	Oh, yes. Because the woman who destroyed him —

Electra.
	was cut down.

Chorus.
	Yes.

Electra.
	I know it, I know it. Someone rose
	to care for the man in his grief.
	For me there is no one now.
	The one who was left
	has been snatched away and gone.

Chorus.
	Wretched, and a wretched lot.

Electra.
	And I know it, too well,
	in a life swept along
	month after month by a flood
	of terrible and hateful things.

Chorus.
	We have seen what you cry for.

Electra.
	Then do not turn me aside
	any longer, since no —

Chorus.
	What do you mean?

Electra.
	— no help is left me
	from the brother I hoped in,
	born of the same noble father.

Chorus.
	Death was born for everyone.

Electra.
	Death like that? Among racing hooves,
	the way that poor man met it,
	tangled in the cutting reins?

Chorus.
	The mangling is past imagining.

Electra.
	How could it not be? In a strange land,
	without my hands to tend him —

Chorus.
	Oh, no.

Electra.
	— he lies buried, given no tomb by me
	and no lament from us.

(Chrysothemis comes in, hurrying.)

Chrysothemis.
Dearest sister, joy is driving me. I have dropped all decorum to come at
a run. I bring you delight — and rest from the troubles you had before,
and groaned under.

Electra.
And where would you find help for my sufferings? There is no cure for
them anywhere in sight.

Chrysothemis.
Orestes is here with us. Know it from my mouth: as plainly as you see
me now.

Electra.
Have you gone mad, poor girl? Are you laughing at your own troubles and
mine?

Chrysothemis.
By our father's hearth, I am not saying it to mock you. I am saying he
is here, with us two.

Electra.
Oh, poor girl. Whose story is this that you have heard and believe so
far?

Chrysothemis.
Nobody's. I believe it from myself and no one else. I saw the clear
signs.

Electra.
What proof, poor girl? What did you look at, that warms you with this
fever there is no curing?

Chrysothemis.
In the gods' name, listen, and then, when you have heard the rest from
me, call me sane or a fool.

Electra.
Go on, then, if talking gives you pleasure.

Chrysothemis.
I will tell you everything I saw. When I came to our father's old tomb,
I saw fresh streams of milk running from the top of the mound, and the
grave wreathed all round with every flower there is. I was amazed at
the sight, and I looked about in case anyone was coming near me. But
when I saw that the whole place was quiet, I crept closer to the tomb,
and at the edge of the pyre I saw a lock of hair, newly cut.

The moment I saw it — oh, I am wretched — a familiar image struck into
my heart, and I felt I was looking at a token of Orestes, of everyone
alive the one I love best. I took it up in my hands, and I said nothing
ill-omened, but my eyes filled with tears of joy at once. And now, as
much as then, I know for certain that this bright gift came from no one
but him. Who else has any business there but you and me? I did not do
it, that I know. Nor did you — how could you? You cannot even leave
this house to worship the gods without paying for it in tears. And our
mother's mind does not run to doing such things, and she could not have
done them without our knowing. No. These offerings at the tomb are
from Orestes.

Take heart, my dear. The same god does not stand by the same people for
ever. Ours was hateful to us before; but this day, perhaps, will set
the seal on many good things.

Electra.
Oh, the folly. How I have been pitying you.

Chrysothemis.
What is it? Is what I say not welcome?

Electra.
You do not know where on earth you are, or where your mind is
wandering.

Chrysothemis.
How can I not know what I saw with my own eyes?

Electra.
He is dead, poor girl. Your rescue by him is gone. Do not look to him
for anything.

Chrysothemis.
Oh, no. Who told you that?

Electra.
The man who was there beside him when he died.

Chrysothemis.
And where is he? I am beginning to feel very strange.

Electra.
In the house — a welcome guest, and not at all unpleasant to our
mother.

Chrysothemis.
Oh, no. Then whose were all those offerings at our father's tomb?

Electra.
Most likely, I think, someone left them there as a memorial to the
dead Orestes.

Chrysothemis.
Oh, this is wretched. And I came hurrying here with news like that,
with joy in it, not knowing after all where our ruin stood. And now
that I have come, I find the old troubles, and new ones on top of them.

Electra.
That is how it stands with you. But if you will listen to me, you will
lift the weight of the trouble we have now.

Chrysothemis.
Am I going to raise the dead to life some day?

Electra.
That is not what I said. I was not born that much of a fool.

Chrysothemis.
Then what are you telling me to do, that I am able to do?

Electra.
To have the courage to do what I advise.

Chrysothemis.
If there is any good in it, I will not refuse.

Electra.
Look — nothing succeeds without hard work.

Chrysothemis.
I know. I will carry my share of it, as far as I have strength.

Electra.
Then hear how I have decided to act. You know as well as I do that we
have no friends standing by us: Hades has taken them and robbed us, and
we two are left alone. As long as I heard that my brother was alive and
thriving, I had hopes that he would come one day to exact payment for
our father's murder. But now that he is gone, I look to you. Do not
flinch from helping me, your sister, to kill the man who did the murder
with his own hand — Aegisthus. I have no need to hide anything from
you any more.

Because what are you waiting for, doing nothing? What hope is there
still standing that you can look to? You have reason to groan that you
have been robbed of your father's wealth; you have reason to grieve
that you have come this far in years unmarried, without a bridal song.
And do not go on hoping you will ever get those things. Aegisthus is
not such a fool as to let a child of yours, or of mine, ever grow up to
be a plain danger to him. But if you follow my plan, first you will
earn the name of piety from our dead father below, and from our brother
with him. And then you will be called free for the rest of your life,
as you were born, and you will find a marriage worthy of you; because
everyone likes to look at what is good.

And the fame of it. Do you not see how much glory you will bring on
yourself and on me by listening to me? What citizen or stranger, seeing
us, will not greet us with praise like this: "Look at these two
sisters, friends. They saved their father's house. Their enemies were
secure, and they took no thought for their own lives and stood up to do
the killing. Those two should be loved; those two should be honoured by
everybody; at the festivals, and wherever the whole city is gathered,
everyone must honour them for their courage." That is what everyone
will say of us, alive or dead, so that our glory never fails.

My dear, do as I say. Work alongside our father; share the labour with
our brother; put an end to my troubles and to your own. And know this:
for people born well, living shamefully is a shame.

Chorus.
In matters like this, forethought is an ally to the one who speaks and
the one who listens.

Chrysothemis.
Yes — and before she opened her mouth, ladies, if she had had a sound
mind she would have kept hold of caution, as she has not. Where were
your eyes looking, that you arm yourself with boldness like this and
call me to serve under you? Can you not see? You were born a woman, not
a man, and your arm is weaker than your enemies'. Their fortune
prospers day by day; ours is draining away and coming to nothing. Who
could plot to bring down a man like that and get clear of ruin unhurt?
Take care that, badly as we are doing now, we do not do worse, if
anyone hears this talk of yours. It is no release for us and no help to
win a fine name and die a disgraceful death. And death is not the worst
thing; the worst is to want to die and not be able to get even that.

I beg you, before we are utterly destroyed, both of us, and the family
is wiped out — hold back your rage. What you have said I will keep for
you unspoken and undone. But you, get some sense at last, and since you
have no strength, give way to the people who have the power.

Chorus.
Listen to her. There is no better gain a human being can get than
foresight and a sensible mind.

Electra.
You have said nothing I did not expect. I knew very well you would
throw away what I offered. Then the job must be done by my own hand,
and by me alone. I will not leave it undone.
