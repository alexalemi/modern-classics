Catharine Beecher and Harriet Beecher Stowe were daughters of Lyman Beecher, one of the best-known Protestant preachers in nineteenth-century America. Catharine, the elder, was an educator: she founded the Hartford Female Seminary in 1823 and spent her life arguing that women should be trained as seriously as men for the work they actually did. Harriet, her younger sister, wrote Uncle Tom's Cabin (1852), the most widely read American novel of the century.

The American Woman's Home, published in New York in 1869, grew out of Catharine Beecher's Treatise on Domestic Economy of 1841, a standard manual for a generation of American households. Its argument is in its opening pages: that women suffer because they are not trained for the work of the home, and that this work, done well, is a profession requiring science, skill and planning. The book sets out to supply that training, in what it calls the principles of domestic science.

It covers an astonishing range. There are plans for a compact family house, with a movable screen that turns one large room into several, a kitchen arranged as compactly as a steamship's galley, and built-in storage for everything. There are chapters on ventilation, stoves and chimneys, the anatomy of the lungs and the skin, food, clothing, cooking, the care of infants and of the sick, sewing, gardening, domestic animals, and the earth-closet. The last chapters propose a church, school and family home in a single building for neglected neighborhoods. The voice throughout is practical and confident, and religious: the home is a Christian institution, and the household's work is service.

It should also be read as a book of its time and its authors' convictions. Catharine Beecher opposed votes for women, and the book places a woman's power in the home, though it calls the Woman's Rights Conventions a protest against "absurd, unreasonable ideas" of womanhood. The chapters on servants and charity carry the class and religious assumptions of a Protestant middle-class household of the 1860s, and the medical advice reflects what was believed then, not now.

This is a restored edition of the 1869 first edition. The text is that of Project Gutenberg, checked against two scans of the 1869 printing, which corrected it in more than a hundred places where the transcription had misread the page. Gutenberg's text has no pictures, so all 81 illustrations, from the frontispiece and engraved title to the seventy-seven numbered figures, were cut from a 500-dpi scan of the first edition and returned to their places. Each has a description written for this edition. The chapter summaries printed in the book's Contents are set at the head of each chapter, and the Index is omitted. The book is in the public domain.
