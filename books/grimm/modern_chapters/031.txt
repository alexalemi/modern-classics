The Two Brothers

(Part 3 of 3)

Now at the royal table the King said to his daughter, "What did all those wild animals want, that have been coming to you and going in and out of my palace?"

She answered, "I'm not allowed to tell you; but send for the master of these animals and have him brought here, and you'll do well."

The King sent a servant to the inn to invite the stranger, and the servant arrived just as the huntsman had laid his wager with the innkeeper.

"There, sir host," said the huntsman, "now the King sends his servant and invites me; but I don't go in this fashion." And he said to the servant, "I request the Lord King to send me royal clothing, and a carriage with six horses, and servants to attend me."

When the King heard the answer he said to his daughter, "What shall I do?"

She said, "Have him fetched as he wishes to be fetched, and you'll do well."

So the King sent royal apparel, a carriage with six horses, and servants to wait on him. When the huntsman saw them coming he said, "There, sir host, now I'm fetched just as I wanted to be" — and he put on the royal garments, took the handkerchief with the dragon's tongues with him, and drove off to the King.

When the King saw him coming he said to his daughter, "How shall I receive him?"

She answered, "Go to meet him, and you'll do well."

So the King went to meet him and led him in, and his animals followed. The King gave him a seat beside himself and his daughter; and the marshal, as bridegroom, sat on the other side, but did not know the huntsman any more.

And now at that very moment the dragon's seven heads were carried in to be shown, and the King said, "These seven heads were cut off the dragon by the marshal; and so today I give him my daughter for a wife."

At that the huntsman stood up, opened the seven mouths, and said, "Where are the dragon's seven tongues?"

Then the marshal was terrified, and grew pale, and did not know what answer to make; and at last in his distress he said, "Dragons have no tongues."

The huntsman said, "Liars ought to have none. But the dragon's tongues are the victor's proof." And he unfolded the handkerchief, and there all seven of them lay inside it. And he put each tongue into the mouth it belonged to, and every one fitted exactly.

Then he took the handkerchief with the princess's name embroidered on it, showed it to her, and asked whom she had given it to; and she answered, "To the man who killed the dragon."

And then he called his animals and took the collar off each of them, and the golden clasp from the lion, and showed them to her, and asked whom they belonged to. She answered, "The necklace and the golden clasp were mine, and I divided them among the animals that helped to conquer the dragon."

Then the huntsman said, "When I was resting after the fight, and was asleep with weariness, the marshal came and cut off my head. Then he carried the King's daughter away and gave out that it was he who had killed the dragon. That he lied, I prove with the tongues, the handkerchief and the necklace."

And then he told how his animals had healed him with a wonderful root, and how he had travelled about with them for a year, and had come there again at last and learned the marshal's treachery from the innkeeper's story.

Then the King asked his daughter, "Is it true that this man killed the dragon?"

And she answered, "Yes, it's true. Now I can reveal the marshal's wicked deed, since it has come out without my breaking my word — for he wrung a promise of silence out of me. And that was why I made the condition that the marriage should not be celebrated for a year and a day."

Then the King ordered twelve councillors to be summoned to pass judgement on the marshal, and they sentenced him to be torn to pieces by four bulls. So the marshal was executed; but the King gave his daughter to the huntsman and named him viceroy over the whole kingdom.

The wedding was celebrated with great joy, and the young King sent for his father and his foster-father and loaded them with treasures. He did not forget the innkeeper either, but sent for him and said, "There, sir host — I've married the King's daughter, and your house and yard are mine."

The host said, "Yes, by rights that's so."

But the young King said, "It shall go by mercy instead," and told him he should keep his house and yard, and gave him the thousand gold pieces as well.

And now the young King and Queen were thoroughly happy and lived in gladness together. He often went out hunting, because it was a delight to him, and the faithful animals had to go with him. In the neighbourhood, though, there was a forest that was said to be haunted, and that whoever once went into it did not easily get out again. But the young King had a great longing to hunt in it, and gave the old King no peace until he was allowed to.

So he rode out with a great following; and when he came to the forest he saw a snow-white hart, and said to his people, "Wait here until I come back. I want to chase that beautiful creature." And he rode into the forest after it, with nobody following him but his animals.

His attendants halted and waited until evening, but he did not come back; so they rode home and told the young Queen that the young King had followed a white hart into the enchanted forest and had not returned. Then she was in the greatest anxiety about him.

He, meanwhile, had gone riding on and on after the beautiful creature, and had never been able to overtake it. Whenever he thought he was near enough to take aim, he saw it bound away into the far distance; and at last it vanished altogether. And now he realised how deep into the forest he had come, and blew his horn; but no answer came back, for his attendants could not hear it. And as night was falling too, he saw he could not get home that day; so he dismounted, lit himself a fire by a tree, and made up his mind to spend the night there.

While he was sitting by the fire, with his animals lying down beside him, he thought he heard a human voice. He looked round but could see nothing. Soon afterwards he heard a groan again, as if from above him; and then he looked up and saw an old woman sitting in the tree, wailing without a stop, "Oh, oh, oh, how cold I am!"

Said he, "Come down and warm yourself, if you're cold."

But she said, "No, your animals will bite me."

He answered, "They'll do you no harm, old mother. Do come down."

She was a witch, however, and said, "I'll throw a wand down out of the tree; and if you strike them on the back with it, they'll do me no harm."

So she threw him down a little wand, and he struck them with it; and instantly they lay still and were turned to stone. And when the witch was safe from the animals, she jumped down and touched him with a wand as well, and changed him to stone. Then she laughed, and dragged him and the animals into a vault where a great many more such stones were already lying.

But as the young King did not come back at all, the Queen's anguish and worry grew greater and greater.

And it happened that at this very time the other brother, who had turned east when they separated, came into that kingdom. He had looked for a position and found none, and had gone travelling about here and there, making his animals dance. Then it came into his head that he would just go and look at the knife they had driven into the tree trunk when they parted, so as to learn how his brother was faring. When he got there, his brother's side of the knife was half rusty and half bright.

Then he was alarmed and thought, "Some great misfortune must have come to my brother; but perhaps I can still save him, since half the knife is still bright."

So he and his animals travelled westwards; and when he came in at the town gate the guard came to meet him and asked whether he should announce him to his consort the young Queen, who had been in the greatest distress for a couple of days about his staying away, and was afraid he had been killed in the enchanted forest. The sentries had no other idea than that he was the young King himself, for he looked so like him and had wild animals running behind him.

Then he saw that they were speaking of his brother, and thought, "It will be better if I let them take me for him. Then I can rescue him more easily." So he let the guard escort him into the castle, and was received with the greatest joy.

The young Queen truly believed he was her husband, and asked him why he had stayed away so long.

He answered, "I lost myself in a forest and couldn't find my way out any sooner."

At night he was taken to the royal bed, but he laid a two-edged sword between himself and the young Queen. She did not know what that could mean, but did not dare to ask.

He stayed in the palace a couple of days, and in that time he found out everything there was to know about the enchanted forest; and at last he said, "I must hunt there once more."

The King and the young Queen tried to talk him out of it, but he held out against them and went off with a still larger following.

When he had got into the forest it went with him as it had with his brother. He saw a white hart and said to his people, "Stay here and wait until I come back. I want to chase that lovely creature." And then he rode into the forest, and his animals ran after him.

But he could not overtake the hart, and got so deep into the forest that he had to spend the night there. And when he had lit a fire he heard somebody wailing above him, "Oh, oh, oh, how cold I am!"

Then he looked up, and there was the very same witch sitting in the tree.

Said he, "If you're cold, come down, little old mother, and warm yourself."

She answered, "No, your animals will bite me."

But he said, "They won't hurt you."

Then she cried, "I'll throw a wand down to you, and if you strike them with it they'll do me no harm."

When the huntsman heard that, he had no faith in the old woman, and said, "I won't strike my animals. Come down, or I'll fetch you."

Then she cried, "What do you want? You shan't touch me."

But he answered, "If you don't come, I'll shoot you."

Said she, "Shoot away — I'm not afraid of your bullets."

So he took aim and fired at her; but the witch was proof against all leaden bullets, and laughed and shrieked and cried, "You shan't hit me!"

The huntsman knew what to do. He tore three silver buttons off his coat and loaded his gun with them, for against those her arts were useless; and when he fired she fell down at once with a scream.

Then he set his foot on her and said, "Old witch, if you don't tell me this instant where my brother is, I'll take hold of you with both hands and throw you into the fire."

She was in a great fright, and begged for mercy, and said, "He and his animals are lying in a vault, turned to stone."

Then he made her go there with him, and threatened her, and said, "Old sea-cat, now you shall bring my brother and all the human beings lying here back to life, or you go into the fire."

She took a wand and touched the stones; and then his brother came to life again with his animals, and many others as well — merchants, craftsmen and shepherds — who got up and thanked him for their deliverance and went off to their homes.

And when the twin brothers saw each other again they kissed each other and rejoiced with all their hearts. Then they seized the witch and bound her and laid her on the fire; and when she was burnt, the forest opened of its own accord and grew light and clear, and the King's palace could be seen about three hours' walk away.

After that the two brothers went home together, and on the way told each other their histories. And when the younger said that he was ruler of the whole country in the King's place, the other remarked, "I noticed that very well. When I came to the town and was taken for you, all royal honours were paid to me; the young Queen looked on me as her husband, and I had to eat at her side and sleep in your bed."

When the other heard that, he grew so jealous and angry that he drew his sword and struck off his brother's head. But when he saw him lying there dead, and saw his red blood flowing, he repented terribly. "My brother delivered me," he cried, "and I've killed him for it!" — and he wept aloud over him.

Then his hare came and offered to go and fetch some of the root of life, and bounded away and brought it while there was still time; and the dead man was brought back to life and knew nothing about the wound.

After this they journeyed on, and the younger said, "You look like me, you have royal clothes on as I do, and the animals follow you as they do me. We'll go in by opposite gates and come before the old King from the two sides at the same moment."

So they separated; and at the same moment the watchmen came from the one gate and from the other, and announced that the young King had come back from the chase with his animals.

The King said, "That's not possible. The gates are a good mile apart."

But meanwhile the two brothers were coming into the palace courtyard from opposite sides, and both climbed the steps.

Then the King said to his daughter, "Say which is your husband. They look exactly alike; I can't tell."

Then she was in great distress and could not tell. But at last she remembered the necklace she had given to the animals; and she looked, and found her little golden clasp on the lion, and cried out in her delight, "The one this lion follows is my true husband!"

Then the young King laughed and said, "Yes, he's the right one" — and they sat down to table together, and ate and drank and were merry.

That night, when the young King went to bed, his wife said, "Why have you laid a two-edged sword in our bed every night lately? I thought you had a mind to kill me."

Then he knew how faithful his brother had been.
