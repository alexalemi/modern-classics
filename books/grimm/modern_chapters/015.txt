The Girl Without Hands

A certain miller had little by little fallen into poverty, and had nothing left but his mill and a big apple tree behind it.

Once, when he had gone into the forest to fetch wood, an old man he had never seen before came up to him and said, "Why do you plague yourself with cutting wood? I will make you rich, if you will promise me what is standing behind your mill."

"What can that be but my apple tree?" thought the miller; and he said yes, and gave the stranger a promise in writing.

But the stranger laughed mockingly and said, "When three years have gone by I shall come and carry away what is mine" — and then he went off.

When the miller got home his wife came to meet him and said, "Tell me, miller, where has this sudden wealth in our house come from? All at once every box and chest is full. Nobody brought anything in, and I don't know how it happened."

He answered, "It comes from a stranger who met me in the forest and promised me great treasure. In return I promised him what stands behind the mill. We can very well let him have the big apple tree for it."

"Oh, husband," said his wife in terror, "that must have been the Devil. He didn't mean the apple tree — he meant our daughter, who was standing behind the mill sweeping the yard."

The miller's daughter was a beautiful, pious girl, and she lived through the three years in the fear of God and without sin. So when the time was up, and the day came when the Evil One was to fetch her, she washed herself clean and drew a circle round herself with chalk.

The Devil appeared very early, but he could not come near her. He said angrily to the miller, "Take all the water away from her, so that she can't wash herself any more; otherwise I have no power over her."

The miller was afraid, and did it. Next morning the Devil came again, but she had wept on her hands and they were perfectly clean. Again he could not get near her, and he said furiously to the miller, "Cut her hands off, or I can't get the better of her."

The miller was horrified and answered, "How could I cut off my own child's hands?"

Then the Evil One threatened him and said, "If you don't do it, you are mine and I shall take you yourself."

The father took fright and promised to obey. So he went to the girl and said, "My child, if I don't cut off both your hands the Devil will carry me off, and in my terror I promised to do it. Help me in my need, and forgive me the harm I do you."

She answered, "Dear father, do what you like with me. I am your child."

And with that she laid down both her hands and let them be cut off.

The Devil came for the third time; but she had wept so long and so much on the stumps that they too were perfectly clean. So he had to give in, and had lost all right over her.

The miller said to her, "Through you I have come into such great wealth that I will keep you in the greatest comfort as long as you live."

But she answered, "I cannot stay here. I will go out into the world; compassionate people will give me as much as I need."

So she had her maimed arms bound behind her back, and at sunrise she set out and walked the whole day until night fell.

Then she came to a royal garden, and by the shimmer of the moon she saw that trees covered with beautiful fruit grew in it. But she could not get in, because there was a moat of water all round it. And as she had walked the whole day and not eaten a mouthful, and hunger tormented her, she thought, "Ah, if only I were inside, so that I could eat some of the fruit! Otherwise I shall die of hunger."

Then she knelt down and called on God the Lord, and prayed. And suddenly an angel came to her, who made a dam in the water so that the moat went dry and she could walk through it.

So she went into the garden, and the angel went with her. She saw a tree covered with beautiful pears, but they had all been counted. She went up to it and, to still her hunger, ate one of them with her mouth off the tree, but no more.

The gardener was watching; but as the angel was standing there he was afraid, and thought the girl was a spirit, and said nothing, and did not dare to call out or to speak to the spirit. When she had eaten the pear she was satisfied, and went and hid herself among the bushes.

The King, whose garden it was, came down to it next morning, and counted, and saw that one of the pears was missing, and asked the gardener what had become of it, since it was not lying under the tree but was simply gone.

The gardener answered, "Last night a spirit came in, with no hands, and ate one of the pears off with its mouth."

The King said, "How did the spirit get across the water, and where did it go after it had eaten the pear?"

The gardener answered, "Somebody in a snow-white garment came from heaven and dammed the water and held it back, so that the spirit could walk through the moat. And as it must have been an angel, I was afraid, and asked no questions and did not call out. When the spirit had eaten the pear, it went back again."

The King said, "If it is as you say, I will watch with you tonight."

When it grew dark the King came into the garden and brought a priest with him, who was to speak to the spirit. All three sat down under the tree and watched.

At midnight the girl came creeping out of the thicket, went to the tree and again ate one pear off it with her mouth; and beside her stood the angel in white.

Then the priest came out to them and said, "Do you come from heaven or from earth? Are you a spirit or a human being?"

She answered, "I am no spirit, but an unhappy mortal deserted by everybody but God."

The King said, "If you are forsaken by all the world, I will not forsake you."

He took her home with him to his royal palace; and as she was so beautiful and so good he loved her with all his heart, and had silver hands made for her, and married her.

After a year the King had to go to war, so he entrusted his young Queen to his mother's care and said, "If she is brought to bed, take care of her, nurse her well, and let me know of it at once by letter."

Then she gave birth to a fine boy. So the old mother hurried to write and tell him the joyful news. But the messenger rested by a brook on the way, and as he was tired out by the great distance, he fell asleep. And the Devil came, who was always looking for a way to injure the good Queen, and changed the letter for another, in which it was written that the Queen had brought a monster into the world.

When the King read the letter he was shocked and very much distressed, but he wrote back that they were to take great care of the Queen and nurse her well until he came home. The messenger went back with the letter, but rested in the same place and again fell asleep. And the Devil came once more and put a different letter in his pocket, in which it was written that they were to put the Queen and her child to death.

The old mother was horrified when she got the letter and could not believe it. She wrote to the King again, but got no other answer, because each time the Devil substituted a false letter; and in the last letter it was written as well that she was to keep the Queen's tongue and eyes as proof that she had obeyed.

But the old mother wept to think of such innocent blood being shed, and had a hind brought in the night and cut out its tongue and eyes and kept them.

Then she said to the Queen, "I cannot have you killed as the King commands; but you cannot stay here any longer. Go out into the wide world with your child, and never come back here again."

The poor woman tied her child on her back and went away with her eyes full of tears. She came into a great wild forest, and there she fell on her knees and prayed to God; and the angel of the Lord appeared to her and led her to a little house with a sign on it that said, "Here all dwell free."

A snow-white girl came out of the little house and said, "Welcome, my lady Queen" — and led her inside. Then they untied the little boy from her back and held him to her breast to feed, and laid him in a beautifully made little bed.

Then the poor woman said, "How did you know that I was a queen?"

The white girl answered, "I am an angel sent by God to watch over you and your child."

The Queen stayed seven years in the little house and was well looked after; and by God's grace, because of her piety, the hands that had been cut off grew again.

At last the King came home from the war, and his first wish was to see his wife and the child. Then his aged mother began to weep and said, "You wicked man, why did you write to me that I was to take those two innocent lives?" And she showed him the two letters the Evil One had forged, and went on, "I did as you told me" — and she showed him the tokens, the tongue and the eyes.

Then the King began to weep for his poor wife and his little son so much more bitterly than she was weeping, that the old mother took pity on him and said, "Be comforted — she is still alive. I secretly had a hind killed and took the tokens from it; but I tied the child on your wife's back and told her to go out into the wide world, and made her promise never to come back here, because you were so angry with her."

Then the King said, "I will go as far as the sky is blue, and I will neither eat nor drink until I have found my dear wife and my child again — if they have not been killed or died of hunger in the meantime."

So the King travelled about for seven long years and looked for her in every cleft of the rocks and every cave, and did not find her, and thought she must have died of want. During the whole of that time he neither ate nor drank, but God kept him alive.

At length he came into a great forest and found in it the little house with the sign, "Here all dwell free." Out came the white girl, took him by the hand, led him in and said, "Welcome, my lord King" — and asked him where he came from.

He answered, "I have been travelling about for nearly seven years now, looking for my wife and her child, and I cannot find them."

The angel offered him food and drink, but he would take nothing and only wanted to rest a little. So he lay down to sleep, and put a handkerchief over his face.

Then the angel went into the room where the Queen was sitting with her son, whom she usually called Sorrowful, and said to her, "Go out with your child. Your husband has come."

So she went to the place where he was lying, and the handkerchief fell off his face. Then she said, "Sorrowful, pick up your father's handkerchief and cover his face again."

The child picked it up and put it over his face again. The King heard it in his sleep, and enjoyed letting the handkerchief fall once more. But the child grew impatient and said, "Dear mother, how can I cover my father's face when I have no father in this world? I have learned to say the prayer 'Our Father, which art in Heaven'; you told me my father was in Heaven and was the good God — and how can I know a wild man like this? He isn't my father."

When the King heard that, he got up and asked who they were.

Then she said, "I am your wife, and this is your son, Sorrowful."

And he saw her living hands and said, "My wife had silver hands."

She answered, "The good God has made my own hands grow again" — and the angel went into the inner room and brought out the silver hands and showed them to him.

Then he knew for certain that it was his dear wife and his dear child, and he kissed them and was glad, and said, "A heavy stone has fallen from my heart."

Then the angel of God gave them one meal with her; and after that they went home to the King's aged mother. There were great rejoicings everywhere, and the King and Queen were married again and lived contentedly to their happy end.


Clever Hans

Hans's mother said, "Where are you off to, Hans?"

Hans answered, "To Gretel."

"Behave yourself, Hans."

"Oh, I'll behave. Goodbye, mother."

"Goodbye, Hans."

Hans comes to Gretel. "Good day, Gretel."

"Good day, Hans. What have you brought that's good?"

"I've brought nothing. I want to be given something."

Gretel gives Hans a needle. Hans says, "Goodbye, Gretel."

"Goodbye, Hans."

Hans takes the needle, sticks it into a hay cart and follows the cart home.

"Good evening, mother."

"Good evening, Hans. Where have you been?"

"With Gretel."

"What did you take her?"

"Took nothing. Had something given me."

"What did Gretel give you?"

"Gave me a needle."

"Where is the needle, Hans?"

"Stuck it in the hay cart."

"That was badly done, Hans. You should have stuck the needle in your sleeve."

"Never mind, I'll do better next time."

"Where are you off to, Hans?"

"To Gretel, mother."

"Behave yourself, Hans."

"Oh, I'll behave. Goodbye, mother."

"Goodbye, Hans."

Hans comes to Gretel. "Good day, Gretel."

"Good day, Hans. What have you brought that's good?"

"I've brought nothing. I want to be given something."

Gretel gives Hans a knife. "Goodbye, Gretel."

"Goodbye, Hans."

Hans takes the knife, sticks it in his sleeve and goes home.

"Good evening, mother."

"Good evening, Hans. Where have you been?"

"With Gretel."

"What did you take her?"

"Took her nothing. She gave me something."

"What did Gretel give you?"

"Gave me a knife."

"Where is the knife, Hans?"

"Stuck in my sleeve."

"That was badly done, Hans. You should have put the knife in your pocket."

"Never mind, I'll do better next time."

"Where are you off to, Hans?"

"To Gretel, mother."

"Behave yourself, Hans."

"Oh, I'll behave. Goodbye, mother."

"Goodbye, Hans."

Hans comes to Gretel. "Good day, Gretel."

"Good day, Hans. What good thing have you brought?"

"I've brought nothing. I want something given me."

Gretel gives Hans a young goat. "Goodbye, Gretel."

"Goodbye, Hans."

Hans takes the goat, ties its legs and puts it in his pocket. By the time he gets home it has smothered.

"Good evening, mother."

"Good evening, Hans. Where have you been?"

"With Gretel."

"What did you take her?"

"Took nothing. She gave me something."

"What did Gretel give you?"

"She gave me a goat."

"Where is the goat, Hans?"

"Put it in my pocket."

"That was badly done, Hans. You should have put a rope round the goat's neck."

"Never mind, I'll do better next time."

"Where are you off to, Hans?"

"To Gretel, mother."

"Behave yourself, Hans."

"Oh, I'll behave. Goodbye, mother."

"Goodbye, Hans."

Hans comes to Gretel. "Good day, Gretel."

"Good day, Hans. What good thing have you brought?"

"I've brought nothing. I want something given me."

Gretel gives Hans a piece of bacon. "Goodbye, Gretel."

"Goodbye, Hans."

Hans takes the bacon, ties it to a rope and drags it along behind him. The dogs come and eat the bacon. When he gets home he has the rope in his hand and there is nothing on the end of it.

"Good evening, mother."

"Good evening, Hans. Where have you been?"

"With Gretel."

"What did you take her?"

"Took her nothing. She gave me something."

"What did Gretel give you?"

"Gave me a bit of bacon."

"Where is the bacon, Hans?"

"Tied it to a rope, brought it home, dogs took it."

"That was badly done, Hans. You should have carried the bacon on your head."

"Never mind, I'll do better next time."

"Where are you off to, Hans?"

"To Gretel, mother."

"Behave yourself, Hans."

"I'll behave. Goodbye, mother."

"Goodbye, Hans."

Hans comes to Gretel. "Good day, Gretel."

"Good day, Hans. What good thing have you brought?"

"I've brought nothing, but I'd like something given me."

Gretel gives Hans a calf. "Goodbye, Gretel."

"Goodbye, Hans."

Hans takes the calf, puts it on his head, and the calf kicks him in the face.

"Good evening, mother."

"Good evening, Hans. Where have you been?"

"With Gretel."

"What did you take her?"

"Took nothing, but had something given me."

"What did Gretel give you?"

"A calf."

"Where have you got the calf, Hans?"

"Set it on my head and it kicked my face."

"That was badly done, Hans. You should have led the calf and put it in the stall."

"Never mind, I'll do better next time."

"Where are you off to, Hans?"

"To Gretel, mother."

"Behave yourself, Hans."

"I'll behave. Goodbye, mother."

"Goodbye, Hans."

Hans comes to Gretel. "Good day, Gretel."

"Good day, Hans. What good thing have you brought?"

"I've brought nothing, but I'd like something given me."

Gretel says to Hans, "I'll come with you myself."

Hans takes Gretel, ties her to a rope, leads her to the manger and ties her up fast. Then Hans goes to his mother.

"Good evening, mother."

"Good evening, Hans. Where have you been?"

"With Gretel."

"What did you take her?"

"Took her nothing."

"What did Gretel give you?"

"She gave me nothing. She came with me."

"Where have you left Gretel?"

"Led her by the rope, tied her to the manger and scattered some grass for her."

"That was badly done, Hans. You should have cast friendly eyes on her."

"Never mind, I'll do better."

Hans went into the stable, cut out all the calves' and the sheep's eyes and threw them in Gretel's face. Then Gretel got angry, tore herself loose and ran away — and became the bride of Hans.


The Three Languages

There once lived an aged count in Switzerland who had an only son; but the son was stupid and could learn nothing.

So the father said, "Listen to me, my son. I can get nothing into your head, try as I may. You must go away from here. I will put you in the care of a famous master, who shall see what he can do with you."

The young man was sent to a strange town and stayed a whole year with the master. At the end of that time he came home again, and his father asked, "Now, my son, what have you learned?"

"Father, I have learned what the dogs say when they bark."

"Lord have mercy on us!" cried the father. "Is that all you have learned? I will send you to another town, to another master."

The young man was taken there and stayed a year with this master too. When he came back his father asked again, "My son, what have you learned?"

He answered, "Father, I have learned what the birds say."

Then the father flew into a rage and said, "Oh, you lost creature, you have spent all that precious time and learned nothing! Aren't you ashamed to come before my eyes? I will send you to a third master; but if you learn nothing this time either, I will be your father no longer."

The young man stayed a whole year with the third master as well; and when he came home again and his father asked, "My son, what have you learned?" — he answered, "Dear father, this year I have learned what the frogs croak."

Then the father fell into the most furious rage, sprang up, called his people in and said, "This man is no son of mine. I drive him out, and I order you to take him into the forest and kill him."

They took him out; but when it came to killing him, they could not do it for pity, and let him go; and they cut the eyes and tongue out of a deer to carry to the old man as proof.

The young man wandered on, and after a while came to a fortress where he begged for a night's lodging.

"Yes," said the lord of the castle, "if you will spend the night down there in the old tower, go and do it; but I warn you, it is at the risk of your life, because it is full of wild dogs that bark and howl without stopping, and at certain hours a man has to be given to them, and they devour him at once."

The whole district was in grief and dismay because of them, and yet nobody could do anything about it.

The young man, though, was not afraid, and said, "Just let me go down to the barking dogs, and give me something to throw to them. They will do me no harm."

As he insisted on it himself, they gave him some food for the wild animals and led him down to the tower. When he went inside the dogs did not bark at him, but wagged their tails round him quite amicably, ate what he set in front of them, and did not hurt a hair of his head.

Next morning, to everybody's astonishment, he came out again safe and unharmed, and said to the lord of the castle, "The dogs have told me, in their own language, why they live there and bring trouble on the country. They are under a spell, and are made to guard a great treasure that lies below in the tower, and they can have no rest until it is taken away; and I have learned from their talk how that is to be done."

Then everybody who heard it rejoiced, and the lord of the castle said he would adopt him as a son if he brought it off. He went down again; and as he knew what he had to do, he did it thoroughly, and brought out a chest full of gold. From then on the howling of the wild dogs was heard no more; they had disappeared, and the country was free of the trouble.

After some time he took it into his head to travel to Rome. On the way he passed a marsh where a number of frogs were sitting and croaking. He listened to them; and when he understood what they were saying, he grew very thoughtful and sad.

At last he arrived in Rome, where the Pope had just died and there was great difficulty about whom they should appoint as his successor. At last they agreed that the man should be chosen as pope who was marked out by some divine and miraculous sign. And just as that had been decided, the young count came into the church, and suddenly two snow-white doves flew onto his shoulders and stayed sitting there.

The churchmen recognised the sign from above and asked him on the spot whether he would be pope. He was undecided and did not know whether he was worthy of it; but the doves advised him to do it, and at last he said yes.

So he was anointed and consecrated; and so what he had heard from the frogs on his way, which had affected him so much, was fulfilled: that he was to be his Holiness the Pope.

Then he had to sing a mass, and did not know one word of it; but the two doves sat on his shoulders the whole time and said it all in his ear.
