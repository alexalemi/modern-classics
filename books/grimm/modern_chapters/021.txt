The Juniper-Tree

It is a long time ago now, quite two thousand years, since there was a rich man who had a beautiful and pious wife, and they loved each other dearly. They had no children, though, however much they wished for them; and the woman prayed for them day and night, and still they had none.

Now in front of their house there was a courtyard, and in it stood a juniper tree; and one day in winter the woman was standing under it paring an apple, and while she was paring it she cut her finger, and the blood fell on the snow.

"Ah," said the woman, and sighed heavily, and looked at the blood in front of her, and was very unhappy, "if only I had a child as red as blood and as white as snow!"

And as she said it she grew quite happy in her mind, and felt just as if it were going to happen. Then she went into the house.

And a month went by, and the snow was gone; and two months, and everything was green; and three months, and all the flowers came up out of the earth; and four months, and all the trees in the wood grew thicker and the green branches were closely twined together, and the birds sang until the wood rang, and the blossom fell from the trees. And the fifth month passed, and she stood under the juniper tree, which smelt so sweet that her heart leapt, and she fell on her knees and was beside herself with joy. And when the sixth month was over the fruit was large and fine, and then she grew quite still. And in the seventh month she snatched at the juniper berries and ate them greedily, and then she grew sick and sorrowful. And the eighth month went by, and she called her husband to her, and wept, and said, "If I die, bury me under the juniper tree."

Then she was quite comforted and happy until the next month was over; and then she had a child as white as snow and as red as blood; and when she saw it she was so delighted that she died.

So her husband buried her under the juniper tree, and began to weep bitterly. After a time he grew easier, and though he still wept he could bear it; and after a while longer he took another wife.

By this second wife he had a daughter; but the first wife's child was a little son, and he was as red as blood and as white as snow. When the woman looked at her daughter she loved her very much; but then she looked at the little boy, and it seemed to cut her to the heart, because the thought came into her mind that he would always stand in her way; and she was for ever thinking how she could get the whole fortune for her daughter. And the Evil One filled her mind with this until she was quite furious with the little boy, and slapped him here and cuffed him there, so that the unhappy child lived in constant terror; for when he came out of school there was nowhere he could have any peace.

One day the woman had gone upstairs to her room, and her little daughter went up too, and said, "Mother, give me an apple."

"Yes, my child," said the woman, and gave her a fine apple out of the chest — but the chest had a great heavy lid with a great sharp iron lock.

"Mother," said the little daughter, "isn't brother to have one too?"

That made the woman angry; but she said, "Yes, when he comes out of school."

And when she saw from the window that he was coming, it was just as if the Devil got into her, and she snatched the apple away from her daughter again and said, "You shan't have one before your brother."

Then she threw the apple into the chest and shut it.

Then the little boy came in at the door; and the Devil made her say to him kindly, "My son, would you like an apple?" — and she looked at him wickedly.

"Mother," said the little boy, "how dreadful you look! Yes, give me an apple."

Then it seemed to her that she was forced to say to him, "Come with me" — and she opened the lid of the chest and said, "Take out an apple for yourself." And while the little boy was stooping inside, the Devil prompted her, and crash! she slammed the lid down, and his head flew off and fell among the red apples.

Then she was overwhelmed with terror, and thought, "If only I could make them think it wasn't me!" So she went up to her room to her chest of drawers and took a white handkerchief out of the top drawer, and set the head on the neck again and tied the handkerchief so that nothing could be seen; and she sat him on a chair in front of the door and put the apple in his hand.

After this Marlinchen came into the kitchen to her mother, who was standing by the fire with a pan of hot water in front of her, stirring it round and round.

"Mother," said Marlinchen, "brother is sitting at the door and he looks quite white and has an apple in his hand. I asked him to give me the apple, but he didn't answer me, and I was quite frightened."

"Go back to him," said her mother, "and if he won't answer you, box his ears."

So Marlinchen went to him and said, "Brother, give me the apple." But he said nothing; so she boxed his ears — and his head fell off.

Marlinchen was terrified and began crying and screaming, and ran to her mother and said, "Oh, mother, I've knocked my brother's head off!" — and she wept and wept and could not be comforted.

"Marlinchen," said her mother, "what have you done? But be quiet and let nobody know about it. It can't be helped now. We'll make him into black puddings."

Then the mother took the little boy and chopped him in pieces and put him in the pan and made him into black puddings; and Marlinchen stood by weeping and weeping, and all her tears fell into the pan, so that there was no need of any salt.

Then the father came home and sat down to dinner and said, "But where is my son?"

And the mother served up a great dish of black puddings; and Marlinchen wept and could not stop.

Then the father said again, "But where is my son?"

"Ah," said the mother, "he's gone off across the country to his mother's great-uncle. He'll stay there a while."

"And what has he gone to do there? He didn't even say goodbye to me."

"Oh, he wanted to go, and asked me whether he might stay six weeks. He's well looked after there."

"Ah," said the man, "I feel so unhappy, in case something isn't right. He ought to have said goodbye to me."

With that he began to eat, and said, "Marlinchen, why are you crying? Your brother will certainly come back."

And then he said, "Ah, wife, how delicious this food is. Give me some more." And the more he ate, the more he wanted, and he said, "Give me some more. You shan't have any of it. It seems to me as if it were all mine."

And he ate and ate and threw all the bones under the table, until he had finished the whole lot.

But Marlinchen went to her chest of drawers and took her best silk handkerchief out of the bottom drawer, and got all the bones from under the table and tied them up in her silk handkerchief and carried them outside the door, weeping tears of blood.

Then the juniper tree began to stir itself, and the branches parted and moved together again, just as if somebody were rejoicing and clapping his hands. At the same time a mist seemed to rise from the tree, and in the middle of the mist it burned like a fire; and out of the fire flew a beautiful bird, singing magnificently, and he flew high up into the air. And when he was gone, the juniper tree was just as it had been before, and the handkerchief with the bones was no longer there.

But Marlinchen was as gay and happy as if her brother were still alive; and she went merrily into the house and sat down to dinner and ate.

But the bird flew away and settled on a goldsmith's house, and began to sing:

	"My mother, she killed me;
	my father, he ate me;
	my sister, little Marlinchen,
	gathered up all my bones,
	tied them in a silken handkerchief
	and laid them under the juniper tree.
	Kywitt, kywitt, what a beautiful bird am I!"

The goldsmith was sitting in his workshop making a gold chain when he heard the bird singing on his roof, and the song seemed very beautiful to him. He stood up, and as he crossed the threshold he lost one of his slippers. But he went right on up the middle of the street with one shoe on and one sock; he had his apron on, and in one hand he had the gold chain and in the other his pincers, and the sun was shining brightly on the street.

Then he went right up and stood still and said to the bird, "Bird, how beautifully you sing! Sing me that piece again."

"No," said the bird, "I won't sing it twice for nothing. Give me the golden chain, and then I'll sing it again for you."

"There," said the goldsmith, "there's the golden chain for you. Now sing me that song again."

Then the bird came and took the golden chain in his right claw, and went and sat in front of the goldsmith and sang:

	"My mother, she killed me;

	my sister, little Marlinchen,
	gathered up all my bones,

	and laid them under the juniper tree.
	Kywitt, kywitt, what a beautiful bird am I!"

Then the bird flew away to a shoemaker's, and settled on his roof and sang:

	"My mother, she killed me;
	my father, he ate me;
	my sister, little Marlinchen,
	gathered up all my bones,
	tied them in a silken handkerchief
	and laid them under the juniper tree.
	Kywitt, kywitt, what a beautiful bird am I!"

The shoemaker heard it and ran out of doors in his shirtsleeves and looked up at his roof, and had to hold his hand in front of his eyes to keep the sun from blinding him.

"Bird," he said, "how beautifully you sing!" Then he called in at his door, "Wife, just come outside — there's a bird here. Look at that bird. He really can sing well."

Then he called his daughter and his children and his apprentices, boys and girls, and they all came up the street and looked at the bird and saw how beautiful he was, and what fine red and green feathers he had, and how his neck was like real gold, and how the eyes in his head shone like stars.

"Bird," said the shoemaker, "now sing me that song again."

"No," said the bird, "I don't sing twice for nothing. You must give me something."

"Wife," said the man, "go up to the garret. On the top shelf there's a pair of red shoes; bring them down."

So his wife went and fetched the shoes.

"There, bird," said the man, "now sing me that piece again."

Then the bird came and took the shoes in his left claw and flew back up onto the roof and sang:

And when he had sung it all through he flew away. In his right claw he had the chain and in his left the shoes; and he flew far away to a mill, and the mill went klipp klapp, klipp klapp, klipp klapp. And in the mill sat twenty of the miller's men hewing a stone, and cutting, hick hack, hick hack, hick hack; and the mill went klipp klapp, klipp klapp, klipp klapp.

In the room sat the father, the mother and Marlinchen at dinner; and the father said, "How light-hearted I feel, how happy I am!"

"No," said the mother, "I feel so uneasy, just as if a heavy storm were coming."

Marlinchen, though, sat weeping and weeping.

Then the bird came flying up; and as it settled on the roof, the father said, "Ah, I feel so truly happy, and the sun is shining so beautifully outside. I feel just as if I were going to see an old friend again."

Old Sultan

A farmer once had a faithful dog called Sultan, who had grown old and lost all his teeth, so that he could no longer hold onto anything.

One day the farmer was standing with his wife in front of the house door and said, "Tomorrow I mean to shoot Old Sultan. He's no use any more."

His wife, who felt sorry for the faithful creature, answered, "He's served us so long and been so faithful that we might well keep him."

"Eh, what?" said the man. "You're not very sharp. He hasn't a tooth left in his head, and not a thief is afraid of him. Now he can go. If he's served us, he's had good feeding for it."

The poor dog, who was lying stretched out in the sun not far off, had heard everything, and was sorry that tomorrow was to be his last day. He had a good friend, the wolf; and in the evening he crept out into the forest to him and complained of the fate that was waiting for him.

"Listen, old friend," said the wolf, "be of good cheer. I'll help you out of your trouble. I've thought of something. Tomorrow morning early your master is going haymaking with his wife, and they'll take their little child with them, because nobody will be left behind in the house. While they're working they always lay the child down under the hedge in the shade. You lie down there too, just as if you meant to guard it. Then I'll come out of the wood and carry the child off. You must rush after me as fast as you can, as if you were going to take it back from me. I'll let it fall, and you'll bring it back to its parents; and they'll think you saved it, and they'll be far too grateful to do you any harm. On the contrary, you'll be in high favour, and they'll never let you want for anything again."

The dog liked the plan, and it was carried out exactly as arranged. The father screamed when he saw the wolf running across the field with his child; but when Old Sultan brought it back, then he was full of joy, and stroked him and said, "Not a hair of yours shall be harmed. You shall eat my bread free as long as you live."

And to his wife he said, "Go home at once and make Old Sultan some bread and milk that he won't have to bite; and bring the pillow out of my bed — I'll give him that to lie on."

From then on Old Sultan was as well off as he could wish to be.

Soon afterwards the wolf came to visit him, and was pleased that everything had gone so well. "But, old friend," he said, "you'll just wink an eye, won't you, if I get a chance to carry off one of your master's fat sheep?"

"Don't count on that," said the dog. "I shall stay true to my master. I can't agree to that."

The wolf, who did not think this could be meant seriously, came creeping about in the night and was going to take the sheep. But the farmer, who had been told the wolf's plan by faithful Sultan, caught him and dressed his hide soundly with the flail. The wolf had to make off; but he called out to the dog, "Just wait, you scoundrel, you'll pay for this."

Next morning the wolf sent the boar to challenge the dog to come out into the forest and settle the matter. Old Sultan could find nobody to stand by him but a cat with only three legs; and as they went out together the poor cat limped along, and at the same time stuck her tail up in the air with the pain.

The wolf and his friend were already at the appointed place; but when they saw their enemy coming they thought he was bringing a sabre with him, because they took the cat's outstretched tail for one. And whenever the poor creature hopped on her three legs, they could only think each time that she was picking up a stone to throw at them.

So they were both frightened. The wild boar crept into the undergrowth and the wolf jumped up a tree.

When the dog and the cat came up they wondered that there was nobody to be seen. The wild boar, though, had not been able to hide himself completely, and one of his ears was still showing. While the cat was looking carefully about, the boar moved his ear; and the cat, thinking it was a mouse moving there, jumped on it and bit it hard. The boar made a fearful noise and ran off, crying out, "The guilty one is up in the tree!"

The dog and the cat looked up and saw the wolf, who was ashamed of having shown himself so timid, and made friends with the dog.
