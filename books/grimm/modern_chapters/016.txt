Clever Elsie

There was once a man who had a daughter called Clever Elsie. And when she had grown up her father said, "We must get her married."

"Yes," said her mother, "if only somebody would come who would have her."

At last a man came from a distance and courted her, whose name was Hans; but he made it a condition that Clever Elsie should really be wise.

"Oh," said her father, "she's sharp enough."

And her mother said, "Oh, she can see the wind coming up the street and hear the flies coughing."

"Well," said Hans, "if she isn't really wise, I won't have her."

When they were sitting at dinner and had eaten, the mother said, "Elsie, go down to the cellar and fetch some beer."

So Clever Elsie took the jug down from the wall and went into the cellar, tapping the lid briskly as she went, so that the time should not seem long. When she was down there she fetched herself a chair and set it in front of the barrel, so that she need not stoop and would not hurt her back or do herself any unexpected injury. Then she put the can in front of her and turned the tap; and while the beer was running she would not let her eyes be idle either, but looked up at the wall; and after a good deal of peering here and there she saw a pickaxe directly above her, which the masons had left behind by accident.

Then Clever Elsie began to weep, and said, "If I get Hans, and we have a child, and he grows big, and we send him down into the cellar here to draw beer, then the pickaxe will fall on his head and kill him."

And she sat and wept and shrieked with all the strength in her body over the misfortune that lay ahead of her.

Upstairs they were waiting for their drink, and still Clever Elsie did not come. So the mother said to the servant girl, "Just go down into the cellar and see where Elsie is."

The girl went, and found her sitting in front of the barrel screaming loudly. "Elsie, what are you weeping for?" she asked.

"Ah," she answered, "haven't I reason to weep? If I get Hans, and we have a child, and he grows big, and has to draw beer down here, the pickaxe may fall on his head and kill him."

Then the girl said, "What a clever Elsie we have!" — and sat down beside her and began to weep loudly over the misfortune herself.

After a while, as the servant girl did not come back either, and they were thirsty for their beer upstairs, the man said to the boy, "Just go down into the cellar and see where Elsie and the girl have got to."

The boy went down, and there sat Clever Elsie and the girl weeping together. So he asked, "What are you weeping for?"

"Ah," said Elsie, "haven't I reason to weep? If I get Hans, and we have a child, and he grows big, and has to draw beer down here, the pickaxe will fall on his head and kill him."

Then the boy said, "What a clever Elsie we have!" — and sat down beside her and began to howl loudly as well.

Upstairs they waited for the boy; and as he did not come back either, the man said to his wife, "Just go down into the cellar and see where Elsie is."

The woman went down and found all three of them in the middle of their lamentations, and asked what the cause was. And Elsie told her too that her future child was to be killed by the pickaxe, when it grew big and had to draw beer and the pickaxe fell down.

Then the mother said, "What a clever Elsie we have!" — and sat down and wept with them.

The man upstairs waited a little while; but as his wife did not come back and his thirst grew greater and greater, he said, "I shall have to go down into the cellar myself and see where Elsie is."

But when he got into the cellar and they were all sitting there crying, and he heard the reason — that Elsie's child was the cause of it, and that Elsie might perhaps bring one into the world some day, and that it might be killed by the pickaxe if it happened to be sitting underneath it drawing beer at the very moment when it fell — he cried, "Oh, what a clever Elsie!" — and sat down and wept with them too.

The bridegroom sat upstairs alone for a long time; and then, as nobody came back, he thought, "They must be waiting for me downstairs. I'd better go and see what they're up to."

When he got down there, all five of them were sitting screaming and lamenting most pitifully, each of them outdoing the last.

"What misfortune has happened, then?" he asked.

"Ah, dear Hans," said Elsie, "if we marry each other and have a child, and he grows big, and we perhaps send him down here to draw something to drink, then that pickaxe that's been left up there might dash his brains out if it fell down. So haven't we reason to weep?"

"Come," said Hans, "more understanding than that isn't needed in my household. As you are such a clever Elsie, I'll have you" — and he took her by the hand and led her upstairs and married her.

When Hans had had her for some time, he said, "Wife, I'm going out to work and earn some money for us. Go into the field and cut the corn, so that we can have some bread."

"Yes, dear Hans, I'll do that."

After Hans had gone away she cooked herself some good broth and took it out to the field with her. When she got to the field she said to herself, "What shall I do — shall I cut first, or shall I eat first? Oh, I'll eat first."

So she emptied her bowl of broth; and when she was thoroughly full, she said again, "What shall I do — shall I cut first, or shall I sleep first? I'll sleep first." And she lay down among the corn and fell asleep.

Hans had been at home a long time, but Elsie did not come. So he said, "What a clever Elsie I have! She's so hard-working that she doesn't even come home to eat."

But as she still stayed away, and it was getting to be evening, Hans went out to see how much she had cut. Nothing was cut at all, and she was lying among the corn asleep.

Then Hans hurried home and fetched a fowler's net with little bells on it, and hung it round her, and still she went on sleeping. Then he ran home, shut the house door, sat down in his chair and got on with his work.

At last, when it was quite dark, Clever Elsie woke up; and when she got to her feet there was a jingling all round her, and the bells rang at every step she took. Then she was alarmed, and grew uncertain whether she really was Clever Elsie or not, and said, "Is it me, or isn't it me?"

But she did not know what answer to give to that, and stood there a while in doubt. At last she thought, "I'll go home and ask whether it's me or not. They'll be sure to know."

She ran to the door of her own house, but it was shut. So she knocked at the window and called, "Hans, is Elsie inside?"

"Yes," answered Hans, "she is inside."

At that she was terrified, and said, "Ah, heavens — then it isn't me!" — and went to another door. But when the people heard the jingling of the bells they would not open it, and she could get in nowhere.

Then she ran out of the village, and nobody has seen her since.


The Tailor in Heaven

One very fine day it came about that the good God wished to take his pleasure in the heavenly garden, and took all the apostles and saints with him, so that nobody was left in heaven but Saint Peter. The Lord had ordered him to let nobody in while he was away, so Peter stood by the door and kept watch.

Before long somebody knocked. Peter asked who was there and what he wanted.

"I am a poor, honest tailor, praying to be let in," said a smooth voice.

"Honest indeed," said Peter, "like the thief on the gallows! You've been light-fingered and snipped bits off people's cloth. You won't get into heaven. The Lord has forbidden me to let anybody in while he's out."

"Come, do be merciful," cried the tailor. "Little scraps that fall off the table of their own accord aren't stolen and aren't worth talking about. Look, I'm lame, and I've got blisters on my feet from walking here — I can't possibly turn back again. Just let me in, and I'll do all the rough work. I'll carry the children and wash their clothes, and wash and scrub the benches they've been playing on, and patch all their torn clothes."

Saint Peter let himself be moved by pity, and opened the door of heaven just wide enough for the lame tailor to slip his lean body through. He had to sit down in a corner behind the door and stay there quietly and peaceably, so that the Lord, when he came back, should not notice him and be angry.

The tailor obeyed. But once, when Saint Peter had gone outside the door, he got up and, full of curiosity, went poking round into every corner of heaven and inspecting how everything was arranged.

At last he came to a place where a great many beautiful and delightful chairs were standing, and in the middle of them a seat all of gold, set with shining jewels, much higher than the other chairs, with a footstool of gold in front of it. It was in fact the seat the Lord sat on when he was at home, and from which he could see everything that happened on earth.

The tailor stood still and looked at the seat for a long time, because it pleased him better than anything else. At last he could not master his curiosity any longer, and climbed up and sat down in the chair.

Then he saw everything that was happening on earth; and he noticed an ugly old woman standing washing by a stream, secretly laying two veils aside for herself. The sight of it made the tailor so angry that he took hold of the golden footstool and threw it down through heaven to earth at the old thief.

But as he could not get the stool back again, he slipped quietly out of the chair, sat down in his own place behind the door, and behaved as if he had never moved from the spot.

When the Lord and master came back with his heavenly companions, he did not see the tailor behind the door; but when he sat down on his chair, the footstool was missing. He asked Saint Peter what had become of the stool, but Peter did not know. Then he asked whether he had let anybody in.

"I know of nobody who has been here," answered Peter, "except a lame tailor, who is still sitting behind the door."

Then the Lord had the tailor brought before him, and asked whether he had taken the stool away, and where he had put it.

"Oh Lord," answered the tailor cheerfully, "I threw it down to earth in my anger, at an old woman I saw stealing two veils at the washing."

"Oh, you rogue," said the Lord. "If I were to judge the way you judge, how do you suppose you would have got off so long? I should have had no chairs left, nor benches, nor seats — no, nor so much as an oven-fork — but should have thrown everything down at the sinners. From now on you can stay in heaven no longer, but must go outside the door again. Then go where you like. Nobody hands out punishment here but I alone, the Lord."

So Peter had to take the tailor out of heaven again; and as his shoes were torn and his feet covered with blisters, he took a stick in his hand and went off to Wait-a-Bit, where the good soldiers sit and make merry.
