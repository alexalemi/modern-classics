The Bremen Town-Musicians

A certain man had a donkey which had carried sacks of corn to the mill tirelessly for many a long year; but his strength was failing and he was growing less and less fit for work. So his master began to consider how he could save the cost of feeding him. But the donkey, seeing that there was no good wind blowing, ran away and set out on the road to Bremen. "There," he thought, "I can surely be a town musician."

When he had walked a little way he found a hound lying in the road, panting like somebody who has run himself out.

"What are you panting like that for, you big fellow?" asked the donkey.

"Ah," said the hound, "because I'm old and getting weaker every day and can't hunt any more, my master wanted to kill me, so I ran for it. But how am I to earn my bread now?"

"I'll tell you what," said the donkey. "I'm going to Bremen to be a town musician there. Come with me and take a job as a musician too. I'll play the lute and you shall beat the kettledrum."

The hound agreed, and on they went.

Before long they came to a cat sitting by the road with a face like three rainy days.

"Now then, old whiskers, what's gone wrong with you?" asked the donkey.

"Who can be cheerful when his neck's in danger?" said the cat. "Because I'm getting old now, and my teeth are worn to stumps, and I'd rather sit by the fire and purr than go chasing about after mice, my mistress wanted to drown me. So I ran away. But good advice is scarce. Where am I to go?"

"Come to Bremen with us. You know all about night music — you can be a town musician."

The cat thought well of the idea and went with them.

After this the three runaways came to a farmyard, where the cock was sitting on the gate crowing with all his might.

"That crow of yours goes right through a man," said the donkey. "What's the matter?"

"I've been foretelling fine weather," said the cock, "because it's the day when Our Lady washes the Christ-child's little shirts and wants to dry them. But guests are coming for Sunday, so the mistress has no pity and has told the cook she means to eat me in the soup tomorrow, and this evening I'm to have my head cut off. So I'm crowing at full pitch while I still can."

"Ah, red-comb," said the donkey, "you'd do better to come away with us. We're going to Bremen. You can find something better than death anywhere. You have a good voice, and if we make music together it's bound to be something worth hearing."

The cock liked the plan, and all four went on together.

They could not reach the city of Bremen in one day, though; and in the evening they came to a forest where they meant to spend the night. The donkey and the hound lay down under a big tree, and the cat and the cock settled themselves in the branches — but the cock flew right to the top, where he was safest.

Before he went to sleep he looked round on all four sides, and thought he saw a little spark burning in the distance. So he called down to his companions that there must be a house not far off, because he could see a light.

The donkey said, "In that case we'd better get up and go on, because the lodgings here are bad." And the hound thought a few bones with some meat on them would do him no harm either.

So they made their way towards the light; and soon they saw it shining brighter and growing bigger, until they came to a well-lit robbers' house.

The donkey, as the tallest, went to the window and looked in.

"What do you see, old grey horse?" asked the cock.

"What do I see?" said the donkey. "A table covered with good things to eat and drink, and robbers sitting round it enjoying themselves."

"That would be the sort of thing for us," said the cock.

"Yes indeed. Ah, how I wish we were there," said the donkey.

Then the animals put their heads together to work out how they could drive the robbers away, and at last they hit on a plan. The donkey was to put his forefeet up on the window ledge, the hound was to jump on the donkey's back, the cat was to climb up on the dog, and last of all the cock was to fly up and perch on the cat's head.

When that was done, at a given signal, they began to perform their music together: the donkey brayed, the hound barked, the cat mewed and the cock crowed. Then they burst in through the window into the room, so that the glass went clattering.

At this horrible din the robbers sprang up, thinking it could be nothing but a ghost coming in, and fled in great terror out into the forest.

Then the four companions sat down at the table, well satisfied with what was left, and ate as if they were going to fast for a month.

As soon as the four minstrels had finished, they put out the light and each looked for a sleeping place to suit his own nature. The donkey lay down on some straw in the yard, the hound behind the door, the cat on the hearth near the warm ashes, and the cock perched on a roof beam; and as they were tired from their long walk, they soon fell asleep.

When it was past midnight, and the robbers saw from a distance that the light was no longer burning in their house and everything seemed quiet, the captain said, "We shouldn't have let ourselves be frightened out of our wits" — and ordered one of them to go and examine the house.

The messenger found everything still, and went into the kitchen to light a candle; and, taking the cat's glittering fiery eyes for live coals, he held a match to them to light it. But the cat did not see the joke and flew at his face, spitting and scratching.

He was dreadfully frightened and ran for the back door; but the dog, who was lying there, sprang up and bit him in the leg. And as he ran across the yard past the straw heap, the donkey gave him a smart kick with his hind foot. And the cock, who had been woken by the noise and was thoroughly lively by now, called down from the beam, "Cock-a-doodle-doo!"

Then the robber ran back to his captain as fast as he could and said, "Ah, there's a horrible witch sitting in the house, who spat at me and scratched my face with her long claws; and by the door there's a man with a knife, who stabbed me in the leg; and out in the yard there's a black monster lying, who hit me with a wooden club; and up on the roof sits the judge, who called out, 'Bring the rascal here to me!' So I got away as best I could."

After that the robbers never dared go into the house again; but it suited the four Bremen musicians so well that they never cared to leave it.

And the mouth of the man who last told this story is still warm.


The Singing Bone

In a certain country there was once great grief on account of a wild boar which laid waste the farmers' fields, killed the cattle, and ripped people's bodies open with its tusks. The King promised a large reward to anybody who would rid the land of this plague; but the beast was so big and so strong that nobody dared go near the forest it lived in. At last the King announced that whoever captured or killed the wild boar should have his only daughter for a wife.

Now there lived in that country two brothers, the sons of a poor man, who declared themselves willing to undertake the dangerous business: the elder, who was crafty and cunning, out of pride; the younger, who was innocent and simple, out of kindness of heart.

The King said, "So that you may be the surer of finding the beast, you must go into the forest from opposite sides." So the elder went in on the west side and the younger on the east.

When the younger had gone a little way, a little man came up to him. He had a black spear in his hand, and he said, "I give you this spear because your heart is pure and good. With this you can attack the wild boar boldly, and it will do you no harm."

He thanked the little man, shouldered the spear and went on without fear.

Before long he saw the beast, which rushed at him; but he held the spear out towards it, and in its blind fury it ran so hard against the point that its heart was split in two. Then he took the monster on his back and set off home with it to the King.

As he came out on the other side of the wood, there stood a house at the edge of it where people were making merry with wine and dancing. His elder brother had gone in there; and, thinking that the boar was not going to run away from him after all, he meant to drink until he felt brave. But when he saw his young brother coming out of the wood loaded with his prize, his envious, evil heart gave him no peace.

He called out to him, "Come in, dear brother, rest and refresh yourself with a cup of wine."

The young man, suspecting nothing, went in and told him about the good little man who had given him the spear he had killed the boar with.

The elder brother kept him there until the evening, and then they went away together. And when they came in the darkness to a bridge over a brook, the elder brother let the other go first; and when he was half way across he struck him such a blow from behind that he fell down dead.

He buried him under the bridge, took the boar and carried it to the King, pretending that he had killed it; and so he got the King's daughter in marriage. And when his younger brother did not come back he said, "The boar must have killed him" — and everybody believed it.

But as nothing stays hidden from God, this black deed too was to come to light.

Years afterwards a shepherd was driving his flock across the bridge, and saw a snow-white little bone lying in the sand below. He thought it would make a good mouthpiece, so he climbed down and picked it up and cut a mouthpiece for his horn out of it. But when he blew through it for the first time, to his great astonishment the bone began, of its own accord, to sing:

	"Ah, friend, you're blowing on my bone.
	Long have I lain here by the water.
	My brother killed me for the boar,
	and took for his wife the King's own daughter."

"What a wonderful horn," said the shepherd. "It sings by itself. I must take it to my lord the King."

And when he came with it to the King, the horn began to sing its little song again. The King understood it all, and had the ground below the bridge dug up, and the whole skeleton of the murdered man came to light. The wicked brother could not deny the deed, and was sewn up in a sack and drowned; but the bones of the murdered man were laid to rest in a beautiful tomb in the churchyard.
