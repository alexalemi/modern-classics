The Frog-King, or Iron Henry

In the old days, when wishing still worked, there lived a king whose daughters were all beautiful — but the youngest was so beautiful that the sun itself, which has seen so much, was astonished every time it shone on her face. Close by the King's castle lay a great dark forest, and in the forest, under an old lime tree, there was a well. When the day was very hot the King's child would go out into the forest and sit down at the edge of the cool water; and when she was bored she would take a golden ball and throw it up in the air and catch it, and this ball was her favourite plaything.

Now one day it happened that the princess's golden ball did not fall into the little hand she was holding up for it, but past it onto the ground, and rolled straight into the water. The King's daughter followed it with her eyes, but it was gone, and the well was deep — so deep that you could not see the bottom. At that she began to cry, and cried louder and louder, and could not be comforted.

And while she was crying like this, somebody said to her, "What is the matter, princess? You are crying so hard that even a stone would feel sorry for you."

She looked round to see where the voice came from, and saw a frog poking its thick ugly head out of the water.

"Oh, it's you, is it, you old water-splasher?" she said. "I am crying for my golden ball, which has fallen into the well."

"Be quiet and stop crying," answered the frog. "I can help you. But what will you give me if I fetch your plaything up again?"

"Whatever you like, dear frog," she said. "My clothes, my pearls and jewels, even the golden crown I am wearing."

The frog answered, "I don't want your clothes, or your pearls and jewels, or your golden crown. But if you will love me and let me be your companion and playmate, and sit beside you at your little table, and eat off your little golden plate, and drink out of your little cup, and sleep in your little bed — if you will promise me that, I will go down and bring your golden ball up again."

"Oh yes," she said, "I promise you anything you like, if only you will bring me my ball back." But what she thought was: "What nonsense the silly frog talks! He lives in the water with the other frogs and croaks, and he can't be any sort of companion to a human being."

But the frog, once he had her promise, put his head under the water and sank down; and in a little while he came swimming up again with the ball in his mouth, and threw it out onto the grass. The King's daughter was delighted to see her pretty plaything again, and picked it up and ran off with it.

"Wait, wait!" called the frog. "Take me with you. I can't run the way you can." But what good did it do him to shout his croak, croak after her as loudly as he could? She did not listen. She ran home and soon forgot the poor frog, who had to go back down into his well.

The next day, when she had sat down at table with the King and all the court, and was eating off her little golden plate, something came creeping splish splash, splish splash up the marble staircase; and when it got to the top it knocked at the door and called, "Princess, youngest princess, open the door for me."

She ran to see who was outside; but when she opened the door, there sat the frog in front of it. She slammed the door shut in a great hurry, sat down at the table again, and was thoroughly frightened. The King could see plainly that her heart was thumping, and said, "My child, what are you so afraid of? Is there a giant outside who wants to carry you off?"

"Oh, no," she answered. "It isn't a giant, it's a disgusting frog."

"What does a frog want with you?"

"Oh, father dear, yesterday when I was in the forest sitting by the well, playing, my golden ball fell into the water. And because I cried so much, the frog fetched it out for me; and because he insisted on it, I promised he could be my companion — but I never thought he would be able to come up out of his water. And now he is outside and wants to come in to me."

Just then it knocked a second time, and called:

	"Princess, youngest princess,
	open the door for me!
	Have you forgotten what you promised
	yesterday, by the cool well water?
	Princess, youngest princess,
	open the door for me!"

Then the King said, "What you have promised, you must keep. Go and let him in."

She went and opened the door, and the frog hopped in and followed her, step by step, to her chair. There he sat and called, "Lift me up beside you." She hung back, until at last the King ordered her to do it. Once the frog was on the chair he wanted to be on the table, and once he was on the table he said, "Now push your little golden plate closer, so that we can eat together." She did it, but anybody could see she did not do it willingly. The frog thoroughly enjoyed his dinner, but almost every mouthful stuck in her throat.

At last he said, "I have eaten enough and I am full. Now I am tired — carry me up to your little room and get your little silk bed ready, and we will both lie down and go to sleep."

The King's daughter began to cry, because she was frightened of the cold frog that she did not even like to touch, and that was now to sleep in her pretty clean little bed. But the King grew angry and said, "The one who helped you when you were in trouble is not to be despised afterwards."

So she picked the frog up with two fingers, carried him upstairs and put him down in a corner. But when she was in bed he came creeping over to her and said, "I am tired. I want to sleep as much as you do. Lift me up, or I'll tell your father."

At that she lost her temper completely, picked him up and threw him with all her strength against the wall. "Now you'll be quiet, you horrible frog!"

But when he fell down he was no frog. He was a king's son with beautiful, kind eyes. And by her father's will he was now her dear companion and her husband. He told her how he had been put under a spell by a wicked witch, and how nobody in the world could have got him out of that well but her, and that tomorrow they would go together to his kingdom.

Then they went to sleep; and next morning, when the sun woke them, a carriage came driving up with eight white horses, with white ostrich feathers on their heads and harness of golden chains; and standing behind it was the young King's servant, Faithful Henry. Faithful Henry had been so unhappy when his master was turned into a frog that he had had three iron bands fastened round his heart to stop it bursting with grief.

The carriage was to take the young King home to his kingdom. Faithful Henry helped the two of them in and took his place behind again, and he was full of joy that his master was free.

When they had gone part of the way, the King's son heard a cracking behind him, as if something had broken. So he turned round and called, "Henry, the carriage is breaking!"

"No, master, it isn't the carriage. It is a band from around my heart, which was put there when I was in such pain, when you were a frog and shut up in that well."

Again, and once again, something cracked while they were on their way, and each time the King's son thought the carriage was breaking. But it was only the bands springing off the heart of faithful Henry, because his master was free and happy.


Cat and Mouse in Partnership

A certain cat got to know a mouse, and talked to her so much about the great love and friendship she felt for her that in the end the mouse agreed that they should live together and keep house.

"But we must lay in something for the winter, or we shall go hungry," said the cat. "And you, little mouse, can't go venturing everywhere — you'll get caught in a trap one of these days."

The good advice was taken, and they bought a pot of fat; but they did not know where to put it. At last, after a great deal of thought, the cat said, "I don't know anywhere it would be better stored than in the church, for nobody dares to take anything from there. We'll set it under the altar and not touch it until we really need it."

So the pot was put in a safe place. But it was not long before the cat began to have a great longing for it, and said to the mouse, "I want to tell you something, little mouse. My cousin has brought a little son into the world and has asked me to be godmother. He's white with brown spots, and I'm to hold him over the font at the christening. Let me go out today, and you look after the house on your own."

"Yes, yes," said the mouse, "off you go by all means; and if you get anything really good, think of me — I'd like a drop of the sweet red christening wine myself."

But none of it was true. The cat had no cousin and had not been asked to be anybody's godmother. She went straight to the church, crept to the pot of fat, and began to lick, and licked the top of the fat off. Then she took a stroll over the roofs of the town, looked out for opportunities, stretched herself in the sun, and licked her lips every time she thought of that pot of fat; and it was evening before she came home.

"Well, here you are again," said the mouse. "I'm sure you've had a lovely day."

"It all went off very well," said the cat.

"What name did they give the child?"

"Top-off," said the cat, quite coolly.

"Top-off!" cried the mouse. "What a very odd, uncommon name. Is it usual in your family?"

"What does it matter?" said the cat. "It's no worse than Crumb-stealer, which is what your godchildren are called."

Before long the cat was seized by another fit of longing. She said to the mouse, "You must do me a favour and manage the house alone for a day again. I have been asked to be godmother a second time, and since the child has a white ring round its neck, I can't refuse."

The good mouse agreed; but the cat slipped along behind the town walls to the church and ate half the pot of fat. "Nothing ever tastes as good as what you keep to yourself," she said, and was thoroughly satisfied with her day's work.

When she came home the mouse asked, "And what was this child christened?"

"Half-gone," answered the cat.

"Half-gone! What are you talking about? I never heard the name in my life. I'll wager anything it isn't in the calendar."

Soon the cat's mouth began to water for more licking. "Good things come in threes," she said. "I've been asked to stand godmother again. The child is completely black, only it has white paws; apart from that there isn't a single white hair on its whole body. That only happens once in a few years. You'll let me go, won't you?"

"Top-off! Half-gone!" said the mouse. "Such odd names — they set me thinking."

"That's because you sit at home," said the cat, "in your dark grey fur coat and your long tail, getting full of fancies. It comes of never going out in the daytime."

While the cat was away the mouse cleaned the house and put it in order. And the greedy cat emptied the pot of fat completely. "Once everything's eaten up, you get some peace," she said to herself; and, well filled and fat, she did not come home till night.

The mouse asked at once what name the third child had been given.

"It won't please you any better than the others," said the cat. "He's called All-gone."

"All-gone!" cried the mouse. "That's the most suspicious name of the lot. I've never seen it in print. All-gone — what can it mean?" And she shook her head, curled herself up, and went to sleep.

After that nobody invited the cat to be godmother any more. But when the winter came and there was nothing to be found outside, the mouse remembered their store and said, "Come along, cat, let's go to our pot of fat that we saved up. We shall enjoy that."

"Yes," said the cat, "you'll enjoy it about as much as you'd enjoy sticking that dainty tongue of yours out of the window."

They set off; and when they got there, the pot of fat was certainly still in its place — but it was empty.

"Oh!" said the mouse. "Now I see what has happened. Now it all comes out. Some friend you are! You ate the whole lot while you were standing godmother. First top off, then half gone, then —"

"Will you hold your tongue!" cried the cat. "One more word and I'll eat you too."

"All gone" was already on the poor mouse's lips. She had barely said it before the cat sprang, caught her, and swallowed her down.

And that is the way of the world.


Our Lady’s Child

At the edge of a great forest there lived a woodcutter and his wife, who had one child, a little girl three years old. They were so poor, though, that they no longer had their daily bread and did not know how to find food for her.

One morning the woodcutter went out to his work in the forest full of care; and while he was cutting wood, suddenly there stood in front of him a tall and beautiful woman with a crown of shining stars on her head, who said, "I am the Virgin Mary, the mother of the child Jesus. You are poor and in need. Bring me your child; I will take her with me and be her mother and care for her."

The woodcutter did as he was told, brought his child and gave her to the Virgin Mary, who took her up to heaven. There the child did well: she ate sugar cakes and drank sweet milk, her clothes were of gold, and the little angels played with her.

And when she was fourteen years old, the Virgin Mary called her one day and said, "Dear child, I am going on a long journey. Take the keys of the thirteen doors of heaven into your keeping. Twelve of them you may open, and see the glory inside; but the thirteenth, which this little key belongs to, is forbidden you. Take care not to open it, or you will bring misery on yourself."

The girl promised to obey; and when the Virgin Mary was gone she began to look through the dwellings of the kingdom of heaven. Every day she opened one of them, until she had been through all twelve. In each of them one of the Apostles sat in the middle of a great light, and she delighted in all the magnificence and splendour, and the little angels who always went with her delighted in it too.

Then only the forbidden door was left, and she felt a great longing to know what could be hidden behind it, and said to the angels, "I won't open it right up, and I won't go inside — I'll only unlock it so that we can see a little through the crack."

"Oh no," said the little angels, "that would be a sin. The Virgin Mary has forbidden it, and it could very easily be the ruin of you."

Then she said nothing more; but the longing in her heart was not quieted. It gnawed at her and tormented her and gave her no peace. And once, when the angels had all gone out, she thought, "Now I am quite alone, and I could peep in. If I do it, nobody will ever know."

She hunted out the key; and when she had it in her hand she put it in the lock, and when she had put it in, she turned it. Then the door sprang open, and there she saw the Trinity sitting in fire and splendour. She stood a while and looked at it all in amazement; then she touched the light with her finger, just a little, and her finger turned entirely to gold.

Immediately a great fear came over her. She slammed the door and ran away. And her terror would not leave her, whatever she did; her heart beat and beat and would not be still, and the gold stayed on her finger and would not come off, however much she rubbed it and washed it.

It was not long before the Virgin Mary came back from her journey. She called the girl to her and asked for the keys of heaven. When the girl handed her the bunch, the Virgin looked into her eyes and said, "Did you not open the thirteenth door as well?"

"No," she answered.

Then the Virgin laid her hand on the girl's heart and felt how it beat and beat, and saw perfectly well that she had disobeyed and had opened the door. So she said again, "Are you certain you did not do it?"

"Yes," said the girl, a second time.

Then the Virgin noticed the finger that had turned to gold from touching the fire of heaven, and saw plainly that the child had sinned, and said a third time, "Did you not do it?"

"No," said the girl, for the third time.

Then the Virgin Mary said, "You have not obeyed me, and on top of that you have lied. You are not fit to be in heaven any longer."

Then the girl fell into a deep sleep; and when she woke she was lying on the earth below, in the middle of a wilderness. She wanted to call out, but she could not make a sound. She jumped up and tried to run away, but whichever way she turned she was held back by thick hedges of thorns that she could not break through.

In the wilderness where she was shut in there stood an old hollow tree, and this had to be her home. She crept into it when night came, and slept there; and there she found shelter from storm and rain. But it was a miserable life, and she wept bitterly when she remembered how happy she had been in heaven and how the angels had played with her.

Roots and wild berries were all she had to eat, and she went as far as she could to look for them. In the autumn she gathered up the fallen nuts and leaves and carried them into her hollow. The nuts were her food in winter; and when the snow and ice came she crept in among the leaves like a poor little animal, so as not to freeze. Before long her clothes were all torn and fell off her, one piece after another. But as soon as the sun shone warm again she went out and sat in front of the tree, and her long hair covered her on every side like a cloak. And so she sat, year after year, and felt the pain and misery of the world.

One day, when the trees were freshly green again, the King of that country was hunting in the forest and chasing a deer; and as it had fled into the thicket that closed off this part of the forest, he got down from his horse, tore the bushes apart and cut himself a path with his sword. When at last he had forced his way through, he saw a wonderfully beautiful girl sitting under the tree, covered from head to foot in her own golden hair.

He stood still and looked at her, full of astonishment; then he spoke to her and said, "Who are you? Why are you sitting here in the wilderness?"

But she gave him no answer, because she could not open her mouth.

The King went on, "Will you come with me to my castle?"

At that she just nodded her head a little. The King took her up in his arms, carried her to his horse and rode home with her; and when he came to the royal castle he had her dressed in beautiful clothes and gave her everything in abundance. And although she could not speak, she was so beautiful and so charming that he began to love her with his whole heart, and it was not long before he married her.

When a year or so had gone by, the Queen brought a son into the world. And that night, as she lay in her bed alone, the Virgin Mary appeared to her and said, "If you will tell the truth and admit that you unlocked the forbidden door, I will open your mouth and give you back your speech. But if you go on in your sin and stubbornly deny it, I will take your new-born child away with me."

Then the Queen was allowed to answer; but she stayed hard, and said, "No, I did not open the forbidden door." And the Virgin Mary took the new-born child out of her arms and vanished with it.

Next morning, when the child was nowhere to be found, it was whispered among the people that the Queen was an eater of human flesh and had killed her own child. She heard it all and could say nothing against it; but the King would not believe it, because he loved her so much.

When a year had gone by the Queen bore another son, and in the night the Virgin Mary came to her again and said, "If you will admit that you opened the forbidden door, I will give you your child back and untie your tongue. But if you go on in your sin and deny it, I will take this new child too."

Then the Queen said again, "No, I did not open the forbidden door." And the Virgin took the child out of her arms and away with her to heaven.

Next morning, when this child had disappeared as well, the people said out loud that the Queen had devoured it, and the King's councillors demanded that she be brought to trial. But the King loved her so dearly that he would not believe it, and ordered the councillors, on pain of death, to say no more about it.

The following year the Queen gave birth to a beautiful little daughter; and for the third time the Virgin Mary appeared to her in the night and said, "Follow me."

She took the Queen by the hand and led her to heaven, and showed her there her two eldest children, who smiled at her and were playing with the ball of the world. When the Queen was gladdened by the sight, the Virgin Mary said, "Is your heart not softened yet? If you will own that you opened the forbidden door, I will give you back your two little sons."

But for the third time the Queen answered, "No, I did not open the forbidden door."

Then the Virgin let her sink down to earth again, and took her third child from her as well.

Next morning, when the loss was reported, all the people cried out, "The Queen is an eater of human flesh! She must be judged." And this time the King could not hold his councillors back. So a trial was held; and since she could not answer and could not defend herself, she was condemned to be burned alive.

The wood was piled up; and when she was tied fast to the stake and the fire began to burn around her, the hard ice of her pride melted, her heart was moved to repentance, and she thought, "If only I could confess before I die that I opened that door."

Then her voice came back to her, and she cried out loudly, "Yes, Mary, I did it!"

And at once rain fell from the sky and put out the flames, and a light broke out above her, and the Virgin Mary came down with the two little sons at her side and the new-born daughter in her arms. She spoke kindly to her and said, "Whoever repents of a sin and admits it is forgiven." Then she gave her the three children, untied her tongue, and granted her happiness for the whole of her life.
