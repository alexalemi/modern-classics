The Goose-Girl

There was once an old Queen whose husband had been dead many years, and she had a beautiful daughter. When the princess grew up she was betrothed to a prince who lived a great way off.

When the time came for her to be married and she had to travel away into that distant kingdom, the old Queen packed up for her a great many costly vessels of silver and gold, and ornaments of gold and silver too, and cups and jewels — in short, everything that belonged to a royal dowry, for she loved her child with all her heart. She sent her waiting maid with her as well, who was to ride beside her and hand her over to the bridegroom; and each of them had a horse for the journey. But the King's daughter's horse was called Falada, and could speak.

So when the hour of parting came, the old mother went into her bedroom, took a little knife and cut her finger with it until it bled; then she held a white handkerchief under it and let three drops of blood fall on it, and gave it to her daughter and said, "Dear child, take good care of this. It will be of service to you on your way."

So they took a sorrowful leave of each other. The princess put the piece of cloth in her bosom, mounted her horse, and set off to her bridegroom.

After she had ridden a while she felt a burning thirst, and said to her waiting maid, "Get down and take my cup, the one you're carrying for me, and fetch me some water from the stream. I should like a drink."

"If you're thirsty," said the waiting maid, "get off your horse yourself and lie down and drink out of the water. I don't choose to be your servant."

So in her great thirst the princess got down, bent over the water in the stream and drank, and was not allowed to drink out of the golden cup. Then she said, "Ah, heaven!" — and the three drops of blood answered:

	"If your mother knew, her heart would break."

But the King's daughter was humble, and said nothing, and got back on her horse.

She rode some miles further; but the day was warm and the sun scorched her, and she was thirsty again; and when they came to a stream she called out to her waiting maid once more, "Get down and give me some water in my golden cup" — for she had long since forgotten the girl's unkind words.

But the waiting maid said, more haughtily still, "If you want to drink, drink as best you can. I don't choose to be your maid."

Then in her great thirst the King's daughter got down, bent over the running stream, wept and said, "Ah, heaven!" — and the drops of blood answered again:

	"If your mother knew, her heart would break."

And as she was drinking and leaning right out over the stream, the handkerchief with the three drops of blood fell out of her bosom and floated away with the water, without her noticing it, so great was her distress. But the waiting maid had seen it, and she rejoiced to think that she now had power over the bride; for now that the princess had lost the drops of blood she had become weak and helpless.

So when she wanted to get back on her horse — the one called Falada — the waiting maid said, "Falada suits me better; my nag will do for you." And the princess had to be content with that.

Then the waiting maid, with a great many hard words, told the princess to change her royal clothes for her own shabby ones; and at last she was made to swear by the clear sky above her that she would not say one word of this to anybody at the royal court. And if she had not sworn that oath, she would have been killed on the spot. But Falada saw it all, and marked it well.

So the waiting maid mounted Falada and the true bride the poor horse, and they travelled on until at last they came to the royal palace. There were great rejoicings over her arrival, and the prince sprang forward to meet her and lifted the waiting maid down from her horse, believing that she was his bride. She was led up the stairs; but the real princess was left standing below.

Then the old King looked out of the window and saw her standing in the courtyard, and saw how dainty and delicate and beautiful she was; and he went at once up to the royal apartment and asked the bride about the girl she had brought with her, who was standing down there in the courtyard, and who she was.

"I picked her up on the way, for company. Give the girl some work to do, so that she isn't standing about idle."

But the old King had no work for her and could think of none, so he said, "I have a little boy who looks after the geese. She can help him."

The boy was called Conrad, and the true bride had to help him look after the geese.

Soon afterwards the false bride said to the young King, "Dearest husband, I beg you to do me a favour."

He answered, "I'll do it most willingly."

"Then send for the knacker and have the head cut off the horse I rode here on. It vexed me on the way."

In truth she was afraid the horse might tell how she had treated the King's daughter. And she managed to get the King to promise that it should be done, and faithful Falada was to die.

This came to the real princess's ears, and she secretly promised the knacker a piece of gold if he would do her a small service. There was a great dark gateway in the town through which she had to pass with the geese every morning and evening; would he be so good as to nail Falada's head up on it, so that she might see him again, more than just once?

The knacker's man promised to do it, and cut off the head and nailed it fast under the dark gateway.

Early in the morning, when she and Conrad drove their flock out under this gateway, she said as she passed:

	"Alas, Falada, hanging there!"

Then the head answered:

	"Alas, young Queen, how ill you fare!
	If this your tender mother knew,
	her heart would surely break in two."

Then they went on out of the town and drove their geese into the country. And when they came to the meadow she sat down and unbound her hair, which was like pure gold; and Conrad saw it and was delighted by its brightness, and wanted to pull out a few hairs. Then she said:

	"Blow, blow, you gentle wind, I say,
	blow Conrad's little hat away,
	and make him chase it here and there
	until I have braided all my hair
	and bound it up again."

And there came such a violent gust of wind that it blew Conrad's hat far away across the country, and he had to run after it. When he came back she had finished combing her hair and was putting it up again, and he could not get a single hair of it. So Conrad was angry and would not speak to her; and they watched the geese until evening, and then went home.

Next day, as they were driving the geese out through the dark gateway, the girl said:

	"Alas, Falada, hanging there!"

And Falada answered:

	"Alas, young Queen, how ill you fare!
	If this your tender mother knew,
	her heart would surely break in two."

And she sat down again in the field and began to comb out her hair, and Conrad ran up and tried to snatch at it; so she said quickly:

	"Blow, blow, you gentle wind, I say,
	blow Conrad's little hat away,
	and make him chase it here and there
	until I have braided all my hair
	and bound it up again."

Then the wind blew and blew his little hat off his head and far away, and Conrad had to run after it; and when he came back her hair had been up a long while, and he could get none of it. And so they looked after their geese till evening came.

But that evening, after they had got home, Conrad went to the old King and said, "I won't look after the geese with that girl any longer."

"Why not?" asked the old King.

"Oh, because she vexes me the whole day long."

Then the old King ordered him to tell what it was that she did to him. And Conrad said, "In the morning, when we go under the dark gateway with the flock, there's a sorry old horse's head on the wall, and she says to it, 'Alas, Falada, hanging there!' — and the head answers, 'Alas, young Queen, how ill you fare! If this your tender mother knew, her heart would surely break in two.'"

And Conrad went on to tell what happened out on the goose pasture, and how he had to chase his hat there.

The old King told him to drive his flock out again next day; and as soon as morning came he stationed himself behind the dark gateway and heard how the girl spoke to Falada's head. And then he went out into the country too and hid himself in a thicket in the meadow. There he soon saw with his own eyes the goose-girl and the goose-boy bringing their flock, and how after a while she sat down and unplaited her hair, which shone with a radiance of its own. And soon she said:

	"Blow, blow, you gentle wind, I say,
	blow Conrad's little hat away,
	and make him chase it here and there
	until I have braided all my hair
	and bound it up again."

Then came a gust of wind and carried off Conrad's hat, so that he had to run a long way after it, while the girl quietly went on combing and plaiting her hair; and the King saw it all.

Then he went away unseen; and when the goose-girl came home in the evening he called her aside and asked why she did all these things.

"I may not tell you that, and I dare not pour out my sorrows to any human being; for I've sworn not to, by the heaven above me. If I hadn't sworn it, I should have lost my life."

He pressed her and gave her no peace, but he could get nothing out of her. So he said, "If you won't tell me anything, then tell your sorrows to that iron stove there" — and went away.

Then she crept into the iron stove and began to weep and lament and empty out her whole heart, and said, "Here I am, deserted by the whole world, and yet I'm a King's daughter; and a false waiting maid has forced me to such a pass that I've had to take off my royal clothes, and she has taken my place with my bridegroom, and I have to do servant's work as a goose-girl. If my mother only knew, her heart would break."

But the old King was standing outside by the stovepipe, listening to what she said, and heard it all. Then he came back in and told her to come out of the stove. And royal clothes were put on her, and it was a marvel how beautiful she was.

The old King called his son and told him that he had got the false bride, who was only a waiting maid, and that the true one was standing there — the girl who had been the goose-girl. The young King rejoiced with all his heart when he saw her beauty and her youth; and a great feast was prepared, to which all the people and all their good friends were invited.

At the head of the table sat the bridegroom, with the King's daughter on one side of him and the waiting maid on the other; but the waiting maid was blinded, and did not recognise the princess in her dazzling clothes.

When they had eaten and drunk and were merry, the old King put a riddle to the waiting maid: what did a person deserve who had behaved in such and such a way to her mistress? And he told the whole story, and asked what sentence such a one deserved.

Then the false bride said, "She deserves no better than to be stripped naked and put in a barrel studded on the inside with pointed nails; and two white horses should be harnessed to it and drag her along through one street after another until she is dead."

"That is you," said the old King, "and you have pronounced your own sentence; and that is what shall be done to you."

And when the sentence had been carried out, the young King married his true bride, and the two of them ruled over their kingdom in peace and happiness.
