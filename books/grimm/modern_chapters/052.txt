The Skilful Huntsman

There was once a young fellow who had learned the locksmith's trade, and told his father he would now go out into the world and seek his fortune.

"Very well," said his father, "I'm quite content with that" — and gave him some money for the journey.

So he travelled about and looked for work. After a time he decided not to follow the locksmith's trade any more, for he had lost his liking for it; but he took a fancy to hunting.

Then on his wanderings he met a huntsman dressed in green, who asked where he came from and where he was going. The young man said he was a locksmith's apprentice, but that the trade no longer pleased him and he had a liking for huntsmanship; would he teach it to him?

"Oh, yes," said the huntsman, "if you'll come with me."

So the young fellow went with him, bound himself to him for some years, and learned the art of hunting. After that he wanted to try his luck elsewhere; and the huntsman gave him nothing by way of payment but an air gun, which had this property, though: that it hit its mark without fail whenever he shot with it.

Then he set out, and found himself in a very large forest that he could not get to the end of in a day. When evening came he sat himself up in a high tree to be out of reach of the wild beasts. Towards midnight it seemed to him that a tiny little light was glimmering in the distance. So he looked down through the branches towards it, and fixed firmly in his mind where it was. But first of all he took off his hat and threw it down in the direction of the light, so that when he had climbed down he could go to the hat as a marker. Then he got down and went to his hat, put it on again, and went straight on.

The further he went, the bigger the light grew; and when he got close to it he saw that it was an enormous fire, with three giants sitting by it, who had an ox on a spit and were roasting it.

Presently one of them said, "I must just taste whether the meat will soon be fit to eat" — and pulled a piece off, and was about to put it in his mouth when the huntsman shot it out of his hand.

"Well, really," said the giant, "if the wind hasn't blown the bit out of my hand!" — and helped himself to another. But just as he was about to bite into it, the huntsman shot it away from him again.

At this the giant gave the one sitting next to him a box on the ear, and cried angrily, "Why are you snatching my piece away from me?"

"I haven't snatched it away," said the other. "Some sharpshooter must have shot it away from you."

The giant took another piece, but he could not keep it in his hand either, for the huntsman shot it out. Then the giant said, "That must be a good shot, to shoot the bit out of a man's very mouth. Somebody like that would be useful to us." And he called out loudly, "Come here, sharpshooter! Sit down by the fire beside us and eat your fill. We won't hurt you. But if you won't come, and we have to bring you by force, you're a lost man."

At this the young man went up to them and told them he was a skilled huntsman, and that whatever he aimed his gun at, he was sure to hit.

Then they said that if he would come with them he should be well treated; and they told him that outside the forest there was a great lake, and behind it stood a tower, and in the tower was a lovely princess shut up, whom they very much wanted to carry off.

"Yes," said he, "I'll soon get her for you."

Then they added, "But there's something else. There's a tiny little dog that starts barking the moment anybody comes near; and as soon as it barks, everybody in the royal palace wakes up. That's why we can't get in. Can you undertake to shoot it dead?"

"Yes," said he, "that will be a bit of fun for me."

After this he got into a boat and rowed across the lake; and as soon as he landed the little dog came running out and was about to bark, but the huntsman took his air gun and shot it dead.

When the giants saw that they rejoiced, and thought they had the King's daughter safe already; but the huntsman wanted first to see how things stood, and told them they must stay outside until he called them.

Then he went into the castle, and inside all was perfectly quiet and everybody was asleep. When he opened the door of the first room, there was a sword hanging on the wall made of pure silver, with a golden star on it and the King's name; and on a table near it lay a sealed letter, which he broke open, and inside it was written that whoever had the sword could kill everything that opposed him. So he took the sword down from the wall, hung it at his side and went on.

Then he came into the room where the King's daughter lay sleeping; and she was so beautiful that he stood still and held his breath and looked at her. He thought to himself, "How can I hand an innocent girl over to those wild giants, who have evil in their minds?"

He looked further about him; and under the bed stood a pair of slippers, with her father's name and a star on the right one and her own name and a star on the left. She was wearing a great silk neckerchief embroidered with gold, and on the right side was her father's name and on the left her own, all in golden letters.

So the huntsman took a pair of scissors and cut the right corner off it and put it in his knapsack; and then he took the right slipper too, with the King's name on it, and pushed that in. And the girl still lay sleeping; and she was sewn right into her nightdress, and he cut a piece from that as well and pushed it in with the rest — but he did it all without touching her. Then he went out and left her lying asleep and undisturbed.

And when he came back to the gate, the giants were still standing outside waiting for him, expecting that he was bringing the princess. But he called to them to come in, since the girl was already in their power; and said that he could not open the gate to them, but there was a hole they must creep through.

So the first came up; and the huntsman wound the giant's hair round his hand, pulled his head through, and cut it off at one stroke with his sword, and then dragged the rest of him in. He called the second and cut his head off likewise, and then he killed the third as well.

And he was well pleased that he had freed the beautiful girl from her enemies; and he cut out the giants' tongues and put them in his knapsack. Then he thought, "I'll go home to my father and let him see what I've done already, and after that I'll travel about the world. The luck God is pleased to grant me will find me easily enough."

But when the King in the castle woke up, he saw the three giants lying there dead. So he went into his daughter's bedroom and woke her and asked who could have killed the giants.

Then she said, "Dear father, I don't know. I was asleep."

But when she got up and went to put on her slippers, the right one was gone; and when she looked at her neckerchief it was cut, and the right corner was missing; and when she looked at her nightdress a piece was cut out of it.

The King called his whole court together, soldiers and everybody else who was there, and asked who had set his daughter free and killed the giants. Now it happened that he had a captain who was one-eyed and a hideous man, and he said that he had done it. So the old King said that as he had accomplished it, he should marry his daughter.

But the girl said, "Rather than marry him, dear father, I'll go away into the world as far as my legs will carry me."

But the King said that if she would not marry him, she should take off her royal clothes and wear peasant's clothing and go out; and that she should go to a potter and start a trade in earthenware pots.

So she took off her royal clothes and went to a potter and borrowed enough crockery for a stall, and promised him too that if she sold it by the evening she would pay him for it. Then the King said she was to sit down in a corner with it and sell it; and he arranged with some peasants to drive their carts over it, so that everything would be broken into a thousand pieces.

So when the King's daughter had set out her stall in the street, along came the carts and broke everything she had into tiny fragments. She began to weep and said, "Alas, how shall I ever pay for the pots now?"

The King had meant by this to force her into marrying the captain; but instead she went to the potter again and asked whether he would lend to her once more. He said no; she must first pay for the things she had already had.

Then she went to her father and cried and lamented, and said she would go out into the world.

Then he said, "I'll have a little hut built for you out in the forest, and you shall stay in it all your life long and cook for everybody — but you shall take no money for it."

When the hut was ready, a sign was hung on the door with these words on it: "Today given, tomorrow sold."

She stayed there a long time, and word went about the world that there was a girl there who cooked without asking for payment, and that this was set out on a sign outside her door. The huntsman heard it too, and thought to himself, "That would suit you. You're poor, and you have no money."

So he took his air gun and his knapsack — in which all the things he had carried away from the castle as proofs of his honesty were still lying — and went into the forest and found the hut with its sign, "Today given, tomorrow sold." He had put on the sword he had cut off the three giants' heads with, and so he went into the hut and asked to be given something to eat.

He was charmed by the beautiful girl, who was indeed as lovely as a picture. She asked him where he came from and where he was going, and he said, "I'm roaming about the world."

Then she asked him where he had got the sword, since her father's name was on it. He asked her whether she was the King's daughter.

"Yes," she answered.

"With this sword," said he, "I cut off the heads of three giants." And he took their tongues out of his knapsack as proof. Then he showed her the slipper too, and the corner of the neckerchief and the piece of the nightdress.

At this she was overjoyed, and said that he was the man who had delivered her.

So the two of them went to the old King and brought him back to the hut; and she led him into her room and told him that the huntsman was the man who had really freed her from the giants. And when the old King saw all the proofs of it, he could doubt no longer, and said he was very glad to know how it had all happened, and that the huntsman should have her for a wife; at which the girl was glad at heart.

Then she dressed the huntsman up as though he were a foreign lord, and the King ordered a feast to be prepared. When they went to table, the captain sat on the left side of the King's daughter and the huntsman on the right; and the captain thought he was a foreign lord who had come on a visit.

When they had eaten and drunk, the old King said to the captain that he would set him something to guess. "Suppose somebody said that he had killed three giants, and he were asked where the giants' tongues were, and he were made to go and look, and there were none in their heads — how could that happen?"

The captain said, "Then they can't have had any."

"Not so," said the King. "Every animal has a tongue." And then he asked as well what a man would deserve who gave such an answer.

The captain replied, "He ought to be torn to pieces."

Then the King said he had pronounced his own sentence; and the captain was put in prison and then torn into four pieces. But the King's daughter was married to the huntsman.

After this he fetched his father and mother, and they lived with their son in happiness; and after the old King's death he received the kingdom.

The Flail From Heaven

A countryman was once going out to plough with a pair of oxen. When he got to the field, both the animals' horns began to grow, and went on growing; and when he wanted to go home they were so big that the oxen could not get through the gateway for them.

By good luck a butcher came by just then, and he handed them over to him, and made the bargain this way: that he should bring the butcher a measure of turnip seed, and then the butcher was to count him out a Brabant thaler for every seed. Now, I call that well sold.

The peasant went home and carried the measure of turnip seed to him on his back. On the way, though, he lost one seed out of the bag. The butcher paid him fairly, as agreed; and if the peasant had not lost that seed he would have had one thaler more.

Meanwhile, as he went back along the road, the seed had grown into a tree that reached right up to the sky. Then the peasant thought, "Since you have the chance, you must just see what the angels up there are doing, and have them before your eyes for once."

So he climbed up, and saw that the angels up above were threshing oats, and he stood watching them. And while he was watching, he noticed that the tree he was standing on was beginning to sway. He peeped down and saw that somebody was just about to cut it down.

"It would be a bad business if I fell down from here," he thought. And in his need he could think of no better way to save himself than to take the chaff of the oats, which was lying about in heaps, and twist a rope out of it. He also grabbed a hoe and a flail that were lying about in heaven, and let himself down by the rope.

But he came down on earth exactly in the middle of a deep, deep hole. So it was a real piece of luck that he had brought the hoe, for he hoed himself a flight of steps with it and climbed up; and he took the flail with him as a token of his truthfulness, so that nobody could have any doubt about his story.
