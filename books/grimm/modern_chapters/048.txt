Bearskin

There was once a young fellow who enlisted as a soldier, conducted himself bravely, and was always the first man forward when it rained bullets. As long as the war lasted all went well; but when peace was made he got his discharge, and the captain said he might go where he liked.

His parents were dead and he had no home any more, so he went to his brothers and begged them to take him in and keep him until war broke out again. But his brothers were hard-hearted, and said, "What can we do with you? You're no use to us. Go and make a living for yourself."

The soldier had nothing left but his gun. He put that on his shoulder and went out into the world. He came to a wide heath with nothing to be seen on it but a ring of trees; and he sat down under them sorrowfully and began to think over his lot.

"I've no money," he thought. "I've learned no trade but fighting; and now that they've made peace they don't want me any longer. So I can see already that I shall have to starve."

All at once he heard a rustling; and when he looked round, a strange man was standing in front of him, wearing a green coat and looking very grand — but with a hideous cloven foot.

"I know already what you need," said the man. "Gold and property you shall have, as much of it as you can get through, do what you will. But first I must know whether you're fearless, so that I don't spend my money for nothing."

"A soldier and fear — how do those two go together?" he answered. "You can put me to the test."

"Very well, then," answered the man. "Look behind you."

The soldier turned round and saw a great bear coming growling towards him. "Oho," cried the soldier, "I'll tickle your nose for you, and you'll soon lose your taste for growling" — and he took aim at the bear and shot it through the muzzle. It fell down and never stirred again.

"I can see quite plainly," said the stranger, "that you're not lacking in courage. But there's another condition you'll have to fulfil."

"If it doesn't endanger my salvation," replied the soldier, who knew very well who was standing beside him. "If it does, I'll have nothing to do with it."

"You'll have to see to that for yourself," answered Greencoat. "For the next seven years you shall neither wash yourself nor comb your beard or your hair, nor cut your nails, nor say one Our Father. I'll give you a coat and a cloak, which you must wear all that time. If you die during these seven years you're mine; if you stay alive, you're free — and rich into the bargain for the rest of your life."

The soldier thought of the desperate straits he was now in; and as he had so often gone out to meet death, he decided to risk it now as well, and agreed to the terms.

The Devil took off his green coat, gave it to the soldier and said, "If you have this coat on your back and put your hand in the pocket, you'll always find it full of money." Then he pulled the skin off the bear and said, "This shall be your cloak and your bed as well, for you shall sleep on it and lie in no other bed; and because of these clothes you shall be called Bearskin."

And with that the Devil vanished.

The soldier put the coat on and felt in the pocket at once, and found that the thing was true. Then he put on the bearskin and went out into the world, and enjoyed himself, keeping back from nothing that did him good and his money harm.

During the first year his appearance was passable; but during the second he began to look like a monster. His hair covered nearly the whole of his face, his beard was like a piece of coarse felt, his fingers had claws, and his face was so caked with dirt that if cress had been sown on it, it would have come up. Everybody who saw him ran away; but as he gave the poor money everywhere to pray that he might not die within the seven years, and as he paid well for everything, he still always found shelter.

In the fourth year he came to an inn where the landlord would not take him in, and would not even let him have a place in the stable, because he was afraid the horses would take fright. But when Bearskin put his hand in his pocket and pulled out a handful of ducats, the landlord let himself be persuaded and gave him a room in an outhouse — though Bearskin had to promise not to let himself be seen, in case the inn got a bad name.

One evening, as Bearskin was sitting alone and wishing from the bottom of his heart that the seven years were over, he heard loud lamenting in a room nearby. He had a compassionate heart, so he opened the door and saw an old man weeping bitterly and wringing his hands.

Bearskin went closer, but the man sprang to his feet and tried to get away from him. At last, when the man realised that Bearskin's voice was a human voice, he let himself be talked round; and by kind words Bearskin got so far that the old man told him the cause of his grief. His property had dwindled away bit by bit, he and his daughters would have to starve, and he was so poor that he could not pay the innkeeper and was to be put in prison.

"If that's your only trouble," said Bearskin, "I have plenty of money." He had the innkeeper fetched, paid him, and put a purse full of gold into the poor old man's pocket besides.

When the old man found himself free of all his troubles, he did not know how to be grateful enough. "Come with me," he said to Bearskin. "My daughters are all marvels of beauty; choose one of them for a wife. When she hears what you've done for me, she won't refuse you. You do look a little strange, it's true, but she'll soon put you to rights again."

That pleased Bearskin well, and he went.

When the eldest saw him she was so terrified by his face that she screamed and ran away. The second stood still and looked him over from head to foot, but then she said, "How can I take a husband who has no human shape left? The shaven bear that came here once and passed itself off as a man pleased me far better — at least it wore a hussar's uniform and white gloves. If it were nothing but ugliness, I could get used to that."

The youngest, though, said, "Dear father, that must be a good man, to have helped you out of your trouble. So if you promised him a bride for doing it, your promise must be kept."

It was a pity that Bearskin's face was covered with dirt and hair; otherwise they might have seen how delighted he was when he heard these words. He took a ring off his finger, broke it in two, and gave her one half and kept the other for himself. But he wrote his name on her half and hers on his, and begged her to keep her piece carefully. And then he took his leave and said, "I still have three years to wander; and if I don't come back then, you're free, for I shall be dead. But pray to God to preserve my life."

The poor betrothed bride dressed herself all in black; and when she thought of her future bridegroom, tears came into her eyes. From her sisters she got nothing but contempt and mockery.

"Take care," said the eldest. "If you give him your hand, he'll dig his claws into it."

"Beware," said the second. "Bears like sweet things, and if he takes a fancy to you he'll eat you up."

"You'll always have to do as he likes," the elder began again, "or he'll growl."

And the second went on, "But it'll be a merry wedding, for bears dance well."

The bride kept silent and would not let them vex her.

Bearskin, meanwhile, travelled about the world from one place to another, did good where he could, and gave generously to the poor so that they would pray for him.

At length, as the last day of the seven years dawned, he went out on the heath once more and sat down under the ring of trees. It was not long before the wind whistled and the Devil stood in front of him, looking at him angrily. Then he threw Bearskin his old coat and asked for his own green one back.

"We haven't got that far yet," answered Bearskin. "First you must make me clean."

Whether the Devil liked it or not, he had to fetch water and wash Bearskin and comb his hair and cut his nails. And after this he looked like a fine soldier, and was much handsomer than he had ever been before.

When the Devil had gone away, Bearskin was quite light-hearted. He went into the town, put on a magnificent velvet coat, seated himself in a carriage drawn by four white horses, and drove to his bride's house. Nobody recognised him; her father took him for a distinguished general, and led him into the room where his daughters were sitting.

He was made to sit between the two eldest; they helped him to wine and gave him the best pieces of meat, and thought they had never seen a handsomer man in all the world. But the bride sat opposite him in her black dress and never raised her eyes or spoke a word.

When at length he asked her father whether he would give him one of his daughters for a wife, the two eldest jumped up and ran into their bedrooms to put on splendid dresses, for each of them imagined that she was the chosen one.

As soon as the stranger was alone with his bride, he brought out his half of the ring and dropped it into a glass of wine, which he passed across the table to her. She took the wine; but when she had drunk it and found the half ring lying at the bottom, her heart began to beat. She fetched the other half, which she wore on a ribbon round her neck, and joined them, and saw that the two pieces fitted exactly.

Then he said, "I'm your betrothed bridegroom, the one you saw as Bearskin. But by God's grace I've got my human shape back and become clean again." He went up to her and embraced her and gave her a kiss.

Meanwhile the two sisters came back in full dress; and when they saw that the handsome man had fallen to the youngest, and heard that he was Bearskin, they ran out full of anger and rage. One of them drowned herself in the well and the other hanged herself on a tree.

In the evening somebody knocked at the door; and when the bridegroom opened it, it was the Devil in his green coat, who said, "You see — now I've got two souls in place of your one."

The Willow-Wren and the Bear

One summertime the bear and the wolf were walking in the forest, and the bear heard a bird singing so beautifully that he said, "Brother wolf, what bird is that, singing so well?"

"That's the King of the birds," said the wolf, "and we must bow down before him." In fact it was only the willow-wren — the little bird the Germans call the Zaunkönig, the hedge-king.

"If that's so," said the bear, "I should very much like to see his royal palace. Come, take me there."

"That isn't done quite as easily as you seem to think," said the wolf. "You must wait until the Queen comes."

Soon afterwards the Queen arrived with some food in her beak, and the lord King came too, and they began to feed their young ones. The bear would have liked to go straight over, but the wolf held him back by the sleeve and said, "No, you must wait until my lord and lady have gone away again."

So they took note of the hole the nest was in, and trotted off.

The bear, though, could get no rest until he had seen the royal palace, and after a short while he went back to it. The King and Queen had just flown out, so he peeped in and saw five or six young ones lying in it.

"Is that the royal palace?" cried the bear. "It's a miserable palace, and you're no king's children — you're disreputable children!"

When the young wrens heard that they were furious, and screamed, "No, we're not! Our parents are honest people! Bear, you'll pay for that!"

The bear and the wolf grew uneasy, and turned back and went into their holes. But the young willow-wrens went on crying and screaming; and when their parents brought food again they said, "We won't so much as touch one fly's leg — no, not if we were dying of hunger — until you've settled whether we're respectable children or not. The bear has been here and insulted us!"

Then the old King said, "Be easy, he shall be punished" — and flew off at once with the Queen to the bear's cave and called in, "Old Growler, why have you insulted my children? You shall suffer for it. We'll punish you with a bloody war."

So war was declared on the bear, and all the four-footed animals were summoned to take part in it — oxen, donkeys, cows, deer and every other animal the earth held. And the willow-wren summoned everything that flew in the air: not only birds large and small, but midges and hornets, bees and flies had to come.

When the time came for the war to begin, the willow-wren sent out spies to find out who was the enemy's commander-in-chief. The gnat, who was the craftiest, flew into the forest where the enemy was gathered, and hid under a leaf of the tree where the watchword was to be given.

There stood the bear; and he called the fox in front of him and said, "Fox, you're the cunningest of all the animals. You shall be general and lead us."

"Good," said the fox, "but what signal shall we agree on?"

Nobody knew, so the fox said, "I have a fine long bushy tail that looks almost like a plume of red feathers. When I hold my tail right up in the air, all is going well and you must charge; but if I let it hang down, run away as fast as you can."

When the gnat had heard that, she flew back and reported everything to the willow-wren, in the greatest detail.

When day broke and the battle was to begin, all the four-footed animals came running up with such a noise that the earth trembled. The willow-wren came flying through the air too with his army, with such a humming and whirring and swarming that everybody was uneasy and afraid; and the two sides advanced against each other.

But the willow-wren sent the hornet down with orders to get under the fox's tail and sting with all his might. When the fox felt the first sting he started so with the pain that he drew one leg up; but he bore it, and still kept his tail high in the air. At the second sting he had to let it drop for a moment. At the third he could hold out no longer, and screamed out and put his tail between his legs.

When the animals saw that, they thought all was lost and began to run, each into his own hole; and the birds had won the battle.

Then the King and Queen flew home to their children and cried, "Children, rejoice! Eat and drink to your hearts' content — we've won the battle!"

But the young wrens said, "We won't eat yet. The bear must come to the nest and beg our pardon and say that we're honourable children, before we'll do that."

So the willow-wren flew to the bear's hole and cried, "Growler, you're to come to the nest and beg my children's pardon, or else every rib in your body shall be broken."

So the bear crept over in the greatest fear and begged their pardon. And now at last the young wrens were satisfied, and sat down together and ate and drank and made merry until quite late into the night.

Sweet Porridge

There was a poor but good little girl who lived alone with her mother, and they had nothing left to eat.

So the child went into the forest, and there an old woman met her who knew all about her trouble, and gave her a little pot which, when she said, "Cook, little pot, cook," would cook good sweet porridge; and when she said, "Stop, little pot," it stopped cooking.

The girl took the pot home to her mother, and now they were free of their poverty and hunger, and ate sweet porridge as often as they liked.

Once, when the girl had gone out, her mother said, "Cook, little pot, cook." And it did cook, and she ate until she had had enough; and then she wanted the pot to stop cooking, but she did not know the word. So it went on cooking, and the porridge rose over the edge, and still it cooked on, until the kitchen and the whole house were full, and then the next house, and then the whole street — just as if it wanted to satisfy the hunger of the whole world. And there was the greatest distress, but nobody knew how to stop it.

At last, when only one single house was left, the child came home and just said, "Stop, little pot" — and it stopped and gave up cooking; and anybody who wanted to get back into the town had to eat his way there.
