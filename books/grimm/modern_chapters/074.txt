The Goose-Girl at the Well

There was once a very old woman who lived with her flock of geese in a waste place among the mountains, where she had a little house. The waste was surrounded by a large forest, and every morning the old woman took her crutch and hobbled into it.

Once there, though, the old dame was quite active — more so than anybody would have thought, considering her age. She gathered grass for her geese, picked all the wild fruit she could reach, and carried everything home on her back. Anybody would have thought that the heavy load must weigh her to the ground, but she always brought it safely home.

If anyone met her, she greeted him quite courteously. "Good day, dear neighbour, it's a fine day. Ah, you wonder that I should drag grass about; but everybody has to take his burden on his back."

All the same, people did not like to meet her if they could help it, and would take a roundabout way by choice. And when a father passed her with his boys, he whispered to them, "Beware of that old woman. She has claws under her gloves; she's a witch."

One morning a handsome young man was going through the forest. The sun shone bright, the birds sang, a cool breeze crept through the leaves, and he was full of joy and gladness. He had met nobody yet, when he suddenly caught sight of the old witch kneeling on the ground cutting grass with a sickle. She had already stuffed a whole load into her cloth, and near it stood two baskets filled with wild apples and pears.

"But, good little mother," said he, "how can you carry all that away?"

"I have to carry it, dear sir," answered she. "Rich folk's children have no need to do such things; but among peasant folk the saying goes, 'Don't look behind you — you'll only see how crooked your back is.'"

"Will you help me?" she said, as he stayed standing beside her. "You still have a straight back and young legs; it would be nothing to you. Besides, my house isn't so very far from here — it stands over there on the heath behind the hill. How soon you'd be up there!"

The young man took pity on the old woman. "My father is certainly no peasant," replied he, "but a rich count; still, so that you can see that it isn't only peasants who can carry things, I'll take your bundle."

"If you'll try it," said she, "I shall be very glad. You'll certainly have to walk for an hour — but what will that matter to you? Only you must carry the apples and pears as well."

It began to seem a little serious to the young man when he heard about the hour's walk; but the old woman would not let him off, and packed the bundle on his back and hung the two baskets on his arm.

"See, it's quite light," said she.

"No, it isn't light," answered the count, and pulled a rueful face. "Truly, the bundle weighs as heavily as if it were full of cobblestones, and the apples and pears are as heavy as lead. I can hardly breathe."

He had a mind to put everything down again, but the old woman would not allow it. "Just look," said she mockingly, "the young gentleman won't carry what I, an old woman, have dragged along so often. You're ready enough with fine words; but when it comes to earnest, you want to take to your heels. Why are you standing there loitering?" she went on. "Step out. Nobody's going to take that bundle off you again."

As long as he walked on level ground it was still bearable; but when they came to the hill and had to climb, and the stones rolled away under his feet as if they were alive, it was beyond his strength. The drops of sweat stood on his forehead and ran down his back, hot and cold.

"Dame," said he, "I can go no further. I want to rest a little."

"Not here," answered the old woman. "When we've reached our journey's end you can rest; but now you must go on. Who knows what good it may do you?"

"Old woman, you're becoming shameless," said the count, and tried to throw off the bundle. But he laboured in vain; it stuck as fast to his back as if it had grown there. He turned and twisted, but could not get rid of it.

The old woman laughed at this, and sprang about on her crutch quite delighted. "Don't get angry, dear sir," said she. "You're going as red in the face as a turkey-cock. Carry your bundle patiently; I'll give you a good present when we get home."

What could he do? He had to submit to his fate and crawl along patiently behind the old woman. She seemed to grow more and more nimble, and his burden heavier and heavier. All at once she made a spring, jumped up onto the bundle and seated herself on top of it; and however withered she might be, she was heavier than the stoutest country lass.

The young man's knees trembled; and when he did not go on, the old woman hit him about the legs with a switch and with stinging nettles. Groaning all the way, he climbed the mountain, and at length reached the old woman's house just as he was about to drop.

When the geese saw the old woman they flapped their wings and stretched out their necks and ran to meet her, cackling all the while. Behind the flock walked an old girl, stick in hand, strong and big, but as ugly as night.

"Good mother," said she to the old woman, "has anything happened to you? You've stayed away so long."

"Not at all, my dear daughter," answered she. "Nothing bad has happened to me. On the contrary, I met this kind gentleman, who has carried my burden for me. Only think — he even took me on his back when I was tired. The way hasn't seemed long to us either; we've been merry, and cracking jokes with each other the whole time."

At last the old woman slid down and took the bundle off the young man's back and the baskets off his arm, and looked at him quite kindly and said, "Now sit down on the bench in front of the door and rest. You've fairly earned your wages, and they shan't be wanting."

Then she said to the goose-girl, "Go into the house, my dear daughter. It isn't fitting for you to be alone with a young gentleman; one mustn't pour oil on the fire — he might fall in love with you."

The count did not know whether to laugh or cry. "A sweetheart like that," thought he, "couldn't touch my heart, even if she were thirty years younger."

Meanwhile the old woman stroked and fondled her geese as if they were children, and then went into the house with her daughter.

The young man lay down on the bench under a wild apple tree. The air was warm and mild; on all sides stretched a green meadow set with cowslips and wild thyme and a thousand other flowers; through the middle of it rippled a clear brook with the sun sparkling on it, and the white geese walked to and fro or paddled in the water.

"It's quite delightful here," said he, "but I'm so tired that I can't keep my eyes open. I'll sleep a little. If only a gust of wind doesn't come and blow my legs off my body — they're as rotten as tinder."

When he had slept a while, the old woman came and shook him till he woke. "Sit up," said she. "You can't stay here. I've certainly treated you hard, but it hasn't cost you your life. Of money and land you have no need; here's something else for you."

And with that she thrust a little book into his hand, which was cut out of a single emerald. "Take great care of it," said she. "It will bring you good fortune."

The count sprang up; and as he felt himself quite fresh and with his strength back, he thanked the old woman for her present and set off without so much as one backward look at her beautiful daughter. When he was already some way off he could still hear the noisy cry of the geese in the distance.

The count had to wander in the wilderness for three days before he could find his way out. He then reached a large town; and as nobody knew him, he was taken into the royal palace, where the King and Queen were sitting on their thrones.

The count fell on one knee and drew the emerald book out of his pocket and laid it at the Queen's feet. She told him to rise and hand her the little book. But she had hardly opened it and looked inside before she fell to the ground as if dead.

The count was seized by the King's servants and was being led off to prison, when the Queen opened her eyes and ordered them to release him; and everybody was to go out, as she wished to speak with him in private.

When the Queen was alone she began to weep bitterly, and said, "What use to me are the splendours and honours I'm surrounded by? Every morning I wake in pain and sorrow. I had three daughters, the youngest of whom was so beautiful that the whole world looked on her as a wonder. She was as white as snow, as rosy as apple blossom, and her hair as radiant as sunbeams. When she cried, it was not tears that fell from her eyes but pearls and jewels.

"When she was fifteen years old, the King summoned all three sisters to come before his throne. You should have seen how all the people stared when the youngest came in — it was just as if the sun were rising.

"Then the King said, 'My daughters, I don't know when my last day may come. I'll decide today what each of you shall receive at my death. You all love me; but whichever of you loves me best shall fare best.'

"Each of them said she loved him best.

"'Can't you tell me,' said the King, 'how much you love me? Then I shall see what you mean.'

"The eldest said, 'I love my father as dearly as the sweetest sugar.'

"The second said, 'I love my father as dearly as my prettiest dress.'

"But the youngest was silent. So her father said, 'And you, my dearest child, how much do you love me?'

"'I don't know, and I can compare my love with nothing.'

"But her father insisted that she should name something. So at last she said, 'The best food doesn't please me without salt; and so I love my father like salt.'

"When the King heard that he flew into a passion, and said, 'If you love me like salt, then your love shall be repaid you with salt.'

"Then he divided the kingdom between the two elder ones; but he had a sack of salt bound on the youngest one's back, and two servants had to lead her out into the wild forest.

"We all begged and prayed for her," said the Queen, "but the King's anger was not to be softened. How she cried when she had to leave us! The whole road was strewn with the pearls that flowed from her eyes.

"The King soon afterwards repented of his great severity and had the whole forest searched for the poor child, but nobody could find her. When I think that the wild beasts have devoured her, I don't know how to contain myself for sorrow; and many a time I comfort myself with the hope that she's still alive, and may have hidden herself in a cave, or found shelter with compassionate people.

"But imagine — when I opened your little emerald book, a pearl lay inside it, of exactly the same kind as those that used to fall from my daughter's eyes; and then you can imagine too how the sight of it stirred my heart. You must tell me how you came by that pearl."

The count told her that he had been given it by the old woman in the forest, who had seemed very strange to him and must be a witch; but that he had neither seen nor heard anything of the Queen's child.

The King and the Queen resolved to seek out the old woman. They thought that where the pearl had been, they would get news of their daughter.

The old woman was sitting in that lonely place at her spinning wheel, spinning. It was already dusk, and a log burning on the hearth gave a scanty light. All at once there was a noise outside: the geese were coming home from the pasture, uttering their hoarse cries. Soon afterwards the daughter came in too.

But the old woman hardly thanked her, and only shook her head a little. The daughter sat down beside her and took up her spinning wheel and twisted the threads as nimbly as a young girl. So the two of them sat for two hours and never exchanged a word.

At last something rustled at the window, and two fiery eyes peered in. It was an old night owl, which cried "Uhu!" three times. The old woman looked up just a little, and then she said, "Now, my little daughter, it's time for you to go out and do your work."

She got up and went out — and where did she go? Over the meadows, on and on into the valley. At last she came to a well with three old oak trees standing beside it. Meanwhile the moon had risen large and round over the mountain, and it was so light that you could have found a needle.

She took off a skin that covered her face, then bent down to the well and began to wash herself. When she had finished, she dipped the skin in the water too, and then laid it on the meadow to bleach in the moonlight and dry again.

But how the girl was changed! Such a change was never seen. When the grey mask fell away, her golden hair broke out like sunbeams and spread about her whole figure like a mantle. Her eyes shone out as brightly as the stars in heaven, and her cheeks bloomed a soft red like apple blossom.

But the fair girl was sad. She sat down and wept bitterly. One tear after another forced itself out of her eyes and rolled down through her long hair to the ground. There she sat, and would have gone on sitting a long time, if there had not been a rustling and cracking in the boughs of a nearby tree.

She sprang up like a deer that has been caught by the hunter's shot. Just then the moon was hidden by a dark cloud, and in an instant the girl had put on the old skin and vanished, like a light blown out by the wind.

She ran home again, trembling like an aspen leaf. The old woman was standing on the threshold, and the girl was about to tell her what had happened; but the old woman laughed kindly and said, "I know it all already."

She led her into the room and lit a new log. But she did not sit down to her spinning again; she fetched a broom and began to sweep and scour. "Everything must be clean and sweet," she said to the girl.

"But, mother," said the girl, "why do you begin work at such a late hour? What are you expecting?"

"Do you know what time it is, then?" asked the old woman.

"Not midnight yet," answered the girl, "but past eleven o'clock already."

"Don't you remember," the old woman went on, "that it's three years today since you came to me? Your time is up; we can stay together no longer."

The girl was terrified, and said, "Alas, dear mother, will you cast me off? Where shall I go? I have no friends and no home to go to. I've always done as you told me, and you've always been satisfied with me. Don't send me away."

The old woman would not tell the girl what lay ahead of her. "My stay here is over," she said to her, "but when I go, house and parlour must be clean; so don't hinder me in my work. Have no fear for yourself. You shall find a roof to shelter you, and the wages I'll give you will content you too."

"But tell me what's about to happen," the girl went on entreating.

"I tell you again, don't hinder me in my work. Don't say another word. Go to your room, take the skin off your face, and put on the silk gown you were wearing when you came to me; and then wait in your room until I call you."

But I must tell once more of the King and Queen, who had set out with the count to seek the old woman in the wilderness.

The count had strayed away from them in the wood by night, and had to walk on alone. Next day it seemed to him that he was on the right track. He kept going on until darkness came, and then he climbed a tree, meaning to spend the night there, for he was afraid of losing his way.

When the moon lit up the countryside he saw a figure coming down the mountain. She had no stick in her hand, but he could see all the same that it was the goose-girl he had seen before at the old woman's house.

"Oho," cried he, "there she comes; and if I once get hold of one of the witches, the other shan't escape me."

But how astonished he was when she went to the well and took off the skin and washed herself, and her golden hair fell down all about her, and she was more beautiful than anybody he had ever seen in the whole world. He hardly dared to breathe, but stretched his head as far forward through the leaves as he dared, and stared at her.

Either he leaned over too far, or whatever the cause may have been, the bough suddenly cracked — and that very moment the girl slipped into the skin and sprang away like a deer, and as the moon was suddenly covered, she disappeared from his sight.

Hardly had she gone before the count climbed down from the tree and hurried after her with quick steps. He had not been going long before he saw two figures coming over the meadow in the twilight. It was the King and Queen, who had seen the light shining in the old woman's little house from a distance and were making for it.

The count told them the wonderful things he had seen at the well, and they did not doubt that it had been their lost daughter. They walked on full of joy, and soon came to the little house. The geese were sitting all round it with their heads tucked under their wings, asleep, and not one of them stirred.

The King and Queen looked in at the window. The old woman was sitting there quite quietly spinning, nodding her head and never looking round. The room was perfectly clean, as if the little mist men, who carry no dust on their feet, lived there. But they did not see their daughter.

They gazed at all this for a long time; and at last they took heart and knocked softly at the window. The old woman seemed to have been expecting them; she got up and called out quite kindly, "Come in — I know you already."

When they had come into the room, the old woman said, "You could have spared yourselves the long walk, if three years ago you hadn't unjustly driven away your child, who is so good and lovable. No harm has come to her; for three years she has had to mind the geese, and with them she has learned no evil, but has kept her purity of heart. You, though, have been punished enough by the misery you've lived in."

Then she went to the room and called, "Come out, my little daughter."

At this the door opened, and the princess stepped out in her silk clothes, with her golden hair and her shining eyes, and it was as if an angel from heaven had come in.

She went up to her father and mother and fell on their necks and kissed them; there was no help for it, they all had to weep for joy. The young count stood near them; and when she caught sight of him she turned as red in the face as a moss rose, and she herself did not know why.

The King said, "My dear child, I've given away my kingdom. What shall I give you?"

"She needs nothing," said the old woman. "I give her the tears she has wept on your account; they are precious pearls, finer than any that are found in the sea, and worth more than your whole kingdom. And I give her my little house as payment for her services."

When the old woman had said that, she disappeared from their sight. The walls rattled a little; and when the King and Queen looked round, the little house had turned into a splendid palace, and a royal table had been spread, and servants were running here and there.

The story goes on further still; but my grandmother, who told it to me, had partly lost her memory and had forgotten the rest. I shall always believe that the beautiful princess married the count, and that they stayed together in the palace and lived there in all happiness as long as God willed it.

Whether the snow-white geese that were kept near the little hut were truly young girls (let nobody take offence) whom the old woman had taken under her protection, and whether they now got their human shapes back and stayed as handmaids to the young Queen, I don't exactly know — but I suspect it.

This much is certain: that the old woman was no witch, as people thought, but a wise woman who meant well. Very likely it was she who at the princess's birth gave her the gift of weeping pearls instead of tears. That doesn't happen nowadays, or else the poor would soon become rich.
