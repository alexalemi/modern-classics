Little Brother and Little Sister

Little brother took his little sister by the hand and said, "Since our mother died we have had no happiness. Our stepmother beats us every day, and if we go near her she kicks us away with her foot. Our meals are the hard crusts of bread that are left over, and the little dog under the table is better off than we are, because she often throws it a good bit. Heaven help us — if our mother only knew! Come, we'll go out into the wide world together."

They walked the whole day over meadows and fields and stony places; and when it rained the little sister said, "Heaven and our hearts are weeping together." In the evening they came to a great forest, and they were so worn out with grief and hunger and the long walk that they crept into a hollow tree and fell asleep.

Next day, when they woke, the sun was already high and shining hot into the tree. Then the brother said, "Sister, I'm thirsty. If I knew of a little stream I'd go and have a drink. I think I can hear one running."

The brother got up and took his little sister by the hand, and they set off to find the stream.

But the wicked stepmother was a witch, and she had seen the two children go, and had crept after them in secret, the way witches creep, and had put a spell on every stream in the forest.

Now when they found a little stream leaping brightly over the stones, the brother was going to drink out of it; but the sister heard how it said as it ran, "Whoever drinks of me will be a tiger; whoever drinks of me will be a tiger."

So the sister cried, "Please, dear brother, don't drink, or you'll turn into a wild beast and tear me to pieces."

The brother did not drink, thirsty as he was, but said, "I'll wait for the next spring."

When they came to the next stream, the sister heard it say, "Whoever drinks of me will be a wolf; whoever drinks of me will be a wolf."

So the sister cried out, "Please, dear brother, don't drink, or you'll turn into a wolf and eat me."

The brother did not drink, but said, "I'll wait till we come to the next spring; but then I must drink, whatever you say, because my thirst is too great."

And when they came to the third stream, the sister heard it say as it ran, "Whoever drinks of me will be a roebuck; whoever drinks of me will be a roebuck."

The sister said, "Oh, I beg you, dear brother, don't drink, or you'll turn into a roebuck and run away from me."

But the brother had already knelt down by the stream and bent over and drunk some of the water; and as soon as the first drops touched his lips, there he lay, a young roebuck.

And now the sister wept over her poor enchanted brother, and the little roe wept too, and sat close beside her, full of sorrow. But at last the girl said, "Be quiet, dear little roe. I will never, never leave you."

Then she untied her golden garter and put it round the roebuck's neck, and she picked rushes and wove them into a soft cord. With this she led the little creature away, deeper and deeper into the forest.

And when they had gone a very long way they came at last to a little house; and the girl looked in, and as it was empty she thought, "We can stay here and live." Then she gathered leaves and moss to make a soft bed for the roe, and every morning she went out and gathered roots and berries and nuts for herself, and brought tender grass for the roe, who ate out of her hand and was contented and played about her. In the evening, when the sister was tired and had said her prayers, she laid her head on the roebuck's back — that was her pillow, and she slept soundly on it. And if only her brother had had his human shape, it would have been a delightful life.

For a while they were alone like this in the wilderness. But then it happened that the King of the country held a great hunt in the forest. The blasts of the horns, the barking of the dogs and the merry shouts of the huntsmen rang through the trees, and the roebuck heard it all and wanted more than anything to be there.

"Oh," he said to his sister, "let me go to the hunt, I can't bear it any longer" — and he begged so hard that at last she agreed.

"But," she said to him, "come back to me in the evening. I shall have to shut my door against the rough huntsmen; so knock and say, 'My little sister, let me in,' so that I know it is you. And if you don't say that, I shan't open the door."

Then the young roebuck sprang away, so happy and so merry in the open air.

The King and the huntsmen saw the beautiful creature and set off after him, but they could not catch him; and just when they thought they had him for certain, away he sprang through the bushes and was gone. When it was dark he ran to the cottage, knocked, and said, "My little sister, let me in." And the door was opened for him, and he jumped in and rested all night on his soft bed.

Next day the hunt began again, and when the roebuck heard the horns and the hallooing of the huntsmen he had no peace, and said, "Sister, let me out, I must go."

His sister opened the door for him and said, "But you must be back here in the evening and say your password."

When the King and his huntsmen saw the young roebuck with the golden collar again, they all gave chase; but he was too quick and nimble for them. It went on the whole day; but at last, towards evening, the huntsmen surrounded him, and one of them wounded him slightly in the foot, so that he limped and ran slowly. Then a huntsman crept after him to the cottage and heard him say, "My little sister, let me in," and saw the door open for him and shut again at once. The huntsman took note of it all, and went to the King and told him what he had seen and heard.

Then the King said, "Tomorrow we will hunt once more."

But the little sister was dreadfully frightened when she saw that her roe was hurt. She washed the blood off him, laid herbs on the wound, and said, "Go to your bed, dear roe, so that you get well again." But the wound was so slight that next morning the roebuck felt nothing of it any more; and when he heard the hunt outside again he said, "I can't bear it, I must be there. They shan't find it so easy to catch me."

The sister cried, and said, "This time they'll kill you, and here I shall be alone in the forest and forsaken by all the world. I won't let you out."

"Then you'll have me die of grief," said the roe. "When I hear those horns I feel as if I must jump out of my skin."

Then the sister could do nothing else, and opened the door for him with a heavy heart, and the roebuck bounded off into the forest, full of health and joy.

When the King saw him he said to his huntsmen, "Now chase him all day long, till nightfall; but take care that nobody does him any harm."

As soon as the sun had set, the King said to the huntsman, "Now come and show me the cottage in the wood." And when he was at the door he knocked and called, "Dear little sister, let me in."

The door opened and the King walked in, and there stood a girl more lovely than any he had ever seen. She was frightened when she saw that it was not her little roe but a man who came in, with a golden crown on his head. But the King looked at her kindly, held out his hand and said, "Will you come with me to my palace and be my dear wife?"

"Yes, gladly," said the girl, "but the little roe must come with me. I can't leave him."

The King said, "He shall stay with you as long as you live, and shall want for nothing."

Just then the roe came running in, and the sister tied him again with the rush cord, took it in her own hand and went away from the cottage with the King.

The King set the lovely girl on his horse and carried her to his palace, where the wedding was held with great splendour. She was Queen now, and they lived happily together for a long time; and the roebuck was cared for and cherished, and ran about in the palace garden.

But the wicked stepmother, who had driven the children out into the world, had been supposing all this time that the sister had been torn to pieces by the wild beasts in the wood, and that the brother had been shot for a roebuck by the huntsmen. So when she heard that they were so happy and so well off, envy and hatred rose in her heart and gave her no peace, and she thought of nothing but how to bring them to grief again.

Her own daughter, who was as ugly as night and had only one eye, grumbled at her and said, "A Queen! That ought to have been my luck."

"Just be quiet," said the old woman, and comforted her: "When the time comes, I shall be ready."

As time went on the Queen had a pretty little boy; and it happened that the King was out hunting. So the old witch took the shape of the chambermaid, went into the room where the Queen lay and said to her, "Come, your bath is ready. It will do you good and give you fresh strength. Be quick, before it gets cold."

Her daughter was close by too; and the two of them carried the weak Queen into the bathroom and put her in the bath, and then shut the door and ran away. But they had made up such a deadly heat in the bathroom that the beautiful young Queen was soon suffocated.

When that was done the old woman took her daughter, put a nightcap on her head and laid her in the bed in the Queen's place. She gave her the Queen's shape and the Queen's face as well; only she could not make good the missing eye. So that the King should not notice, she was to lie on the side where she had no eye.

In the evening, when he came home and heard that he had a son, he was heartily glad and was going to his dear wife's bed to see how she was. But the old woman called out quickly, "For your life, leave the curtains closed. The Queen must not see the light yet, and she must have rest." So the King went away, and never found out that a false Queen was lying in the bed.

But at midnight, when everybody was asleep, the nurse, who was sitting in the nursery by the cradle and was the only person awake, saw the door open and the true Queen come in. She took the child out of the cradle, laid it on her arm and fed it. Then she shook up its pillow, laid the child down again and covered it with the little quilt. And she did not forget the roebuck either, but went to the corner where he lay and stroked his back. Then she went out through the door again without a sound.

Next morning the nurse asked the guards whether anybody had come into the palace during the night, but they answered, "No, we saw nobody."

She came like this many nights and never spoke a word. The nurse always saw her, but did not dare tell anybody about it.

When some time had passed like this, the Queen began to speak in the night, and said:

	"How fares my child? How fares my roe?
	Twice more I come, then I must go."

The nurse did not answer; but when the Queen had gone she went to the King and told him everything.

The King said, "Ah, heavens, what can this be? Tomorrow night I will watch by the child myself."

In the evening he went into the nursery; and at midnight the Queen appeared again and said:

	"How fares my child? How fares my roe?
	Once more I come, then I must go."

And she nursed the child as she always did, before she disappeared. The King did not dare speak to her; but the next night he watched again. Then she said:

	"How fares my child? How fares my roe?
	This time I come, then I must go."

Then the King could not hold himself back. He sprang towards her and said, "You can be nobody but my dear wife."

She answered, "Yes, I am your dear wife" — and at that very moment her life came back to her, and by God's grace she was fresh and rosy and full of health again.

Then she told the King what a wicked thing the witch and her daughter had done to her. The King had them both brought before the judge, and sentence was passed on them. The daughter was taken out into the forest, where the wild beasts tore her to pieces; and the witch was thrown into the fire and miserably burnt. And the moment she was burnt, the roebuck changed his shape and had his human form again; so the sister and brother lived happily together all their lives.


Rapunzel

There were once a man and a woman who had long wished for a child in vain. At last the woman began to hope that God was going to grant her wish.

These people had a little window at the back of their house, and from it you could see a splendid garden, full of the most beautiful flowers and herbs. But it was surrounded by a high wall, and nobody dared go into it, because it belonged to an enchantress who had great powers and was feared by everybody in the world.

One day the woman was standing at this window looking down into the garden, when she saw a bed planted with the most beautiful rampion — rapunzel — and it looked so fresh and green that she longed for it and wanted more than anything to eat some. The longing grew stronger every day; and as she knew she could never have any, she pined away and looked pale and miserable.

Then her husband was alarmed, and asked, "What is the matter, dear wife?"

"Ah," she said, "if I can't get some of that rampion out of the garden behind our house, I shall die."

Her husband, who loved her, thought, "Rather than let your wife die, fetch her some of the rampion yourself, whatever it costs."

So in the dusk of the evening he climbed down over the wall into the enchantress's garden, hurriedly snatched a handful of rampion and took it to his wife. She made herself a salad of it at once and ate it with great relish. But she liked it so much — so very much — that the next day she longed for it three times as badly as before. If he was to have any peace, her husband would have to go down into the garden once more.

So in the gloom of the evening he let himself down again; but when he had climbed down the wall he was terrified, because there stood the enchantress in front of him.

"How dare you," she said, with a furious look, "climb into my garden and steal my rampion like a thief? You shall suffer for it."

"Ah," he answered, "let mercy take the place of justice. I only brought myself to do it out of necessity. My wife saw your rampion from the window, and she felt such a longing for it that she would have died if she hadn't got some to eat."

Then the enchantress let her anger cool, and said to him, "If it is as you say, I will let you take away as much rampion as you like — only on one condition: you must give me the child your wife is going to bring into the world. It shall be well treated, and I will care for it like a mother."

In his terror the man agreed to everything; and when the child was born, the enchantress appeared at once, gave the child the name Rapunzel, and took her away.

Rapunzel grew into the most beautiful child under the sun. When she was twelve years old, the enchantress shut her up in a tower that stood in a forest and had neither stairs nor door, but only a little window right at the top. When the enchantress wanted to go in, she stood underneath and called:

	"Rapunzel, Rapunzel,
	let down your hair to me."

Rapunzel had magnificent long hair, as fine as spun gold; and when she heard the enchantress's voice she undid her braided tresses and wound them round one of the hooks of the window, and then the hair fell twenty ells down, and the enchantress climbed up by it.

After a year or two it happened that a king's son rode through the forest and passed the tower. And he heard a song so lovely that he stood still and listened. It was Rapunzel, who in her loneliness passed her time letting her sweet voice ring out. The King's son wanted to climb up to her and looked for the door of the tower, but there was none to be found. He rode home; but the singing had gone so deep into his heart that he went out into the forest every day to listen to it.

Once, when he was standing behind a tree like this, he saw an enchantress come to the tower, and heard her call:

	"Rapunzel, Rapunzel,
	let down your hair to me."

Then Rapunzel let down the braids of her hair, and the enchantress climbed up to her.

"So that is the ladder people go up by," he said. "Then I'll try my luck too." And the next day, when it was beginning to get dark, he went to the tower and called:

	"Rapunzel, Rapunzel,
	let down your hair to me."

At once the hair came down, and the King's son climbed up.

At first Rapunzel was terribly frightened when a man came in, such as her eyes had never seen before. But the King's son began to talk to her quite like a friend, and told her that his heart had been so stirred that it had given him no peace, and he had had to see her. Then Rapunzel lost her fear; and when he asked whether she would have him for a husband, and she saw that he was young and handsome, she thought, "He will love me better than old Mother Gothel does" — and she said yes, and laid her hand in his.

She said, "I will gladly go away with you, but I don't know how to get down. Bring a skein of silk with you every time you come, and I will weave a ladder out of it; and when it is ready I will climb down and you shall take me up on your horse."

They agreed that until then he should come to her every evening, because the old woman came by day.

The enchantress noticed nothing, until one day Rapunzel said to her, "Tell me, Mother Gothel, how is it that you are so much heavier for me to pull up than the young King's son? He is up here in a moment."

"You wicked child!" cried the enchantress. "What is this I hear you say? I thought I had cut you off from all the world, and you have deceived me!"

In her rage she seized Rapunzel's beautiful hair, wound it twice round her left hand, snatched up a pair of scissors in her right, and snip, snap, it was cut off, and the lovely braids lay on the ground. And she was so pitiless that she took poor Rapunzel out into a wilderness, where she had to live in great grief and misery.

But on the same day that she cast Rapunzel out, the enchantress fastened the braids she had cut off to the hook of the window; and in the evening, when the King's son came and called:

	"Rapunzel, Rapunzel,
	let down your hair to me,"

she let the hair down. The King's son climbed up — and up there he did not find his dearest Rapunzel, but the enchantress, glaring at him with wicked, poisonous looks.

"Aha!" she cried mockingly. "You have come to fetch your darling; but the pretty bird is no longer sitting in the nest, singing. The cat has got her, and will scratch your eyes out as well. Rapunzel is lost to you. You will never see her again."

The King's son was beside himself with grief, and in his despair he leapt down from the tower. He escaped with his life, but the thorns he fell into put his eyes out. Then he wandered blind about the forest, ate nothing but roots and berries, and did nothing but grieve and weep for the loss of his dearest wife.

So he roamed about in misery for some years, and at last came to the wilderness where Rapunzel was living wretchedly with the twins she had given birth to, a boy and a girl. He heard a voice, and it seemed so familiar to him that he went towards it; and when he came near, Rapunzel knew him and fell on his neck and wept. Two of her tears fell on his eyes, and they grew clear again and he could see with them as he had before.

He took her home to his kingdom, where he was welcomed with joy, and they lived a long time afterwards, happy and content.
