The True Sweethearts

There was once a girl who was young and beautiful; but she had lost her mother when she was quite a child, and her stepmother did everything she could to make the girl's life wretched.

Whenever this woman gave her anything to do, she worked at it tirelessly and did everything that lay in her power. Still she could not touch the wicked woman's heart by it; she was never satisfied, it was never enough. The harder the girl worked, the more work was put on her; and all the woman thought about was how to weigh her down with still heavier burdens and make her life still more miserable.

One day she said to her, "Here are twelve pounds of feathers that you must strip; and if they aren't done by this evening, you can expect a good beating. Do you imagine you're going to idle the whole day away?"

The poor girl sat down to the work; but the tears ran down her cheeks as she did it, for she saw plainly enough that it was quite impossible to finish the work in one day. Whenever she had a little heap of feathers lying in front of her, and she sighed or clapped her hands together in her anguish, they flew away, and she had to pick them up again and start her work afresh.

Then she put her elbows on the table and laid her face in her two hands and cried, "Is there nobody, then, on God's earth to have pity on me?"

Then she heard a low voice, which said, "Be comforted, my child; I've come to help you."

The girl looked up, and an old woman was beside her. She took the girl kindly by the hand and said, "Just tell me what's troubling you."

As she spoke so kindly, the girl told her about her miserable life, and how one burden after another was laid on her, and how she could never get to the end of the work she was given. "If I haven't done these feathers by this evening, my stepmother will beat me. She's threatened she will, and I know she keeps her word."

Her tears began to flow again; but the good old woman said, "Don't be afraid, my child. Rest a while, and I'll see to your work in the meantime."

The girl lay down on her bed and soon fell asleep. The old woman sat down at the table with the feathers — and how they flew off the quills, which she scarcely touched with her withered hands! The twelve pounds were soon finished; and when the girl woke, great snow-white heaps were lying there piled up, and everything in the room was neatly cleared away; but the old woman had vanished.

The girl thanked God and sat still until evening came, when her stepmother came in and marvelled to see the work finished.

"Just look, you clumsy creature," said she, "what can be done when people work hard. And why couldn't you have set about something else? There you sit with your hands folded."

When she went out she said, "The creature is worth more than her salt. I must give her some work that's harder still."

Next morning she called the girl and said, "Here's a spoon for you. With that you must empty out the great pond beside the garden for me; and if it isn't done by night, you know what will happen."

The girl took the spoon and saw that it was full of holes; but even if it had not been, she could never have emptied the pond with it. She set to work at once, kneeling down beside the water, into which her tears were falling, and began to empty it.

But the good old woman appeared again; and when she learned the cause of her grief she said, "Be of good cheer, my child. Go into the thicket and lie down and sleep; I'll soon do your work."

As soon as the old woman was alone, she barely touched the pond, and a vapour rose up high out of the water and mingled with the clouds. Little by little the pond was emptied; and when the girl woke before sunset and came there, she saw nothing but the fish struggling in the mud.

She went to her stepmother and showed her that the work was done. "It ought to have been done long before this," said she, and went white with anger; but she was already planning something new.

On the third morning she said to the girl, "You must build me a castle on the plain over there, and it must be ready by evening."

The girl was dismayed and said, "How can I finish such a great work?"

"I'll put up with no arguing," screamed her stepmother. "If you can empty a pond with a spoon full of holes, you can build a castle too. I'll take possession of it this very day; and if anything is missing — even the most trifling thing in the kitchen or the cellar — you know what lies in store for you."

She drove the girl out; and when she came into the valley, the rocks lay there piled one above another, and all her strength would not have let her move even the smallest of them. She sat down and wept, and still she hoped the old woman would help her.

The old woman was not long in coming. She comforted her and said, "Lie down there in the shade and sleep, and I'll soon build the castle for you. If it would give you pleasure, you can live in it yourself."

When the girl had gone away, the old woman touched the grey rocks. They began to rise, and moved together at once as if giants had built the walls; and on these the building rose up, and it seemed as if countless hands were working invisibly and setting one stone on another. There was a dull heavy noise from the ground; pillars rose of their own accord and set themselves in order beside each other. The tiles laid themselves in order on the roof; and when midday came, the great weathervane was already turning itself on the top of the tower, like a golden figure of the Virgin with fluttering garments.

The inside of the castle was being finished as evening drew on. How the old woman managed it I don't know; but the walls of the rooms were hung with silk and velvet, embroidered chairs stood there and richly ornamented armchairs by marble tables; crystal chandeliers hung down from the ceilings and were mirrored in the smooth floor; green parrots sat in gilt cages, and strange birds too that sang most beautifully; and there was as much magnificence on every side as if a king were going to live there.

The sun was just setting when the girl woke and the brightness of a thousand lights flashed in her face. She hurried to the castle and went in by the open door. The steps were spread with red cloth and the golden banister set about with flowering trees. When she saw the splendour of the rooms she stood as if turned to stone.

Who knows how long she might have stood there, if she had not remembered her stepmother? "Alas," she said to herself, "if only she could be satisfied at last, and would give up making my life a misery."

The girl went and told her that the castle was ready. "I'll move into it at once," said she, and rose from her seat.

When they came into the castle she had to hold her hand in front of her eyes, the brilliance of everything was so dazzling. "You see," said she to the girl, "how easy it's been for you to do this. I ought to have given you something harder."

She went through all the rooms and examined every corner to see whether anything was missing or wrong; but she could find nothing.

"Now we'll go down below," said she, looking at the girl with malicious eyes. "The kitchen and the cellar still have to be looked at; and if you've forgotten anything, you shan't escape your punishment."

But the fire was burning on the hearth and the meat cooking in the pans, the tongs and shovel were leaning against the wall, and the shining brass utensils were all set out in sight. Nothing was missing, not even a coal box and a water pail.

"Which is the way to the cellar?" she cried. "If that isn't well filled, it will go badly with you."

She raised the trapdoor herself and went down; but she had hardly taken two steps before the heavy trapdoor, which was only laid back, fell down. The girl heard a scream and lifted the door very quickly to go to her help; but she had fallen, and the girl found her lying lifeless at the bottom.

And now the magnificent castle belonged to the girl alone. At first she did not know how to reconcile herself to her good fortune. Beautiful dresses hung in the wardrobes, the chests were filled with gold and silver, or with pearls and jewels; and she never felt a wish that she could not satisfy.

And soon the fame of the girl's beauty and riches went over all the world. Suitors presented themselves daily, but none of them pleased her. At length the King's son came, and he knew how to touch her heart, and she betrothed herself to him.

In the garden of the castle was a lime tree, and they were sitting under it together one day when he said to her, "I'll go home and get my father's consent to our marriage. I beg you to wait for me here under this lime tree; I'll be back with you in a few hours."

The girl kissed him on his left cheek and said, "Stay true to me, and never let anybody else kiss you on this cheek. I'll wait here under the lime tree until you come back."

The girl stayed under the lime tree until sunset, but he did not come back. She sat there three days from morning till evening, waiting for him, but in vain.

As he was still not there on the fourth day, she said, "Some accident must have happened to him. I'll go out and look for him, and won't come back until I've found him."

She packed up three of her most beautiful dresses — one embroidered with bright stars, the second with silver moons, the third with golden suns — tied a handful of jewels up in her handkerchief, and set out.

She asked everywhere for her betrothed, but nobody had seen him; nobody knew anything about him. Far and wide she wandered through the world, but she did not find him. At last she hired herself out to a farmer as a cowherd, and buried her dresses and jewels under a stone.

And now she lived as a herdswoman, minding her herd, and was very sad and full of longing for her beloved. She had a little calf that she taught to know her and fed out of her own hand; and when she said:

	"Little calf, little calf, kneel by my side,
	and do not forget your shepherd maid,
	as the prince forgot his betrothed bride
	who waited for him in the lime tree's shade" —

the little calf knelt down, and she stroked it.

And when she had lived alone and full of grief for a couple of years, word went round the whole country that the King's daughter was about to celebrate her marriage. The road to the town passed through the village where the girl was living; and it happened that once, when she was driving her herd out, her bridegroom travelled past. He sat proudly on his horse and never looked round; but when she saw him she recognised her beloved, and it was just as if a sharp knife had pierced her heart.

"Alas," said she, "I believed he was true to me; but he has forgotten me."

Next day he came along the road again. When he was near her she said to the little calf:

	"Little calf, little calf, kneel by my side,
	and do not forget your shepherd maid,
	as the prince forgot his betrothed bride
	who waited for him in the lime tree's shade."

When he caught the sound of her voice he looked down and reined in his horse. He looked into the herdswoman's face, and then put his hands in front of his eyes as if he were trying to remember something; but he soon rode on and was out of sight.

"Alas," said she, "he doesn't know me any more" — and her grief was greater than ever.

Soon after this a great festival, three days long, was to be held at the King's court, and the whole country was invited to it.

"Now I'll try my last chance," thought the girl. And when evening came she went to the stone she had buried her treasures under. She took out the dress with the golden suns and put it on and adorned herself with the jewels. She let down her hair, which she had kept hidden under a handkerchief, and it fell about her in long curls; and so she went into the town, and in the darkness nobody noticed her.

When she came into the brightly lit hall everybody drew back in amazement, but nobody knew who she was. The King's son went to meet her, but he did not recognise her. He led her out to dance, and was so enchanted with her beauty that he thought no more of his other bride. When the feast was over she vanished into the crowd, and hurried back before daybreak to the village, where she put her herdswoman's clothes on again.

Next evening she took out the dress with the silver moons and put a half-moon of precious stones in her hair. When she appeared at the festival every eye turned on her; but the King's son hurried to meet her, and, filled with love for her, danced with her alone and no longer so much as glanced at anybody else. Before she went away she was made to promise to come to the festival again on the last evening.

When she appeared for the third time she wore the star dress, which sparkled at every step she took, and her hair ribbon and her girdle were starred with jewels. The prince had already been waiting for her a long while, and forced his way through to her.

"Only tell me who you are," said he. "I feel just as if I'd known you a long time already."

"Don't you know what I did when you left me?"

Then she stepped up to him and kissed him on his left cheek; and in a moment it was as if scales fell from his eyes, and he recognised his true bride.

"Come," said he to her, "I'll stay here no longer" — and gave her his hand and led her down to the carriage. The horses hurried away towards the magic castle as if the wind had been harnessed to the carriage. The lit windows were already shining in the distance. When they drove past the lime tree, countless glow-worms were swarming about it; it shook its branches and sent out their fragrance. On the steps flowers were blooming, and the room echoed with the song of strange birds; and in the hall the whole court was assembled, and the priest was waiting to marry the bridegroom to his true bride.

The Hare and the Hedgehog

This story, my dear young folks, seems to be false, but it really is true; for my grandfather, whom I had it from, always used to say complacently when he told it, "It must be true, my son, or else nobody could tell it to you." The story goes like this.

One Sunday morning about harvest time, just as the buckwheat was in bloom, the sun was shining brightly in heaven, the east wind was blowing warm over the stubble fields, the larks were singing in the air, the bees buzzing among the buckwheat, and the people were all going to church in their Sunday clothes; and every creature was happy, and the hedgehog was happy too.

The hedgehog was standing by his door with his arms folded, enjoying the morning breeze and slowly trilling a little song to himself, which was neither better nor worse than the songs hedgehogs are in the habit of singing on a blessed Sunday morning.

And while he was singing half aloud to himself like this, it suddenly occurred to him that, while his wife was washing and drying the children, he might very well take a walk into the field and see how his turnips were getting on. The turnips were in fact right beside his house, and he and his family were used to eating them, which was why he looked on them as his own.

No sooner said than done. The hedgehog shut the house door behind him and took the path to the field. He had not gone very far from home, and was just turning round the sloe bush that stands out there at the edge of the field to go up into the turnip field, when he noticed the hare, who had come out on business of the same kind — namely, to visit his cabbages.

When the hedgehog caught sight of the hare he wished him a friendly good morning. But the hare, who was a distinguished gentleman in his own way, and frightfully haughty, did not return the hedgehog's greeting, but said to him — putting on a very contemptuous manner at the same time — "How do you come to be running about in the field so early in the morning?"

"I'm taking a walk," said the hedgehog.

"A walk!" said the hare with a smile. "It seems to me you might use your legs for a better purpose."

This answer made the hedgehog furiously angry, for he can bear anything but an attack on his legs, just because they are crooked by nature. So the hedgehog said to the hare, "You seem to imagine that you can do more with your legs than I can with mine."

"That's just what I do think," said the hare.

"That can be put to the test," said the hedgehog. "I'll wager that if we run a race, I'll outstrip you."

"That's ridiculous — you, with your short legs!" said the hare. "But for my part I'm willing, if you have such a monstrous fancy for it. What shall we wager?"

"A gold sovereign and a bottle of brandy," said the hedgehog.

"Done," said the hare. "Shake hands on it, and then we may as well start at once."

"No," said the hedgehog, "there's no such great hurry. I'm still fasting; I'll go home first and have a little breakfast. In half an hour I'll be back here."

At this the hedgehog went off, for the hare was quite satisfied with the arrangement.

On his way the hedgehog thought to himself, "The hare relies on his long legs, but I'll contrive to get the better of him. He may be a great man, but he's a very silly fellow, and he shall pay for what he's said."

So when the hedgehog reached home he said to his wife, "Wife, get dressed quickly; you must come out to the field with me."

"What's going on, then?" said his wife.

"I've made a wager with the hare for a gold sovereign and a bottle of brandy. I'm to run a race with him, and you must be there."

"Good heavens, husband," his wife cried now, "are you not right in your mind? Have you completely lost your wits? What can make you want to run a race with the hare?"

"Hold your tongue, woman," said the hedgehog. "That's my affair. Don't start arguing about matters for men. Off with you, get dressed and come with me."

What could the hedgehog's wife do? She had to obey him, whether she liked it or not.

So when they had set out together, the hedgehog said to his wife, "Now pay attention to what I'm going to say. Look — I'll make the long field our racecourse. The hare shall run in one furrow and I in another, and we'll start running from the top. All you have to do is take your place down here in the furrow; and when the hare arrives at the end of the furrow on the other side of you, you must call out to him, 'I'm here already!'"

Then they reached the field, and the hedgehog showed his wife her place, and then walked up the field. When he reached the top, the hare was there already.

"Shall we start?" said the hare.

"Certainly," said the hedgehog.

"Then both at once." And so saying, each placed himself in his own furrow. The hare counted, "Once, twice, thrice — and away!" and went off down the field like a whirlwind.

The hedgehog, though, only ran about three paces, and then he crouched down in the furrow and stayed quietly where he was.

So when the hare arrived at full speed at the lower end of the field, the hedgehog's wife met him with the cry, "I'm here already!"

The hare was shocked and wondered not a little; he thought it was nothing other than the hedgehog himself calling to him, for the hedgehog's wife looked just like her husband. But the hare thought to himself, "That wasn't done fairly" — and cried, "It must be run again; let's have it again." And once more he went off like the wind in a storm, so that he seemed to fly.

But the hedgehog's wife stayed quietly in her place. So when the hare reached the top of the field, the hedgehog himself cried out to him, "I'm here already!"

The hare, quite beside himself with anger, cried, "It must be run again; we must have it again."

"All right," answered the hedgehog. "For my part, we'll run as often as you choose."

So the hare ran seventy-three times more, and the hedgehog always held out against him; and every time the hare reached either the top or the bottom, either the hedgehog or his wife said, "I'm here already."

At the seventy-fourth time, though, the hare could no longer reach the end. In the middle of the field he fell to the ground, blood streamed out of his mouth, and he lay dead on the spot.

But the hedgehog took the sovereign he had won and the bottle of brandy, called his wife out of the furrow, and the two of them went home together in great delight; and if they aren't dead, they're living there still.

That is how it came about that the hedgehog made the hare run races with him on the Buxtehude heath until he died; and since that time no hare has ever had any fancy for running races with a Buxtehude hedgehog.

The moral of this story, though, is, first, that nobody, however great he may be, should let himself make fun of anybody beneath him, even if it's only a hedgehog. And second, it teaches that when a man marries, he should take a wife of his own kind, who looks just as he looks himself. So whoever is a hedgehog, let him see to it that his wife is a hedgehog too, and so on.
