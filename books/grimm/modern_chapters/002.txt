The Wolf and the Seven Little Kids

Once upon a time there was an old goat who had seven little kids, and she loved them with all the love a mother has for her children.

One day she wanted to go into the forest and fetch some food, so she called all seven to her and said, "Dear children, I have to go into the forest. Be on your guard against the wolf. If he gets in, he will eat every one of you — skin, hair and all. The wretch often disguises himself, but you will know him at once by his rough voice and his black feet."

The kids said, "Dear mother, we will take good care of ourselves. You can go without worrying." So the old one bleated and set off with an easy mind.

It was not long before somebody knocked at the door and called, "Open the door, dear children. Your mother is here, and she has brought something back for each of you."

But the little kids knew from the rough voice that it was the wolf. "We won't open the door," they cried. "You are not our mother. She has a soft, gentle voice, and your voice is rough. You are the wolf."

So the wolf went off to a shopkeeper and bought himself a great lump of chalk, and ate it, and it made his voice soft. Then he came back, knocked at the door of the house and called, "Open the door, dear children. Your mother is here, and she has brought something back for each of you."

But the wolf had laid his black paws on the window sill, and the children saw them and cried, "We won't open the door. Our mother hasn't got black feet like yours. You are the wolf."

So the wolf ran to a baker and said, "I've hurt my feet — rub some dough over them for me." And when the baker had done it, he ran to the miller and said, "Sprinkle some white flour on my feet for me."

The miller thought to himself, "The wolf means to deceive somebody," and refused; but the wolf said, "If you won't do it, I'll eat you." So the miller was frightened, and whitened his paws for him. That is what people are like.

So now the wretch went to the door for the third time, knocked and said, "Open the door for me, children. Your dear little mother has come home, and she has brought something back from the forest for every one of you."

The little kids called out, "Show us your paws first, so that we can be sure you are our dear little mother." So he put his paws in at the window; and when the kids saw that they were white, they believed everything he said and opened the door.

And in walked the wolf.

They were terrified and tried to hide. One sprang under the table, the second into the bed, the third into the stove, the fourth into the kitchen, the fifth into the cupboard, the sixth under the washing-bowl, and the seventh into the clock-case. But the wolf found them all and did not stand on ceremony: one after another he swallowed them down. The only one he did not find was the youngest, in the clock-case.

When the wolf had eaten his fill he took himself off, lay down under a tree in the green meadow outside, and went to sleep.

Soon afterwards the old goat came home from the forest. Oh, what a sight met her there! The door stood wide open; the table, the chairs and the benches were thrown over, the washing-bowl lay in pieces, and the quilts and pillows had been dragged off the bed. She looked for her children, but they were nowhere to be found. She called them by name, one after another, and nobody answered. At last, when she came to the youngest, a small voice cried, "Dear mother, I am in the clock-case."

She got the kid out, and it told her how the wolf had come and eaten all the others. You can imagine how she wept over her poor children.

At last, in her grief, she went outside, and the youngest kid ran with her. When they came to the meadow, there lay the wolf under the tree, snoring so loudly that the branches shook. She looked him over from every side, and saw that something was moving and struggling inside his stuffed belly.

"Good heavens," she said, "can it be that my poor children, that he swallowed down for his supper, are still alive?"

So the kid had to run home and fetch scissors and a needle and thread; and the goat cut open the monster's stomach. She had hardly made one cut before a little kid stuck its head out; and as she cut further, all six sprang out one after another, and every one of them was still alive and had come to no harm at all, because in his greed the monster had swallowed them down whole.

What rejoicing there was! They hugged their dear mother and jumped about like a tailor at his wedding.

But their mother said, "Now go and find some big stones, and we'll fill the wicked beast's stomach with them while he is still asleep."

So the seven kids dragged the stones over as fast as they could and put as many into his stomach as would go in; and their mother sewed him up again in the greatest hurry, so that he noticed nothing and never so much as stirred.

When the wolf had finally had his sleep out, he got to his feet; and because the stones in his stomach made him very thirsty, he wanted to go to a well and drink. But when he began to walk and move about, the stones knocked against each other and rattled. And he cried:

	"What rumbles and tumbles
	about in my bones?
	I thought it was six kids,
	and it's nothing but stones."

And when he got to the well and leaned over the water and was just about to drink, the heavy stones pulled him in, and there was no help for it — he drowned miserably.

When the seven kids saw it they came running up, calling out, "The wolf is dead! The wolf is dead!" and danced for joy round the well with their mother.


Faithful John

Once upon a time there was an old king who was ill, and he thought to himself, "I am lying on what must be my deathbed."

Then he said, "Send Faithful John to me." Faithful John was his favourite servant, and he was called that because he had been true to him all his life long.

So when he came to the bedside, the King said to him, "My most faithful John, I feel my end coming, and I have no anxiety about anything except my son. He is still young and cannot always know how to manage himself. Unless you promise me to teach him everything he ought to know, and to be a foster-father to him, I cannot close my eyes in peace."

And Faithful John answered, "I will not desert him, and I will serve him faithfully, even if it costs me my life."

At that the old King said, "Now I can die in comfort and peace." And he went on: "After my death you shall show him the whole castle — all the rooms, the halls and the vaults, and all the treasure that lies in them. But the last room in the long gallery, where the portrait of the princess of the Golden Dwelling hangs, you must not show him. If he sees that picture he will fall violently in love with her and faint away, and he will go through great danger for her sake. You must keep him from it."

And when Faithful John had given the old King his promise on that too, the King said no more, but laid his head on the pillow and died.

When the old King had been carried to his grave, Faithful John told the young King everything he had promised his father on his deathbed, and said, "I will keep that promise, and I will be as faithful to you as I was to him, even if it costs me my life."

When the mourning was over, Faithful John said to him, "It is time you saw your inheritance. I will show you your father's palace."

So he took him everywhere, up and down, and showed him all the riches and the magnificent rooms — only there was one room he did not open, the one where the dangerous picture hung. But the picture was so placed that when the door was opened you looked straight at it, and it was so wonderfully painted that it seemed to breathe and live; and there was nothing lovelier or more beautiful in the whole world.

The young King noticed clearly that Faithful John always walked past this one door, and said, "Why do you never open that one for me?"

"There is something inside," he answered, "that would frighten you."

But the King said, "I have seen the whole palace, and I want to know what is in this room too" — and he went and tried to force the door open.

Then Faithful John held him back and said, "I promised your father before he died that you should not see what is in that room. It could bring the greatest misfortune on you and on me."

"Oh no," said the young King, "if I don't go in, it will be the ruin of me. I should have no peace day or night until I had seen it with my own eyes. I am not leaving this spot until you have unlocked the door."

Then Faithful John saw there was no help for it, and with a heavy heart and many sighs he hunted the key out of the great bunch. When he had opened the door he went in first, meaning to stand in front of the portrait and hide it so that the King should not see it. But what good was that? The King stood on tiptoe and looked over his shoulder.

And when he saw the portrait of the girl, so magnificent and shining with gold and precious stones, he fell to the ground in a faint.

Faithful John picked him up, carried him to his bed, and thought sorrowfully, "The misfortune has come. Lord God, what will be the end of it?" Then he gave him wine to strengthen him until he came round.

The first words the King said were, "Ah, that beautiful portrait! Whose is it?"

"That is the princess of the Golden Dwelling," answered Faithful John.

And the King went on, "My love for her is so great that if all the leaves on all the trees were tongues, they could not tell it. I will give my life to win her. You are my most faithful John — you must help me."

The faithful servant thought for a long time about how to go about it, because it was difficult even to get a sight of the King's daughter. At last he hit on a way, and said to the King, "Everything she has about her is of gold — tables, chairs, dishes, glasses, bowls, all her furniture. Among your treasures there are five tons of gold. Let the goldsmiths of the kingdom work them up into every kind of vessel and utensil, and into all sorts of birds and wild beasts and strange animals such as she would like; and we will go there with them and try our luck."

The King had all the goldsmiths brought to him, and they had to work night and day until at last the most splendid things were ready. When everything had been stowed aboard a ship, Faithful John put on a merchant's clothes, and the King had to do the same so as to be quite unrecognisable. Then they sailed across the sea, and sailed on until they came to the town where the princess of the Golden Dwelling lived.

Faithful John told the King to stay behind on the ship and wait for him. "Perhaps I shall bring the princess back with me," he said. "So see that everything is in order. Have the golden things set out and the whole ship decorated."

Then he gathered up all sorts of gold pieces in his apron, went ashore and walked straight to the royal palace.

When he came into the courtyard of the palace, a beautiful girl was standing by the well with two golden buckets in her hands, drawing water. And as she turned to carry the sparkling water away she saw the stranger and asked who he was.

"I am a merchant," he answered, and opened his apron and let her look inside.

"Oh, what beautiful golden things!" she cried, and put down her pails and looked at the golden wares one after another. Then she said, "The princess must see these. She takes such pleasure in golden things that she will buy everything you have."

She took him by the hand and led him upstairs, for she was the waiting-maid.

When the King's daughter saw the wares she was delighted and said, "They are so beautifully made that I will buy the whole lot from you."

But Faithful John said, "I am only the servant of a rich merchant. What I have here is nothing to what my master has in his ship. Those are the most beautiful and valuable things that were ever made in gold."

She wanted everything brought to her there, but he said, "There are so many of them it would take a great many days, and so many rooms would be needed to show them that your house is not big enough."

At that her curiosity and her longing were roused still more, until at last she said, "Take me to the ship. I will go there myself and see your master's treasures."

Faithful John was delighted, and led her to the ship. And when the King saw her, he saw that her beauty was even greater than the picture had shown, and he thought his heart would burst in two.

She came aboard and the King led her inside. But Faithful John stayed behind with the pilot and gave orders to cast off, calling, "Set every sail, and let her fly like a bird in the air."

Inside, the King showed her the golden vessels, every one of them, and the wild beasts and the strange animals too. Many hours went by while she was looking at everything, and in her delight she did not notice that the ship was sailing away. When she had looked at the last of them, she thanked the merchant and made to go home; but when she came to the side of the ship she saw that it was out on the deep sea, far from land, and hurrying on under full sail.

"Oh," she cried in alarm, "I am betrayed! I have been carried off and fallen into the hands of a merchant. I would rather die!"

But the King took her hand and said, "I am not a merchant. I am a king, and no lower born than you are. If I have carried you off by a trick, it is because I love you so very much. The first time I looked at your portrait I fell to the ground in a faint."

When the princess of the Golden Dwelling heard that she was comforted, and her heart turned to him, so that she willingly agreed to be his wife.

But it happened, as they were sailing on over the deep sea, that Faithful John, who was sitting in the bows making music, saw three ravens in the air, flying towards them. So he stopped playing and listened to what they were saying to one another, because he understood it perfectly well.

The first cried, "There he goes, carrying home the princess of the Golden Dwelling."

"Yes," said the second, "but he hasn't got her yet."

Said the third, "He has got her — she is sitting beside him in the ship."

Then the first began again: "What good will that do him? When they reach land, a chestnut horse will come bounding up to meet him, and the prince will want to mount it; and if he does, it will bolt with him and rise up into the air with him, and he will never see his bride again."

"But is there no escape?" said the second.

"Oh yes — if somebody else gets on it quickly and takes the pistol that will be in its holster and shoots the horse dead, the young King is saved. But who knows that? And whoever does know it and tells him will be turned to stone from his toes to his knees."

Then the second said, "I know more than that. Even if the horse is killed, the young King still won't keep his bride. When they go into the castle together there will be a wedding garment lying ready in a dish, and it will look as if it were woven of gold and silver; but it is nothing but sulphur and pitch, and if he puts it on it will burn him to the bone and the marrow."

"Is there no escape at all?" said the third.

"Oh yes," said the second. "If somebody with gloves on takes hold of the garment and throws it in the fire and burns it, the young King is saved. But what good is that? Whoever knows it and tells him will turn to stone from his knees to his heart."

Then the third said, "I know still more. Even if the wedding garment is burnt, the young King still won't have his bride. After the wedding, when the dancing begins and the young queen is dancing, she will suddenly turn pale and fall down as if she were dead; and unless somebody lifts her up and draws three drops of blood from her right breast and spits them out again, she will die. But anybody who knows that and says it out loud will turn to stone from the crown of his head to the soles of his feet."

When the ravens had talked it over they flew on; and Faithful John had understood every word. From that time he was quiet and sad, for if he kept what he had heard from his master, his master would come to grief; and if he told him, he must give his own life. At last, though, he said to himself, "I will save my master, even if it destroys me."

So when they came ashore, everything happened as the ravens had foretold, and a magnificent chestnut horse came bounding up.

"Good," said the King, "this shall carry me to my palace" — and he was about to mount it when Faithful John got in front of him, jumped up on it, pulled the pistol out of the holster and shot the horse.

Then the King's other attendants, who were not very fond of Faithful John anyway, cried, "What a shameful thing, to kill that beautiful animal that was to have carried the King to his palace!"

But the King said, "Hold your tongues and leave him alone. He is my most faithful John, and who knows what good may come of it?"

They went into the palace; and in the hall there stood a dish, and in it lay the wedding garment, looking for all the world as if it were made of gold and silver. The young King went towards it and was about to take hold of it, but Faithful John pushed him away, took it up with his gloves on, carried it quickly to the fire and burnt it.

The other attendants began muttering again and said, "Look at that — now he is even burning the King's wedding garment."

But the young King said, "Who knows what good he may have done? Leave him alone. He is my most faithful John."

And now the wedding was celebrated. The dancing began, and the bride joined in; and Faithful John kept watch and looked into her face — and suddenly she turned pale and fell to the ground as if she were dead.

He ran to her at once, lifted her up and carried her into a side room; then he laid her down, knelt, and sucked the three drops of blood from her right breast and spat them out. Immediately she breathed again and came to herself.

But the young King had seen it, and, not knowing why Faithful John had done it, he grew angry and cried, "Throw him into the dungeon."

Next morning Faithful John was condemned and led out to the gallows; and when he stood up there and was about to be executed, he said, "Everybody who has to die is allowed one last speech before the end. May I claim that right too?"

"Yes," answered the King, "it is granted you."

Then Faithful John said, "I have been condemned unjustly, and I have always been true to you." And he told how he had listened to the ravens talking out at sea, and how he had been forced to do all these things to save his master.

Then the King cried, "Oh, my most faithful John! Pardon, pardon — bring him down!"

But as Faithful John spoke his last word, he had already fallen down lifeless and turned to stone.

At that the King and the Queen were in great distress, and the King said, "Oh, how badly I have repaid such faithfulness!" And he had the stone figure taken up and set in his bedroom beside his bed. And every time he looked at it he wept and said, "Ah, if only I could bring you back to life, my most faithful John."

Some time passed, and the Queen bore twins, two sons who grew fast and were her delight. Once, when the Queen was at church and the two children were sitting playing beside their father, he looked at the stone figure again, full of grief, and sighed and said, "Ah, if only I could bring you back to life, my most faithful John."

Then the stone began to speak, and said, "You can bring me back to life, if you will give up for it what is dearest to you."

The King cried, "I will give everything I have in the world for you."

The stone went on, "If you will cut off the heads of your two children with your own hand, and sprinkle me with their blood, I shall come back to life."

The King was horrified when he heard that he must kill his own dearest children; but he thought of Faithful John's great faithfulness, and how he had died for him, and drew his sword and with his own hand cut off the children's heads. And when he had smeared the stone with their blood, life came back into it, and Faithful John stood before him again, safe and well.

He said to the King, "Your faithfulness shall not go unrewarded" — and he took the children's heads, set them on again and rubbed the wounds with their blood, and at once they were whole again and jumped about and went on playing as if nothing had happened.

Then the King was full of joy; and when he saw the Queen coming, he hid Faithful John and the two children in a great cupboard. When she came in he said to her, "Have you been praying in the church?"

"Yes," she answered, "but I have been thinking all the time of Faithful John, and what misfortune came to him through us."

Then he said, "Dear wife, we can give him his life back — but it will cost us our two little sons, whom we must sacrifice."

The Queen turned pale and her heart was full of horror, but she said, "We owe it to him, for his great faithfulness."

Then the King was glad that she thought as he had thought; and he went and opened the cupboard and brought out Faithful John and the children, and said, "God be praised, he is delivered, and we have our little sons again as well" — and he told her how it had all happened.

And they lived together in great happiness to the end of their days.
