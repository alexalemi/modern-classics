The Two Brothers

(Part 1 of 3)

There were once two brothers, one rich and the other poor. The rich one was a goldsmith and evil-hearted; the poor one kept himself by making brooms, and was good and honourable.

The poor one had two children, twin brothers, as like each other as two drops of water. The two boys went back and forth to the rich house and often got some of the scraps to eat.

It happened once, when the poor man was going into the forest to fetch brushwood, that he saw a bird that was all of gold and more beautiful than any he had ever come across. He picked up a small stone and threw it at the bird and was lucky enough to hit it; but only one golden feather fell down, and the bird flew away.

The man took the feather and carried it to his brother, who looked at it and said, "It's pure gold" — and gave him a great deal of money for it.

Next day the man climbed into a birch tree and was about to cut off a couple of branches when the same bird flew out; and when the man searched, he found a nest with an egg lying in it, and the egg was of gold. He took the egg home and carried it to his brother, who again said, "It's pure gold" — and gave him what it was worth.

At last the goldsmith said, "I should very much like to have the bird itself."

The poor man went into the forest for the third time, and again saw the golden bird sitting in the tree; so he took a stone and brought it down and carried it to his brother, who gave him a great heap of gold for it.

"Now I can get along," he thought, and went home contented.

The goldsmith was crafty and cunning, and knew perfectly well what kind of bird it was. He called his wife and said, "Roast me that golden bird, and take care that none of it is lost. I have a fancy to eat it all myself."

For the bird was no common one, but of such a wondrous kind that whoever ate its heart and liver found a piece of gold under his pillow every morning.

The woman made the bird ready, put it on the spit and let it roast. Now it happened that while it was at the fire, and the woman had to leave the kitchen for some other work, the two children of the poor broom-maker ran in, stood by the spit and turned it round once or twice. And just at that moment two little bits of the bird fell down into the dripping pan, and one of the boys said, "We'll eat these two little bits. I'm so hungry, and nobody will ever miss them."

So the two of them ate the pieces. But the woman came into the kitchen and saw that they were eating something, and said, "What have you been eating?"

"Two little scraps that fell out of the bird," they answered.

"That must have been the heart and the liver," said the woman, thoroughly frightened; and so that her husband should not miss them and be angry, she quickly killed a young cockerel, took out its heart and liver and put them with the golden bird.

When it was ready she carried it to the goldsmith, who ate it all by himself and left nothing. But next morning, when he felt under his pillow and expected to bring out a piece of gold, there were no more gold pieces there than there had always been.

The two children had no idea what a piece of good fortune had fallen to them. Next morning when they got up, something fell rattling to the floor; and when they picked it up there were two gold pieces. They took them to their father, who was astonished and said, "How can that have happened?"

And when they found two more the next morning, and so on every day, he went to his brother and told him the strange story.

The goldsmith knew at once how it had come about, and that the children had eaten the heart and liver of the golden bird; and to revenge himself, and because he was envious and hard-hearted, he said to their father, "Your children are in league with the Evil One. Don't take the gold, and don't let them stay in your house any longer, because he has them in his power and may ruin you as well."

The father feared the Evil One; and painful as it was to him, he led the twins out into the forest and left them there with a sad heart.

And now the two children ran about the forest looking for the way home again, but could not find it, and only lost themselves more and more.

At length they met a huntsman, who asked, "Whose children are you?"

"We're the poor broom-maker's boys," they answered; and they told him that their father would not keep them in the house any longer, because a piece of gold lay under their pillows every morning.

"Come," said the huntsman, "there's nothing so very bad in that, so long as you stay honest and don't get idle."

And as the good man liked the children and had none of his own, he took them home with him and said, "I'll be your father and bring you up till you're grown."

They learned huntsmanship from him; and the gold piece that each of them found when he woke was put away for them, in case they should need it in the future.

When they were grown up, their foster-father took them into the forest with him one day and said, "Today you shall make your trial shot, so that I can release you from your apprenticeship and make you huntsmen."

They went with him and lay in wait a long time, but no game appeared. Then the huntsman looked up and saw a flight of wild geese flying in the shape of a triangle, and said to one of them, "Shoot me down one from each corner."

He did it, and so made his trial shot. Soon afterwards another flight came by in the shape of a figure two, and the huntsman told the other to bring down one from each corner too; and his trial shot was just as successful.

"Now," said their foster-father, "I pronounce you out of your apprenticeship. You are skilled huntsmen."

At this the two brothers went off together into the forest and took counsel with one another and made a plan. And in the evening, when they had sat down to supper, they said to their foster-father, "We won't touch food or take one mouthful until you have granted us a request."

Said he, "And what is your request?"

They answered, "We've finished learning now, and we must prove ourselves in the world. Let us go away and travel."

Then the old man said joyfully, "You talk like brave huntsmen. What you ask has been my own wish. Go — everything will go well with you."

And with that they ate and drank joyfully together.

When the appointed day came, their foster-father gave each of them a good gun and a dog, and let each take as many of his saved-up gold pieces as he liked. Then he went part of the way with them; and as he took his leave he gave them a bright knife and said, "If you ever separate, stick this knife into a tree at the place where you part; and when one of you comes back he'll be able to see how his absent brother is faring, because the side of the knife turned in the direction he took will rust if he dies, and will stay bright as long as he's alive."

The two brothers went on further, and came to a forest so large that they could not get out of it in a day. So they spent the night in it and ate what they had in their hunting bags; and they walked all the second day as well, and still did not get out.

As they had nothing to eat, one of them said, "We must shoot something for ourselves or we shall go hungry" — and loaded his gun and looked about him. And when an old hare came running towards them, he raised his gun to his shoulder; but the hare cried:

	"Dear huntsman, do but let me live,
	and two young ones to you I'll give"

— and sprang instantly into the thicket and brought two young ones. But the little creatures played so merrily, and were so pretty, that the huntsmen could not find it in their hearts to kill them. So they kept them with them, and the little hares followed on foot.

Soon after this a fox crept past. They were just going to shoot it, but the fox cried:

	"Dear huntsman, do but let me live,
	and two young ones to you I'll give."

He too brought two little foxes; and the huntsmen did not like to kill them either, but gave them to the hares for company, and they followed on behind.

It was not long before a wolf came striding out of the thicket. The huntsmen made ready to shoot him, but the wolf cried:

	"Dear huntsman, do but let me live,
	and two young ones to you I'll give."

The huntsmen put the two wolves with the other animals, and they followed on behind.

Then a bear came, who wanted to trot about a little longer, and cried:

	"Dear huntsman, do but let me live,
	and two young ones to you I'll give."

The two young bears were added to the rest, and there were eight of them already.

And at length who should come but a lion, tossing his mane. But the huntsmen did not let themselves be frightened, and took aim at him too; and the lion said as well:

	"Dear huntsman, do but let me live,
	and two young ones to you I'll give."

And he brought them his young ones; and now the huntsmen had two lions, two bears, two wolves, two foxes and two hares, who followed them and served them.

Meanwhile their hunger was not satisfied by any of this, so they said to the foxes, "Listen, you cunning fellows, get us something to eat. You're crafty and deep."

They answered, "Not far from here there's a village where we've already fetched many a fowl. We'll show you the way."

So they went into the village, bought themselves something to eat, had food given to their beasts, and then travelled on. The foxes knew their way very well about the district and where the poultry yards were, and were able to guide the huntsmen.

Now they travelled about for a while, but could find no positions where they could stay together; so they said, "There's nothing else for it — we must part."

They divided the animals, so that each of them had a lion, a bear, a wolf, a fox and a hare; then they took leave of one another, promised to love each other like brothers until they died, and stuck the knife their foster-father had given them into a tree; after which one went east and the other went west.

The younger one came with his beasts to a town that was all hung with black crape. He went into an inn and asked the landlord whether he could put his animals up.

The innkeeper gave him a stable with a hole in the wall; and the hare crept out and fetched himself a cabbage head, and the fox fetched himself a hen, and when he had eaten that, got the cock as well. But the wolf, the bear and the lion could not get out, because they were too big. So the innkeeper had them taken to a place where a cow was lying on the grass just then, and let them eat their fill.

And when the huntsman had seen to his animals, he asked the innkeeper why the town was all hung with black crape.

Said the landlord, "Because our King's only daughter is to die tomorrow."

The huntsman asked whether she was sick to death.

"No," said the landlord, "she's strong and healthy; and all the same she must die."

"How is that?" asked the huntsman.

"There's a high hill outside the town where a dragon lives, and every year he must have a pure virgin, or he lays the whole country waste. And now all the girls have been given to him already and there's nobody left but the King's daughter; and there's no mercy for her — she must be handed over to him, and that is to be done tomorrow."

Said the huntsman, "Why isn't the dragon killed?"

"Ah," said the landlord, "so many knights have tried it, and it has cost every one of them his life. The King has promised that whoever conquers the dragon shall have his daughter for a wife, and shall govern the kingdom after his own death."

The huntsman said nothing more to that; but next morning he took his animals and went up the dragon's hill with them.

At the top of it stood a little church, and on the altar were three full cups, with the inscription: "Whoever empties these cups will become the strongest man on earth, and will be able to wield the sword that is buried in front of the threshold."

The huntsman did not drink, but went out and looked for the sword in the ground; and he could not shift it from its place. So he went in and emptied the cups; and now he was strong enough to take the sword up, and his hand could wield it quite easily.

When the hour came for the girl to be given up to the dragon, the King, the marshal and the courtiers went with her. From a long way off she saw the huntsman on the dragon's hill and thought it was the dragon standing there waiting for her, and did not want to go up to him; but at last, because otherwise the whole town would have been destroyed, she was forced to make that miserable journey.

The King and the courtiers went home full of grief; but the King's marshal was to stay and watch it all from a distance.

When the King's daughter got to the top of the hill it was not the dragon standing there but the young huntsman, who comforted her and said he would save her, and led her into the church and locked her in.

It was not long before the seven-headed dragon came there with a loud roaring. When he caught sight of the huntsman he was astonished and said, "What business have you here on this hill?"

The huntsman answered, "I want to fight you."

Said the dragon, "Many knights have left their lives here; I shall soon have made an end of you too" — and he breathed fire out of seven jaws. The fire was meant to set the dry grass alight and the huntsman was meant to be suffocated in the heat and the smoke; but the animals came running up and trampled the fire out.

Then the dragon rushed at the huntsman; but he swung his sword until it sang through the air, and struck off three of his heads.

At that the dragon grew truly furious, and rose up into the air and spat flames of fire down over the huntsman, and was about to plunge down on him; but the huntsman drew his sword once more and cut off three more heads.

The monster grew faint and sank down; and yet it still just managed to rush at the huntsman, and he with his last strength struck its tail off; and as he could fight no longer he called up his animals, who tore it to pieces.

When the struggle was over the huntsman unlocked the church and found the King's daughter lying on the floor, for she had lost her senses with fear and terror during the fight. He carried her out; and when she came to herself and opened her eyes, he showed her the dragon all cut to pieces and told her that she was delivered.

She rejoiced and said, "Now you will be my dearest husband, for my father promised me to whoever killed the dragon."

And with that she took off her necklace of coral and divided it among the animals to reward them, and the lion had the golden clasp. But her handkerchief, with her name on it, she gave to the huntsman, who went and cut the tongues out of the dragon's seven heads, wrapped them in the handkerchief and put them carefully away.
