How Six Men Got on in the World

There was once a man who was master of all kinds of skills. He served in the war and behaved well and bravely; but when the war was over he got his discharge and three farthings for his expenses on the road.

"Wait a bit," said he. "I shan't be content with this. If I can only meet the right people, the King will yet have to give me the whole treasure of the country."

Then he went into the forest full of anger, and saw a man standing there who had pulled up six trees as if they were stalks of corn. He said to him, "Will you be my servant and come along with me?"

"Yes," he answered, "but first I'll take this little bundle of sticks home to my mother." And he took one of the trees and wound it round the other five, lifted the bundle onto his back and carried it away. Then he came back and went off with his master, who said, "The two of us ought to be able to get through the world very well."

When they had walked a little way they found a huntsman kneeling down with his gun at his shoulder, about to fire.

The master said to him, "Huntsman, what are you going to shoot?"

He answered, "Two miles from here there's a fly sitting on the branch of an oak tree, and I want to shoot its left eye out."

"Oh, come with me," said the man. "If the three of us are together we certainly ought to get on in the world."

The huntsman was willing and went along with him; and they came to seven windmills whose sails were turning round at great speed, although no wind was blowing on the right hand or the left and not a leaf was stirring.

Then the man said, "I don't know what is driving those windmills — there isn't a breath of air." And he went on with his servants; and when they had walked two miles they saw a man sitting in a tree, holding one nostril shut and blowing out of the other.

"Good gracious, what are you doing up there?"

He answered, "Two miles away there are seven windmills. Look — I'm blowing to keep them turning."

"Oh, come with me," said the man. "If the four of us are together we'll carry the whole world before us."

So the blower came down and went with him; and after a while they saw a man standing on one leg, who had taken the other one off and laid it down beside him.

The master said, "You've made yourself very comfortable for a rest."

"I'm a runner," he replied, "and to stop myself running much too fast I've taken one of my legs off; for if I run with both, I go quicker than any bird can fly."

"Oh, come with me. If the five of us are together we'll carry the whole world before us."

So he went along with them; and it was not long before they met a man wearing a cap, but wearing it right over one ear.

The master said to him, "Gently, gently — don't stick your cap over one ear like that. You look like a perfect fool."

"I mustn't wear it any other way," said he, "for if I set my hat straight, a terrible frost comes on, and all the birds in the air freeze and drop dead to the ground."

"Oh, come with me," said the master. "If the six of us are together we can carry the whole world before us."

Now the six came to a town where the King had proclaimed that whoever ran a race against his daughter and won should be her husband — but whoever lost should lose his head.

Then the man presented himself and said, "I'll do it; but I'll let my servant run for me."

The King replied, "Then his life must be staked as well, so that his head and yours are both set on the victory."

When that was settled and agreed, the man buckled the other leg onto the runner and said to him, "Now be quick, and help us to win."

It was arranged that whoever was first to bring back water from a far-off well should be the winner. The runner was given a pitcher, and the King's daughter one too, and they set off at the same moment. But in an instant, when the King's daughter had gone only a very little way, the people watching could see no more of the runner; it was just as if the wind had whistled past.

In a short time he reached the well, filled his pitcher with water and turned back. Halfway home, though, he was overcome with tiredness, and set his pitcher down and lay down himself and fell asleep. He had made a pillow of a horse's skull that was lying on the ground, so that he should lie uncomfortably and soon wake up again.

Meanwhile the King's daughter, who could also run very well — quite as well as any ordinary person can — had reached the well, and was hurrying back with her pitcher full of water; and when she saw the runner lying there asleep she was glad and said, "My enemy is delivered into my hands" — and she emptied his pitcher and ran on.

And now all would have been lost, if by good luck the huntsman had not been standing at the top of the castle and seen the whole thing with his sharp eyes.

"The King's daughter shan't get the better of us all the same," said he. And he loaded his gun and shot so cleverly that he shot the horse's skull out from under the runner's head without hurting him.

Then the runner woke and jumped up, and saw that his pitcher was empty and that the King's daughter was already far ahead. But he did not lose heart. He ran back to the well with his pitcher, drew water again, and was home ten minutes before the King's daughter.

"There," said he. "I hadn't stirred myself till now. What went before didn't deserve to be called running."

But it galled the King, and still more his daughter, that she should be carried off by a common discharged soldier like that; and they took counsel together about how to be rid of him and his companions.

Then the King said to her, "I've thought of a way. Don't be afraid — they shan't come back."

And he said to them, "You shall make merry together now, and eat and drink." And he led them into a room that had an iron floor; the doors were iron too, and the windows were guarded with iron bars. In the room stood a table covered with delicious food, and the King said to them, "Go in and enjoy yourselves."

And when they were inside, he ordered the doors to be shut and bolted. Then he sent for the cook and told him to light a fire under the room until the iron was red hot. The cook did it, and the six sitting at the table began to feel quite warm, and thought the heat came from the food. But as it grew still greater, and they wanted to get out and found the doors and windows bolted, they realised that the King must mean them harm and was trying to suffocate them.

"He shan't manage it, though," said the one with the cap. "I'll bring on a frost that will make the fire ashamed of itself and creep away."

Then he put his cap on straight, and immediately there came such a frost that all the heat vanished, and the food on the dishes began to freeze.

When an hour or two had gone by, and the King believed that they had died in the heat, he had the doors opened so as to see for himself. But when the doors were opened, all six were standing there alive and well, and said that they would very much like to get out and warm themselves, since the very food was frozen fast to the dishes with the cold.

Then the King went down to the cook in a fury, and scolded him, and asked why he had not done as he was told. But the cook replied, "There's heat enough there. Look for yourself."

Then the King saw that a fierce fire was burning under the iron room, and understood that there was no getting the better of the six that way.

The King thought again about how to be rid of his unwelcome guests, and had their leader brought to him, and said, "If you'll take gold and give up my daughter, you shall have as much as you like."

"Oh yes, Lord King," he answered. "Give me as much as my servant can carry, and I won't ask for your daughter."

The King was satisfied with that, and the other went on, "I'll come and fetch it in a fortnight."

Then he called together all the tailors in the whole kingdom, and they had to sit for a fortnight and sew a sack. And when it was ready, the strong one who could pull up trees had to take it on his back and go with it to the King.

Then the King said, "Who can that powerful fellow be, carrying a bundle of linen on his back as big as a house?" And he took fright and said, "What a lot of gold he'll be able to carry away!"

Then he ordered a ton of gold to be brought; it took sixteen of his strongest men to carry it, but the strong one snatched it up in one hand, put it in his sack and said, "Why don't you bring more at the same time? That hardly covers the bottom."

So little by little the King had all his treasure brought there, and the strong one shoved it into the sack, and even then the sack was not half full with it.

"Bring more," he cried. "These few crumbs don't fill it."

Then seven thousand cartloads of gold had to be gathered from the whole kingdom, and the strong one thrust them into his sack, oxen and all.

"I won't look them over any longer," said he. "I'll just take what comes, so long as the sack gets full."

When all that was inside there was still room for a great deal more; so he said, "I'll just make an end of the business. People do sometimes tie up a sack even when it isn't full."

So he took it on his back and went off with his comrades.

When the King saw one single man carrying away the entire wealth of the country, he flew into a rage, and ordered his horsemen to mount and go after the six and take the sack away from the strong one.

Two regiments soon overtook the six and called out, "You're prisoners! Put down that sack of gold, or you'll all be cut to pieces."

"What's that you say?" cried the blower. "That we're prisoners? Sooner than that, the lot of you shall go dancing about in the air." And he closed one nostril and blew on the two regiments with the other. Then they were scattered away from one another and carried up into the blue sky over all the mountains, one here and another there.

One sergeant cried for mercy; he had nine wounds and was a brave fellow who did not deserve rough treatment. The blower left off for a moment, so that he came down without harm; and then the blower said to him, "Now go home to your King and tell him he had better send some more horsemen — I'll blow every one of them into the air."

When the King was told this he said, "Let the rascals go. They have the better of it."

Then the six carried the riches home, divided them among themselves, and lived contented until their deaths.

The Wolf and the Man

Once upon a time the fox was talking to the wolf about the strength of man: how no animal could stand against him, and how all of them had to use cunning to keep themselves safe from him.

Then the wolf answered, "If I only had the chance of seeing a man just once, I'd go for him all the same."

"I can help you to that," said the fox. "Come to me early tomorrow morning and I'll show you one."

The wolf turned up in good time, and the fox took him out to the road the huntsmen used every day.

First an old discharged soldier came along. "Is that a man?" asked the wolf.

"No," answered the fox. "That was one."

Afterwards a little boy came by, going to school. "Is that a man?"

"No. That's going to be one."

At length a hunter came along, with his double-barrelled gun on his back and a short sword at his side.

Said the fox to the wolf, "Look, there comes a man. You must go for him — but I'll be off to my hole."

Then the wolf rushed at the man. When the huntsman saw him he said, "What a pity I haven't loaded with a bullet" — and took aim and fired his small shot into his face.

The wolf pulled a very sour face, but would not let himself be frightened, and went at him again; whereupon the huntsman gave him the second barrel. The wolf swallowed down the pain and rushed at the huntsman; but he drew out his bright blade and gave him a few cuts with it, right and left, so that he ran back to the fox howling and bleeding all over.

"Well, brother wolf," said the fox, "how did you get on with man?"

"Ah," replied the wolf, "I never imagined the strength of man was anything like that. First he took a stick off his shoulder and blew into it, and something flew into my face that tickled me horribly. Then he breathed into the stick once more, and it flew into my nose like lightning and hail. And when I was quite close, he drew a white rib out of his side and beat me with it until I was as good as left there dead."

"See what a braggart you are," said the fox. "You throw your hatchet so far that you can't fetch it back again."

The Wolf and the Fox

The wolf had the fox with him, and whatever the wolf wanted, the fox was obliged to do, because he was the weaker; and he would gladly have been rid of his master.

It happened once, as they were going through the forest, that the wolf said, "Red-fox, get me something to eat, or I'll eat you instead."

Then the fox answered, "I know a farmyard where there are two young lambs. If you like, we'll fetch one of them."

That suited the wolf, and they went there; and the fox stole the little lamb, took it to the wolf and went away. The wolf devoured it, but was not satisfied with one; he wanted the other as well, and went to get it. But he did it so clumsily that the little lamb's mother heard him and began to cry out terribly and to bleat, so that the farmer came running. They found the wolf and beat him so mercilessly that he went back to the fox limping and howling.

"You've led me into a fine thing," said he. "I wanted to fetch the other lamb, and the country folk caught me and beat me to a jelly."

The fox replied, "Why are you such a glutton?"

Next day they went out into the country again, and the greedy wolf said once more, "Red-fox, get me something to eat, or I'll eat you instead."

Then the fox answered, "I know a farmhouse where the woman is baking pancakes tonight. We'll get ourselves some of them."

They went there, and the fox slipped round the house and peeped and sniffed about until he found where the dish was; and then he pulled down six pancakes and carried them to the wolf.

"There's something for you to eat," he said to him, and then went on his way.

The wolf swallowed the pancakes down in an instant and said, "They only make you want more" — and went off there and dragged the whole dish down, so that it smashed to pieces. That made such a noise that the woman came out; and when she saw the wolf she called the people, who came hurrying and beat him as long as their sticks held together, until he got back to the fox in the forest with two lame legs, howling loudly.

"What a shameful way you've led me into," he cried. "The peasants caught me and tanned my hide for me."

But the fox replied, "Why are you such a glutton?"

On the third day, when they were out together and the wolf could only limp along painfully, he said again, "Red-fox, get me something to eat, or I'll eat you instead."

The fox answered, "I know a man who has been killing his pig, and the salted meat is lying in a barrel in the cellar. We'll get that."

Said the wolf, "I'll go when you do, so that you can help me if I can't get away."

"Willingly," said the fox; and he showed him the byways and paths by which they came at last to the cellar.

There was meat in plenty, and the wolf set on it at once and thought, "There's plenty of time before I need stop." The fox liked it too, but he kept looking about him, and often ran back to the hole they had come in by to try whether his body was still thin enough to slip through it.

The wolf said, "Dear fox, tell me why you're running about like that and jumping in and out?"

"I have to see that nobody's coming," replied the crafty fellow. "Don't eat too much!"

Then the wolf said, "I shan't leave until the barrel is empty."

Meanwhile the farmer, who had heard the noise of the fox's jumping, came into the cellar. When the fox saw him he was out of the hole at one bound. The wolf tried to follow him; but he had made himself so fat with eating that he could not get through any more, and stuck fast. Then the farmer came with a cudgel and struck him dead; but the fox bounded off into the forest, glad to be rid of the old glutton.

The Fox and His Cousin

The she-wolf had a cub, and invited the fox to be its godfather. "After all, he's a near relation of ours," said she. "He has a good head on him and plenty of talent; he can teach my little son and help him get on in the world."

The fox, too, seemed perfectly honest about it, and said, "Worthy cousin, I thank you for the honour you're doing me. I'll behave in such a way that you'll be well repaid for it."

He enjoyed himself at the feast and made merry, and afterwards he said, "Dear cousin, it's our duty to take care of the child. It must have good food, so that it grows strong. I know a sheepfold where we could fetch a nice morsel."

The wolf liked the sound of that, and went out with the fox to the farmyard. He pointed out the fold from a distance and said, "You'll be able to creep in there without being seen, and meanwhile I'll look about on the other side and see if I can pick up a chicken."

But he did not go there at all. He sat down at the edge of the forest, stretched out his legs and rested.

The she-wolf crept into the shed. A dog was lying there, and it made such a noise that the farm people came running out, caught cousin wolf, and poured a strong burning wash mixture over her hide. At last she got away and dragged herself outside.

There lay the fox, pretending to be full of complaints, and saying, "Ah, my dear cousin, what a bad time I've had! The peasants set on me and broke every limb I have. If you don't want me to lie here and die, you'll have to carry me away."

The she-wolf could barely walk herself; but she was in such concern about the fox that she took him on her back and slowly carried him — perfectly safe and sound — all the way to her house.

Then the fox called out to her, "Goodbye, dear cousin, and much good may your roasting do you!" — and he laughed heartily at her and bounded off.

The Fox and the Cat

It happened that the cat met the fox in a forest; and as she thought to herself, "He's clever and full of experience, and much respected in the world," she spoke to him in a friendly way. "Good day, dear Mr. Fox. How are you? How is everything with you? How are you getting through these hard times?"

The fox, full of every kind of arrogance, looked the cat over from head to foot, and for a long while did not know whether he would give any answer at all. At last he said, "Oh, you wretched whisker-washer, you piebald fool, you starving mouse-hunter, what can you be thinking of? Do you dare to ask how I'm getting on? What have you learned? How many arts do you understand?"

"I understand only one," replied the cat modestly.

"And what art is that?" asked the fox.

"When the hounds are after me, I can spring up into a tree and save myself."

"Is that all?" said the fox. "I'm master of a hundred arts, and I have a sackful of cunning into the bargain. I'm sorry for you. Come along with me and I'll teach you how people get away from hounds."

Just then a hunter came by with four dogs. The cat sprang nimbly up a tree and sat down at the top of it, where the branches and leaves hid her completely.

"Open your sack, Mr. Fox, open your sack!" the cat called down to him. But the dogs had already seized him and were holding him fast.

"Ah, Mr. Fox," cried the cat, "you and your hundred arts are left in the lurch. If you'd been able to climb like me, you wouldn't have lost your life."
