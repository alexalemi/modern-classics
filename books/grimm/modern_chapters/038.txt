Gambling Hansel

Once upon a time there was a man who did nothing but gamble, and for that reason people never called him anything but Gambling Hansel; and as he never left off gambling, he played away his house and everything he had.

Now on the very day before his creditors were to take his house from him, the Lord and St. Peter came and asked him to give them shelter for the night. Gambling Hansel said, "For my part you may stay the night, but I can't give you a bed or anything to eat."

So the Lord said he was just to take them in and they would buy something to eat themselves; and Gambling Hansel made no objection to that.

Then St. Peter gave him three groschen and told him to go to the baker's and fetch some bread. So Gambling Hansel went; but when he reached the house where the other gambling vagabonds gathered, they greeted him with a great shout — although they had won everything he owned — and said, "Hansel, do come in."

"Oh," said he, "do you want to win the three groschen as well?"

At that they would not let him go. So he went in, and played away the three groschen too.

Meanwhile St. Peter and the Lord were waiting; and as he was so long coming, they set out to meet him. But when Gambling Hansel saw them he pretended that the money had fallen down a drain, and kept raking about in it to find it — though our Lord already knew that he had lost it at play.

St. Peter gave him three groschen again; and this time he did not let himself be led astray, but brought them the loaf.

Then our Lord asked whether he had no wine, and he said, "Alas, sir, the casks are all empty."

But the Lord told him to go down to the cellar, because the best wine was still there. For a long time he would not believe it, but at length he said, "Well, I'll go down, but I know there's none there."

When he turned the tap, though — lo and behold, the best of wine ran out. So he brought it up to them, and the two of them spent the night there.

Early next day our Lord told Gambling Hansel that he might ask for three favours. The Lord expected that he would ask to go to Heaven; but Gambling Hansel asked for a pack of cards with which he could win everything, and for dice with which he could win everything, and for a tree on which every kind of fruit would grow, and from which nobody who had climbed up could get down again until he told them to.

The Lord gave him everything he had asked for, and went away with St. Peter.

And now Gambling Hansel set about his gambling in real earnest, and before long he had won half the world.

At this St. Peter said to the Lord, "Lord, this can't go on. He'll win the whole world and you'll lose it. We must send Death to him."

When Death appeared, Gambling Hansel had just sat down at the gaming table, and Death said, "Hansel, come outside a moment."

But Gambling Hansel said, "Just wait a little until the game's done; and in the meantime climb up into that tree out there and gather a little fruit, so that we have something to munch on the way."

So Death climbed up; but when he wanted to come down again he could not, and Gambling Hansel left him up there for seven years, during which time nobody died at all.

So St. Peter said to the Lord, "Lord, this can't go on. People aren't dying any more. We must go ourselves."

And they went themselves, and the Lord commanded Hansel to let Death come down. So Hansel went straight to Death and said to him, "Come down" — and Death took hold of him at once and made an end of him.

They went off together and came to the next world; and then Gambling Hansel made straight for the door of Heaven and knocked at it.

"Who's there?"

"Gambling Hansel."

"Ah, we'll have nothing to do with him. Be off!"

So he went to the door of Purgatory and knocked again.

"Who's there?"

"Gambling Hansel."

"Ah, there's quite enough weeping and wailing in here without him. We don't want to gamble. Just go away again."

Then he went to the door of Hell, and there they let him in. But there was nobody at home except old Lucifer and the crooked devils, who had just been about their evil work in the world. And no sooner was Hansel there than he sat down to gamble again. Lucifer, though, had nothing to lose except his misshapen devils; and Gambling Hansel won them off him, as with those cards of his he could not fail to do.

And now he was off again with his crooked devils; and they went to Hohenfurth and pulled up a hop-pole, and with it they went to Heaven and began to shove the pole against it, and Heaven began to crack.

So St. Peter said again, "Lord, this can't go on. We shall have to let him in, or he'll knock us out of Heaven."

And they let him in. But Gambling Hansel started playing again at once, and there was such a noise and confusion that they could not hear what they were saying to each other.

So St. Peter said once more, "Lord, this can't go on. We must throw him out, or he'll set the whole of Heaven in rebellion."

So they went to him and threw him down, and his soul broke into fragments, and went into the gambling vagabonds who are living to this very day.

Hans in Luck

Hans had served his master for seven years, so he said to him, "Master, my time is up. Now I should be glad to go back home to my mother. Give me my wages."

The master answered, "You've served me faithfully and honestly; as the service was, so shall the reward be" — and gave Hans a lump of gold as big as his head. Hans pulled his handkerchief out of his pocket, wrapped the lump up in it, put it on his shoulder and set off on the road home.

As he was going along, always putting one foot in front of the other, he saw a horseman come trotting by, quick and cheerful, on a lively horse.

"Ah," said Hans out loud, "what a fine thing riding is! There you sit as if you were on a chair, you stumble over no stones, you save your shoes, and you get along without knowing how."

The rider had heard him, and stopped and called out, "Hullo, Hans! Why are you going on foot, then?"

"I have to," he answered. "I've this lump to carry home. It's gold, true enough, but I can't hold my head straight for it and it hurts my shoulder."

"I'll tell you what," said the rider. "We'll swap. I'll give you my horse and you can give me your lump."

"With all my heart," said Hans, "but I can tell you, you'll have to crawl along under it."

The rider got down, took the gold and helped Hans up; then he put the bridle tight into his hands and said, "If you want to go at a really good pace, you must click your tongue and call out, 'Gee up! Gee up!'"

Hans was heartily delighted as he sat on the horse and rode away, so bold and free. After a little while he thought it ought to go faster, and he began to click his tongue and call out, "Gee up! Gee up!" The horse broke into a sharp trot, and before Hans knew where he was he had been thrown off and was lying in a ditch that separated the field from the highway. The horse would have gone off too if it had not been stopped by a countryman who was coming along the road driving a cow in front of him.

Hans got his limbs together and stood up on his feet again; but he was annoyed, and said to the countryman, "It's a poor joke, this riding, especially when you get hold of a mare like that, which kicks and throws you off so that you stand a good chance of breaking your neck. I'll never mount her again. Now, I like your cow. You can walk along quietly behind her, and have your milk and butter and cheese into the bargain, every day without fail. What wouldn't I give to have a cow like that!"

"Well," said the countryman, "if it would give you so much pleasure, I don't mind swapping the cow for the horse."

Hans agreed with the greatest delight; the countryman jumped up on the horse and rode quickly away.

Hans drove his cow quietly along in front of him, and thought over his lucky bargain. "If only I have a bit of bread — and that can hardly fail me — I can eat butter and cheese with it as often as I like; and if I'm thirsty I can milk my cow and drink the milk. Good heart, what more can I want?"

When he came to an inn he made a halt, and in his great contentment ate up everything he had with him — his dinner and his supper and all of it — and had half a glass of beer with his last few farthings. Then he drove his cow on along the road to his mother's village.

As it drew on towards midday the heat grew more oppressive, and Hans found himself on a moor that took about an hour to cross. He felt it very hot, and his tongue stuck to the roof of his mouth with thirst. "There's a cure for this," thought Hans. "I'll milk the cow now and refresh myself with the milk."

He tied her to a withered tree; and as he had no pail he put his leather cap underneath. But try as he might, not a drop of milk came. And as he set about it in such a clumsy way, the beast lost patience at last and gave him such a blow on the head with her hind foot that he fell to the ground and for a long time could not think where he was.

By good luck a butcher just then came along the road with a wheelbarrow, with a young pig lying in it. "What sort of trick is this?" he cried, and helped good Hans up.

Hans told him what had happened. The butcher gave him his flask and said, "Have a drink and refresh yourself. That cow will certainly give no milk; she's an old beast. At best she's only fit for the plough, or the butcher."

"Well, well," said Hans, smoothing his hair down on his head, "who would have thought it? Of course it's a fine thing when you can kill a beast like that at home — what a lot of meat you have! But I don't care much for beef; it isn't juicy enough for me. A young pig like that, now, is the thing to have. It tastes quite different — and then there are the sausages!"

"Listen, Hans," said the butcher, "out of pure kindness to you I'll swap, and let you have the pig for the cow."

"Heaven reward you for your goodness!" said Hans, and gave up the cow; and the pig was untied from the barrow and the cord it was tied with was put into his hand.

Hans went on, thinking to himself how everything was going just as he wanted; if he did meet with any annoyance, it was set right immediately.

Presently a lad joined him who was carrying a fine white goose under his arm. They said good morning to each other, and Hans began telling of his good luck and how he had always made such good bargains. The boy told him he was taking the goose to a christening feast. "Just lift her," he added, taking hold of her by the wings. "See how heavy she is — she's been fattened up for the last eight weeks. Whoever gets a bit of her when she's roasted will have to wipe the fat off both sides of his mouth."

"Yes," said Hans, weighing her in one hand, "she's a good weight; but my pig's not a bad one either."

Meanwhile the lad was looking suspiciously from one side to the other, and shaking his head. "Look here," he said at length, "it may not be all right about your pig. In the village I came through, the mayor himself had just had one stolen out of its sty. I'm afraid — I'm afraid that's where you got hold of that one. They've sent people out, and it would be a bad business if they caught you with the pig. At the very least you'd be shut up in the dark hole."

Good Hans was terrified. "Goodness," he said, "help me out of this. You know more about this place than I do — take my pig and let me have your goose."

"I'll be running some risk at that game," answered the lad, "but I won't be the cause of your getting into trouble." So he took the cord in his hand and drove the pig quickly away along a side path.

Good Hans, free of his worry, went off homewards with the goose under his arm. "When I think it over properly," he said to himself, "I've actually gained by the exchange. First there's the good roast meat; then all the fat that will drip out of it, which will give me dripping for my bread for a quarter of a year; and last of all the beautiful white feathers. I'll have my pillow stuffed with them, and then I shall certainly go to sleep without any rocking. How glad my mother will be!"

As he was going through the last village there stood a scissors-grinder with his barrow, and as his wheel whirred he sang:

	"I sharpen scissors and quickly grind,
	my coat blows out in the wind behind."

Hans stood still and watched him; and at last he spoke to him and said, "All's well with you, since you're so merry at your grinding."

"Yes," answered the scissors-grinder, "the trade has a golden foundation. A real grinder is a man who finds gold in his pocket as often as he puts his hand in it. But where did you buy that fine goose?"

"I didn't buy it. I swapped my pig for it."

"And the pig?"

"That I got for a cow."

"And the cow?"

"I took that instead of a horse."

"And the horse?"

"For that I gave a lump of gold as big as my head."

"And the gold?"

"Well, that was my wages for seven years' service."

"You've known how to look after yourself every time," said the grinder. "If you can only get on so far as to hear the money jingle in your pocket whenever you stand up, you'll have made your fortune."

"How shall I manage that?" said Hans.

"You must be a grinder, as I am. Nothing much is needed for it but a grindstone; the rest takes care of itself. I've one here — it's certainly a little worn, but you needn't give me anything for it but your goose. Will you do it?"

"How can you ask?" answered Hans. "I shall be the luckiest fellow on earth. If I have money whenever I put my hand in my pocket, what have I got to worry about ever again?" And he handed over the goose and took the grindstone in exchange.

"Now," said the grinder, picking up an ordinary heavy stone that lay beside him, "here's a good strong stone for you into the bargain. You can hammer away on it and straighten your old nails. Take it with you and look after it carefully."

Hans loaded himself with the stones and went on with a contented heart, his eyes shining with joy. "I must have been born lucky," he cried. "Everything I want happens to me just as if I were a Sunday child."

Meanwhile, as he had been on his legs since daybreak, he began to feel tired. Hunger tormented him too, for in his joy over the bargain that got him the cow he had eaten up his whole store of food at once. At last he could only get along with great difficulty, and had to stop every minute; and the stones weighed him down dreadfully. Then he could not help thinking how nice it would be if he did not have to carry them just now.

He crept like a snail to a well in a field, meaning to rest there and refresh himself with a cool drink of water; and so that he should not damage the stones by sitting down on them, he laid them carefully beside him on the rim of the well. Then he sat down on it, and was going to stoop and drink, when he slipped and knocked against the stones, and both of them fell into the water.

When Hans saw them sinking to the bottom with his own eyes, he jumped for joy; and then knelt down and with tears in his eyes thanked God for having shown him this favour too, and delivered him in such a good way — and without his having anything to reproach himself with — from those heavy stones, which had been the only things troubling him.

"There's no man under the sun as fortunate as I am," he cried out. And with a light heart and free of every burden he ran on until he was home with his mother.

Hans Married

There was once a young peasant named Hans, whose uncle wanted to find him a rich wife. So he sat Hans down behind the stove and had it made very hot. Then he fetched a pot of milk and plenty of white bread, and put a bright new-minted farthing in his hand, and said, "Hans, hold that farthing tight, crumble the white bread into the milk, and stay where you are, and don't stir from that spot till I come back."

"Yes," said Hans, "I'll do all that."

Then the matchmaker put on a pair of old patched trousers, went to a rich farmer's daughter in the next village, and said, "Won't you marry my nephew Hans? You'll get an honest, sensible man who'll suit you well."

The greedy father asked, "How does he stand for means? Has he bread to break?"

"My dear friend," replied the matchmaker, "my young nephew has a snug berth, a nice bit of money in hand, and plenty of bread to break; besides, he has quite as many patches as I have." (And as he said it, he slapped the patches on his trousers — but in that district small pieces of land were called patches too.) "If you'll take the trouble to come home with me, you'll see at once that it's all as I've said."

Then the miser did not want to lose such a good opportunity, and said, "If that's how it is, I have nothing further to say against the marriage."

So the wedding was celebrated on the appointed day; and when the young wife went out of doors to see the bridegroom's property, Hans took off his Sunday coat and put on his patched smock and said, "I might spoil my good coat."

Then they went out together; and wherever a boundary line came into sight, or fields and meadows were divided off from one another, Hans pointed with his finger and then slapped a large or a small patch on his smock and said, "That patch is mine, and that one too, my dearest — just look at it" — meaning by this that his wife should not stand staring at the broad lands, but look at his coat, which was his own.

"And were you at the wedding yourself?"

"Yes, indeed I was there, and in full dress. My headdress was made of snow, and then the sun came out and it melted. My coat was made of cobwebs, and I had to pass some thorns, which tore it off me. My shoes were made of glass, and I knocked against a stone, and they went klink and broke in two."
