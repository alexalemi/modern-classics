Jacob and Wilhelm Grimm were philologists, and they did not put this book together for children. They were collecting evidence — old German speech, old German story — and the first volume, published in 1812, came with scholarly notes and no pictures. It sold badly. Over the next forty-five years they brought out seven editions, and Wilhelm, who did most of the rewriting, kept working on the tales: lengthening them, smoothing the prose, taking the sex out, putting more God in, and turning several murderous mothers into stepmothers. The punishments he mostly left, and sometimes sharpened. The version everybody knows is the seventh edition of 1857, which is the one here.

Their sources were not peasants at a spinning wheel. They were mostly literate, mostly middle-class, mostly women, and mostly known to the brothers personally — neighbours, the daughters of a Kassel family the Grimms were friendly with, one of whom Wilhelm married, and a tailor's wife named Dorothea Viehmann who had a good memory and a good ear. One of those families was of Huguenot descent and knew its Perrault, so some of what the brothers wrote down as German had come to them through French print.

This edition has all of it: two hundred numbered tales, the ten children's legends, and the extra tale the Grimms hung on number 151 without giving it a number of its own. That is more than any modern Grimm in print, which is normally a selection of thirty or forty of the famous ones. The rest are the reason to have the whole thing. A hedgehog wins a footrace by cheating. A soldier traps the Devil in a boot with no sole. Two roasted chickens fly past with their breasts turned to heaven.

Three of the tales — numbers 7, 110 and 115 — are antisemitic caricature. They are translated in full and nothing in them is softened. There is a single editor's note, at 110, saying what this edition did and why, and it makes no further comment.

The text underneath is Margaret Hunt's of 1884, the first complete English Grimm and still the only complete one out of copyright. The obvious obstacle in it is the Victorian surface, the thees and thous and the inverted word order. The real one is quieter. Hunt's conversation means conduct; her want means lack; her prevent means go before. A reader who hits hath knows to go and look it up, and walks straight past prevent having understood the sentence backwards. The rhymes the animals and the dead speak in are the oldest layer of the whole collection, and they have been kept as rhymes.
