TONE CALIBRATION
  Book One is the textbook: definitions, the three kinds of speech, and the
  surveys (happiness, the good, virtue, pleasure, wrongdoing) a speaker needs
  to hand. Book Two is the most readable part of the book and the most often
  quoted: the emotions, and the characters of youth, age and the prime of
  life. Book Three is about style and is full of quoted sentences held up as
  good or bad examples: the point of an example must survive translation, so
  where a Greek example turns on a Greek word, say so and give the sense.

FROM THE PILOT (000, Book One Chapter 1) -- now locked
  GLOSSES SPENT in 1.1 (use plainly from now on): dialectic; enthymeme ("the
  argument of rhetoric, a kind of syllogism"); syllogism ("a chain of
  reasoning from premises"); proofs ("the means of persuasion"); the
  counterpart (antistrophos); the Areopagus; the Topics ("my book on
  dialectic"); the Athenian juror ("judge and jury at once").
  dikastês = juror; kritês = judge / "the people who judge"
  dêmêgorikon = speeches to the assembly / political speaking; the
    "deliberative" gloss is given ONCE, in 1.3, where sumbouleutikon appears
  dikanikon / dikologia = speeches in the courts / pleading in court
  ta exô tou pragmatos = "what lies outside the subject", identically,
    every time it recurs
  diabolê = slander (in 3.15 "prejudice" where the sense is the hearer's)
  ta endoxa = what people generally believe (NOT "probabilities", which
    would collide with eikos = what is likely)
  prohairesis = choice
  VOICE: plain and dry, the joints visible ("There are three reasons.
  First... Second..."); concrete images kept concrete ("bending the ruler
  you are about to measure with"). The first sentence of the book is
  "Rhetoric is the counterpart of dialectic" and later references to it use
  those words.
  FOLLOW THE GREEK where Freese softens or pads: he is a crib, not an
  authority (the pilot found four such places in one chapter).

  GLOSSES SPENT: topics ("the stock lines of argument", 1.2); political

  sophists ("the paid teachers of clever talk", 1.4); oligarchy ("the rule

  tekmêrion = sure sign. aretê: "virtue" for the soul, "excellence" for a
  body or a thing. to sumpheron = what is advantageous / the useful.
  THE VIRTUES (1.9, following the Greek's EIGHT; Freese adds a ninth that
  the Greek lacks): justice, courage, self-control (sôphrosunê),
  magnificence (megaloprepeia, "magnificence in spending"), greatness of
  soul (megalopsuchia), generosity (eleutheriotês), practical wisdom
  (phronêsis), wisdom (sophia). Their opposites: meanness, shabbiness.
  akrasia = lack of self-control; akolasia = SELF-INDULGENCE (not "lack of
  self-control"); orexis = wanting; boulêsis = wish; epithumia = appetite.
  kolasis = punishment (for the sufferer's sake); timôria = revenge (for the
  avenger's).
  hubris = INSOLENCE everywhere (not "outrage", not "insult"); oligôria =
  slight; kataphronêsis = contempt; epêreasmos = spite; atimia = dishonour.
  praotês = calm; praünsis = calming down.
  philein = love (in definitions) / like (in the lists).
  phronêsis, aretê, eunoia (2.1) = good sense, virtue, goodwill.
  pathê = "those feelings that change people so that they judge
  differently, and that bring pain and pleasure with them" (2.1).
  eleutheros = free man (not "gentleman"). philautos = lover of himself.
  mimêsis = imitation. autourgoi = small farmers who work their own land.
  sukophantês = informer ("who brings prosecutions for gain").
  The Antigone line (1.13, 1.15), set identically both times: "For this is
  not a thing of now or yesterday, but always / it lives, and no one knows
  when it first appeared."
