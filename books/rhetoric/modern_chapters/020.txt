Chapter 6: Shame

What things people are ashamed of, and what they are shameless about, before whom they feel shame, and in what state of mind, will be clear from what follows.

Let us define shame as a kind of pain or disturbance over bad things, present, past or future, that seem likely to bring us into disrepute; and shamelessness as a kind of indifference and lack of feeling about those same things.

If shame is what we have defined it to be, then people must be ashamed of those bad things that seem disgraceful, either to themselves or to people they care about. Such are all the acts that come from vice. For instance, throwing away your shield in battle, or running away, since that comes from cowardice. Or keeping back money someone left in your safekeeping, or wronging someone, since that comes from injustice.

Or having sex with people you should not, or where you should not, or when you should not, since that comes from self-indulgence.

Or making a profit out of petty things, or disgraceful things, or out of people who cannot defend themselves, such as the poor or the dead. Hence the proverb about someone who "would steal from a corpse". That comes from greed for shameful gain and from meanness.

Or not helping someone with money when you are able to, or helping less than you could. Or accepting help from people less well off than yourself.

Or borrowing when it will look like begging; begging when it will look like demanding back what you are owed; demanding back what you are owed when it will look like begging; praising something in a way that will look like asking for it; and going on asking after you have been refused. All these are signs of meanness.

Praising people to their faces is a sign of flattery, and so is praising their good points too much and smoothing over their bad ones, and showing more grief than the grieving man himself when you are with him, and everything else of that kind. All these are signs of flattery.

Or not putting up with hardships that are borne by people older than you, or people living in luxury, or people in higher positions, or in general people less able to bear them than you are. All these are signs of softness.

Or accepting favours from someone else, and accepting them often, and then throwing in his face the good you did him yourself. All these are signs of smallness of soul and of a low spirit.

Or talking about yourself all the time and making great promises, and claiming what someone else did as your own, since that comes from boastfulness. And in the same way with each of the other vices of character: the acts that come from them, the signs of them, and the things that resemble them are all disgraceful, and things to be ashamed of.

On top of these, it is shameful not to have a share in the fine things that everyone has a share in, or that everyone like you has, or most of them. By "people like you" I mean people of the same nation, the same city, the same age, the same family, and in general people on an equal footing with you. For then it is already shameful not to have a share, for example, in education to the same degree as they do, and so with the rest. All these things are more shameful if the fault seems to be your own, because then they come more from vice, if you yourself are the cause of the way things were, or are, or will be.

People are also ashamed when they are suffering, have suffered, or are going to suffer the kinds of thing that lead to dishonour and reproach. These are the things that involve putting your body at someone else's service, or submitting to shameful acts, and being treated with insolence is one of them. Some of these things are done for self-indulgence, whether willingly or unwillingly; others are done unwillingly, under force. For putting up with them and not fighting back comes from unmanliness or cowardice.

Those, then, and things like them, are what people are ashamed of.

Shame is a picture in the mind of disrepute, and we feel it for the disrepute itself and not for its consequences; and nobody cares what is thought of him except because of the people who think it. So people must feel shame before those whose opinion counts with them.

And the opinion that counts with a man is the opinion of those who admire him, of those he admires, of those by whom he wants to be admired, of those he competes with for honour, and of those whose opinion he does not despise.

People want to be admired by, and themselves admire, those who have some good thing that is held in honour, or those from whom they badly need something that those people have in their power to give, as lovers do.

They compete for honour with their equals. And they care about the opinion of sensible people, because they take them to be telling the truth; such people are the older and the educated.

They are also more ashamed of what is done before their eyes and out in the open. Hence the proverb, drawn from a line of Euripides, that shame lives in the eyes. That is why people feel more shame before those who will always be around them and before those who are paying attention to them, since in both cases they are under someone's eyes.

They feel shame before people who are not guilty of the same faults, because it is clear that such people think the opposite. And before people who do not forgive those who seem to have done wrong. For it is said that nobody holds against his neighbours what he does himself, so it is clear that what a man does hold against them is what he does not do.

They feel shame before people who are likely to spread things about to many others, since there is no difference between not thinking a thing wrong and not passing it on. The people likely to spread things are those who have been wronged, because they are watching us, and slanderers, because if they speak badly even of people who have done nothing wrong, they will be all the more ready to speak badly of those who have. And they feel shame before people who spend their time on their neighbours' faults, such as mockers and the writers of comedies, since these are a kind of slanderer and gossip. And before people in whose eyes they have never yet failed, because they are in the position of someone who is admired. That is also why people feel shame before those who ask them for something for the first time, since in their eyes they have not yet lost any of their good name. Such are people who have only just begun to want to be their friends, because these have seen only the best of them. That is why the answer Euripides is said to have given the Syracusans is a good one: sent to ask for their friendship and refused, he told them they ought to respect the Athenians' admiration, if only because it was new. And, among old acquaintances, those who know nothing against them.

People are ashamed not only of the shameful things themselves that we have described, but also of the signs of them. For instance, not only of having sex but of the signs of it; and not only of doing

shameful things but of saying them. In the same way, they feel shame not only before the people we have described but also before those who will tell those people, such as their servants and friends.

In general, people do not feel shame before those whose opinion they greatly despise as a judge of the truth; nobody feels shame before small children or animals. Nor are they ashamed of the same things before people they know and before strangers: before people they know they are ashamed of what seems truly shameful, and before people far off, of what is shameful by convention.

As for the state of mind in which people would feel shame: first, if there are people who stand towards them in the relation we said belongs to those before whom people feel shame. These, we said, are the people they admire, or who admire them, or by whom they want to be admired, or from whom they need some service that they will not get if they lose their good name. And these people must be either looking on, as when Cydias, speaking to the assembly about the settlement of Athenian colonists on Samos, asked the Athenians to imagine that the Greeks were standing all round them, so that they would not only hear of whatever they decided but see it; or close by; or likely to find out. That is also why people who are down on their luck do not want to be seen by those who once envied them, for people who envy you admire you.

People also feel shame when they have deeds and circumstances that bring disgrace on them, whether their own, or their ancestors', or those of anyone else they are closely connected with. And in general they feel shame on behalf of those for whom they themselves feel shame. These are the people already mentioned, and those whose conduct reflects on them, such as people whose teachers or advisers they have been, and other people like themselves with whom they compete for honour.

For there are many things people do, and many they refrain from doing, out of shame before people like these.

And they are more prone to shame when they are going to be seen, and to live out in the open, among people who know what they have done. That is why the poet Antiphon, when he was about to be put to death on the plank by Dionysius, the tyrant of Syracuse, saw the men who were to die with him covering their faces as they went out through the gates, and said, "Why do you hide your faces? Is it so that none of these people will see you tomorrow?"

So much for shame. As for shamelessness, it is clear that we shall have plenty to say about it from the opposites of all this.
