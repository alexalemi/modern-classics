Chapter 13: The Old

Such, then, is the character of the young. Older men, the ones who are past their prime, have characters that are for the most part made up of the opposites of these. They have lived many years; they have been deceived more often and have made more mistakes; and most things in life turn out badly. So they are sure of nothing, and they are less keen about everything than they ought to be.

They "think"; they never "know". Being of two minds, they always add "perhaps" or "maybe". Everything they say is put that way, and nothing is said outright.

They are bad-natured, for being bad-natured means taking everything in the worse sense. They are suspicious, too, because they do not trust anyone, and they do not trust anyone because of what experience has taught them.

For the same reasons they neither love nor hate very strongly, but follow the advice of Bias, another of the wise men: they love as though they will one day hate, and hate as though they will one day love.

They are small of soul, because life has humbled them. They want nothing great or out of the ordinary, only what they need to live.

They are mean with money, because property is one of the things you need to live, and at the same time they know from experience how hard it is to get and how easy to lose.

They are cowardly, and afraid of everything in advance, because their condition is the opposite of the young man's. They have been chilled, where the young are warm, so that old age has paved the way for cowardice; and fear, in fact, is a kind of chill.

They love life, and love it most on their last day, because what we want is what we do not have, and what we lack is what we want most.

They love themselves more than they should, for this too is a kind of smallness of soul. And they live for what is useful rather than for what is noble, more than they should, because they love themselves: what is useful is good for oneself, while what is noble is good without qualification.

They are shameless rather than easily ashamed. Because they do not care about the noble as much as about the useful, they think little of what people will think of them.

They are slow to hope, because of their experience, since most of what happens is bad, and most things turn out for the worse; and also because of their cowardice.

They live by memory more than by hope, because what is left of their life is short and what is past is long, and hope is about the future while memory is about the past. This is also why they talk so much: they go on and on about what has happened, because remembering gives them pleasure.

Their fits of temper are sharp but weak. Of their appetites, some have gone altogether and the rest are weak, so that they neither feel strong appetites nor act on them; they act for gain instead. That is why men of this age seem to have self-control: their appetites have slackened, and they are slaves to gain.

They live by calculation more than by character, because calculation aims at what is useful, and character at virtue. When they do wrong, they do it out of malice and not out of insolence.

The old are quick to pity too, but not for the same reason as the young. The young pity out of love for their fellow men; the old pity out of weakness, because they think that everything bad is close at hand for them to suffer, and that, as we saw, is what makes people pity. That is why they are given to complaining, and are neither witty nor fond of laughter, since a complaining temper is the opposite of a love of laughter.

Those, then, are the characters of the young and the old. Since everyone welcomes speeches that are spoken in his own character, and speakers who are like himself, it is not hard to see how we should use our speeches so that both we and our words will seem to be of that kind.
