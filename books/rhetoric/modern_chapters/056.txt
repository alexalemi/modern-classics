Chapter 16: The Narrative

So much, then, for slander and the prejudice it creates. Next comes the narrative, the part of a speech that tells what happened. In ceremonial speeches the narrative should not run straight through from start to finish, but should come piece by piece. The speaker has to go through the actions the speech is about, and a speech of this kind is made up of two parts. One part lies outside the art, since the speaker is in no way responsible for the actions he describes. The other part comes from the art, and it consists in showing one of these things: that the action really happened, if it is hard to believe; or what kind of action it was; or how great it was; or all three at once.

That is why it is sometimes right not to tell all the facts in one continuous run. A demonstration given all in a row like that is hard to remember. Better to go piece by piece: from these facts the man is shown to be brave, from those facts wise or just. A speech built this way is also simpler, whereas the other is intricate and cluttered.

Deeds that are well known need only to be recalled. That is why most such speeches need no narrative at all. If you want to praise Achilles, for instance, there is nothing to tell, because everyone knows what he did; you only have to make use of it. But if you want to praise Critias, you do need a narrative, because not many people know what he did.

Nowadays people lay down, absurdly, that the narrative should be quick. But this is like the man who was asked by the baker whether he wanted his bread kneaded hard or soft, and answered, "What? Is it impossible to knead it well?" It is the same here. The narrative should not be long, any more than the introduction should be long, or the proofs either. Here too, what makes it good is not speed and not brevity, but the right measure. That means saying as much as will make the facts clear, or as much as will make the listener believe that the thing happened, or that it did harm, or that it was a wrong, or that it was as great as you want it to seem. And the other side must do the opposite.

Along the way you should work into the narrative anything that points to your own virtue: "I kept warning him, and always said what was right, that he must not abandon his children." Or anything that points to your opponent's wickedness: "But he answered me that wherever he found himself, there would be other children." That is the answer Herodotus says the Egyptian soldiers gave when they deserted their king, and he begged them not to leave behind their wives and children. Or work in anything that will please the jurors.

For the defendant the narrative is shorter. The points he disputes are that the thing did not happen, or that it did no harm, or that it was not a wrong, or that it was not as serious as claimed. So he should not waste time on what both sides agree about, unless something in it helps his case: if, for instance, he admits the act was done but argues that it was not a wrong.

Further, whatever would not stir pity or horror if you told it as though it were happening in front of the listeners, you should tell briefly, as done and finished. The model is the story Odysseus tells at length to King Alcinous, which Homer has him tell over again to Penelope in sixty lines; or the way Phayllus cut down the whole cycle of epic poems; or the prologue of Euripides' play the Oeneus.

The narrative should show character, and it will, if we know what it is that shows character. One thing is to make the speaker's choice plain. The character is of a certain kind because the choice is of that kind, and the choice is of that kind because of the end it aims at. That is why mathematical writings show no character: they show no choice, because they have no purpose that anything is done for. The Socratic dialogues do show character, because they talk about questions of this kind.

Other things show character too: the habits that go with each type of character. For instance, "As he talked, he kept walking along." This shows a brazen and boorish character. And do not speak as though from calculation, the way speakers do nowadays, but as though from choice: "But I wanted it; I chose it, too; and even if I gained nothing by it, it is better." Speaking from calculation marks the sensible man, and speaking from choice marks the good one, because the sensible man shows himself in going after what is useful, and the good man in going after what is noble. If something you say is hard to believe, then add the reason for it, as Sophocles does. The example is from the Antigone, where Antigone says she cared more for her brother than for a husband or for children, because those could be replaced if they died, but

	with father and mother both gone down to Hades,
	no brother could ever be born to me again.

If you have no reason to give, at least say that you are well aware that what you are saying is hard to believe, but that this is simply how you are made. People do not believe that anyone ever does anything willingly except for his own advantage.

Further, draw on the emotions while you narrate. Tell the details that go along with them, the ones the listeners recognise, and the ones that are peculiar to you or to your opponent: "And he went off, glaring at me over his shoulder." Or the way Aeschines, the follower of Socrates, describes Cratylus as "hissing hard, and shaking his fists hard." Details like these are persuasive because the listeners know them, and so they become tokens of the things the listeners do not know. You can find a great many of them in Homer. When Eurycleia, the old nurse, speaks to the disguised Odysseus of the master she thinks is lost, Homer writes:

	So she spoke, and the old woman covered her face with her hands.

That is what people do when they begin to cry: they put their hands to their eyes. Also, bring yourself on straight away as a certain kind of person, so that the listeners see you as that kind of person, and your opponent too; but do it without being noticed. You can see how easy this is from people who bring us news. We know nothing about what they are going to tell us, and yet we form some idea of it all the same. The narrative should come in many places, and sometimes not at the beginning.

In political speeches there is very little narrative, because nobody narrates what is still to come. If there is a narrative, let it be about the past, so that the listeners, once reminded of those things, may decide better about what comes after. The speaker may be using the past to slander someone or to praise him; but when he does that, he is not doing the adviser's work. If something is hard to believe, you must vouch for it, give the reason at once, and set it out in whatever way the listeners want. So Jocasta, in the Oedipus of Carcinus, the tragic poet, keeps making promises whenever the man searching for her son questions her; and so does Haemon in Sophocles.
