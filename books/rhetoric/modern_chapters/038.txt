Chapter 24: Sham Arguments

Some syllogisms are real, and others are not real but only look like syllogisms. So, since the enthymeme is a kind of syllogism, some enthymemes too must be real, and others not real enthymemes but only apparent ones.

Of the lines of argument that produce apparent enthymemes, the first comes from the wording, and it has two parts. The first part is the one we meet in dialectic too: stating the last step as if it were a conclusion, when no reasoning has taken place at all, with a "so it is not this and that" or a "so it must be this and that". In the same way, in enthymemes, something said compactly and in balanced opposites looks like an enthymeme, because that style of speaking is the natural home of the enthymeme. A fallacy of this kind seems to depend on the shape of the wording. And a useful trick for sounding as if you are reasoning is to string together the headings of several syllogisms: "He saved some, he avenged others, and he set the Greeks free." Each of these was proved from other premises, earlier; but once they are put side by side, it looks as though something new follows from them too.

The second part is the one that comes from words with two meanings. Say someone argued that the mouse is an important creature, since the most honoured of all religious rites takes its name from it: the Greek for the Mysteries sounds as though it came from *mus*, a mouse. Or say someone who was praising the dog threw in the Dog in the sky, the Dog Star; or threw in Pan, because Pindar wrote of him as the attendant of Cybele, the great goddess:

	O blessed one, whom the Olympians call
	the dog of the great goddess, taking every shape.

Or say someone argued that since it is the lowest disgrace to be "not even a dog", as the saying goes, being a dog must plainly be an honour. Or that Hermes is the most sharing of the gods, because he is the only one people call "shared Hermes", which is what Greeks say when someone finds a lucky windfall and his companion claims half. Or that speech is the most excellent thing there is, because good men are said to be worth, not money, but *logos*; for "worth a word" does not have one simple meaning, since it can mean worth talking about, worth esteem.

Another line comes from putting together what is separate, or pulling apart what is put together. Since something often seems to be the same when it is not, you do whichever of the two suits you better. This is the argument of Euthydemus the sophist: for example, that a man knows there is a warship in the Piraeus, the harbour of Athens, because he knows each of the two things, the warship and the Piraeus. Or that someone who knows the letters knows the whole line of verse, since the line is the same thing as its letters. Or, since a double dose is bad for you, that not even a single dose can be called healthy; for it would be absurd if two good things made one bad thing. Put that way it refutes; put this way it proves: "For one good thing does not make two bad ones." But the whole line of argument is fallacious. Again, there is what Polycrates said of Thrasybulus, the democrat who overthrew the Thirty: that he had put down thirty tyrants. There the thirty men who made up one tyranny are counted one by one, and he adds them together. Or there is the passage in the Orestes of Theodectes, which works by division:

	It is just, when a woman kills her husband,

that she should die, and just too for a son to avenge his father. So, the argument runs, what Orestes has done is just as well. But when the two are put together, perhaps it is no longer just. It could also count as a fallacy of leaving something out, since it leaves out by whose hand she should die.

Another line of argument is to build a case up, or knock it down, by making the thing sound terrible. This happens when a speaker, without having shown either that the man did it or that he did not, blows the act up to enormous size. When it is the defendant who does this, it makes it look as though he did not do it; when it is the accuser, it makes it look as though he did. So this is not an enthymeme at all: the listener concludes, falsely, that the man did it or did not do it, when nothing has been shown.

Another comes from a sign; for this too proves nothing. Suppose someone said that lovers are useful to cities, because the love between Harmodius and Aristogiton brought down the tyrant Hipparchus. Or suppose someone said that Dionysius is a thief, because he is a bad man. That is no proof at all: not every bad man is a thief, although every thief is a bad man.

Another comes from what is accidental. For example, what Polycrates says in his speech in praise of mice: that they came to the rescue by gnawing through the bowstrings of an invading army. Or suppose someone said that there is no greater honour than being invited to dinner, since it was because he was not invited that Achilles was furious with the Greeks at Tenedos. In fact he was furious because he was being treated with disrespect; it merely happened that this took the form of not being invited.

Another comes from what follows. For example, in the speech on Paris, the Alexander, it is argued that Paris had greatness of soul, because he despised the company of the crowd and spent his time on Mount Ida by himself. Since men with greatness of soul are like that, he too might seem to have greatness of soul. Or: since a man dresses with great care and wanders about at night, he is an adulterer, because adulterers are like that. It is the same with the argument that beggars sing and dance in the temples, and that exiles can live wherever they like. These things are true of people who seem to be happy, so the people they are true of might seem to be happy too. But the difference lies in how: the beggar and the exile do these things for other reasons than the happy man. So this line too falls under leaving something out.

Another comes from treating what is not a cause as the cause, because it happened at the same time as something, or before it. People take "after this" as "because of this", and politicians do it most of all. So Demades said that Demosthenes' policy was the cause of all the city's troubles, because the war with Philip came after it.

Another comes from leaving out when and how. For example, the argument that Paris took Helen justly, because her father had given her the choice of husband. But the choice was surely not for ever, only the first time, since a father's authority lasts only that long. Or suppose someone said that to strike a free man is insolence. That is not so in every case, but only when the one who strikes is the one who starts the fight.

Further, just as in the arguments people have for the sake of winning, an apparent syllogism comes from confusing what is so without qualification with what is not so without qualification, but only in some respect. So in dialectic people argue that what is not, is, because what is not is what-is-not; or that the unknown can be known, because it can be known that the unknown is unknown. In the same way, in rhetoric, an apparent enthymeme comes from confusing what is likely without qualification with what is likely only in some respect. But such likelihood does not hold in general, as Agathon, the tragic poet, says:

	Perhaps one might say that this very thing is likely:
	that many things happen to mortals that are not likely.

For what is against likelihood does happen, so that what is against likelihood is likely too; and if so, what is unlikely will be likely. But not without qualification. In those arguments for the sake of winning, it is the leaving out of "in what respect", "in relation to what" and "in what way" that produces the swindle; and here too it comes from the fact that the unlikely is likely not without qualification, but only in some respect.

The handbook of Corax, one of the first writers on rhetoric, is built out of this line of argument. Suppose a man is not open to the charge: for instance, he is weak, and he is accused of assault. His defence is that it is not likely. Suppose he is open to it: he is strong. His defence is still that it is not likely, because it was bound to look likely. And the same in every other case: a man must either be open to the charge or not. Both defences look likely; but one of them really is likely, while the other is likely not without qualification, but only in the way just described. And this is what "making the weaker argument the stronger" means. That is why people were right to resent the promise Protagoras made to his pupils, to teach them exactly that. It is a lie: not a real likelihood but an apparent one, and it has no place in any art except rhetoric and the art of arguing to win.
