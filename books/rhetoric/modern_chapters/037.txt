Chapter 23: Lines of Argument

The first line of argument for enthymemes that prove something is the one from opposites. You look at whether the opposite quality belongs to the opposite thing. If it does not, that demolishes the argument; if it does, that builds it. For example: self-control is good, because its opposite, lack of self-control, is harmful. Or, as in the speech on the Messenians: "If the war is to blame for our present troubles, then it is with peace that we must put them right." Or these lines, whose author is unknown, where the argument runs from being wronged against your will to being helped against the helper's will:

	For if it is not just to fly into a rage
	with those who did us wrong without meaning to,
	then if a man was forced to do somebody good,
	it is not right that he should be owed thanks for it.

Or this, from Euripides' Thyestes, where the opposite of a believable lie is an unbelievable truth:

	But if among mortals it is possible to tell lies
	that win belief, you must hold the opposite as well:
	that many true things happen to mortals that nobody believes.

The second line of argument comes from the related forms of a word, such as "just" and "justly". What holds of one form must hold, or fail to hold, in the same way of the other. For example, someone might argue that not everything just is good: for then everything that happens justly would be good as well, and yet to be put to death justly is nobody's choice.

The third comes from things that are relative to each other. If it belongs to one party to have acted honourably or justly, then it belongs to the other to have been treated so; and if one gave the order, the other carried it out. So Diomedon, who had bought from the city the right to collect its taxes, said about the taxes: "If it is not shameful for you to sell, then it is not shameful for us to buy." And if the honourable or just belongs to the one who suffered, it belongs to the one who acted too. But there is room here for a false conclusion. If a man suffered justly, then he suffered justly; but perhaps not justly at your hands. That is why you must look separately at whether the sufferer deserved to suffer and whether the doer was the right person to do it, and then use whichever way of putting it suits. For sometimes the two come apart, and nothing stops them, as in the Alcmaeon of Theodectes, the tragic poet who was a pupil of Plato and Isocrates. Alcmaeon has killed his mother Eriphyle, and his wife Alphesiboea asks him:

	And was your mother hated by nobody on earth?

He answers that the question has to be divided before it can be looked at; and when Alphesiboea asks how, he takes her up and says:

	They judged that she should die; not that I should kill her.

Another example is the trial of Demosthenes and the men who killed Nicanor: since the court decided that they had killed him justly, it seemed that he had died justly. Another is the case of the man who was killed at Thebes, where the defendant demanded that the court decide whether the dead man had deserved to die, on the ground that it cannot be unjust to kill a man who died justly.

The fourth comes from more and less. For example: if not even the gods know everything, human beings hardly can. What this amounts to is that if a quality does not belong to the thing it is more likely to belong to, then plainly it does not belong to the thing it is less likely to belong to. On the other hand, the argument that a man who beats even his father will beat his neighbours comes from the rule that if the less likely thing is true, the more likely thing is true as well; for people beat their fathers less often than they beat their neighbours. So you can put it either way: that the quality does not belong where it is more likely, or that it does belong where it is less likely, whichever you need to show, whether that something is so or that it is not.

Under the same heading comes the case where it is neither more nor less, but equal. That is where these lines come from, in Antiphon's tragedy Meleager:

	Your father is pitiable, you say, because he lost his children;
	and is Oeneus not, who lost the glorious son of Greece?

Oeneus was the father of Meleager, and his loss is at least as great. Other examples: if Theseus did no wrong when he carried off Helen, then neither did Paris when he carried her off; and if the sons of Tyndareus, Castor and Polydeuces, did no wrong, then neither did Paris; and if Hector did no wrong in killing Patroclus, neither did Paris in killing Achilles. And: if no other practitioners of a skill are worthless, then neither are philosophers. And: if generals are not worthless because they are often put to death, then neither are sophists. And: if a private citizen ought to care about your good opinion, then you ought to care about the good opinion of the Greeks.

The fifth comes from looking at the time. For example, Iphicrates, the general, in his speech against Harmodius, who opposed the statue the city had voted him for his victory over the Spartans: "If, before I did it, I had asked to be given the statue once I had done it, you would have given it. Now that I have done it, will you refuse? Do not, then, make promises while you are still hoping for something, and take them back once you have had it." And again, to persuade the Thebans to let Philip of Macedon march through their land into Attica: if he had asked this before he helped them against the Phocians, they would have promised it; so it would be absurd to refuse him passage now, just because he let that chance go and trusted them.

The sixth takes what has been said against yourself and turns it on the man who said it, as in Sophocles' Teucer. Iphicrates used it in a different way against Aristophon. He asked him whether he would betray the fleet for a bribe, and when Aristophon said no, he said: "So you, who are Aristophon, would not betray it; and would I, who am Iphicrates?" But for this to work, the opponent must be the man who seems more likely to have done wrong. Otherwise it would look ridiculous: suppose someone said it in answer to Aristides the Just when he was the accuser. It is a way of making people distrust the accuser. For in general the accuser sets himself up as a better man than the defendant, and that is what has to be exposed. And in general, it is absurd when someone blames others for what he does himself, or would do, or urges others to do what he does not do himself and would not.

The seventh comes from definition. For example: what is the divine sign, the voice that Socrates said warned him? Is it a god, or the work of a god? But anyone who thinks it is the work of a god must think that gods exist. So Socrates, accused of not believing in the gods, answered the charge by defining the thing he was accused over. Or take Iphicrates, arguing that the best man is the noblest, although the Greek word for noble means well-born: there was nothing noble about Harmodius and Aristogiton, the men who killed the tyrant, until they had done something noble. And he argued that he himself was more their kin than his opponent was: "At any rate my deeds are more akin to the deeds of Harmodius and Aristogiton than yours are." Or, as in Polycrates' speech in defence of Paris, the Alexander: everyone would agree that men who lack restraint are not content with enjoying one woman's body. So Paris, who was content with Helen, was not a man who lacked restraint. Or the reason Socrates gave for refusing to go to Archelaus, the king of Macedonia: he said it was insolence to be unable to pay back a benefit, just as it is to be unable to pay back an injury. All these speakers define the thing, take hold of what it is, and then reason from that about the matter they are discussing.

The eighth comes from the number of senses a word has, as in the Topics, where we discussed the word "rightly".

The ninth comes from division. For example: all men do wrong for one of three reasons, this, that or the other; two of them are impossible here; and as for the third, not even the accusers say it.

The tenth comes from induction. For example, from the case of the woman of Peparethus, someone argues that everywhere it is women who decide the truth about their children. At Athens, when Mantias the orator was in a dispute with his son, who claimed to be his, it was the mother who declared the truth. At Thebes, when Ismenias and Stilbon were in dispute over a child, Dodonis, the mother, showed that he was Ismenias' son, and for that reason Thettaliscus was taken to be the son of Ismenias. Again, from the Law of Theodectes: we do not hand over our own horses to men who have looked after other people's horses badly, nor our own ships to men who have capsized other people's; so if this holds in every case, we must not use men who have guarded other people's safety badly to keep our own safe. And Alcidamas argues in the same way that everyone honours the wise. The people of Paros honoured Archilochus, though he was a poet of abuse; the people of Chios honoured Homer, though he was not a citizen of theirs; the people of Mytilene honoured Sappho, though she was a woman; the Spartans made Chilon one of their council of elders, though they are the least bookish of people; the Greeks of Italy honoured Pythagoras; and the people of Lampsacus buried Anaxagoras, a foreigner, and still honour him to this day. The Athenians prospered under the laws of Solon and the Spartans under those of Lycurgus; and at Thebes, as soon as the men who led the city became philosophers, the city prospered.

The eleventh comes from a judgement already given on the same question, or a similar one, or its opposite. Best of all is a judgement given by everyone and at all times; failing that, by most people; or by the wise, all or most of them; or by the good; or by the judges themselves, or by people whose judgement they accept; or by people it is impossible to contradict, such as those in power; or by people it is not decent to contradict, such as the gods, a father, or one's teachers. So Autocles said in his attack on Mixidemides, who refused to stand trial before the court of the Areopagus: "The Awful Goddesses were content to accept that court's verdict, and is it not good enough for Mixidemides?" He meant the Furies, who in Aeschylus' play submit their case to the Areopagus. Or as Sappho argued that dying is an evil: the gods have judged it so, for otherwise they would die. Or as Aristippus did, when Plato, as he thought, had said something too much like a boast: "Well, our friend never said anything of the kind", meaning Socrates. And Hegesipolis, the Spartan king, having first consulted the oracle of Zeus at Olympia on whether he could refuse a holy truce the enemy claimed in bad faith, and been told he could, then asked Apollo at Delphi whether his opinion was the same as his father's, since it would be shameful for a son to contradict his father. And Isocrates wrote that Helen was a good woman, since Theseus had judged her so by choosing her; and that Paris was, since the goddesses chose him above all others to judge between them; and that Evagoras, the king of Cyprus, was a good man, as Isocrates says, because at any rate when Conon the Athenian general met with disaster, he passed over everybody else and went to Evagoras.

The twelfth comes from the parts, as in the Topics: what kind of motion is the soul? It must be this one or that one. There is an example in Theodectes' Socrates. The charge is impiety, so the defence takes impiety to pieces: "What temple has he violated? Which of the gods the city believes in has he failed to honour?"

The thirteenth: in most cases both something good and something bad follow from the same thing, so you can use what follows to urge or to dissuade, to accuse or to defend, to praise or to blame. For example, education brings envy, which is bad, and it brings wisdom, which is good. So: "We should not be educated, because we should not be envied." Or: "We should be educated, because we should be wise." This line of argument is the whole of Callippus' handbook, once you add the line of argument from what is possible and the others we have described.

The fourteenth is used when you must urge or dissuade about two opposite courses, and apply the method just described to both of them. The difference is that there any two things are set against each other, whereas here they are opposites. For example, a priestess would not let her son speak in the assembly. "If you say what is just," she said, "men will hate you; if you say what is unjust, the gods will." But it can be turned: "No, you should speak in the assembly; for if you say what is just, the gods will love you, and if you say what is unjust, men will." This is the same as the saying about buying the marsh along with the salt, since you cannot have the salt pans without the swamp that comes with them. And this is what is called *blaisôsis*, the bow-legged argument that bends back the other way: when each of two opposites has a good and a bad consequence, and the consequences are opposite to each other just as the two things are.

The fifteenth: people do not praise the same things in public as they do in private. In public they praise above all what is just and noble; in private they prefer what is to their advantage. So try to draw from one of these the conclusion that goes against the other. Of the lines of argument that lead to a paradox, this is the most powerful.

The sixteenth comes from things turning out in proportion. For example, when the Athenians tried to force Iphicrates' son to pay for a public service, a duty laid on rich citizens, because he was tall, though he was under age, Iphicrates said: "If you count tall boys as men, you will have to vote that short men are boys." And Theodectes in his Law: "You make mercenaries citizens, men like Strabax and Charidemus, because of their decent conduct. Will you not make exiles of those mercenaries who have done damage that cannot be undone?"

The seventeenth: if the result is the same, the things it results from are the same. For example, Xenophanes said that those who say the gods were born are as impious as those who say the gods die, since either way the result is that at some time there are no gods. And in general you may take the result of each of two things to be one and the same: "You are about to judge not Isocrates, but a whole way of life: whether one ought to study philosophy." Or: to give earth and water, the tokens a Persian king demanded of those who submitted to him, is to be a slave; and to have a share in the common peace is to do what you are told. You should take whichever of the two ways of putting it is useful.

The eighteenth comes from the fact that the same people do not always choose the same thing later as they did before, but the reverse. For example, this enthymeme: "When we were in exile we fought to come home; and now we have come home, shall we go into exile so as not to fight?" At one time, that is, they chose to stay in their city at the price of fighting; at another, to avoid fighting at the price of not staying.

The nineteenth is to say that the reason something might be, or might have come about, is the reason it is, or came about. For example, someone might give a person a gift in order to hurt him by taking it away. That is why it has been said, by a poet whose name is not known:

	To many the god gives great good fortune,
	not because he wishes them well, but so that
	the disasters they meet may be the more striking.

And the lines from Antiphon's Meleager, about the heroes gathered for the hunt of the great boar:

	Not so that they might kill the beast, but so that they might be
	witnesses to Greece of Meleager's courage.

And the passage from Theodectes' Ajax, that when Diomedes chose Odysseus as his companion for the raid by night, it was not to honour him, but so that the man who went with him would be the lesser man; for that could be why he did it.

The twentieth is common to courtroom speeches and political ones. You look at the things that urge people on and the things that hold them back, the reasons for which people act and for which they refrain. These are the things that, if they are present, mean we should act, and if they are absent, mean we should not. For instance: whether a thing is possible, easy, and beneficial to oneself or one's friends, or harmful to one's enemies; or, if it carries a penalty, whether the penalty is smaller than what is gained. People urge others on from these, and dissuade from their opposites. And from these same things they accuse and defend: they defend from the things that hold a man back, and accuse from the things that urge him on. This line of argument is the whole of the handbooks of Pamphilus and of Callippus.

The twenty-first comes from things that are believed to happen and yet are unbelievable. People would not have believed them, you argue, unless they were true or nearly true. And the argument can be pushed further: they are all the more likely to be true. For what people accept is either what is, or what is likely; so if a thing is unbelievable and not likely, it must be true, since it is certainly not because it is likely and believable that people think it so. For example, Androcles of Pitthus, the politician, was speaking against a law, and was shouted down when he said, "The laws need a law to correct them." So he went on: "And fish need salt, though it is neither likely nor believable that creatures raised in salt water should need salt; and pressed olives need oil, though it is unbelievable that the very thing oil is made from should need oil."

The twenty-second is suited to refutation. You look for what does not agree, whether in places, times, actions or words. First, in your opponent's case alone: "He says he loves you, and yet he was in the conspiracy of the Thirty", the oligarchs who ruled Athens by terror. Next, in your own case alone: "He says I love going to law, but he cannot show a single lawsuit I have ever brought." Last, in his case and yours together: "He has never lent anybody anything, while I have paid the ransom for many of you."

The twenty-third is for people or things that have been slandered beforehand, or seem to have been: state the reason why the thing looks so strange, since there is always something that makes it look that way. For example, a woman had passed off her own son as somebody else's child, and because she kept embracing the young man, people thought she was sleeping with him; but once the reason was told, the slander fell apart. And in Theodectes' Ajax, Odysseus explains to Ajax why, though he is braver than Ajax, he is not thought to be.

The twenty-fourth comes from the cause. If the cause is there, the thing is; if the cause is not there, the thing is not. For a cause and what it causes go together, and without a cause nothing exists. For example, Thrasybulus, the democrat, accused Leodamas of having been inscribed as a traitor on the pillar on the Acropolis, and of having had his name chiselled off in the time of the Thirty. Leodamas, in his defence, said this could not have happened, because the Thirty would have trusted him all the more if his hatred of the people had been carved there in stone.

The twenty-fifth is to look at whether it was, or is, possible to do better in some other way than what a man is advising, or doing, or has done. For plainly, if things were not so, he did not do it, since nobody willingly and knowingly chooses what is bad. But this argument is false, since it often becomes clear only afterwards how the thing could have been done better, when beforehand it was not clear at all.

The twenty-sixth: when something is about to be done that goes against what has been done already, look at the two together. For example, when the people of Elea asked Xenophanes whether they should sacrifice to Leucothea and sing laments for her, or not, he advised them: if they thought she was a goddess, they should not lament her; if they thought she was a human being, they should not sacrifice to her. Leucothea had been a mortal woman, Ino, who threw herself into the sea and became a goddess of it, so they were doing both.

The twenty-seventh is to accuse or defend on the basis of mistakes made. For example, in the Medea of Carcinus, the tragic poet, her accusers say that she has killed her children: at any rate the children are nowhere to be seen, because Medea made the mistake of sending them away. She defends herself by saying that it is not her children she would have killed, but Jason; for it would have been a mistake not to kill him, if she had done the other thing. This line of argument, and this kind of enthymeme, is the whole of Theodorus' earlier handbook.

The twenty-eighth comes from the name. So Sophocles, speaking to the cruel stepmother Sidero, whose name comes from *sidêros*, iron:

	Iron plainly, and you carry it in your name.

People commonly do this in praising the gods, too. And so Conon called Thrasybulus "bold in counsel", which is what his name means; and Herodicus said to Thrasymachus, "You are always bold in battle", which is what his name means; and to Polus, whose name means a colt, "You are always a colt"; and of Draco the lawgiver, that his laws were not a man's but a dragon's, because they were so harsh. And so Hecuba in Euripides says of Aphrodite, whose name begins like the Greek for folly, *aphrosunê*:

	And rightly the goddess's name begins with folly.

And so Chaeremon, of Pentheus, whose name echoes *penthos*, grief:

	Pentheus, named for the grief that was to come.

Enthymemes that refute are better liked than enthymemes that prove, because an enthymeme that refutes brings opposites together in a small space, and things set side by side are clearer to the listener. But of all syllogisms, whether they refute or prove, the ones that are applauded most are these: the ones whose conclusion the listeners see coming from the very start, provided it is not because the argument is shallow (for the listeners are pleased with themselves for seeing it coming); and the ones they fall just far enough behind to understand the moment they are finished.
