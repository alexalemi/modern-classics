Chapter 26: What Is Not a Line of Argument

Making a thing out to be bigger, or smaller, than it is, is not an element of the enthymeme. By an element I mean the same as a line of argument: an element, or line of argument, is a heading under which many enthymemes fall. Making bigger and making smaller, by contrast, are themselves enthymemes, used to show that a thing is great or small, just as other enthymemes are used to show that it is good or bad, just or unjust, or anything else.

All these are simply the subjects that syllogisms and enthymemes are about. So if none of them is a line of argument for enthymemes, then neither is making bigger or making smaller.

Nor are the enthymemes that refute an argument a different kind of enthymeme from the ones that build one. It is clear that a speaker refutes either by proving something or by bringing an objection, and when he proves something, what he proves in reply is the opposite of what the other side proved. If his opponent showed that a thing happened, he shows that it did not; if his opponent showed that it did not happen, he shows that it did. So this could not be the difference between them, since both sides use the same means: each brings enthymemes to show that the thing is so, or that it is not.

And the objection is not an enthymeme at all. As we said in the Topics, it is stating some opinion that makes it plain that the other side has not reasoned validly, or has taken something false as a premise.

There are three things that have to be worked out about a speech. For examples, maxims and enthymemes, and in general for everything to do with the thinking in a speech, where we shall find plenty to say and how we shall refute it, let what we have said be enough. What remains is to go through style and arrangement.
