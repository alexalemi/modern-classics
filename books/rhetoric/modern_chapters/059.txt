Chapter 19: The Conclusion

The conclusion is made up of four things. First, putting the listener in a good frame of mind towards yourself and a bad one towards your opponent. Second, making things great or small. Third, putting the listener into the emotions. Fourth, reminding him of what has been said.

This order is the natural one. Once you have shown that you are telling the truth and your opponent is lying, the natural next step is to praise, to blame, and to hammer the point home. You should aim at one of two things: to show that you are good towards these listeners, or good without qualification; and that your opponent is bad towards these listeners, or bad without qualification. The lines of argument from which to show people good or bad have already been given.

After this, now that the facts have been shown, the natural thing is to make them greater or smaller. The facts have to be agreed on first if you mean to say how great they are, just as a body grows only out of what is already there. The lines of argument for making things great and small were set out earlier.

Next, once it is clear what kind of thing was done and how great it was, you should lead the listener into the emotions: pity and horror, anger and hatred, envy, emulation, and the urge to fight. The lines of argument for these, too, have been given earlier.

So what remains is to remind the listeners of what has been said before. It is right to do this here, and not, as some people wrongly say, in the introduction. They tell you to say things over and over in the introduction, so that the listeners may learn them easily. But in the introduction you should state the subject, so that the question to be decided does not escape anyone. Here, in the conclusion, you should give the points by which your case has been proved, in summary.

Begin by saying that you have done what you promised, and so you must say what you have shown and why. The way to say it is by setting your case beside your opponent's. You can compare what the two of you have said on the same point. Or you can compare them without setting them face to face: "He said this about that; I said this, and for these reasons." Or you can do it with irony: "He said this, and I said that. And what would he have done if he had proved this, and not merely that?" Or you can do it with a question: "So what has been proved?" or "What, then, has he proved?"

So you may sum up either in this way, by comparison, or in the natural order, going through your own points as they were made, and then again, if you wish, taking your opponent's speech separately.

For the very end of the speech the fitting style is the one without connecting words, so that it is a conclusion and not yet another speech: "I have spoken; you have heard; you have the facts; judge."
