Chapter 25: Refutation

So much for enthymemes, both the real ones and the apparent ones. The next thing to discuss is how to refute them. You can refute an argument in two ways: by reasoning to the opposite conclusion with a syllogism of your own, or by bringing an objection.

Reasoning to the opposite conclusion can clearly be done from the same lines of argument we have already set out. Syllogisms are drawn from what people generally believe, and many of the things people believe contradict one another.

Objections are brought in four ways, as in the Topics. They come either from the opponent's enthymeme itself, or from what is similar, or from what is opposite, or from what has already been judged.

By an objection from the enthymeme itself I mean something like this. Suppose the enthymeme were meant to show that love is a good thing. The objection can be made in two ways. One is to state a general rule: every kind of want, every lack of something, is bad, and love is a kind of want. The other is to point to a particular case: nobody would talk of "Caunian love", the proverbial name for love between brother and sister, unless there were bad kinds of love as well.

An objection from the opposite is brought like this. Suppose the enthymeme was that a good man does good to all his friends. The objection is: "But a bad man does not do harm to all his friends either."

An objection from what is similar: suppose the enthymeme was that people who have been badly treated always hate the people who did it. The objection is: "But people who have been well treated do not always love the people who did it either."

The judgements, finally, are the ones that come from well-known men. For instance, suppose someone argued in an enthymeme that we should forgive people who are drunk, since they do wrong without knowing what they are doing. The objection is: "Then Pittacus deserves no praise; otherwise he would not have laid down heavier penalties for anyone who does wrong when drunk." Pittacus was the wise lawgiver of Mytilene, and his law, which punished the drunk offender more heavily, is a judgement against the argument.

Enthymemes, as we said, are drawn from four sources: what is likely, examples, sure signs, and signs. Enthymemes drawn from what is likely are built from things that happen, or are thought to happen, for the most part. Those drawn from examples work by induction from something similar, one case or several, when the speaker takes the general rule and then reasons from it to the particular case. Those drawn from sure signs work through what is necessary and always so. Those drawn from signs work through something general or something particular, whether it is true or not. Now what is likely is not what always happens, but what happens for the most part. So it is clear that enthymemes of this kind can always be refuted by bringing an objection.

But the refutation is often only apparent, not real. For the man who brings the objection shows, not that the thing is not likely, but only that it is not necessary.

That is why this fallacy always gives the defence an advantage over the prosecution. The accuser proves his case from what is likely. But showing that something is not likely is not the same as showing that it is not necessary; and whatever happens only for the most part is always open to an objection, since otherwise it would not be merely likely but would always happen and be necessary. And when an argument is answered in that way, the judge thinks either that it is not likely after all or that it is not for him to decide, and he is misled, as we were saying. For he is not supposed to judge only from what is necessary, but also from what is likely: that is what judging "to the best of my judgement" means. So it is not enough to refute the argument by showing that it is not necessary; you must refute it by showing that it is not likely.

That will happen if the objection itself holds more often, for the most part, than the argument it answers. And there are two ways it can do that: through time, or through the facts. The strongest objection does both, for if a thing happens in more cases and more often, then it is the more likely.

Signs, too, and enthymemes drawn from signs, can be refuted even if the facts they rest on are true, as we said at the beginning; for it is clear to us from the Analytics that no sign by itself makes a valid syllogism.

Enthymemes drawn from examples are refuted in the same way as those drawn from what is likely. If we have one single case that goes the other way, the argument is refuted, on the ground that it is not necessary, even if most of the cases, or the most frequent ones, go the opponent's way. But if most of the cases, and the most frequent ones, go his way, then we must fight on other ground: that the present case is not similar, or did not happen in a similar way, or at least has some difference.

Sure signs, and enthymemes drawn from sure signs, cannot be refuted on the ground that they make no valid syllogism; this too is clear to us from the Analytics. All that is left is to show that the thing asserted is not so. But if it is plain both that it is so and that it is a sure sign, then the argument can no longer be refuted: it has become a demonstration, and everything in it is plain.
