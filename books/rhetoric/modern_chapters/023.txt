Chapter 9: Indignation

The feeling most directly opposed to pity is the one people call indignation. Being pained at someone's undeserved good fortune is, in a way, the opposite of being pained at someone's undeserved bad fortune, and both come from the same kind of character. Both feelings, in fact, belong to a good character.

For we ought to feel sorry for, and pity, those who suffer when they do not deserve it, and we ought to feel indignation at those who prosper when they do not deserve it. Whatever happens to a person against what he deserves is unjust. That is why we even say that the gods feel indignation.

It might seem that envy, too, is opposed to pity in the same way, being close to indignation or even the same thing. But it is different. Envy, too, is a disturbing pain, and it too is felt at someone's good fortune; but not the good fortune of someone who does not deserve it. It is felt at the good fortune of an equal, of someone like ourselves. What both feelings must have in common is this: the pain comes not from thinking that something bad will happen to us as a result, but purely from what is happening to our neighbour. For if the pain and the disturbance come from the thought that some harm will come to us from the other man's good fortune, it will no longer be envy in the one case or indignation in the other. It will be fear.

It is clear, too, that each of these feelings will bring with it the opposite feeling in the opposite case. The man who is pained at people suffering undeservedly will be glad, or at least not pained, at people suffering in the opposite way, that is, deservedly. When men who have murdered their fathers, or committed some other bloody murder, meet their punishment, no good man would be pained. We ought to be glad at things like that, and in the same way at people who prosper and deserve to. Both are just, and both make the decent man glad, since he cannot help hoping that what has come to someone like him might come to him as well.

All these feelings come from the same character, and their opposites from the opposite character. The man who is glad at other people's misfortunes is the same man who envies them. For if someone is pained when another man gets something or has it, he is bound to be glad when the other man loses it or it is destroyed. So all these feelings stand in the way of pity. They differ from one another for the reasons just given, but for the purpose of making things seem not pitiable, all of them are equally useful.

Let us speak first about indignation: whom people feel indignant with, over what, and in what state they themselves are; and after that about the others.

The answer is clear from what has been said. If indignation is being pained at someone who appears to be prospering undeservedly, then, first of all, it is plain that we cannot feel indignation at every kind of good thing.

If a man is just, or brave, or comes to have some virtue, nobody will feel indignant with him for it, just as nobody feels pity for a man who has the opposites of these qualities. People feel indignant over wealth and power and things of that sort: the things which, broadly speaking, good men deserve, and so do men who have the good things that come by nature, such as noble birth and beauty and the like.

Now, since what is old seems to come close to what is natural, it follows that when two people have the same good thing, we feel more indignant with the one who has only lately come by it and is prospering because of it. The newly rich annoy people more than those who have been rich a long time or were born to it. It is the same with holding office, with power, with having many friends or fine children, and with anything else of that kind. And if these things bring them some further good, the same holds: here too the newly rich who get into office because of their money annoy people more than the men whose families have always been rich. And so on in the other cases.

The reason is that the second sort seem to have what is their own, and the first sort do not. What has always looked a certain way seems to be true, and so the others seem to have what is not theirs.

Next, not every good thing suits just anybody: there is a certain proportion, a certain fit, between a good and the man who has it. Splendid armour, for instance, suits not the just man but the brave one, and a grand marriage suits not the newly rich but the well born. So if a good man fails to get what fits him, that is a matter for indignation. So is an inferior man competing with a better one, above all when they are in the same line. That is why the poet says of a Trojan warrior,

	He kept away from battle with Ajax, son of Telamon;
	for Zeus was indignant with him whenever he fought a better man.

And if they are not in the same line, the inferior competing with the better in any way at all is still a matter for indignation: the musician with the just man, for example, since justice is better than music.

From this, then, it is clear whom people feel indignant with and why: these are the people and the reasons, or things like them. As for the people themselves, they are prone to indignation, first, if they happen to deserve the greatest goods and actually possess them. For it is not just that people who are not like them should be thought to deserve the same things.

Second, if they happen to be good men of real worth. For they judge soundly, and they hate what is unjust.

They are prone to it, too, if they are ambitious and want certain things, especially if their ambition is for the very things that others are getting without deserving them.

And, in general, people who think they themselves deserve things that they think others do not deserve are prone to feel indignant with those others, and over those things. That is why people with the souls of slaves, worthless people and people with no ambition are not prone to indignation: there is nothing they think they themselves deserve.

From all this it is clear over what kind of people's bad luck, misfortunes and failures we ought to be glad, or at least not pained, since the opposites follow plainly from what has been said. So if the speech puts the judges into this state of mind, and shows that the people who claim pity, and the things they claim it for, do not deserve to get it, and deserve not to get it, then pity is impossible.
