Chapter 18: What All Speeches Share

Persuasive speech is always used to bring about a judgement, since about things we already know and have already judged there is no longer any need for speaking. And this holds in every case. It holds when someone uses speech on a single person, urging him towards something or turning him away from it, as people do when they give someone a warning or try to talk him round; the single listener is a judge just as much as a crowd is, because, to put it simply, whoever has to be persuaded is a judge. It holds just the same when someone argues against an opponent, or against a thesis. For he has to use speech to knock down the opposing case, and he makes his speech against that case as if it were an opponent standing in front of him. It holds in the same way in ceremonial speeches, since there the speech is put together for the spectator as though he were a judge. Still, strictly speaking, the only person who is a judge in the full sense is the one who decides the questions under dispute in public contests, in the courts and the assembly: in the courts the question is how the facts stand, and in the assembly it is what the people are deliberating about. And the characters that belong to the different forms of government were discussed earlier, when we dealt with political speeches. So it may now be taken as settled how, and by what means, we should make our speeches express character.

Each kind of speech, as we saw, has its own separate end. For all of them we have collected the opinions and premises from which speakers draw their proofs when they advise, when they praise and blame, and when they dispute in court; and we have also settled the means by which it is possible to make a speech express character. What remains for us is to go through the things that all speeches share.

For every speaker is bound to make use, in his speeches, of the line of argument about the possible and the impossible, and to try to show, in some cases, that a thing will happen, and in others that it has happened.

Again, the line of argument about size is common to every kind of speech. For everyone makes things smaller or bigger, whether he is advising, praising or blaming, or accusing or defending.

Once these have been settled, we shall try to say what we can about enthymemes in general, and about examples, so that by adding what is left we may complete the plan we set out with at the start. Of these shared lines of argument, making things bigger, which is called amplification, belongs most properly to ceremonial speeches, as has been said already; what has happened in the past belongs to courtroom speeches, since that is what the court has to judge; and what is possible and what will happen belong to political speeches.
