Chapter 22: Enthymemes

Let us now speak about enthymemes in general, and how to look for them, and after that about their lines of argument, since these are two different kinds of thing.

We have said already that the enthymeme is a syllogism, in what way it is one, and how it differs from the syllogisms of dialectic. You must not draw the conclusion from too far back, and you must not put in every step. The first is unclear because it is so long; the second is mere chatter, because it states what is obvious. This is also why uneducated speakers are more persuasive in front of a crowd than educated ones; as the poets put it, the uneducated are the ones who speak "more musically" to a crowd. The educated talk in commonplaces and generalities; the uneducated talk about what they know, and about things close to their listeners. So a speaker should not argue from everything that anybody believes, but only from definite opinions: those held by the people who are judging, or by the people whose judgement they accept. And it should be clear that these opinions are held by all the listeners, or by most of them. Nor should he draw his conclusions only from what is necessary, but also from what holds as a rule.

The first thing to grasp, then, is this. Whatever subject you have to speak about and reason about, whether with a political syllogism or with any other kind, you must have the facts about it, either all of them or some. If you have none, you will have nothing to draw a conclusion from.

I mean, for instance: how could we advise the Athenians whether or not to go to war, if we did not know what their strength is, whether it lies in ships or in soldiers on land or both, and how great it is; what their revenues are; who their friends and their enemies are; and then what wars they have fought already, and how they fared, and everything else of that kind?

Or how could we praise them, if we did not have to hand the sea battle at Salamis, or the battle of Marathon, or what they did for the children of Heracles, when Athens took them in and defended them, or something else of the kind? Everyone bases his praise on the noble things that belong to a person, or seem to.

In the same way people blame from the opposite things, looking for anything of that kind that belongs to the people they are blaming, or seems to. They say, for instance, that the Athenians enslaved the Greeks; that they reduced to slavery the people of Aegina and Potidaea, who had fought alongside them against the barbarian, the Persian invader, and had distinguished themselves in the fighting; and they bring up everything else of this kind, and any other such wrong the Athenians may have done. It is the same for those who accuse and those who defend in the courts: they look at the facts of the case, and accuse or defend from those.

It makes no difference whether the subject is the Athenians or the Spartans, a man or a god: the method is the same. Whether we are advising Achilles, praising him or blaming him, or accusing him or defending him, we must take what belongs to him, or seems to, and speak from that. If we are praising or blaming, we look for anything noble or shameful about him; if we are accusing or defending, for anything just or unjust; if we are advising, for anything advantageous or harmful.

And the same holds for any subject whatever. Take justice, for instance, and the question whether it is good or not. We must argue from the things that belong to justice and to the good.

It is plain, then, that everybody proves his case in this way, whether his reasoning is strict or loose. Nobody draws his premises from everything in the world, only from the things that belong to the subject in hand; and reasoning itself makes it clear that there is no other way to prove anything. So it is evident that, as we said in the Topics, the first thing a speaker needs is a stock of premises picked out in advance on each subject, about what is possible and what is most to the point.

When something comes up on the spur of the moment, you must look for arguments in the same way. Keep your eyes not on vague generalities but on the facts that belong to the subject of the speech, and mark off as many of them as you can, and those as close to the subject as possible. The more facts you have that belong to the case, the easier it is to prove your point; and the closer they are to the subject, the more they belong to it and the less they are commonplaces anybody could use.

By commonplaces I mean praising Achilles for being a man, or one of the demigods, or for having gone on the expedition against Troy. These things are true of many others as well, so a speaker who praises him like that is praising Achilles no more than Diomedes. By what belongs to him I mean the things that happened to nobody but Achilles: that he killed Hector, the best of the Trojans, and Cycnus, who could not be wounded and who stopped the whole Greek army from landing; that he went to the war when he was very young, and though he was not bound by the oath that bound the other Greek chiefs; and everything else of that kind.

This, then, is one way of choosing premises, and the first: the way by lines of argument. Now let us speak of the elements of enthymemes. By the element of an enthymeme and a line of argument for an enthymeme I mean the same thing. But first let us say what has to be said first.

There are two kinds of enthymeme. One kind proves that something is so or is not so; the other refutes. They differ in the same way as refutation and syllogism differ in dialectic.

The proving enthymeme draws its conclusion from premises the other side agrees to; the refuting enthymeme draws a conclusion the other side does not agree to.

We have, more or less, the lines of argument for each of the special kinds of subject that are useful and necessary, since the premises on each have already been picked out. So the lines of argument from which to draw enthymemes about good and bad, noble and shameful, just and unjust, and in the same way about characters, emotions and settled states of mind, are already in our hands, taken up earlier.

But let us also take up the subject in another way, in general and for every case. We shall point out as we go which lines of argument refute and which prove, and also the lines of argument of apparent enthymemes, which are not really enthymemes, just as they are not really syllogisms either. Once all that has been made clear, we shall settle the matter of answers and objections: where to find them, to bring against enthymemes.
