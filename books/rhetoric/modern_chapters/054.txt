Chapter 14: The Introduction

The introduction is the beginning of a speech. It is what the prologue is in a poem or a play, and what the prelude is in a piece of music for the pipes. All of these are beginnings, and each of them is a kind of road built for what is coming next.

The musical prelude is like the introduction of a ceremonial speech. A piper begins by playing whatever he can play well, and then joins it on to the opening note of the piece proper. Ceremonial speeches should be written the same way: say straight away whatever you like, then strike the keynote of your subject and join the two together. Everyone does this. An example is the introduction to Isocrates' Helen, a speech in praise of Helen of Troy that opens with an attack on the eristics, the professional wranglers who argue for the sake of winning; and the eristics have nothing whatever to do with Helen. At the same time, even if the speaker does wander from the point, that is fitting, and better than having the whole speech all of one piece.

The introductions of ceremonial speeches are drawn from praise or blame. Gorgias, for instance, in his Olympic speech, begins "Men of Greece, you deserve to be admired by many", and so praises the men who founded the great festival gatherings. Isocrates, on the other hand, in his Panegyricus, blames them, because they honoured the excellences of the body with prizes but set up no reward at all for people who think well.

They may also be drawn from advice. A speaker might say, for instance, that we ought to honour good men, and so he himself is praising Aristides, the Athenian who was called "the Just". Or that we ought to honour people who are neither famous nor worthless, but who are good and yet go unnoticed, like Alexander, Priam's son, better known as Paris. That is giving advice.

Again, they may be drawn from the introductions of courtroom speeches, that is, from appeals to the hearer, when the speech is about something that goes against what people expect, or something difficult, or something that everyone has already talked about, so as to win the audience's indulgence. Choerilus of Samos, the epic poet who wrote about the Persian War, did this: he complained that the poets of the old days had the whole field to themselves, while he, coming last, found it all taken, and he begins

	But now, when everything has been shared out.

These, then, are the sources of the introductions to ceremonial speeches: praise, blame, urging people on, warning them off, and appeals to the hearer. And the opening notes of such a speech may be either foreign to the subject or closely connected with it.

As for the introductions of courtroom speeches, you have to grasp that they do the same work as the prologues of plays and the openings of epic poems. The openings of dithyrambs, the wild choral hymns to Dionysus, are different: they are like ceremonial introductions, as in "Because of you and your gifts, or your spoils".

But in prologues and in epics the opening is a sample of the story, so that the audience may know beforehand what it is about and their minds may not be left hanging, since what is left undefined makes the mind wander. So the poet who puts the beginning into the hearer's hand, as it were, lets him hold on to it and follow the story. That is why Homer begins the Iliad

	Sing, goddess, of the wrath,

and why he begins the Odyssey

	Tell me, Muse, about the man,

and why Choerilus begins his poem

	Lead me on to another tale, how from the land of Asia
	a great war came across to Europe.

The tragic poets, too, make clear what the play is about. They may not do it at once, as Euripides does in his prologues, but they do it somewhere, as Sophocles does when Oedipus tells his story in Oedipus the King:

	My father was Polybus.

Comedy is the same. So this is the most necessary job of the introduction, and the one that belongs to it alone: to make clear the end, the purpose for which the speech exists. That is why, if the matter is plain and small, you should not use an introduction at all.

All the other kinds of introduction that people use are remedies, and they are common to every kind of speech. They are drawn from the speaker, from the hearer, from the subject, and from the opponent. Those drawn from the speaker himself and from his opponent are whatever serves to clear away prejudice or to create it. These are not handled the same way by both sides. The defendant must deal with the prejudice against him first; the accuser must deal with it in his conclusion. The reason is obvious enough. When the defendant is about to bring himself before the court, he has to remove whatever stands in his way, so he must clear away the prejudice first. The accuser, who is creating the prejudice, must do it in his conclusion, so that the jurors remember it better.

Those drawn from the hearer aim at making him well disposed, or making him angry, and sometimes at making him attentive, or the opposite. For it is not always to your advantage to make him attentive, which is why many speakers try to get their audience laughing. Everything will serve to make the hearers ready to learn, if you want it to, including seeming to be a decent person, since people pay more attention to decent people. People pay attention to things that are important, that concern themselves, that are astonishing, and that are pleasant. So you should give them the impression that your speech is about things of that kind. If you want them not to pay attention, give the impression that the matter is small, that it has nothing to do with them, or that it is painful.

But we must not lose sight of the fact that all such things lie outside the speech proper. They are aimed at a poor sort of hearer, one who will listen to what lies outside the subject. If the hearer is not like that, there is no need for an introduction beyond stating the matter in a few words, so that the speech has a head, as a body does.

Besides, making the hearers attentive is something every part of the speech may need to do, if need be. Their attention flags everywhere else more than at the beginning, so it is ridiculous to put this at the start, when everyone is listening with the most attention. Instead, wherever the moment comes, you should say something like "And please give me your attention, for this is no more my business than yours", or "I will tell you something more terrible, or more astonishing, than anything you have ever heard". This is what Prodicus the sophist meant when he said that whenever his audience began to nod off, he would slip in a bit of his fifty-drachma lecture, the expensive one.

It is clear that such things are addressed to the hearer not in so far as he is a hearer. For everyone, in the introduction, is either creating prejudice or trying to get rid of his own fears. So the guard in Sophocles' Antigone, who has come to bring King Creon bad news and is afraid of what will happen to him for it, begins

	My lord, I will not say that I have come in such a hurry,

and another character, faced with the same kind of speech, is asked

	Why all this preamble?

People who have a bad case, or seem to have one, do the same, since it is better for them to dwell on anything rather than on the case itself. That is why slaves never answer the question they are asked, but go all round it, and make preambles.

I have already said how to make the hearers well disposed, and how to produce each of the other feelings of that kind. And since it has been well said, in the prayer Odysseus makes before he goes to the court of the Phaeacians,

	Grant that I come to the Phaeacians as a friend and as one they pity,

these are the two things to aim at.

In ceremonial speeches you must make the hearer think that he shares in the praise, whether he himself, or his family, or his way of life, or in some way or other. For what Socrates says in the funeral speech is true: it is not hard to praise Athenians in front of Athenians, but it is hard in front of Spartans.

The introductions of political speeches are borrowed from those of courtroom speeches, but by its nature political speaking has the least need of them. The audience know what the subject is, and the matter needs no introduction, except for the speaker's own sake, or because of those who argue against him, or if the audience do not take the matter to be as important as you want them to, but think it more important or less. Then you must either create prejudice or get rid of it, and either make the matter bigger or make it smaller. It is for these reasons that a political speech needs an introduction, or else simply for ornament, because without one it looks thrown together on the spot. Gorgias' speech in praise of the people of Elis is like that: with no preliminary sparring, no warming up, like a boxer who does not even swing his arms before the bout, he starts at once, "Elis, happy city".
