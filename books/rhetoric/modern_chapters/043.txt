Chapter 3: Frigid Style

Frigid style, the *psuchros* or "cold" style, which reaches for grandeur and falls flat, comes about in four ways.

The first is in compound words. Lycophron, for example, speaks of "the many-faced sky of the mighty-summited earth", *polu-prosôpos* and *megalo-koruphos*, and of "a narrow-passaged shore", *steno-poros*. Gorgias called people "beggar-muse-flatterers", *ptôcho-mouso-kolakes*, a single word welded out of three, and spoke of men "forsworn" against one "well-sworn", *epi-orkêsantes* against *eu-orkêsas*. And Alcidamas writes of "the soul filling with fury and the face becoming fire-coloured", *puri-chrôs*; he "thought their zeal would be end-accomplishing", *telesphoros*, and "established the persuasiveness of his words as end-accomplishing"; and he calls the floor of the sea "dark-blue-coloured", *kuano-chrôs*. All of these sound like poetry because they are compounds.

That is one cause. A second is the use of rare words, the old or outlandish words that poets use. So Lycophron calls Xerxes "a *pelôr* man", using Homer's word for a monster; and he calls Sciron, the robber Theseus killed, "a *sinis* man", turning the name of Sinis, another robber of the same legend, into an adjective meaning "ravaging". Alcidamas calls poetry a "plaything", *athurma*, speaks of "the *atasthalia*", the wanton recklessness, "of nature", and of a man "whetted with the unmixed wrath of his thought". These are poets' words, and in prose they stand out.

The third is in epithets, in using them long, or out of place, or packed too close together. In poetry it is fitting to say "white milk", but in prose such epithets are less fitting, and if they are overdone they give the game away and make it obvious that what you are writing is poetry. You must use some, since they lift the style out of the ordinary and give it an unfamiliar air. But you must aim at the mean, because overdoing it does more harm than speaking carelessly: careless speech merely lacks a good quality, while overdone speech has a bad one. That is why Alcidamas' style seems frigid. He uses epithets not as a seasoning but as the main dish, they come so thick, so large and so conspicuous. He does not say "sweat" but "the moist sweat", as if sweat could be anything else; not "to the Isthmian Games" but "to the solemn gathering of the Isthmian Games"; not "laws" but "the laws, the kings of cities"; not "at a run" but "with the racing impulse of the soul"; not "taking over a school of the Muses" but "taking over Nature's school of the Muses". He speaks of "the scowling care of the soul"; of a man who is the craftsman not of "favour" but of "favour with all the people"; of "the steward of his hearers' pleasure". A man hides "not with branches but with the branches of the forest"; he covers "not his body but his body's shame". He calls desire "counter-imitating", *antimimos*, "desire of the soul", and that word is a compound and an epithet at once, so that it becomes a poem all by itself. And he writes "so out-of-bounds", *exedros*, a poet's word, "the excess of his wickedness".

So people who talk like poets where it does not fit make their style ridiculous and frigid, and unclear too, because of all the chatter: when you pile more words on a listener who already understands, you destroy the clarity by darkening it. People use compound words in the ordinary way when a thing has no name and the compound is easily put together, as with *chronotribein*, "to time-spend", to waste time. But if you use many of them, the style becomes simply poetic. That is why compound words are most useful to the writers of dithyrambs, the choral songs to Dionysus, since those poets love a big noise; rare words to the writers of epic, since they have a grand and haughty air; and metaphor to the iambic verse of drama, since that is what it now uses, as I have said.

And the fourth place where frigidity arises is in metaphors. Some metaphors, too, are unfitting: some because they are ridiculous, for the comic poets use metaphors as well; others because they are too grand and tragic. And they are unclear if they are fetched from too far off. So Gorgias writes, "Matters green and bloodless", and "You sowed this shamefully and reaped it wretchedly". That is far too poetic. And Alcidamas calls philosophy "a fortress against the law", *epiteichisma*, the word for a fort an army builds inside enemy country to harry it; he calls the Odyssey "a beautiful mirror of human life"; and he speaks of "bringing no such plaything to poetry". None of these persuades, for the reasons given.

But what Gorgias said to the swallow is the best of the tragic sort. It flew over him and let its droppings fall on him, and he said, "Shame on you, Philomela!", naming the princess of legend who was turned into a swallow. For a bird it was not shameful to do that, but for a young girl it was. So he scolded her well, by calling her what she had been, and not what she is.
