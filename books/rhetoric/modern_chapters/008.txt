Chapter 9: Virtue, Vice, Praise and Blame

After this, let us talk about virtue and vice, and about the noble and the disgraceful, because these are what a speaker aims at when he praises or blames. And while we are talking about them, we shall at the same time be showing how we can make our audience think of us as people of a certain character, which, as I said, is the second kind of proof. For the same things that let us make other people seem worthy of trust where virtue is concerned will let us do the same for ourselves.

People often praise, sometimes seriously and sometimes in fun, not only a human being or a god but lifeless things too, and any animal that comes to hand. So we need to have the premises for these subjects ready in the same way. Let us, then, say something about them as well, as far as is needed by way of illustration.

The noble, then, is whatever is worth choosing for its own sake and also deserves praise; or whatever is good and is pleasant because it is good. If that is what the noble is, virtue must be noble, since virtue is good and deserves praise.

Virtue, as it is generally thought of, is a power of getting good things and of keeping them; a power of doing many great services to others; in fact, of doing every kind of service in every case.

The parts of virtue are justice, courage, self-control, magnificence, greatness of soul, generosity, practical wisdom and wisdom.

If virtue is a power of doing good to others, then the greatest virtues must be the ones most useful to other people. That is why people honour the just and the brave above everyone else: courage is useful to others in war, and justice both in war and in peace. Next comes generosity. The generous give their money away freely and do not compete for it, and money is the thing other people want most.

Justice is the virtue through which each person has what is his own, and has it in the way the law lays down. Injustice is the vice through which a person has what belongs to others, against the law.

Courage is the virtue that makes people do noble deeds in the midst of danger, as the law commands, and makes them obedient to the law. Cowardice is the opposite.

Self-control is the virtue that makes people stand towards the pleasures of the body as the law commands. Lack of self-control is the opposite.

Generosity is the readiness to do good to others with money. Meanness is the opposite.

Greatness of soul is the virtue that does others great services. Smallness of soul is the opposite.

Magnificence is the virtue that produces something on a grand scale when money is spent, as when a rich citizen pays for a festival or a warship in style. Smallness of soul and shabbiness are its opposites.

Practical wisdom is a virtue of the mind that lets people take good counsel about the good things and the bad things we have already spoken of as bearing on happiness.

That is enough, for the present occasion, about virtue and vice in general and about their parts. The rest is not hard to see. It is plain that whatever produces virtue must be noble, since it leads to virtue; and so must whatever comes from virtue. Of that kind are the signs of virtue and its works.

Now, the signs of virtue, and all the things that are the deeds of a good man or the things done to him, are noble. It follows that whatever is a deed of courage, or a sign of courage, or has been done courageously, is noble. The same goes for just things and for deeds done justly. It does not go, however, for what is done to a person justly. Justice is the only one of the virtues in which "justly" is not always noble: when it comes to being punished, to be punished justly is more disgraceful than to be punished unjustly. And the same holds, in the same way, for the other virtues.

Next, things are noble when the prize for them is honour, and when they are done for honour rather than for money. So too are the things worth choosing that a person does not do for his own sake.

So are things that are good without qualification; whatever a man has done for his country while neglecting his own interests; and things that are good by nature, and good, but not good for the man himself, since the things that are good for him he does for his own sake.

So are the things a man can have when he is dead rather than while he is alive, since what belongs to a man while he lives has more of self-interest in it.

So are all deeds done for the sake of others, since they are less for oneself. So is every success won for other people and not for oneself, and every success won for those who have done one good, since that is just. So are acts of kindness, since they are not aimed at oneself.

So are the opposites of the things people are ashamed of. People are ashamed of what is disgraceful, whether they are saying it, doing it or about to do it. Sappho's poem shows this. The poet Alcaeus said to her:

	I want to say something, but I am held back
	by shame.

And she answered him:

	If what you longed for were good and noble,
	and your tongue were not stirring up some evil to say,
	shame would not have hidden your eyes;
	you would have spoken out about what is right.

Things are noble, too, when people strive for them anxiously but without fear, because that is how people feel about the goods that lead to a good name.

The virtues and the deeds of those who are better by nature are nobler: a man's, for instance, rather than a woman's.

So are the virtues that give enjoyment to others rather than to oneself. That is why justice, and what is just, are noble.

It is noble, too, to take revenge on your enemies and not to make peace with them. Paying back what you have received is just, what is just is noble, and not to be beaten is the mark of a brave man.

Victory and honour are among the noble things. They are worth choosing even when they bring in nothing, and they show that a man excels in virtue. So are things worth remembering, and the more they are remembered, the nobler they are. Nobler still are the things that follow a man when he is no longer alive, the things that honour attends, the things that are out of the ordinary, and the things that belong to one person alone, because all of these are better remembered.

So are possessions that bring in no profit, since they are more fitting for a free man. So are the things peculiar to a particular people, and whatever marks what that people admire. In Sparta, for instance, it is noble to wear your hair long. Long hair is the mark of a free man, since with long hair it is not easy to do any work of the kind a hired labourer does.

It is noble, too, not to work at any mechanical trade, since the mark of a free man is that he does not live at another man's beck and call.

For praise and for blame alike, we should also treat the qualities that lie close to a man's real ones as if they were the same thing. To blame him, call the cautious man cold and scheming; to praise, call the simpleton good-natured and the man who feels nothing calm.

In every case take your word from the neighbouring qualities, always choosing the best of them. Call the quick-tempered man and the man who flies into a frenzy straightforward; call the stubborn man grand and dignified. Speak of those who go to an extreme as though they had the matching virtue: the rash man is brave, and the spendthrift is generous. Most people will think so, and at the same time a false inference can be drawn from the man's motive. If he runs risks where there is no need to, people will think that he would all the more run them where it is noble to do so; and if he lavishes money on anyone who comes along, that he will do the same all the more for his friends. For to do good to everyone is virtue carried to excess.

Consider also who the audience is in front of whom you are giving the praise. As Socrates used to say, in Plato's Menexenus, it is not hard to praise Athenians in front of Athenians. You should speak of whatever a particular audience values, whether they are Scythians, Spartans or philosophers, as though the man you are praising had it. And in general, treat what is valued as though it were noble, since the two seem to lie close together.

Actions are noble, too, when they are fitting: when, for instance, they are worthy of a man's ancestors or of what he has already achieved, since winning further honour both makes for happiness and is noble. They are noble also when they go beyond what could be expected, but in the direction of the better and the nobler: when, for instance, a man is modest in good fortune and great of soul in bad, or grows better and more ready to be reconciled as he grows greater. Of this kind is the saying of Iphicrates, the general who had risen from being a cobbler's son: "Look what I started from, and look what I have become." Of this kind too is the line about the Olympic victor, who remembered carrying fish from Argos to Tegea:

	Once, with a rough basket across my shoulders...

and the epitaph Simonides wrote for Archedice, the daughter of Hippias, the tyrant of Athens, which says that her mind was never lifted up to arrogance, though she was

	daughter and wife and sister of tyrants.

Since praise is based on actions, and acting by choice is the mark of a good man, we must try to show that the man we praise acted by choice. It helps if he appears to have done so many times. That is why we should treat even coincidences and strokes of luck as if they were chosen, because if many similar things are brought forward, they will be taken as a sign of virtue and of choice.

Praise is a speech that brings out the greatness of a man's virtue, so we must show that his actions are of that kind. An encomium, the speech that celebrates a man, deals with his achievements. Everything that surrounds them, such as noble birth and education, serves only to make us believe in them: it is likely that good parents will have good children, and that a man brought up in a certain way will turn out to be that kind of man. That is why we give an encomium to people who have actually done something. But achievements are signs of a settled character, since we would praise a man even if he had done nothing, if we believed he was the sort of man who would.

Calling a man blessed and calling him happy are the same thing as each other, but they are not the same as praise and encomium. Just as happiness contains virtue, calling a man happy contains praise and encomium.

Praise and advice have a shape in common. What you would put forward as advice becomes an encomium when you change the wording.

So, since we know what a man ought to do and what sort of man he ought to be, we should take these things, which we would say as advice, and change the wording and turn them around. Take, for instance, the advice that a man ought not to be proud of what he owes to luck, but only of what he owes to himself. Put that way, it works as advice. Put this way, it works as praise: "He was proud, not of what came to him by luck, but of what he owed to himself." So when you want to praise someone, look at what advice you would give; and when you want to give advice, look at what you would praise.

The wording will necessarily be turned the opposite way when you change the form that forbids into the form that does not forbid: "one ought not to be proud of luck" forbids, while "he was not proud of luck" simply states.

We should also use many of the ways of making a thing greater, which is called amplification. For instance, say that a man did something alone, or first, or with only a few others, or that he was the one mainly responsible; all these make the deed noble. Use also the circumstances of time and occasion, that is, whether he did it when nobody could have expected it. Say that he has succeeded at the same thing many times, since that is a great thing and will look as though it came from the man himself and not from luck. Say that rewards meant to encourage and honour such deeds were invented and set up because of him; or that he was the first to have an encomium written about him, as Hippolochus was; or the first to have a statue set up in the market-place, as Harmodius and Aristogiton were, the two men who killed Hipparchus, the brother of the tyrant of Athens. And do the same, the other way round, for blame. If you do not have enough to say about the man himself, set him beside others, which is what Isocrates used to do, because he was not used to pleading in court. And you should compare him with men who are famous, since it makes him greater, and it is noble, if he is better than men of worth.

Amplification falls naturally among the forms of praise, and with good reason. It consists in superiority, and superiority is one of the noble things. That is why, even if you cannot compare him with the famous, you should compare him with other people, since superiority is thought to point to virtue.

Speaking generally, of the kinds of argument common to all speeches, amplification is best suited to ceremonial speeches. There the actions are taken as agreed, so what is left is to clothe them with greatness and beauty. Examples are best suited to political speeches, since it is from what has happened before that we foretell and judge what will happen. Enthymemes are best suited to courtroom speeches, since what has happened, because it is unclear, most of all calls for causes and demonstration.

These, then, are nearly all the sources from which praise and blame are drawn, the things a speaker should keep his eye on when he praises or blames, and the materials of encomium and of reproach. Once we have these, their opposites are plain, since blame is drawn from the opposites.
