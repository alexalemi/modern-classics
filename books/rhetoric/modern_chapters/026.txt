Chapter 12: The Young

Next, let us go through the different kinds of character, and see what people are like according to their emotions, their settled states, their time of life and their fortunes.

By emotions I mean anger, appetite and the like, which we have already discussed. By settled states I mean the virtues and the vices, which we have also discussed already, together with the sort of things each kind of person chooses and the sort of things he is inclined to do. The times of life are youth, the prime of life, and old age. By fortune I mean good birth, wealth and power, and their opposites, and in general good luck and bad luck.

To begin with the young. In character the young are full of appetites, and the kind of people who do whatever they have an appetite for. Of the bodily appetites, the one they follow most is the appetite for sex, and this is the one they cannot control.

They change their appetites easily and quickly tire of them. They want things violently, but they stop wanting them just as fast, because their wishes are sharp rather than large, like the hunger and thirst of sick people.

They are hot-blooded, quick-tempered, and the sort to follow wherever their anger leads. Their temper gets the better of them: because they love honour, they cannot bear to be slighted, and they are outraged if they think they are being wronged.

They love honour, but they love winning even more, because youth wants to be on top, and winning is a way of being on top. They love both of these more than money. Money they love least of all, because they have not yet learned what it is to go without, as the wise man Pittacus said in his remark about the prophet Amphiaraus.

They are not bad-natured but good-natured, because they have not yet seen much wickedness. They are trusting, because they have not yet been deceived very often.

They are full of hope. The young are warm by nature, the way people are warm with wine; and besides, they have not yet failed very often. They live mostly in hope, because hope is about the future and memory is about the past, and for the young the future is long and the past is short: on the first day of your life you can remember nothing at all, but you can hope for everything. For the same reason they are easy to deceive, since they hope so readily.

They are braver than other people, because they are full of temper and full of hope. The temper keeps them from being afraid, and the hope makes them confident: nobody feels fear while he is angry, and to hope for something good is to feel confident.

They are easily ashamed, because they do not yet imagine that anything else is noble besides what they have been taught; the rules of their own society are the only education they have had.

They have greatness of soul, because life has not yet humbled them and they have no experience of what it is to be forced by necessity. And thinking yourself worthy of great things is greatness of soul, and that belongs to someone full of hope.

They would rather do what is noble than what is useful, because they live by their character more than by calculation, and calculation aims at what is useful, while virtue aims at what is noble.

They are fonder of their friends and companions than people of any other age are, because they enjoy living in company, and they do not yet judge anything by whether it is useful, and so they do not judge their friends that way either.

Everything they get wrong, they get wrong by going too far and too hard, against the maxim of Chilon, the wise man who said "Nothing too much". They do everything too much: they love too much and hate too much, and the same with everything else. And they think they know everything, and insist on it; this, in fact, is the reason they do everything too much.

When they do wrong, they do it out of insolence and not out of malice. They are quick to pity, because they suppose that everyone is good, and better than he really is: they measure other people by their own innocence, and so they suppose that those others are suffering what they do not deserve.

And they are fond of laughter, and so fond of wit as well; for wit is insolence that has been educated.
