Chapter 7: Appropriateness

Style will be appropriate if it expresses emotion and character, and if it is in proportion to the subject it deals with.

It is in proportion if weighty matters are not spoken of in an offhand, slapdash way, nor trivial matters solemnly, and if no ornament is hung on an ordinary word. Otherwise the result looks like comedy. That is what Cleophon does: some of his expressions were like saying "Revered Lady Fig", *potnia sukê*. *Potnia* is the title of reverence you give a goddess, and to fix it to a fig tree is to dress a humble word in finery it cannot carry.

Style expresses emotion when, if the subject is insolence, it is the language of a man who is angry; if the subject is impious or shameful things, the language of a man who is disgusted by them and reluctant even to say them; if it is things worthy of praise, the language of admiration; if it is things that call for pity, the language of a man brought low. And so on with the rest.

Language suited to the subject also makes the facts believable. The listener's mind draws a false conclusion, that the speaker is telling the truth, because in circumstances like these people really do feel the way he seems to feel. So the listener thinks the facts are as the speaker says they are, even when they are not. And the listener always shares the feelings of someone who speaks with feeling, even if what he says amounts to nothing.

That is why many speakers stun their audiences simply by making a great noise.

This showing by signs also expresses character, when the language that goes with each class of person and each settled disposition is used. By class I mean a person's age, a boy, a man or an old man; a woman or a man; a Spartan or a Thessalian. By disposition I mean the settled states that make a man's life the kind of life it is, since not every disposition does that.

So if the speaker uses words that belong to the disposition, he will convey the character. A country bumpkin and an educated man would not say the same things, or say them in the same way. Listeners are also affected by a trick the professional speechwriters, who wrote speeches for others to deliver, use until everyone is sick of it: "Who does not know?", "Everybody knows." The listener agrees, out of embarrassment, so as to share in what everyone else supposedly knows.

Whether you use these things at the right moment or the wrong one is a question common to every kind of speech.

For any exaggeration there is a remedy, the one everybody repeats: you must get in first and rebuke yourself. Then what you say seems true, since it is clear that the speaker knows perfectly well what he is doing.

Next, do not use all your matching effects at once, because it is by not doing so that the listener is taken in without noticing. I mean, for example, that if the words are harsh, you should not make your voice, your face and everything else that goes with them harsh as well. Otherwise each of them is seen for what it is. But if you use one and not the other, you are doing the same thing without anyone noticing. So if gentle things are said harshly, and harsh things gently, the speech becomes persuasive.

Compound words, a crowd of epithets, and above all unusual words suit a speaker who is carried away by emotion. An angry man can be forgiven for calling a wrong *ouranomêkes*, "sky-high", or *pelôrion*, "monstrous". The same goes for a speaker who already has his audience in his grip, and has made them wild with enthusiasm by praise or blame, by anger or friendship. Isocrates does this at the end of his Panegyric, with "fame and memory", *phêmê kai mnêmê*, two words that rhyme in Greek, and with "those who dared to endure", in a grand word taken from poetry. People carried away by enthusiasm really do talk like that, and so, plainly, audiences in the same state accept it. That is why this kind of language has suited poetry, for poetry is inspired, a thing of the gods. It should be used, then, either in this way, or with irony, as Gorgias used to do, and as Socrates does in the Phaedrus, where he blames the nymphs of the place for the torrent of fine words pouring out of him.
