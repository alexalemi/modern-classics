Chapter 4: Simile

The simile is a metaphor too; the difference between them is small. When Homer says of Achilles

	he sprang on them like a lion

that is a simile. When he says "a lion, he sprang on them", that is a metaphor: because both are brave, the poet has carried the name across and called Achilles a lion.

The simile is useful in prose as well, but only now and then, because it has the feel of poetry about it. Similes should be brought in the way metaphors are, since they are metaphors, differing only in the way I have just described: the word "like".

Here are some examples of similes. Androtion, the pupil of Isocrates, said of Idrieus, a Carian prince who had been held prisoner, that he was like a little dog let off its chain: the dogs fly at you and bite, and Idrieus, freed from his chains, was dangerous. Then there is Theodamas, who compared Archidamus to a Euxenus who did not know geometry. That is a simile by proportion, since it follows that Euxenus is an Archidamus who does know geometry: take the geometry away from the one and he is the other. In Plato's Republic, those who strip the dead are compared to little dogs that bite the stones thrown at them and never touch the person throwing them. Plato also says the people are like a ship's owner who is strong but a little deaf. And he says the verses of the poets are like young people who have the bloom of youth without real beauty: once the bloom has gone from the young, and once the verses have been broken up into prose, neither looks the same as it did. Pericles said the Samians were like children who take the mouthful you give them but cry while they take it. He said, too, that the Boeotians were like holm-oaks: holm-oaks are brought down by their own kind, and the Boeotians by fighting one another. Demosthenes compared the people to passengers who are seasick on board ship. Democrates compared the public speakers to nurses who swallow the morsel themselves and wipe the babies' lips with the spit. And Antisthenes compared Cephisodotus, a thin man, to frankincense, because he gives pleasure by wasting away.

All of these can be put either as similes or as metaphors. So any that do well when spoken as metaphors will plainly do as similes too, and similes are metaphors that need a word of explanation.

But a metaphor by proportion must always work in both directions, and must apply to either of the two things of the same kind. If the wine cup is "the shield of Dionysus", then it is fitting to call the shield "the wine cup of Ares": as the cup is to the god of wine, so the shield is to the god of war.
