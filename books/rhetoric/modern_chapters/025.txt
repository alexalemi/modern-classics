Chapter 11: Emulation

In what state people feel emulation, over what things and with regard to whom, is clear from what follows. Let emulation be a kind of pain at the apparent presence, in people who are by nature like ourselves, of good things that are held in honour and that we could get ourselves: a pain felt not because someone else has them, but because we do not have them too. That is why emulation is a decent feeling and belongs to decent people, while envy is a base feeling and belongs to base people. The one, through emulation, gets himself ready to win the good things; the other, through envy, tries to stop his neighbour having them.

It follows that the people prone to emulation are those who think they deserve good things they do not have, when those things are within their reach. For nobody thinks he deserves what looks impossible. That is why the young and the great-souled are like this.

So are people who already have the kind of good things that are fit for men held in honour: wealth, many friends, public office, and the like. Since they feel it is fitting for them to be good men, because goodness is what fits people so well provided, they feel emulation for goods of that kind. So, too, are people whom others think deserving.

People whose ancestors, relatives, household, nation or city are held in honour feel emulation about those honours. For they think these things belong to them, and that they deserve them.

If the good things held in honour are what people feel emulation for, then the virtues must be such things, and so must everything that is useful to other people and does them good, since people honour those who do them good and those who are good. So must those good things that the people around us can share in and enjoy, such as wealth and beauty, rather than health.

It is clear, too, who the people are that others feel emulation for. They are the people who possess these things and things like them: the things already mentioned, such as courage, wisdom and office, since men in office can do good to many. So generals and orators are admired in this way, and everyone who has power of that kind.

So are the people many others want to be like, or want to know, or want as friends; the people many admire, or whom we ourselves admire.

So are those whose praises and tributes are told by the poets or by the prose writers.

People feel contempt for the opposite sort. For contempt is the opposite of emulation, and feeling contempt is the opposite of feeling emulation. People who are in a state to feel emulation for others, or to be the objects of it, are bound to feel contempt for those who have the bad things that are the opposites of the good things that excite emulation, and contempt on account of those bad things. That is why they often feel contempt for people who are lucky, when their luck comes to them without any of the goods held in honour.

We have now said what makes the emotions arise and what makes them go away, and so where the proofs that work through them come from.
