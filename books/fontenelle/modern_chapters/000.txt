Preface

I'm in much the same position Cicero was in when he set out to put philosophy into his own language, when until then it had been treated only in Greek. He tells us that people said his books would be quite useless. Those who loved philosophy, having gone to the trouble of seeking it out in Greek books, would hardly bother afterward to read it in Latin ones that weren't the originals. And those who had no taste for philosophy didn't care to see it in either Latin or Greek.

To this he answered that just the opposite would happen: people who weren't philosophers would be tempted to become philosophers by how easy the Latin books were to read, and those who already were philosophers from reading the Greek would be glad to see how these subjects had been handled in Latin.

Cicero was right to say so. The excellence of his genius and the great reputation he had already earned guaranteed the success of this new kind of work he was offering the public. But I am far from having the same grounds for confidence in an undertaking so close to his. I wanted to treat philosophy in a way that wasn't philosophical at all. I tried to bring it to a point where it would be neither too dry for people of the world nor too frivolous for scholars. But I'm told, much as Cicero was, that a work like this suits neither the scholars, who can learn nothing from it, nor the people of the world, who won't have any wish to learn anything from it. I'm in no position to give the answer he gave. It may well be that in looking for a middle ground where philosophy would suit everyone, I've found one where it suits no one. Middle grounds are too hard to hold, and I don't think I'll ever feel like putting myself through the same trouble a second time.

I should warn readers of this book who already know something about physics that I never meant to teach them anything. I only meant to entertain them, by presenting in a slightly more pleasant and playful way what they already know on firmer footing. I warn those to whom these subjects are new that I believed I could teach them and entertain them at the same time. The first group will be going against my intention if they look here for anything useful, and the second, if they look only for amusement.

I won't waste time saying that out of all of philosophy I chose the subject best suited to stir curiosity. It seems that nothing should interest us more than knowing how this world we live in is built, whether there are other worlds like it, and whether they are inhabited too. But after all, anyone is free to worry about all this or not. Those who have thoughts to waste can waste them on subjects like these, but not everyone can afford so useless an expense.

In these conversations I've put a woman who is being taught, and who has never heard of any of these things. I thought this fiction would make the work more enjoyable, and would encourage women readers by the example of a woman who, without ever stepping outside the limits of a person with no tincture of science, still understands what she is told and arranges the Vortices and the Worlds in her head without confusion. Why should any woman yield to this imaginary Marquise, who grasps only what she can't help grasping?

To be fair, she does apply herself a little. But what does applying oneself mean here? It doesn't mean digging by sheer meditation into something obscure in itself, or obscurely explained. It only means not reading without picturing clearly what you read. For this whole system of philosophy I ask of women readers only the same attention that The Princess of Cleves demands, if you want to follow its plot properly and appreciate all its beauty. It's true that the ideas in this book are less familiar to most women than those in The Princess of Cleves, but they are no more obscure, and I'm sure that by a second reading at the very most, nothing in it will have escaped them.

Since I didn't mean to build a system in the air, with nothing underneath it, I've used real reasoning from physics, and as much of it as was needed. But fortunately, in this subject, the ideas of physics are cheerful in themselves. At the same moment that they satisfy reason, they give the imagination a spectacle that pleases it as much as if it had been made especially for it.

Where I found a few pieces that weren't quite of that kind, I gave them borrowed ornaments. Virgil did the same in his Georgics, where he rescues the substance of his subject, which is thoroughly dry, with frequent and often very pleasant digressions. Even Ovid did as much in The Art of Love, though the substance of his subject was infinitely more pleasant than anything he could mix into it. Apparently he thought it tiresome to talk about one thing all the time, even if that thing was lessons in gallantry. I needed the help of digressions more than he did, yet I've used them with a fair amount of restraint. I've justified them by the natural freedom of conversation. I've put them only in places where I thought readers would be glad to find them. I've put most of them near the beginning of the work, because that's when the mind isn't yet used to the main ideas I'm offering it. And finally, I've drawn them from my subject itself, or from something quite close to it.

I didn't want to imagine anything about the inhabitants of the worlds that was completely impossible or fantastic. I've tried to say everything that can reasonably be thought about them, and even the fancies I've added to that have some real foundation. The true and the false are mixed together here, but they are always easy to tell apart. I won't try to justify a mixture so odd. That is the most important point of this work, and it's exactly the one I can't give a reason for.

All that's left for me to do in this preface is to speak to one kind of person. They may be the hardest to satisfy, not because there aren't very good reasons to give them, but because they have the privilege of not being paid in all the good reasons, if they don't want to be. These are the scrupulous people, who may imagine there's some danger to religion in putting inhabitants anywhere but on Earth. I respect even the excessive delicacies people have created in matters of religion, and I would have respected this one so far as not to offend it in this work, if it were contrary to my view. But here's what may surprise you: it doesn't apply only to this system, in which I fill an infinity of Worlds with inhabitants. It takes only a small error of imagination to untangle. When someone tells you the Moon is inhabited, you immediately picture men built like us, and then, if you're a bit of a theologian, you're full of difficulties. The descendants of Adam couldn't have spread as far as the Moon, or sent colonies to that country. So the men on the Moon are not sons of Adam. But in theology it would be awkward for there to be men who weren't descended from him. There's no need to say more. Every imaginable difficulty comes down to this, and the words one would have to use in a longer explanation are too worthy of respect to be put in a book as unserious as this one. So the objection rests entirely on the men of the Moon. But it is the objectors who like to put men on the Moon. I don't put any there. I put inhabitants there who are not men at all. What are they, then? I haven't seen them, and it's not from having seen them that I speak of them. And don't suspect that saying there are no men on the Moon is a dodge I'm using to slip past your objection. You'll see that it's impossible for there to be any, given my idea of the infinite diversity that nature must have put into its works. That idea runs through the whole book, and no philosopher can dispute it. So I think I'll hear this objection only from people who talk about these conversations without having read them. But is that any reason to feel reassured? No. On the contrary, I have every reason to fear that the objection will be made to me from a good many quarters.

To Monsieur L***

You want me, Monsieur, to give you an exact account of how I spent my time in the country, at the home of Madame la Marquise de G***. Do you realize that this exact account will be a book, and what's worse, a book of philosophy? You're expecting parties, card games, and hunting, and you'll get planets, worlds, and vortices. Those were almost the only things anyone talked about. Luckily you're a philosopher, and you won't laugh at them as much as someone else would. You may even be glad that I've drawn Madame la Marquise over to the side of philosophy. We couldn't have made a more important conquest, because I count beauty and youth as things of great value, always. Don't you think that if Wisdom herself wanted to present herself to men with success, she'd do well to appear in a form that came a little close to the Marquise's? Especially if she could have the same charm in conversation, I'm convinced everyone would run after wisdom. Still, don't expect to hear marvels when I give you an account of the conversations I had with this lady. You'd almost need as much wit as she has to repeat what she says the way she said it. You'll see in her only that quickness of mind you already know. As for me, I consider her learned, because of the extreme ease with which she could become so. What does she lack? Having opened her eyes on books. That's nothing, and plenty of people have done it their whole lives, and I'd deny them the name of scholar, if I dared. For the rest, Monsieur, you'll owe me something. I know that before going into the details of the conversations I had with the Marquise, I'd be entitled to describe for you the château where she'd gone to spend the autumn. People have often described châteaux on lesser occasions, but I'll spare you that. It's enough for you to know that when I arrived at her house, I found no company there, and I was very glad of it. The first two days were nothing remarkable. They went to exhausting the news of Paris, where I'd come from. But then came the conversations I want to share with you. I'll divide them up by evening, because in fact we only had these conversations in the evenings.
