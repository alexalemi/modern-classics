The Fifth Evening: The Fixed Stars Are Suns, Each Lighting a World

The Marquise was truly impatient to learn what would become of the fixed stars. "Will they be inhabited like the planets?" she asked me. "Won't they? What are we going to do with them?"

"You might guess it yourself, if you really wanted to," I said. "The fixed stars can't be any closer to the Earth than 27,660 times the distance from here to the Sun, which is thirty-three million leagues, and if you annoyed an astronomer, he would put them even farther. The distance from the Sun to Saturn, the farthest planet, is only three hundred thirty million leagues. That's nothing next to the distance from the Sun, or the Earth, to the fixed stars. Nobody bothers to count it. Their light, as you can see, is quite bright and brilliant. If they received it from the Sun, it would have to be very faint by the end of such a dreadful journey. And then, by a reflection that would weaken it even more, they would have to send it back to us across that same distance. It would be impossible for light that had gone through a reflection and made the same journey twice to have the strength and brilliance of the light of the fixed stars. So they shine by their own light. In a word, every one of them is a Sun."

"Am I wrong," cried the Marquise, "or do I see where you're leading me? Are you going to say: the fixed stars are so many Suns; our Sun is the center of a vortex that turns around it; so why shouldn't each fixed star also be the center of a vortex that turns around it? Our Sun has planets that it lights, so why shouldn't each fixed star have planets that it lights too?"

"All I can answer," I said, "is what Phaedra answered Oenone: 'You are the one who named him.'"

"But," she went on, "the universe has become so big that I'm lost in it. I don't know where I am anymore. I'm nothing. What, everything is divided into vortices, tossed in a jumble among one another? Every star is the center of a vortex, perhaps as large as the one we live in? All this immense space, which holds our Sun and our planets, is only a tiny scrap of the universe? As many spaces like it as there are fixed stars? It bewilders me, it upsets me, it terrifies me."

"And it puts me at ease," I answered. "When the sky was just that blue dome with the stars nailed to it, the universe seemed small and cramped to me, and I felt almost squeezed. Now that we've given that dome infinitely more breadth and depth by dividing it into thousands upon thousands of vortices, I feel as if I can breathe more freely, as if I'm standing in bigger air, and the universe certainly has a whole different grandeur. Nature spared nothing in making it. She lavished riches on it, in a way entirely worthy of her. Nothing is so beautiful to picture as this staggering number of vortices, each with a Sun at its middle, making planets turn around it. The inhabitants of a planet in one of these endless vortices see the Suns of the surrounding vortices on every side. But they can't possibly see those worlds' planets, which have only a weak light borrowed from their Sun and don't send it beyond their own world."

"You're offering me," she said, "a kind of view so long that the eye can't reach the end of it. I see the people of the Earth clearly. Then you show me those of the Moon and the other planets of our vortex, quite clearly, really, though less clearly than those of the Earth. After them come the inhabitants of the planets of the other vortices. I admit they're far back in the distance, and however hard I try to see them, I can hardly make them out at all. In fact, aren't they almost wiped out by the very phrase you have to use when you talk about them? You have to call them the inhabitants of one of the planets of one of those vortices, of which there are infinitely many. And the same phrase fits us! Admit it, you can hardly pick us out anymore among so many worlds. As for me, I'm beginning to see the Earth as so frightfully small that I don't think I'll be eager about anything from now on. Surely, when people are so hungry to get ahead, when they make plan after plan and take such trouble, it's because they don't know about the vortices. I fully intend to let my laziness profit from my new lights, and when people reproach me for being idle, I'll say: 'Ah, if you only knew what the fixed stars are!'"

"Alexander must not have known," I replied. "A certain author who holds that the Moon is inhabited says, in all seriousness, that Aristotle couldn't possibly have failed to hold such a reasonable opinion (how could a truth have escaped Aristotle?), but that he never wanted to say anything about it, for fear of upsetting Alexander, who would have been in despair at seeing a world he couldn't conquer. All the more reason they would have kept the vortices of the fixed stars a secret from him, if anyone had known about them back then. Mentioning them would have been a terrible way to court his favor. As for me, I do know about them, and I'm very sorry I can't get any use out of what I know. By your reasoning, at most they cure ambition and restlessness, and I don't suffer from those diseases. A little weakness for whatever is beautiful, that's my affliction, and I don't think the vortices can do anything about it. Other worlds make this one seem small to you, but they don't spoil beautiful eyes or a beautiful mouth. Those are still worth what they're worth, in spite of every possible world."

"Love is a strange thing," she answered, laughing. "It gets out of everything, and no system can do it any harm. But now tell me frankly, is your system really true? Don't hide anything from me, I'll keep it secret. It seems to me it rests on nothing but a small, very flimsy point of fitness. A fixed star shines by its own light like the Sun, so it must be, like the Sun, the center and soul of a world, with planets turning around it. Is that really absolutely necessary?"

"Listen, Madame," I said, "since we're in the mood to keep mixing foolish gallantry into our most serious talk, mathematical reasoning works just like love. You can't grant a lover the smallest favor without soon having to grant him a bigger one, and in the end it goes a long way. In the same way, grant a mathematician the slightest principle, and he'll draw a consequence out of it that you'll have to grant him too, and from that consequence another. In spite of yourself, he leads you so far that you can hardly believe it. Both kinds of people always take more than they're given. You agree that when two things are alike in everything I can see, I can believe them alike in what I can't see, as long as nothing else stands in the way. From that I concluded that the Moon is inhabited, because it resembles the Earth, and the other planets are, because they resemble the Moon. I find that the fixed stars resemble our Sun, so I give them everything it has. You're in too deep to turn back. You have to take the step gracefully."

"But," she said, "on the strength of this resemblance you set up between the fixed stars and our Sun, the people of another great vortex must see our Sun as just a small fixed star, one that shows itself to them only during their nights."

"There's no doubt of it," I replied. "Our Sun is so close to us, compared with the Suns of the other vortices, that its light must have infinitely more force on our eyes than theirs does. So when we see it, we see only it, and it wipes out everything else. But in another great vortex, a different Sun rules, and it in turn wipes out ours, which shows up there only at night, along with the rest of the foreign Suns, that is, the fixed stars. They fasten it to that great dome of the sky along with the others, and it makes up part of some Bear or some Bull. As for the planets that turn around it, our Earth for example, since no one can see them from so far away, no one even thinks of them. So every Sun is a daytime Sun for the vortex where it stands, and a nighttime Sun for all the other vortices. In its own world it's one of a kind. Everywhere else it just helps make up the numbers."

"But surely," she went on, "in spite of this equality, the worlds must differ in a thousand ways. A common ground of resemblance still leaves room for endless differences."

"Certainly," I said, "but the trouble is guessing them. What do I know? One vortex has more planets turning around its Sun, another has fewer. In one there are lesser planets that turn around bigger ones; in another there are none. Here they're all bunched up around their Sun in a little ball, beyond which a great empty space stretches out to the neighboring vortices. Elsewhere they run their course out near the edges of the vortex and leave the middle empty. I don't doubt that there might even be some deserted vortices with no planets at all. Others might have a Sun that isn't at the center, so it really moves and carries its planets along with it. Others might have planets that rise or sink relative to their Sun as the balance that holds them suspended shifts. Well, what more do you want? That's plenty for a man who has never left his own vortex."

"It's hardly plenty," she answered, "for the number of worlds. What you're describing would only do for five or six, and from here I can see thousands."

"What would you say, then," I went on, "if I told you that there are many more fixed stars than the ones you see, that with telescopes you discover an infinite number that don't show themselves to the eye, and that in a single constellation where people counted perhaps twelve or fifteen, there are as many as were once seen in the whole sky?"

"Mercy!" she cried. "I give up. You're overwhelming me with worlds and vortices."

"I know what I'm saving for you," I added. "You see that whiteness called the Milky Way. Can you imagine what it is? An infinity of little stars, invisible to the eye because they're so small and scattered so close together that they seem to form one continuous glow. I wish you could see through a telescope this anthill of stars, this seedbed of worlds. In a way they're like the Maldive Islands, those twelve thousand little islands or sandbanks, separated only by channels of sea that you could almost jump across like ditches. In the same way, the little vortices of the Milky Way are so tightly packed that it seems to me people in one world could talk to those in the next, or even shake hands. At least I believe the birds of one world pass easily into another, and that pigeons could be trained there to carry letters, the way they carry them here in the Levant from one city to another. These little worlds evidently escape the general rule by which a Sun in its own vortex wipes out all the foreign Suns the moment it appears. If you're in one of the little vortices of the Milky Way, your Sun is hardly any closer to you, and has no noticeably greater force on your eyes, than a hundred thousand other Suns of the neighboring little vortices. So you see your sky sparkling with an infinite number of fires that are very close to one another and not far from you. When you lose sight of your own particular Sun, you still have plenty left, and your night is no less bright than your day. At least the difference can't be noticeable. To put it more precisely, you never have night at all. The people of those worlds, used to a constant brightness as they are, would be very astonished if you told them there are unfortunate creatures who have real nights, who fall into deep darkness, and who, when they do have light, see only a single Sun. They'd look on us as beings nature had treated shabbily, and our condition would make them shudder with horror."

"I won't ask you," said the Marquise, "whether there are Moons in the worlds of the Milky Way. I can see they'd be no use to the main planets there, which have no night, and which besides move in spaces too narrow to bother with that baggage of lesser planets. But do you know that by multiplying worlds so generously for me, you've raised a real difficulty? The vortices whose Suns we see touch the vortex we're in. Vortices are round, aren't they? And how can so many balls touch a single one? I try to picture it, and I feel I can't."

"It shows a great deal of wit," I answered, "to have that difficulty, and even to be unable to solve it. It's a very good difficulty in itself, and the way you conceive it, it has no answer, and it takes very little wit to find answers to what has none. If our vortex were shaped like a die, it would have six flat faces and be far from round, but on each of those faces you could set a vortex of the same shape.

"If instead of six flat faces it had twenty, fifty, a thousand, then as many as a thousand vortices could rest on it, one on each face. And you understand that the more flat faces a body has bounding it on the outside, the closer it comes to being round. So a diamond cut with facets on every side, if the facets were very small, would be nearly as round as a pearl of the same size. Vortices are round only in that way. They have an infinity of faces on the outside, each of which carries another vortex. These faces are very unequal. Here they're bigger, there smaller. The smallest ones of our vortex, for example, correspond to the Milky Way and support all those little worlds. Suppose two vortices resting on two neighboring faces leave some gap between them underneath, as must happen very often. Immediately nature, who is careful with her ground, fills the gap with a small vortex or two, or perhaps a thousand, which don't get in the others' way and still amount to one, or two, or a thousand worlds more. So we can see many more worlds than our vortex has faces to carry them. I'd bet that although these little worlds were made only to be thrown into corners of the universe that would otherwise have gone to waste, and although they're unknown to the worlds that touch them, they're still very pleased with themselves. These are surely the ones whose little suns can be discovered only with telescopes, and they exist in a truly staggering quantity. In the end, all these vortices fit together with one another as well as they can. Since each one has to turn around its Sun without changing place, each takes on whatever way of turning is most convenient and easy in its situation. They mesh with one another, in a way, like the wheels of a watch, and help one another's motion. It's true, though, that they also work against one another. Each world, they say, is like a balloon that would expand if it were left alone, but it's immediately pushed back by the neighboring worlds and shrinks into itself, after which it starts to swell again, and so on. And some philosophers claim that the fixed stars send us that trembling light, and seem to shine in fits and starts, only because their vortices are constantly pushing on ours and constantly being pushed back."

"I like all these ideas very much," said the Marquise. "I like these balloons that swell and shrink every moment, and these worlds that are always fighting, and above all I like seeing how this fight creates a traffic in light between them, which is apparently the only traffic they can have."

"No, no," I said, "it's not the only one. The neighboring worlds sometimes send us visitors, and quite magnificently too. We get comets from them, which are adorned with a dazzling head of hair, or a venerable beard, or a majestic tail."

"Ah, what deputies!" she said, laughing. "We could easily do without their visit. All it does is frighten people."

"They frighten only children," I replied, "because of their extraordinary outfits. But children are many. Comets are just planets that belong to a neighboring vortex. They were moving near its edges. But that vortex, perhaps pressed differently by the ones around it, is rounder on top and flatter underneath, and it's the underside that faces us. These planets, which began to move in a circle up near the top, didn't foresee that down below the vortex would fail them, because it's as if squashed there. To keep up their circular motion, they have to enter another vortex, which I suppose is ours, and cut across its outer edges. That's why they always stay very high above us. We can suppose they travel beyond Saturn. Given the prodigious distance of the fixed stars, there has to be a great empty space without planets between Saturn and the edges of our vortex. Our enemies reproach us for the uselessness of this great space. They can stop worrying. We've found its use. It's the guest quarters for the foreign planets that enter our world."

"I see," she said. "We don't allow them to come into the heart of our vortex and mix with our planets. We receive them the way the Grand Sultan receives the ambassadors sent to him. He doesn't do them the honor of lodging them in Constantinople, only in a suburb of the city."

"We have another thing in common with the Ottomans," I went on. "They receive ambassadors without sending any back, and we don't send any of our planets to the neighboring worlds."

"Judging by all this," she replied, "we're quite proud. Still, I'm not sure yet what to make of it. These foreign planets look very threatening with their tails and beards, and perhaps they're sent to insult us. Ours, which aren't built the same way, wouldn't be so good at making themselves feared if they went to the other worlds."

"The tails and beards," I answered, "are pure appearance. The foreign planets don't differ at all from ours. But when they enter our vortex they take on the tail or the beard through a certain kind of illumination they receive from the Sun, which hasn't yet been very well explained among us. Still, we're sure it's only a kind of illumination. We'll figure it out when we can."

"Then I'd like our Saturn," she said, "to go pick up a tail or a beard in some other vortex and spread terror there, and afterward, having taken off that terrible getup, come back and settle in here with the other planets to its ordinary duties."

"It's better for Saturn," I answered, "not to leave our vortex. I told you about the shock that happens where two vortices push and push back against each other. I believe a poor planet is tossed around quite roughly as it crosses, and its inhabitants are none the better for it. We think we're very unlucky when a comet appears to us. It's the comet itself that's unlucky."

"I don't believe it," said the Marquise. "It brings us all its inhabitants in good health. Nothing is so entertaining as changing vortices like that. We who never leave our own lead a rather boring life. If the inhabitants of a comet have wit enough to foresee when they'll pass into our world, the ones who have already made the trip tell the others in advance what they'll see. 'You'll soon discover a planet with a great ring around it,' they may say, speaking of Saturn. 'You'll see another with four little ones following it.' Maybe there are even people whose job is to watch for the moment they enter our world, and who cry out at once, 'New Sun! New Sun!' like those sailors who cry, 'Land! Land!'"

"So there's no use trying to make you pity the inhabitants of a comet," I said. "But I hope at least you'll feel sorry for those who live in a vortex whose Sun goes out and who remain in eternal night."

"What?" she cried. "Suns go out?"

"Yes, of course," I answered. "The Ancients saw fixed stars in the sky that we don't see there anymore. Those Suns lost their light. Great desolation, surely, throughout the whole vortex, a general dying-off on all the planets. For what can you do without a Sun?"

"That's too gloomy an idea," she said. "Isn't there some way to spare me it?"

"I can tell you, if you like," I answered, "what some very capable people say: that the fixed stars that have disappeared are not extinguished for that reason, that they are Suns that are only half so, that is, with one dark half and one bright half. As they rotate on themselves, they show us now the bright half and now the dark half, and then we no longer see them. By all appearances, the fifth Moon of Saturn is made that way. For during part of its revolution, it's lost from sight completely, and that isn't because it's farther from the Earth then. On the contrary, it's sometimes closer than at other times, when it lets itself be seen. And although that Moon is a planet, which naturally doesn't prove anything for a Sun, we can easily imagine a Sun partly covered with permanent spots, whereas ours has only passing ones. I'd gladly adopt that opinion to oblige you, since it's gentler than the other. But I can adopt it only for certain stars that have regular times for appearing and disappearing, as people have begun to notice. Otherwise half-Suns can't exist. But what shall we say of the stars that disappear and don't show themselves again after the time in which they should surely have finished turning on themselves? You're too fair-minded to want me to make you believe those are half-Suns. Still, I'll make one more effort in your favor. These Suns haven't gone out. They've only sunk into the immense depth of the heavens, and we can no longer see them. In that case the vortex has followed its Sun, and everything there is going well. It's true that most of the fixed stars don't have this motion that takes them away from us. At other times they'd have to come closer again, and we'd see them now bigger and now smaller, which doesn't happen. But we'll suppose that only a few small vortices, lighter and more agile, slip between the others and make certain loops at the end of which they come back, while the bulk of the vortices stays motionless. But here comes a strange misfortune. There are fixed stars that show themselves to us, spend a long time doing nothing but appearing and disappearing, and finally vanish altogether. Half-Suns would reappear at regular times. Suns that sank into the sky would vanish only once, not to reappear for a long time. Make up your mind, Madame, and be brave. These stars must be Suns that darken enough to stop being visible to our eyes, then light up again, and in the end go out altogether."

"How can a Sun darken and go out," said the Marquise, "when it's in itself a source of light?"

"Very easily, according to Descartes," I answered. "He supposes that the spots on our Sun are scum or mist, which can thicken, several of them gathering together and hooking onto one another. Then they'll go on to form a crust around the Sun that keeps growing, and farewell, Sun. And if the Sun is a fire attached to a solid matter that feeds it, we're no better off, because the solid matter will be used up. They say we've already had a narrow escape. The Sun was very pale for whole years, for example the year that followed the death of Caesar. That was the crust starting to form. The Sun's strength broke it up and dispersed it, but if it had kept on, we'd have been lost."

"You make me tremble," said the Marquise. "Now that I know what the consequences of the Sun's paleness are, I think that instead of going to my mirror every morning to see whether I'm pale, I'll go look at the sky to see whether the Sun is."

"Ah, Madame," I replied, "be reassured. It takes time to ruin a world."

"But in the end," she said, "it takes only time?"

"I admit it," I replied. "This whole immense mass of matter that makes up the universe is in perpetual motion, and no part of it is entirely exempt. As soon as there's motion anywhere, don't trust it. Changes have to come, slow or sudden, but always in times proportioned to the effect. The Ancients were amusing to imagine that the heavenly bodies were of a nature never to change, just because they hadn't yet seen them change. Had they had the time to make sure by experience? The Ancients were young next to us. If roses, which last only a day, wrote histories and left memoirs to one another, the first roses would have painted a certain portrait of their gardener, and over more than fifteen thousand ages of roses, the ones who passed it on to those who were to follow would have changed nothing in it. On that basis they'd say: 'We've always seen the same gardener. As far back as any rose remembers, no one has seen anyone but him. He has always looked just as he does. Surely he doesn't die like us. He doesn't even change.' Would the roses' reasoning be sound? Yet it would have more foundation than what the Ancients reasoned about the heavenly bodies. And even if no change had happened in the heavens up to today, even if they seemed to show that they were made to last forever without any alteration, I still wouldn't believe it. I'd wait for a longer experience. Should we set our own lifespan, which lasts only an instant, as the measure of anyone else's? Does it follow that what has lasted a hundred thousand times longer than we have must last forever? It isn't so easy to be eternal. A thing would have to have lived through many human lifetimes laid end to end before it began to give any sign of immortality."

"Truly," said the Marquise, "I see the worlds are far from being able to claim it. I wouldn't even do them the honor of comparing them to that gardener who lasts so long next to the roses. They're only like the roses themselves, which are born and die in a garden one after another. For I expect that if old stars disappear, new ones appear. The species has to renew itself."

"There's no fear that it will perish," I answered. "Some will tell you these are only Suns that are drawing closer to us after being lost to us for a long time in the depths of the sky. Others will tell you they're Suns that have broken free of the dark crust that was beginning to surround them. I readily believe all that may be so. But I also believe the universe may have been made so that new Suns form from time to time. Why couldn't the matter suited to making a Sun, after being scattered in many different places, gather together in the long run in one particular spot and lay the foundations of a new world there? I'm all the more inclined to believe in these new productions because they fit better with the high idea I have of nature's works. Would she have the power only to bring plants or animals to birth and death in a continual round? I'm convinced, and you already are too, that she uses this same power on worlds, and that it costs her no more. But we have more than mere conjecture on this. The fact is that for nearly a hundred years now, since we've been seeing with telescopes a sky that is entirely new and was unknown to the Ancients, there are not many constellations where some noticeable change hasn't happened. And it's in the Milky Way that people notice the most, as if in that anthill of little worlds there were more motion and restlessness."

"Honestly," said the Marquise, "I find the worlds, the heavens and the heavenly bodies so subject to change now that I'm completely over it."

"Let's get over it even better, if you take my advice," I replied. "Let's not speak of it anymore. Besides, you've now arrived at the last dome of the heavens, and to tell you whether there are still stars beyond, one would have to be cleverer than I am. Put worlds there, or don't. It's up to you. Those great invisible countries are properly the empire of philosophers, who can make them be or not be as they wish, or be whatever they wish. It's enough for me to have led your mind as far as your eyes can go."

"What!" she cried. "I have the whole system of the universe in my head! I'm learned!"

"Yes," I replied, "you're reasonably so, and you have the convenience of being able to disbelieve everything I've told you the moment you feel like it. I ask only one reward for my trouble: that you never see the Sun, or the sky, or the stars, without thinking of me."

Since I have given the public an account of these Conversations, I think I should keep nothing back from it on this subject. I will publish one more Conversation, which took place long after the others but was exactly of the same kind. It will be called an Evening, since the others were, because it's better for everything to go under the same title.
