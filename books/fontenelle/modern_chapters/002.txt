The Second Evening: The Moon Is an Inhabited Earth

The next morning, as soon as anyone could go into the Marquise's apartment, I sent to ask how she was and whether she'd been able to sleep while spinning. She sent back word that she was already quite used to the earth's ways, and that she'd spent the night as peacefully as Copernicus himself could have done. A little later some company arrived and stayed until evening, following the tedious custom of the country. We were even grateful to them, because in the country they had every right to stretch their visit into the next day, if they'd wanted to, and they had the decency not to. So the Marquise and I found ourselves free again that evening. We went out to the park once more, and the conversation naturally turned straight back to our systems. She had understood them so well that she thought it beneath her to go over them a second time, and she wanted me to alarm her with something new.

"Very well, then," I said. "The sun, which is now standing still, has stopped being a planet, and the earth, which moves around it, has become one. So you won't be too surprised to hear that the moon is an earth like this one, and that it's very likely inhabited."

"But I've never heard people talk about the moon being inhabited except as a piece of madness and fantasy," she said.

"Maybe it is one," I answered. "I take sides in these matters the way people take sides in civil wars, where nobody knows what's going to happen, so you keep your contacts in the opposing camp and stay on good terms even with your enemies. Personally, though I believe the moon is inhabited, I still live on civil terms with those who don't, and I always keep myself in a position to join their opinion with honor if it gets the upper hand. But until they gain some serious advantage over us, here is what has made me lean toward the people of the moon."

Suppose there had never been any traffic between Paris and Saint-Denis, and a Parisian who had never left his city stood on the towers of Notre-Dame and saw Saint-Denis in the distance. Someone asks him whether he thinks Saint-Denis is inhabited the way Paris is. He'll answer boldly that it isn't. "I can see the people of Paris," he'll say, "but I can't see the people of Saint-Denis. Nobody has ever heard of them." Someone will point out to him that, true enough, when you stand on the towers of Notre-Dame you can't see the people of Saint-Denis, but distance is the reason; that everything you can see of Saint-Denis looks very much like Paris; that Saint-Denis has bell towers, houses, walls; and that it might well resemble Paris in being inhabited, too. None of this will make any impression on my Parisian. He'll stubbornly insist that Saint-Denis isn't inhabited, since he doesn't see anybody there. Our Saint-Denis is the moon, and each of us is that Parisian who has never left his city.

"Oh!" the Marquise interrupted. "You do us wrong. We're not as foolish as your Parisian. Since he can see that Saint-Denis looks just like Paris, he'd have to have lost his mind not to believe it's inhabited. But the moon doesn't look anything like the earth."

"Careful, Madame," I said, "because if the moon has to resemble the earth in every respect, you're obliged to believe it's inhabited."

"I admit there'll be no way out of it," she answered, "and I can see a look of confidence on your face that already frightens me. The two motions of the earth, which I'd never have suspected, have made me timid about everything else. But is it really possible that the earth is luminous, like the moon? Because that's needed for them to resemble each other."

"Alas, Madame," I replied, "being luminous isn't as big a deal as you think. Only the sun has that quality in any real sense. The sun is luminous by itself, by virtue of a special nature it has. The planets only shine because the sun shines on them. The sun sends its light to the moon, and the moon sends it back to us. So the earth must also send the sun's light back to the moon; it's no farther from the earth to the moon than from the moon to the earth."

"But," said the Marquise, "is the earth as good as the moon at sending sunlight back?"

"You still have a leftover esteem for the moon," I said, "that you can't seem to shake. Light is made up of tiny balls that bounce off whatever is solid and fly off in another direction, whereas they pass right through anything that offers them openings in a straight line, like air or glass. So the reason the moon lights us up is that it's a hard, solid body, and it sends these little balls back to us. Now, I don't think you'll deny the earth that same hardness and solidity. So consider what it means to be well placed! Because the moon is far away, we see it only as a luminous body, and we don't know that it's a big mass like the earth. The earth, on the other hand, has the misfortune of being seen from too close, so it looks to us only like a big mass, good for nothing but providing feed for animals, and we don't notice that it shines, because we can't get some distance away from it."

"So it's the same," said the Marquise, "as when we're dazzled by the splendor of people in stations above our own, and we don't see that at bottom they're all extremely alike."

"It's exactly the same thing," I answered. "We want to judge everything, and we're always standing at a bad vantage point. When we want to judge ourselves, we're too close; when we want to judge others, we're too far. The right place to see everyone properly would be somewhere between the moon and the earth. You'd have to be simply a spectator of the world, not an inhabitant."

"I'll never get over the injustice we do the earth," she said, "or our overly favorable prejudice toward the moon, unless you can assure me that the people of the moon don't know their advantages any better than we know ours, and that they take our earth for a star without knowing that where they live is one too."

"On that point," I said, "I can guarantee it. We appear to them to perform our duties as a star quite regularly. It's true they don't see us travel in a circle around them, but that doesn't matter, and here's why. The half of the moon that was turned toward us at the beginning of the world has been turned toward us ever since. It only ever shows us those eyes, that mouth, and the rest of the face that our imagination assembles for it out of the spots it shows us. If the opposite half were turned toward us, other spots, arranged differently, would no doubt make us imagine some other face. That's not to say the moon doesn't turn on itself. It does, in exactly as much time as it takes to go around the earth, that is, in one month. But whenever it makes some part of that turn on itself, and one cheek, say, of this so-called face ought to hide from us and something else appear, it makes exactly the same part of its circle around the earth, and moving to a new vantage point, it shows us that same cheek again. So the moon, which turns on itself with respect to the sun and the other stars, doesn't turn at all with respect to us. All the others seem to it to rise and set in the space of a fortnight, but our earth it always sees hanging in the same spot in the sky. That apparent immobility hardly suits a body that's supposed to pass for a star, but it isn't perfect. The moon has a certain rocking motion that makes a little corner of the face sometimes hide and a little corner of the opposite half show. And on my word, the moon never fails to blame this trembling on us, imagining that we have some pendulum-like motion in the sky, swinging back and forth."

"These planets are just like us," said the Marquise, "always blaming others for what is in ourselves. The earth says, 'It's not me that's turning, it's the sun.' The moon says, 'It's not me that's trembling, it's the earth.' There's a lot of error everywhere."

"I don't advise you to undertake reforming any of it," I answered. "You'd do better to finish convincing yourself that the earth and the moon are perfectly alike. Picture these two great balls hanging in the heavens. You know that the sun always lights half of any round body, while the other half is in shadow. So there's always one half of the earth, and one half of the moon, that is lit by the sun, that is, that has daytime, and another half that's in night. Notice also that just as a ball has less force and speed after it hits a wall and is sent off in another direction, so light is weakened when it has been reflected by a body. That whitish light that comes to us from the moon is the sun's own light, but it can only get from the moon to us by reflection. So it has lost a great deal of the force and vividness it had when it first struck the moon directly, and that brilliant light we receive from the sun, which the earth reflects onto the moon, must be only a whitish light by the time it gets there. So what looks luminous to us on the moon, and lights our nights, is the parts of the moon that have daytime; and the parts of the earth that have daytime, when they're turned toward the parts of the moon that have night, light those up too. It all depends on how the moon and the earth are facing each other.

"In the first days of the month, when we can't see the moon, it's because the moon is between the sun and us, traveling by day along with the sun. Its whole half that has daytime has to be turned toward the sun, and its whole half that has night has to be turned toward us. Of course we can't see that half, since it has no light to show itself with. But that half of the moon, which is in night, is turned toward the half of the earth that has daytime, and it sees us without being seen, and it sees us in the same shape in which we see the full moon. So for the people of the moon it's the full earth, if I may put it that way. Then the moon, moving along on its monthly circle, slips out from under the sun and begins to turn a small corner of its lit half toward us, and there's the crescent. At that point the parts of the moon that have night begin to no longer see the whole half of the earth that has daytime, and for them we're on the wane."

"That's enough," the Marquise said briskly. "I can work out the rest whenever I like. I only need to think for a moment and carry the moon around its monthly circle. I can already see that on the moon they have a month running backward from ours, and I'll bet that when we have a full moon, it's because the moon's whole lit half is turned toward the whole dark half of the earth; that then they don't see us at all, and they count it as new earth. I wouldn't want it held against me that I had you explain something so easy at such length. But the eclipses: how do those work?"

"It's up to you to guess," I said. "When the moon is new, it's between the sun and us, and its whole dark half is turned toward us, who have daytime. You can see that the shadow of that dark half is thrown toward us. If the moon is exactly under the sun, that shadow hides the sun from us, and at the same time it darkens part of the luminous half of the earth that was being looked at by the dark half of the moon. So that's an eclipse of the sun for us during our day, and an eclipse of the earth for the moon during its night. When the moon is full, the earth is between it and the sun, and the whole dark half of the earth is turned toward the whole lit half of the moon. So the earth's shadow is thrown toward the moon. If it falls on the body of the moon, it darkens the luminous half that we see, and it steals the sun from that half, which was having daytime. So that's an eclipse of the moon during our night, and an eclipse of the sun for the moon during the day it was enjoying. The reason there isn't an eclipse every time the moon is between the sun and the earth, or the earth between the sun and the moon, is that those three bodies are often not lined up in an exactly straight line, so the one that ought to cause the eclipse casts its shadow a little to one side of the one that ought to be covered by it."

"I'm amazed," said the Marquise, "that there's so little mystery to eclipses and that everyone doesn't guess the cause."

"Ah, indeed!" I answered. "There are plenty of peoples who, going about it the way they do, won't be guessing it for a long time yet. All through the East Indies they believe that when the sun and moon are eclipsed, it's because a certain dragon with very black claws is stretching them out over those bodies to seize them. And you see the rivers, during that time, covered with the heads of Indians who have gotten into the water up to their necks, because in their view that is a very devout posture and just the thing to persuade the sun and moon to defend themselves well against the dragon. In America they used to be convinced that the sun and moon were angry when they were eclipsed, and heaven knows what people didn't do to make peace with them. And the Greeks, who were so sophisticated, didn't they believe for a long time that the moon was bewitched, and that sorceresses drew it down from the sky to sprinkle a certain harmful foam on the herbs? And we, didn't we get a fine fright only thirty-two years ago, at a certain eclipse of the sun, which, to be fair, was total? Didn't countless people shut themselves up in cellars, and didn't the philosophers who wrote to reassure us write in vain, or nearly so? Did those who had taken refuge in the cellars ever come out?"

"Really," she replied, "all this is too shameful for mankind. There ought to be a decree from the human race forbidding anyone ever to speak of eclipses again, so that the memory of the foolish things that have been done or said on the subject isn't kept alive."

"Then that same decree would have to wipe out the memory of everything," I replied, "and forbid anyone to speak of anything at all, because I don't know of a single thing in the world that isn't a monument to some human folly."

"Tell me one thing, please," said the Marquise. "Are they as afraid of eclipses on the moon as we are here? It would strike me as perfectly ridiculous if the Indians of that country jumped into the water like ours, if the Americans thought our earth was angry with them, if the Greeks imagined that we were bewitched and were going to spoil their herbs, and if, in short, we gave them the same panic that they cause down here."

"I don't doubt it in the least," I answered. "I'd like to know why the gentlemen of the moon would be any stronger-minded than we are. By what right would they frighten us without our frightening them? I'd even think," I added with a laugh, "that since a prodigious number of men have been foolish enough, and still are, to worship the moon, there are people in the moon who worship the earth too, and that we're down on our knees before each other."

"After that," she said, "we might well claim to send influences to the moon and to bring on crises in its sick. But since it takes only a little wit and skill in the people of that country to demolish all these honors we flatter ourselves with, I confess I still fear we may be at some disadvantage."

"Fear nothing," I answered. "It doesn't seem likely that we're the only foolish species in the universe. Ignorance is just the kind of thing to be found everywhere, and though I'm only guessing at the ignorance of the people of the moon, I'm as sure of it as of the most reliable news that comes to us from there."

"And what is this reliable news?" she interrupted.

"It's what is reported to us," I said, "by those scholars who travel there every day with their telescopes. They'll tell you they've discovered lands there, seas, lakes, very high mountains, very deep chasms."

"You surprise me," she said. "I can see how you might discover mountains and chasms on the moon; you'd recognize those, I suppose, by notable unevenness. But how do you tell lands from seas?"

"You tell them apart," I answered, "because water lets part of the light pass through it and sends back less, so from a distance it looks like dark patches, while land, which because of its solidity sends all the light back, shows up as brighter places. The illustrious Monsieur Cassini, the man to whom the heavens are best known in all the world, has discovered on the moon something that splits in two, joins together again, and goes off to lose itself in a sort of well. We can flatter ourselves, with a good deal of likelihood, that it's a river. In short, all these different parts are known well enough that they've been given names, and often the names of scholars. One place is called Copernicus, another Archimedes, another Galileo. There's a Promontory of Dreams, a Sea of Rains, a Sea of Nectar, a Sea of Crises. In fact the description of the moon is so exact that a scholar who found himself there right now would no more get lost than I would in Paris."

"But," she went on, "I'd be very glad to know in more detail what the interior of the country is like."

"It isn't possible," I replied, "for the gentlemen of the observatory to tell you that. You have to ask Astolfo, who was taken to the moon by Saint John. I'm speaking of one of the most delightful follies of Ariosto, and I'm sure you'll be glad to hear it. I admit he'd have done better not to mix Saint John into it, since his name deserves such respect, but still, it's a poetic license that can only be called a bit too playful. Besides, the whole poem is dedicated to a cardinal, and a great pope honored it with a splendid approval that you can see at the front of some editions.

"Here's what happens. Roland, the nephew of Charlemagne, had gone mad because the beautiful Angelica had preferred Medoro to him. One day Astolfo, a brave paladin, found himself in the Earthly Paradise, which was on the summit of a very high mountain, where his hippogriff had carried him. There he meets Saint John, who tells him that to cure Roland's madness, the two of them needed to make a journey to the moon together. Astolfo, who asked nothing better than to see the world, doesn't need to be asked twice, and right away a chariot of fire sweeps the Apostle and the paladin up through the air. Since Astolfo was no great philosopher, he was very surprised to see the moon much bigger than it had looked from the earth. He was more surprised still to see other rivers, other lakes, other mountains, other cities, other forests, and, what would have surprised me too, nymphs hunting in those forests. But the rarest thing he saw on the moon was a valley where you could find everything of every kind that gets lost on earth: crowns and riches and fame, and countless hopes, and the time people give to gambling, and the alms people have distributed after their death, and the verses people present to princes, and the sighs of lovers."

"As for the sighs of lovers," the Marquise interrupted, "I don't know whether they were lost in Ariosto's day, but in our time I don't know any that go to the moon."

"If there were only you, Madame," I said, "you've sent quite a good number there. The moon is so thorough at collecting what's lost down here that everything is there. Ariosto only whispers this to you, but everything is there, right down to the Donation of Constantine. That's the document by which the popes claimed to be masters of Rome and Italy, on the strength of a gift the Emperor Constantine had made them, and the truth is that nobody can say what became of it. But guess what kind of thing you don't find on the moon? Folly. All the folly there ever was on earth is still there, perfectly preserved. On the other hand, it's unbelievable how many lost wits there are on the moon. They're so many vials full of a very subtle liquid that evaporates easily unless it's kept sealed, and on each of these vials is written the name of the person the wits belong to. I believe Ariosto puts them all in a heap, but I prefer to picture them neatly arranged in long galleries. Astolfo was astonished to see that the vials of many people he'd taken for very wise were quite full all the same. As for me, I'm convinced that mine has filled up considerably since I started entertaining you with visions, some philosophical, some poetic. But what consoles me is that it can't be that everything I say to you won't soon get you a little vial on the moon too.

"The good paladin didn't fail to find his own among so many others. He took it, with Saint John's permission, and got all his wits back through his nose, like smelling salts. But Ariosto says he didn't carry them very far, and that he let them go back to the moon through some folly he committed a little later. He didn't forget Roland's vial, which was the object of the trip. He had quite a job carrying it, because that hero's wits were naturally rather heavy, and not a single drop was missing. Then Ariosto, following his praiseworthy habit of saying whatever he pleases, addresses his mistress and says, in very beautiful verse: 'Who will climb to the heavens, my lovely one, to bring back the wits your charms made me lose? I wouldn't complain about that loss, provided it went no further. But if things go on as they've begun, I can only expect to become what I described Roland to be. Still, I don't think I need to go through the air, all the way to the moon, to get my wits back. My wits don't lodge so high. They wander over your eyes and your mouth, and if you'll let me take them back, allow me to gather them up with my lips.'

"Isn't that pretty? For my part, reasoning as Ariosto does, I'd say a person should never lose his wits except through love. You can see they don't go far, and all it takes is lips that know how to recover them. But when you lose them by other roads, as we lose ours, for example, by philosophizing right now, they go straight to the moon, and you can't get them back whenever you like."

"To make up for it," the Marquise replied, "our vials will at least be honorably placed in the philosophical section, whereas our wits might otherwise have gone wandering over someone who didn't deserve them. But to finish taking mine away, tell me, and tell me quite seriously, whether you believe there are men in the moon. Because up to now you haven't spoken to me about it in a very definite way."

"I?" I answered. "I don't believe at all that there are men in the moon. Look how much the face of nature changes between here and China: other faces, other shapes, other customs, and almost other principles of reasoning. Between here and the moon the change must be far greater. When you travel toward certain newly discovered lands, the inhabitants you find there are hardly men. They're animals with human shape, sometimes rather imperfect at that, and almost without any human reason. If someone could make it as far as the moon, it certainly wouldn't be men he'd find there."

"What sort of people would they be, then?" the Marquise asked impatiently.

"Honestly, Madame," I replied, "I have no idea. Suppose it were possible for us to have reason without being men, and suppose we lived on the moon. Would we ever imagine there was down here that strange kind of creature called the human race? Could we picture anything with such foolish passions and such wise thoughts; a life so short and views so long; so much knowledge of things that hardly matter and so much ignorance of the most important; such ardor for freedom and such a leaning toward servitude; such a strong desire to be happy and such a great inability to be? The people of the moon would have to be very clever indeed to guess all that. We see ourselves constantly, and we still haven't guessed how we're put together. People have been reduced to saying that the gods were drunk on nectar when they made men, and that when they came to look at their work soberly, they couldn't help laughing at it."

"So we're quite safe as far as the people of the moon are concerned," said the Marquise. "They won't figure us out. But I wish we could figure them out, because it truly is unsettling to know they're up there, in that moon we can see, and not be able to picture what they're like."

"And why," I answered, "aren't you uneasy about the inhabitants of that great southern land that's still entirely unknown to us? They and we are carried on the same ship, of which they occupy the bow and we the stern. You can see that from stern to bow there's no communication, and that at one end of the ship nobody knows what sort of people are at the other, or what they're doing there. And you'd like to know what goes on in the moon, in that other ship floating far from us through the heavens!"

"Oh!" she replied. "I count the inhabitants of the southern land as known, because they surely must be a lot like us, and in the end we'll get to know them whenever we take the trouble to go and see them. They'll always be there and won't escape us. But those people of the moon, we'll never know them. It's hopeless."

"If I answered you seriously," I said, "that you can't tell what will happen, you'd laugh at me, and no doubt I'd deserve it. Still, I could defend myself fairly well if I wanted to. I have a perfectly ridiculous thought that has an air of plausibility that surprises me; I don't know where it got it from, being as impertinent as it is. I bet I can bring you to admit, against all reason, that there may someday be traffic between the earth and the moon.

"Call to mind the state America was in before it was discovered by Christopher Columbus. Its inhabitants lived in extreme ignorance. Far from knowing the sciences, they didn't even know the simplest and most necessary arts. They went naked, they had no weapons but the bow, they had never conceived that men could be carried by animals. They regarded the sea as a great space forbidden to men, which joined the sky, and beyond which there was nothing. It's true that after spending whole years hollowing out the trunk of a big tree with sharp stones, they would set out on the sea in that trunk and go along the coast, carried by wind and waves. But since this vessel was liable to capsize often, they had to swim at once to catch hold of it again, and, properly speaking, they were always swimming, except for the times when they rested in it. If someone had told them that there was an incomparably more perfect kind of navigation, that you could cross that infinite stretch of water in whatever direction and on whatever course you wanted, that you could stop there without moving in the midst of the stirring waves, that you were master of the speed at which you went, that in short this sea, however vast it was, was no obstacle to communication between peoples, provided only that there were peoples on the other side, you can be sure they'd never have believed it.

"And yet one fine day the strangest and least expected spectacle in the world presents itself to them. Great enormous bodies that seem to have white wings, that fly over the sea, that spew fire on every side, and that come to throw upon the shore unknown men, all covered in iron scales, commanding as they like monsters that run beneath them, and holding in their hands thunderbolts with which they strike down whatever resists them. Where did they come from? Who could have brought them over the seas? Who put fire at their disposal? Are they children of the sun? Because surely they're not men. I don't know, Madame, whether you share the Americans' astonishment as I do, but there can never have been a surprise like it anywhere in the world. After that I won't swear anymore that there can't be traffic some day between the moon and the earth. Would the Americans have believed that there ought to be any between America and Europe, which they didn't even know existed? It's true we'd have to cross that great space of air and sky between the earth and the moon. But did those great seas seem to the Americans any more suited to being crossed?"

"Really," said the Marquise, looking at me, "you're mad."

"Who says otherwise?" I answered.

"But I want to prove it to you," she went on. "I'm not content with your admission. The Americans were so ignorant that they couldn't possibly suspect that roads could be made across seas that vast. But we, who have so much knowledge, we would certainly imagine it if it were actually possible to travel through the air."

"We do more than imagine it possible," I replied. "We're already beginning to fly a little. Several different people have found the secret of fitting themselves with wings that hold them up in the air, of giving them motion, and of getting across rivers. To be sure, it wasn't the flight of an eagle, and these new birds have sometimes paid for it with an arm or a leg, but all the same that represents only the first planks put on the water, which were the beginning of navigation. From those planks it was a long way to big ships that could sail around the world. And yet, little by little, the big ships came. The art of flying is only just being born. It will be perfected, and someday people will go as far as the moon. Do we claim to have discovered everything, or to have brought things to a point where nothing can be added? Come now, let's agree that there's still something left to do for the centuries to come."

"I won't agree," she said, "that people will ever fly, except in a way that breaks their necks right away."

"Well, then," I answered, "if you insist that people will always fly so badly here, they'll fly better on the moon. The inhabitants will be better suited to the trade than we are. It makes no difference whether we go there or they come here. And we'd be like the Americans, who couldn't imagine that anyone could sail, though at the other end of the world people sailed very well."

"So the people of the moon would already have come?" she said, almost angrily.

"The Europeans didn't get to America until after six thousand years," I replied, bursting out laughing. "That's how long it took them to perfect navigation to the point of being able to cross the ocean. The people of the moon perhaps already know how to make short trips in the air. Right now they're practicing. When they're more skilled and experienced, we'll see them, and heaven knows what a surprise that will be."

"You're insufferable," she said, "pushing me to the limit with an argument as hollow as that."

"If you make me angry," I said, "I know what else I'll add to strengthen it. Notice that the world unfolds little by little. The ancients were quite sure that the torrid zone and the frozen zones couldn't be inhabited, because of the excess of heat or cold. And in the time of the Romans, the general map of the earth was hardly larger than the map of their empire, which had a certain grandeur in one sense and showed a great deal of ignorance in another. Yet people were found, both in very hot countries and in very cold ones. There, the world was already enlarged. Then people decided that the ocean covered all the earth except what was known at the time, and that there were no antipodes, because nobody had ever heard of any, and besides, would they have their feet up and their heads down? After that fine piece of reasoning, they discovered the antipodes. A new revision of the map, a new half of the earth. You take my meaning, Madame. Those antipodes, found against all expectation, should teach us to be cautious in our judgments. The world may finish unfolding for us, and one day we'll know even the moon. We're not there yet, because the whole earth isn't discovered, and apparently all this has to happen in order. When we've come to know our own home well, we'll be allowed to get to know that of our neighbors, the people of the moon."

"Truly," said the Marquise, looking at me closely, "you're so deep on this subject that it can't be that you don't believe in earnest what you're saying."

"I'd be very sorry if I did," I answered. "I only want to show you that a chimerical opinion can be defended well enough to embarrass a person of wit, but not well enough to persuade her. Only the truth persuades, even without needing to appear with all its proofs. It enters the mind so naturally that when you learn it for the first time, it seems you're only remembering it."

"Ah, you relieve me!" said the Marquise. "Your false reasoning was troubling me, and I feel more fit to go to bed peacefully, if you'll agree that we retire."
