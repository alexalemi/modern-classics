FONTENELLE, "ENTRETIENS SUR LA PLURALITÉ DES MONDES" -- STANDING BRIEF
Read this whole file, then running_notes.txt, before translating.

WHAT THIS WORK IS
A scholarly modernization of a canonical text in the history of science.
Bernard de Fontenelle (1657-1757), later secretary of the Paris Academy of
Sciences, published this in 1686: over six evenings in a country park, a
witty young philosopher (the narrator) explains Copernican astronomy and
Descartes's vortices to a clever, teasing Marquise, and argues that the
moon and planets are probably inhabited. It was the first great book of
popular science written for general readers, and for a woman. Source: the
1800 Lyon edition of his final text (French Wikisource). Old spellings
("étoit", "Planete") are just spellings. There is an old English version
(Glanvill, Behn); do NOT use or imitate it. You translate from the French.

THE VOICE
Light, gallant, sparkling, never pedantic: the charm IS the science
lesson. The Marquise is quick and sceptical, often wittier than he is; keep
her sharp. Keep the jokes working in English, the flirtation courtly and
light, the metaphors (the opera machinery, the watch, the fixed stars as
the "shore") exact. His science is his (vortices, inhabited planets, 1680s
astronomy): do not correct it or add notes.

THE STANDARD
Faithful but not literal: every sentence, every image and aside, in his
order. Modern, natural English at an eighth-grade-to-high-school reading
level; contractions where natural; no archaisms, no French word order.
Complete: never summarise, never skip. English runs about the same length
as the French. Dialogue is embedded in narration ("répondit-elle"): render
it as natural English dialogue with quotation marks, splitting a long
French paragraph where the speaker changes, but losing nothing.
