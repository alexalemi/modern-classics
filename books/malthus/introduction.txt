This came out anonymously in London in 1798. Its author was Thomas Robert Malthus, an English clergyman of thirty-two, and by his own account in the preface the book began as a conversation with a friend about an essay of William Godwin's on avarice and extravagance. He sat down to set his side of it out on paper, found he had an argument he had not seen made before, and published.

The argument is arithmetical and takes one sentence. Population, left unchecked, grows by multiplication, doubling itself every twenty-five years or so; the food supply can only be made to grow by addition; so the two cannot both keep going, and population is always and everywhere held down to the level of the food by something. That something is what the rest of the book is about. Malthus sorts the checks into two kinds: preventive, which mostly means people marrying late or not at all, and positive, which means hunger, disease, bad housing, overwork and war. He believes the pressure operates now, in England, and not only in some future crisis.

It is a polemic with named opponents, which is the thing most often forgotten about it. Godwin's Political Justice of 1793 and Condorcet's Sketch for a Historical Picture of the Progress of the Human Mind, written while its author was in hiding during the Terror and published after his death, both argued that human institutions were the cause of human misery and that both could be got rid of. Half of this book is a chapter-by-chapter reply to those two men. Malthus is elaborately polite to them, praises their talent, and then takes their systems apart. He also turns on England's Poor Laws, argues that relief raises the price of food without adding to the supply and so spreads the suffering wider, and calls for the parish laws to be abolished outright.

What is here is the first edition, the short argumentative one. In 1803 Malthus put his name to a second edition three or four times the length, full of statistics gathered on his travels, and added a third check — moral restraint, meaning chastity before a late marriage — which softens the conclusion considerably. He went on to become a professor of political economy at the East India Company's college in Hertfordshire, and his name became an adjective.

Most of what the word now carries was put there by other people. Both Darwin and Wallace said that reading Malthus was what gave them natural selection. Henry George devoted an entire Book of Progress and Poverty to disproving him. Darwin's Origin of Species, George's book, and Adam Smith's Wealth of Nations are all in this collection.
