Proposition III.9 (Theorem)

[Figure aeh: The diagram for Proposition III.9, in Byrne’s colours.]

If *a point be taken within a circle ⟦g:0339⟧, from which more than two equal straight lines (⟦g:033a⟧, ⟦g:0045⟧, ⟦g:00ed⟧) can be drawn to the circumference, that point must be the centre of the circle.*

For if it be supposed that the point ⟦g:033b⟧ in which more than two equal straight lines meet is not the centre, some other point ⟦g:033c⟧ must be; join these two points by ⟦g:0026⟧ and produce it both ways to the circumference.

Then since more than two equal straight lines are drawn from a point which is not the centre, to the circumference, two of them at least must lie at the same side of the diameter ⟦g:033d⟧; and since from a point ⟦g:033e⟧, which is not the centre, straight lines are drawn to the circumference; the greatest is ⟦g:033f⟧, which passes through the centre: and ⟦g:00ed⟧ which is nearer to ⟦g:033f⟧, > ⟦g:0045⟧ which is more remote (prop. III.8); but ⟦g:00ed⟧ = ⟦g:0045⟧ (hyp.) which is absurd.

The same may be demonstrated of any other point, different from ⟦g:033e⟧, which must be the centre of the circle.

Q. E. D.
