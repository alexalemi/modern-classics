Oliver Byrne (1810–1880) was an Irish civil engineer and a writer of mathematics textbooks, and on the title page of this book he calls himself Surveyor of Her Majesty's Settlements in the Falkland Islands. In 1847 the London publisher William Pickering brought out his edition of the first six books of Euclid's Elements, the geometry every schoolboy was made to learn, with nearly every letter in it replaced by colour.

Euclid names his figures by letters: the angle BAC, the triangle ABC, the line AB. A reader has to hold those letters in mind, look across at the diagram, find them, and come back. Byrne thought the letters were the difficulty. In his book the diagram is drawn in red, yellow, blue and black, and the proof is written with small pictures of its parts set into the sentences: to prove that two lines are equal, he puts the red line, an equals sign and the blue line on the page, and the reader sees them. His introduction claims that Euclid can be learned this way in less than a third of the usual time. The printing, in several colours on nearly every page, was difficult and expensive, and the book did not sell. It is now better known as a piece of design than as a textbook.

The book follows Euclid closely: Book I on triangles and parallels, ending with Pythagoras' theorem and its converse; Book II on areas; Books III and IV on circles; Book V on proportion, where Byrne draws his magnitudes as rows of coloured tokens; and Book VI on similar figures. The small references in brackets, such as "(post. I.3)" or "(prop. I.1)", are to the postulate, axiom, definition or proposition that justifies each step. "Q. E. D." marks the end of a proof.

This edition is built on Sergey Slyusarev's recreation of the book in LaTeX and MetaPost, which redraws every diagram in Byrne's colours. Byrne's 1847 text is in the public domain, but the recreation is licensed CC BY-SA 4.0, and so is this edition. The recreation was typeset and its output read directly: the words exactly as it prints them, and each of its 6,115 pictures kept as a vector image in its place in the sentence. Each small picture is described for readers who cannot see it by its colour and the letters Euclid would have used, so that "the red line" is also "line BC". The recreation's table of contents, which lists pages, is left out.
