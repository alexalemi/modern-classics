Bertrand Russell (1872–1970) was by 1930 the best-known philosopher in Britain. With Alfred North Whitehead he had written Principia Mathematica, the attempt to derive mathematics from logic. He had been jailed in 1918 for writing against the war. And he had become, in his fifties, a writer of popular books on marriage, education and politics. In 1927 he and his second wife, Dora, had opened an experimental school, Beacon Hill, and his popular books helped to pay for it. The Conquest of Happiness was published in London by George Allen & Unwin in October 1930, and it mentions a visitor to "my school".

The Preface says what the book is for: it is "not addressed to the learned", and it offers "recipes" for happiness that Russell had tested on himself. It is in two Parts. The first takes the causes of unhappiness one at a time: competition, boredom, fatigue, envy, the sense of sin, self-pity, and fear of what the neighbours think. The second takes the causes of happiness: zest, affection, the family, work, interests outside oneself, and the balance between effort and resignation. The argument running through all of it is that most unhappiness comes from being absorbed in oneself, and that the cure is to turn one's attention outward, to things and people worth caring about.

A few things will read oddly. Russell writes as a man of 1930, and most of his examples come from the professional classes of England and America: the business man and his commute and the household that can no longer find servants. The psychology he draws on is the psycho-analysis of the day. Chapter Eight, "Persecution Mania", begins with the insanity of that name and then sets it aside for a psychiatrist; its subject is the milder, everyday habit of believing oneself ill-used. He is also opinionated and often very funny.

This is a restored edition, and Russell's words are unchanged. The text is the Project Gutenberg transcription of the London first edition, compared word by word with two scans of 1930 London printings; they agree with it throughout. The Whitman epigraph is kept, Russell's four footnotes are set after the paragraphs that cite them, and the index is omitted, since its page numbers mean nothing in a reflowable book. The edition adds modern typesetting, an ebook and this introduction.

The book begins its argument with a short piece of autobiography, and it is worth reading slowly. Russell says that he was not born happy, that as a teenager he was kept from suicide only by the wish to know more mathematics, and that he enjoyed life more with every year.
