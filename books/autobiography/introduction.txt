Benjamin Franklin wrote this book in four sittings spread over eighteen years, in two countries, and never finished it. He started in 1771, at sixty-five, while staying at the Bishop of St Asaph's house at Twyford in England, and he started it as a letter to his son William. He picked it up again thirteen years later at Passy, outside Paris, where he was the American minister to France; two friends had written begging him to go on, and both of their letters are printed in the text where he put them. The last two stints were written in Philadelphia, in 1788 and in the winter before his death in 1790. The complete text, from his own manuscript, was not printed until 1868.

Because he never finished it, the story stops around 1760. There is no Declaration of Independence in it, no Constitutional Convention, no peace treaty. A reader who comes for the statesman gets the printer instead: a chandler's son who runs away from his brother's Boston print shop at seventeen, walks into Philadelphia with three great puffy rolls under his arms, and spends the rest of the book setting type, buying out partners, and starting things — a newspaper, an almanac, a subscription library, a fire company, a militia, a hospital, an academy, a stove, and the kite. The last chapters are colonial politics, a long quarrel with the Penn family over whether their estates could be taxed.

The part everyone remembers is Chapter Nine. Franklin lists thirteen virtues, gives each a one-line precept, and rules a little book with seven columns and thirteen rows so he can mark a black spot for every fault committed that day. He works on one virtue a week, thirteen weeks to a cycle, four cycles a year. He reports how it went. Order defeated him, and the story he tells about that — a man buying an ax who decides he likes a speckled ax best — is the best thing in the chapter. Humility was added twelfth because a Quaker friend told him he was widely thought proud.

The voice is the reason to read it. He brags, then undercuts the brag; he calls his own mistakes errata, which is a printer's word for the misprints listed at the front of a book, and he uses it every time. On slavery the book says almost nothing. Franklin owned enslaved people during the years it covers and does not mention the fact, and the subject enters once, in an exchange of jokes over wine that this edition keeps as he wrote it. His public work against the slave trade came decades later, after the point where the narrative breaks off.
