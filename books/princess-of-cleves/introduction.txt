The Princess of Cleves was published in Paris in March 1678 with no author's name on it. Everyone soon knew it was by Marie-Madeleine de La Fayette (1634–1693), a countess who lived in Paris, apart from her husband, at the centre of a circle of writers that included the Duke of La Rochefoucauld, author of the Maxims, and her old friend Madame de Sévigné. She never acknowledged the book. Women of her rank did not publish novels under their own names.

She set it a hundred and twenty years in the past, at the court of King Henri II in 1558 and 1559. Almost every name in it is real: the King and his mistress Diane de Poitiers, Queen Catherine de' Medici, the young Mary Stuart, Queen of Scots, married to the heir to the throne and called the Queen Dauphine, the Guise brothers, the Constable de Montmorency, and the Duke of Nemours, the handsomest man at court. Only the heroine and her family are invented. The first pages are crowded with this cast, and the novel stops several times so that a character can tell a story about the court's past. These stories are not digressions. Each one is about love, ambition and deceit, and each teaches the heroine something about the world she has married into.

The story is simple. Mademoiselle de Chartres, sixteen and raised by her mother to believe that a woman's only happiness is virtue, marries the Prince of Cleves, who adores her, without loving him. Then she meets the Duke of Nemours. Almost nothing happens outwardly; nearly everything happens inside her, and Lafayette names each feeling as precisely as a doctor naming symptoms. That is why the book is often called the first modern psychological novel. When it came out, the Mercure galant, a Paris magazine, asked its readers to write in and judge one thing the princess does, and the argument has never quite stopped.

The book also contains The Princess of Montpensier, a much shorter novel Lafayette published anonymously in 1662, set during the Wars of Religion of the 1560s. It tells the same kind of story with a different ending, and reads as a first attempt at the later book.

This edition translates both from Lafayette's French, with Thomas Sergeant Perry's 1891 English version consulted for sense only. Her long, balanced sentences are broken into shorter ones, and nothing is added: she rarely describes a face, a room or a dress, and neither does this translation.
