THE RULE FOR NAMES. French personal titles stay French with the name
(Madame de Cleves, Monsieur de Nemours, the Vidame de Chartres, the
Chevalier de Guise, the Marshal de Saint-André, Madame de Valentinois);
great territorial and royal titles take English "of" (the Duke of Guise,
the Duke of Nemours, the Prince of Cleves, the Cardinal of Lorraine, the
King of Navarre, the Prince of Condé, the Duke of Alba, the Duke of Savoy,
the Duke of Ferrara, the Duke of Nevers, the Duchess of Valentinois).
French royals in French (Henri II, François I, François the Dauphin,
Charles IX); English and Spanish royals in English (Henry VIII, Anne
Boleyn, Catherine of Aragon, Cardinal Wolsey, Mary Tudor, Queen
Elizabeth, Philip II, the Emperor Charles V). "Cleves" never takes an
accent; "Elisabeth" never takes one either; "Mercoeur" is spelled oe.

TWO ELISABETHS, THREE MARGUERITES, TWO MARYS -- keep them apart:
  Élisabeth (Henri II's daughter, married by proxy to Philip II of Spain
    through the Duke of Alba) = "Madame Elisabeth", "Elisabeth of France";
    after the wedding "the Queen of Spain".
  Élisabeth of England (001, 004: Nemours's marriage project) = "Queen
    Elizabeth", "Elizabeth of England" (English spelling).
  Marguerite de France, Henri II's sister, who marries the Duke of Savoy
    = "Madame Marguerite" (for "Madame, sœur du roi").
  Marguerite, sister of François I, Duchess of Alençon, later Queen of
    Navarre, author of the Heptameron (005) -- all her titles.
  Marguerite de Valois, Charles IX's sister (Montpensier) = "Madame
    Marguerite".
  Mary Stuart = "the Queen Dauphine". Mary Tudor ("Marie, reine
    d'Angleterre", 001, 004) = "Mary Tudor, Queen of England" / "Queen
    Mary".
  A bare royal "Madame" never survives into English: decide from context
  whether it is Marguerite (the sister; the Savoy match) or Elisabeth (the
  daughter; the Spanish match, the Duke of Alba) -- Perry names her when
  in doubt -- and REPORT the call.

VOCABULARY LOCKS (false friends; full table in text_analysis):
  repos = "peace of mind" (her inner calm); "rest"/"quiet" otherwise.
  inclination = "attraction" / "her feelings for him" -- never bare
    "inclination" (check.py fails it).
  galanterie = love affairs, courtship; "gallantry" only in the first
    sentence (pinned).
  galant (of a person) = a ladies' man / given to love affairs.
  amant = suitor, admirer, the man who loves her; "lover" only for a plain
    liaison.  maîtresse = the woman he loves / is courting; "mistress"
    only for a plain liaison (the King and Diane; Henry and Anne).
  gloire = honor, pride, reputation (not glory).  bienséance = propriety.
  vertu = virtue.  devoir = duty.  honnête homme = gentleman.
  commerce = dealings, company, relations.  aventure = affair, incident,
    story.  trouble = agitation.  embarras = awkward position, dilemma.
  inquiétude = anxiety.  déplaisir, ennui = grief, torment (strong).
  étonnement = shock.  parti = a match / a course / a faction.
  le cercle = the Queen's evening reception.

ADDRESS: "Madame" / "Monsieur" kept in direct address; "Sire" to the
King; "Your Majesty" to queens; "ma fille" = "my daughter", "my child";
"vous" always "you".

VOICES
  Madame de Cleves -- reserved, grave, sincere, self-scrutinising; few
    words in public; lucid and firm when she finally argues (012).
  Monsieur de Cleves -- generous, honorable, deeply in love; eloquent in
    grief; bitter at the end, never a villain.
  Nemours -- witty and dazzling in company (the astrology talk, 005; the
    ball talk, 003); ardent and humble alone with her; quick at a white
    lie for her sake.
  Madame de Chartres -- worldly, lucid, tender, didactic; she teaches by
    telling history.
  the Queen Dauphine -- lively, warm, gossipy, imprudent.
  the Vidame -- a frank, self-aware man of the world, ruefully funny
    about his own entanglements.
  the King -- affable, courteous, fond.  the Queen -- cold, proud, exacting.
  (Montpensier) Guise ardent and ambitious; Chabannes devoted, eloquent,
    self-sacrificing; the Prince of Montpensier jealous and violent;
    Anjou proud and menacing; the Princess vain of being loved, quick,
    imprudent.

REGISTER: restraint and precision. Cut the semicolon chains, never the
logic. No added description, gesture or emotion; no added exclamation
marks; maxims as crisp present-tense sentences; superlatives of the
roll-call kept. Modern, plain, never slangy. Contractions in dialogue
where natural; narration a shade more formal.

FORMAT: line 1 the exact heading; split files "(Part n of k)" on line 3;
quoted dialogue, a paragraph per speaker; long told stories and letters
in the multi-paragraph quote convention (each paragraph opens with ",
only the last closes), speech inside a story in single quotes; no
indented blocks, no short bare lines, no notes, straight quotes only.
Ratio: expect 1.0-1.25 of the French; verify 0.9-1.5; never pad.

000  Barbin's note "Le libraire au lecteur", 1678 spelling (Autheur,
     sçait, &). One paragraph; the publisher speaks ("je"); the author is

001  The roll-call: Henri II and Diane; the Queen; Elisabeth, Mary Stuart,
     Madame Marguerite; the great men one by one (Navarre, Guise, the
     Cardinal, the Chevalier, Condé, Nevers and his son Cleves, the
     Vidame, NEMOURS -- the portrait to make dazzling); the factions; the
     Constable's marriage schemes; the peace talks at Cercamp; Mary
     Tudor's death and the English project (Queen Elizabeth, Lignerolles,
     Randan). Then Mademoiselle de Chartres arrives, her mother's
     education (the famous lesson on the dangers of love affairs and the
     happiness of loving one's husband), the jeweller's shop where Cleves
     first sees her. This file carries the heaviest load of names in the
     book: one identifying phrase each, inside the sentence, and keep the
     sentences moving. Pin: "magnificence and gallantry". Gloss
     "vidame" here.
002  The cabals around the match: the Chevalier de Guise and Cleves both
     in love; the Duke of Nevers forbids; Madame de Chartres aims at the
     Prince Dauphin (the Duke of Montpensier's son -- "prince dauphin" is
     his title, not the King's heir: say so in the sentence), the Vidame
     enlists the Queen Dauphine through d'Anville, and the match is
     blocked; the Queen Dauphine's warning; Nevers dies; Cleves proposes; the marriage at the
     Louvre; Cleves's grief that she does not love him. Nemours returns
     from Brussels; THE BALL (the betrothal of Claude de France and the
     Duke of Lorraine): the King makes them dance without being
     introduced. Madame de Chartres begins her lesson on the court. Pin:
     "what appears is almost never the truth".
003  Opens INSIDE Madame de Chartres's speech: the history of Diane de

004  Opens INSIDE Cleves's speech (the Sancerre / Madame de Tournon /

005  Madame Elisabeth's marriage announced (Madame = Elisabeth here; the

006  The tennis match and Nemours's fall from his horse: she betrays her

008  To Coulommiers. Nemours, at his sister's house nearby, hides in the

012  Nemours resolves to speak; the Vidame arranges it; THE LAST INTERVIEW

013  Montpensier I. Lepetit's three huge paragraphs: re-paragraph freely.

- ORCHESTRATOR READABILITY CORRECTION (read before anything else): 001 is
  accurate but in places it is FRENCH IN ENGLISH WORDS. Do not copy these;
  write what an English writer would say:
    "The Queen's presence gave sanction to hers" -> "The Queen's presence made
      hers respectable"
    "made her find a great sweetness in reigning" -> "made reigning very sweet
      to her"
    "most beautiful and best made, of either sex" -> "the handsomest men and
      most beautiful women"
    "never failed to be found" -> "were always to be found"
    "fill with honor the place of his father" -> "be a worthy successor to his
      father"

  only makes sense because you know the French, rewrite it. Keep Lafayette's
  restraint (no added feeling, gesture or description) — restraint is not
  stiffness. The consistency pass will bring 001 into line with this.

  use contractions. Feelings NAMED, never shown. Portrait superlatives whole.

  Keep a si...que result clause whole.

  double quotes; indirect speech stays indirect.
