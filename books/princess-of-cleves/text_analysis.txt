TEXT ANALYSIS — The Princess of Cleves, with The Princess of Montpensier
Madame de Lafayette (Marie-Madeleine Pioche de La Vergne, comtesse de La
Fayette, 1634-1693). La Princesse de Montpensier (1662) and La Princesse
de Clèves (1678), both published anonymously. TRANSLATED FROM THE FRENCH.

The first novel by a woman in the collection, and the book usually called
the first modern novel in French: a love story told almost entirely from
inside the mind of a sixteen-year-old wife, in a prose so restrained that
its biggest events are a confession, a silence, and a refusal.

THE BOOK

What happens. Autumn 1558 to the winter of 1559-60, the last year of Henri
II. Mademoiselle de Chartres, sixteen, the richest heiress in France,
raised by her widowed mother on the principle that a woman's only
happiness lies in loving her husband, comes to court and is married to the
Prince of Cleves, who adores her; she respects him and does not love him.
At a royal ball she dances, without an introduction, with the Duke of
Nemours, the most accomplished man at court, and the two fall in love at
sight. Neither ever says so for most of the book. Her mother, dying, sees
it and warns her. The rest is a sequence of public scenes in which
private feeling must not show and does: a stolen portrait, a fall from a
horse, a lost love letter that belongs to someone else, the tournament
where the King is killed. To protect herself she does what no wife had
done in a novel before: she tells her husband she loves someone else and
asks him to take her away from court -- and Nemours, hidden, overhears.
The husband dies of grief and suspicion. She is free, Nemours is free,
he proposes, and she refuses him, for her husband's memory and for her
own peace of mind, and withdraws from the world.

The roll-call. The opening ~2,500 words are a parade of the real court of
Henri II: the King, Diane de Poitiers, Catherine de' Medici, Mary Stuart,
the Guises, the Constable, the princes of the blood, each in a sentence
of superlatives. Lafayette's readers knew them all from the memoirs of
Brantôme; ours do not. This is the book's single biggest obstacle, and it
is at the front door. See NAMES below for the remedy: a short identifying
phrase INSIDE the sentence, never a note.

Embedded histories. Four long told stories, each in a character's voice,
each a mirror of the main plot: Madame de Chartres on Diane de Poitiers
and the late King's court (002-003, ~3,000 words, a political history of
mistresses and factions); the Prince of Cleves on Sancerre and Madame de
Tournon, a woman who deceived two men at once (003-004); the Queen Dauphine
on Anne Boleyn (004-005); the Vidame de Chartres on his three-way affair
with the Queen and Madame de Thémines (006-007). They are long speeches in
the French with no quotation marks; in English they become quoted speech
over several paragraphs (FORMAT).

Montpensier (1662) is the sketch for Clèves: the religious wars under
Charles IX, 1567-1572; a young wife, Renée d'Anjou-Mézières, married by
family policy to the Prince of Montpensier while in love with the Duke of
Guise; a loyal friend, the Count of Chabannes, who loves her hopelessly
and carries her letters; a jealous husband; a rival prince (the Duke of
Anjou, the future Henri III). Faster, more plot, and harsher: she yields
(as far as letters and one night visit go), is betrayed, and dies.
Chabannes is killed in the St. Bartholomew's Day Massacre. The last
sentence is a moral. Read side by side, the two show Lafayette learning
to move the drama from events into the mind.

THEMES. Appearances and truth ("what appears is almost never the truth");
the court as a machine of watching; love as a disturbance of the mind
rather than a romance; a woman's "repos" -- her calm, her peace of mind --
set against passion; sincerity, and what it costs; the mother's education
and what it can and cannot protect; marriage as alliance; jealousy as
knowledge that destroys. The novel never states a thesis; it analyses.


STYLE, TONE, AND THE VOICE TO KEEP

Restraint. Lafayette states feelings in abstract nouns (passion,
inclination, trouble, embarras, douleur) and almost never describes a body,
a room, a face, the weather. Her drama is in the precision of the
analysis: what she felt, then what she thought, then why she was wrong.
KEEP THAT. Never add sensory description, inner monologue, gestures or
emotion she does not state. Never add an exclamation mark she does not
have. Never "show" what she "tells": her telling IS the style.

Precision. Each long sentence is a chain of reasoning -- causes, then
consequences, then qualifications: "elle trouvait également impossible que
son mari eût parlé, et qu'il n'eût pas parlé". Modern English cuts the
semicolon chains into sentences, but every logical joint must survive:
because, but, so, nevertheless, all the same, which meant that. Cut the
CHAIN, never the LOGIC. A paragraph of four modern sentences must make
the same argument, in the same order, as the French period.

Maxims. The narrator generalises in the present tense: "Les personnes
galantes sont toujours bien aises qu'un prétexte leur donne lieu de
parler à ceux qui les aiment." These are La Rochefoucauld's form (he was
her closest friend): keep each as one crisp sentence, present tense.

The superlatives of the roll-call ("the most perfect", "incomparable", "a
masterpiece of nature") are a convention of portraiture, not gush; keep
them -- they are also the ironic setup for a court where nothing is what
it seems.

Register. A court of exquisite manners, rendered for a fourteen-year-old
reading aloud. Plain modern words; short-to-medium sentences; no slang, no
"okay", no "guy", no therapy words (trauma, obsessed, toxic, closure). In
dialogue contractions are fine where speech would use them; narration
stays slightly more formal than speech, and never chatty. The
readability test (memory note: readability is the deliverable): every
sentence one a bright fourteen-year-old could read aloud and follow, with
every clause of the French present and nothing summarised.

Dialogue. Lafayette runs speech into narration with no quotation marks
("Vous m'estimez plus que je ne vaux, répliqua madame de Clèves en
soupirant"). Make it standard English dialogue: quotation marks, a new
paragraph for each speaker, tags kept modest ("said", "replied",
"answered", "went on" -- not "hissed", "breathed"). Indirect speech in the
French stays indirect in English.

Pronouns and address: "vous" is always "you" (no thou -- Lafayette's
couples address each other as vous). In direct address keep "Madame" and
"Monsieur" ("Madame, I beg you..."), "Sire" to the King, "Your Majesty"
for queens. "Ma fille" = "my daughter" or "my child".


FALSE FRIENDS AND LAFAYETTE'S MORAL VOCABULARY (the technical-calque
class: memory note technical-calque-justification). Counts are in the
Clèves text. These words look like English and mean something else; a
reader who meets "her inclination for him" or "a gallantry" sails past
having understood less than the French says.

  galanterie (22)  love affairs; the court's business of courtship;
                   "les malheurs d'une galanterie" = "the misery of a love
                   affair". The opening's "la magnificence et la
                   galanterie" is the one place to keep "gallantry" (it is
                   pinned in must_contain) -- the court's whole elegant
                   culture of love.
  galant (31)      of a man: a ladies' man, devoted to love affairs ("Ce
                   prince était galant" = "This king loved women"); "les
                   personnes galantes" = "people in love" / "people with
                   love affairs"; "une femme galante" = a woman of many
                   affairs. Rarely "gallant".
  amant (34)       the man who loves (and usually courts) her: "suitor",
                   "admirer", "the man she loved"; "lover" ONLY where the
                   French makes a liaison plain.
  maîtresse (42)   the woman a man loves and courts, his "lady"; "mistress"
                   ONLY for an acknowledged liaison (the King and Diane,
                   Henry VIII and Anne Boleyn). Nemours "avait plusieurs
                   maîtresses" = he was courting several women. "maîtresse
                   de sa personne et de l'état" (Diane) = "mistress of his
                   person and of the state" -- keep Lafayette's pun.
                   "Je vous aime comme ma maîtresse" (Cleves to his wife,
                   010): "I love you the way a man loves a woman he is
                   courting" -- the point is that marriage has not cooled
                   him.
  passion (122)    "passion" or "love" -- the strong word; keep it strong.
  inclination (32) the pull of feeling toward someone, weaker and earlier
                   than passion: "attraction", "her feelings for him",
                   "the way her heart leaned toward him". NEVER bare
                   "inclination".
  repos (26)       LOCKED: "peace of mind" when it is hers or Cleves's
                   inner calm (the word her final refusal turns on: "les
                   raisons de mon repos"); "rest", "quiet", "calm" for
                   ordinary uses (a night's rest, the peace of Paris).
  devoir (31)      "duty".  vertu (24) "virtue" (the book turns on it --
                   keep the word).  bienséance (13) "propriety", "what was
                   proper", "decorum".
  gloire (7)       honor, pride, reputation -- NOT "glory".
  honnête / honnêteté   decent, honorable / courtesy, decency, good
                   faith; "honnête homme" = a gentleman.
  mérite (22)      worth, merit, distinction.  agrément (9) charm.
  esprit (100)     mind / intelligence / wit, by context.
  aimable          attractive, charming, lovable -- not "amiable".
  bien fait        handsome, well made.
  commerce (11)    dealings, company, relations, correspondence ("le
                   commerce des femmes" = the company of women); never
                   trade.
  intelligence     "avoir une intelligence avec" = a secret understanding.
  aventure (39)    affair, incident, episode, story -- rarely "adventure".
  trouble          agitation, confusion, distress -- not "trouble".
  embarras         awkwardness, a dilemma, an impossible position.
  inquiétude       anxiety, restlessness, unease.
  déplaisir        grief, distress (strong).  ennui (1) torment, misery
                   (the 17th-century word is strong).  chagrin  sorrow,
                   vexation.  douleur  grief, pain.  affliction  grief.
  étonnement / étonné   shock / stunned (strong, not mild surprise).
  parti            a marriage match ("un grand parti" = a great match); a
                   course of action ("prendre un parti" = make a decision);
                   a faction.
  établissement    a settled position; for a girl, a marriage that settles
                   her.
  faire sa cour    pay court, attend at court.  le cercle  the Queen's
                   evening reception ("the circle", glossed once).
  la cour          the court.  le monde  society, "the world".
  filles d'honneur maids of honor.  gentilhomme  a gentleman (in someone's
                   service).  écuyer  equerry, squire.
  les princes du sang   the princes of the blood -- glossed once, in the
                   sentence: the royal family's own branches, next in line
                   to the throne.


NAMES AND TITLES (LOCKED -- the full list, with first-mention epithets,
is in running_notes.txt)

The rule. French personal titles stay French with the person's name
(Madame de Cleves, Monsieur de Nemours, the Vidame de Chartres, the
Chevalier de Guise, the Marshal de Saint-André); great territorial and
royal titles take the English "of" as English history writes them (the
Duke of Guise, the Duke of Nemours, the Cardinal of Lorraine, the King of
Navarre, the Prince of Condé, the Prince of Cleves, the Duke of Alba).
French royals keep French names (Henri II, François I, François the
Dauphin, Charles IX, Elisabeth of France); English and Spanish ones
take English names (Henry VIII, Anne Boleyn, Catherine of Aragon,
Cardinal Wolsey, Mary Tudor, Queen Elizabeth, Philip II, the Emperor
Charles V). No accent on "Cleves" anywhere in English (the title has
none); other accents kept (Élisabeth -> Elisabeth is the one exception,
following the title's simplicity and Perry; Thémines, Saint-André,
Coulommiers keep theirs). "Mercœur" is written "Mercoeur".

The heroine: "Mademoiselle de Chartres" until her marriage, then "Madame
de Cleves" (Lafayette's "cette princesse" = "she", or her name where
"she" is unclear -- NEVER "the princess", which in this court could be
four women).
Her husband: "the Prince of Cleves" (le prince de Clèves), "Monsieur de
Cleves" (M. de Clèves). NEVER the bare surname "Cleves" for him -- it
would blur him into his wife. "her husband" whenever Lafayette's "son
mari" is the sense. check.py fails any "Cleves" not preceded by "de" or
"of".
Nemours: "Monsieur de Nemours" (M. de Nemours, 311 times); "the Duke of
Nemours" (le duc de Nemours). In NARRATION the bare "Nemours" may vary
the rhythm -- sparingly, never in a character's mouth except where
Lafayette drops the title too. Lafayette's "ce prince" for him = "he" or
his name.
Mary Stuart: "the Queen Dauphine" -- for BOTH "la reine dauphine" and
"madame la dauphine" (the court's name for her: Queen of Scots in her
own right, married to the Dauphin). In Part Four, after Henri II's
death, Lafayette sometimes still writes "la reine dauphine": keep "the
Queen Dauphine"; where she is plainly "la reine" (the new queen), write
"the young Queen".
Catherine de' Medici: "the Queen" (la reine); "the Queen Mother" (la
reine mère) after the King's death. Lafayette never names her in Clèves;
the first mention in each file may say "the Queen, Catherine de'
Medici," -- the one name this edition adds, because a modern reader
cannot otherwise tell three queens apart. (Montpensier names her
itself.)
Diane de Poitiers: "Madame de Valentinois" / "the Duchess of
Valentinois" exactly as the French alternates; first mention per file
may add "the King's mistress".
"Madame" as a ROYAL title (capital M, no surname) is a trap: Lafayette
uses it for the King's sister Marguerite de France ("Madame, sœur du roi",
001; she marries the Duke of Savoy) AND for the King's eldest daughter
Élisabeth ("le mariage de Madame" when the Duke of Alba comes to marry
her, 005 and 009; in 008 "chez la reine et chez Madame ... chez madame
Marguerite, sœur du roi" are two different women in one sentence).
Never leave a bare royal "Madame" in English (it reads as a form of
address): write "Madame Marguerite" for the sister, "Madame Elisabeth"
for the daughter, deciding from context -- the Spanish match is
Elisabeth's, the Savoy match Marguerite's; Perry's crib names the woman
when in doubt. Report each call. A third Marguerite, the late King's
sister, Duchess of Alençon and later Queen of Navarre, author of the
Heptameron ("dont vous avez vu les contes", 005), keeps all her titles.
In Montpensier "Madame" is Marguerite de Valois, Charles IX's sister (the
future Queen Margot): "Madame Marguerite" there too.
"Madame Élisabeth" (Henri II's daughter, married by proxy to Philip II)
= "Madame Elisabeth" / "Elisabeth of France"; after the marriage "the
Queen of Spain".
"le roi dauphin" / "M. le dauphin" = "the Dauphin" (François, Mary's
husband; King François II in Part Four: "the young King").

THE VIDAME. "the Vidame de Chartres", "the Vidame" -- Madame de Chartres's
brother, the heroine's uncle. The word is untranslatable and the reader
needs one handhold, folded into the sentence at his first appearance in
001 (no parentheses, no note) -- e.g. that "Vidame" was an old title his
family, a branch of the great house of Vendôme, still bore. Thereafter
"the Vidame", and in other files "the Vidame de Chartres, her uncle" at
first mention.

UNITS AND MONEY: almost none. "lieue" (Coulommiers is "à une demi-lieue";
Champigny) -> miles at 2.5 per lieue, rounded as Lafayette rounds ("half
a league" -> "a mile or so"). Ages and dates stay as written.

DATES AND LAFAYETTE'S HISTORY. The history is hers and stays hers --
including her compressions and her errors (the Verne rule: no silent
corrections, no notes). She is accurate on most of it; where she is not,
the introduction can say so, the translation does not.

SENSITIVE CONTENT -- scholarly modernization of a canonical historical text

Frame: two canonical French novels about the real court of the Valois,
written by a woman of Louis XIV's court. Measured register throughout;
render every judgement as Lafayette's own; never sensationalise, never
bowdlerise, never editorialise.

1. Adultery at court is a fact of the setting: the King and Diane de
   Poitiers, François I and the Duchess of Étampes, Henry VIII and Anne
   Boleyn. Lafayette states it plainly and without salaciousness; so do
   we. Nothing is more explicit in English than in French.
2. A sixteen-year-old married for family policy; the mother's teaching
   that a woman's happiness is in loving her husband; a husband's
   possessiveness; the heroine's sense that a woman's reputation is her
   whole fortune. Render these as the characters' convictions in their
   world, with their full dignity. No modern irony, no feminist gloss,
   no softening. (The introduction, not the text, gives the context.)
3. Death of grief. Cleves dies of a fever brought on by jealousy, and
   tells his wife she is the cause; she half believes it. Keep its full
   weight, no melodrama.
4. Violence: the King's wound at the tournament (009: a splinter of
   Montgomery's lance "lui donna dans l'œil, et y demeura") and his death
   -- as Lafayette tells it, briefly; Saint-Vallier condemned to the
   block and reprieved on the scaffold (003); Anne Boleyn's fall and
   execution (005) -- as told. No added detail.
5. Religion and the Wars of Religion (Montpensier, and the Cardinal of
   Lorraine "defending the Catholic religion, which was beginning to be
   attacked" in 001). Lafayette writes as a Catholic of 1662/1678: Anne
   Boleyn learned "les teintures de la religion nouvelle" and Henry VIII
   dragged England into "le malheureux changement" (005) -- "the new
   religion", "that unhappy change": her view, in her words, no
   correction. Montpensier ends with the St. Bartholomew's Day Massacre
   (1572; "la Saint-Barthelemi" -> "St. Bartholomew's Day"), which she
   calls "cet horrible dessein": keep "horrible", keep "to wipe out the
   Huguenots" (exterminer) at full strength -- her own condemnation; give
   "Huguenots" the handhold "the French Protestants" once. Chabannes, a
   Huguenot turned Catholic, is murdered in it by the people hiding him:
   as written.
6. The Duke of Anjou's threats, the Prince of Montpensier's rage, the
   night visit at Champigny: as written; nothing added.
7. "Maures" (the ballet entrée of Moors, Montpensier 014): "a Moorish
   entrée", "costumed as Moors" -- a court ballet costume, kept.
No slurs, no dialect, no racial content beyond (7) has been found. Any
call an agent makes goes in its consistency notes.


FORMAT

- Line 1: the exact heading from the manifest ("Part One"; "The Princess
  of Montpensier"; "The Publisher to the Reader").
- Split files (every Part, and Montpensier): line 1 heading, blank, then
  "(Part n of k)", blank, then the text. EVERY part repeats the heading
  (assemble drops each file's first line; a part that opened on prose
  would lose a sentence). Parts 2+ never re-introduce the Part; non-final
  parts never conclude it.
- No "Part ...:" divider lines, no "The Princess of Cleves" line (the
  manifest carries the divider), no notes, no brackets, no translator's
  asides.
- Paragraphs: re-paragraph freely for modern reading -- Lepetit's run to
  770 words, Montpensier is three paragraphs for 11,000 words. A new
  paragraph for each speaker; long speeches may run over several
  paragraphs. Keep the ORDER of everything.
- Long told stories and long speeches: standard English convention --
  each paragraph of the speech opens with " and only the last closes.
  Speech inside a told story takes single quotes ('...').
- Letters: the lost letter (006, 794 words, in « » in the French) and
  any other letter read out: introduced in narration, then ordinary
  quoted paragraphs (multi-paragraph convention). No indented block (the
  page would set a long letter as preformatted text), and no bare short
  line (assemble.py turns a short unpunctuated line into a heading;
  check.py --modern fails it).
- Straight quotes and apostrophes only (" and '), never curly; no « ».
- Italics (_underscores_) only for a book title or an emphasised word the
  French marks; Lafayette marks almost none.
