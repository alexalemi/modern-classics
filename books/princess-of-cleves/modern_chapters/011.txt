Part Four

(Part 2 of 3)

He found it impossible to leave without seeing Madame de Cleves. So he went to his sister, Madame de Mercoeur, who was then staying at the house she had near Coulommiers. She was extremely surprised by her brother's arrival. He invented a reason for his journey plausible enough to deceive her, and in the end he managed his design so skillfully that she proposed, of her own accord, that they go to see Madame de Cleves. The visit was made that very day, and Monsieur de Nemours told his sister that he would leave her at Coulommiers and ride back in haste to the King. He had planned to part from her at Coulommiers so that she would be the first to leave; in this way, he thought, he had found a sure means of speaking to Madame de Cleves.

When they arrived she was walking in a broad avenue that borders the flower garden. The sight of Monsieur de Nemours disturbed her more than a little, and it left her no doubt that it was he whom she had seen the night before. That certainty stirred some anger in her, at the boldness and imprudence she saw in what he had done. He noticed a coldness in her face that pained him deeply. The conversation was about nothing in particular, and yet he found the art of showing so much wit in it, so much courtesy, and so much admiration for Madame de Cleves, that he dispelled, in spite of her, part of the coldness she had felt at first.

Once he felt reassured on his first fear, he showed an extreme curiosity to go and see the pavilion in the forest. He spoke of it as the most delightful place in the world, and even described it in such detail that Madame de Mercoeur said he must have been there several times to know all its beauties so well.

"I do not think, though," replied Madame de Cleves, "that Monsieur de Nemours has ever been inside it. It was only finished a short while ago."

"It is not long since I was there either," replied Monsieur de Nemours, looking at her, "and I don't know whether I should be glad that you have forgotten seeing me there."

Madame de Mercoeur, who was admiring the beauty of the gardens, paid no attention to what her brother was saying. Madame de Cleves blushed, and, lowering her eyes without looking at Monsieur de Nemours, she said:

"I do not remember seeing you there, and if you have been there, it was without my knowing it."

"It is true, Madame," replied Monsieur de Nemours, "that I went there without your leave, and I spent there the sweetest and the cruelest moments of my life."

Madame de Cleves understood only too well everything he was saying, but she made no answer. She was thinking how to keep Madame de Mercoeur out of the inner room of the pavilion, because the portrait of Monsieur de Nemours was there and she did not want her to see it. She managed it so well that the time slipped by, and Madame de Mercoeur spoke of going home. But when Madame de Cleves saw that Monsieur de Nemours and his sister were not leaving together, she saw plainly what she was about to be exposed to. She found herself in the same awkward position she had been in at Paris, and she took the same course. The fear that this visit might be one more confirmation of her husband's suspicions did a great deal to decide her; and to prevent Monsieur de Nemours from being left alone with her, she told Madame de Mercoeur that she would go with her as far as the edge of the forest, and ordered her carriage to follow.

The grief Monsieur de Nemours felt at finding Madame de Cleves as unbending as ever was so violent that he turned pale on the spot. Madame de Mercoeur asked him if he was ill, but he looked at Madame de Cleves, without anyone noticing, and let her read in his eyes that his only illness was despair. All the same, he had to let them go without daring to follow, and after what he had said he could not go back with his sister. So he returned to Paris, and left it the next day.

Monsieur de Cleves's gentleman had watched him the whole time. He too went back to Paris, and when he saw that Monsieur de Nemours had set out for Chambord, he took the post road so as to arrive before him and give an account of his journey. His master was waiting for his return as for the news that would decide the misery of his whole life.

As soon as he saw him, he judged from his face and his silence that the man had nothing but painful things to tell him. For some time he sat gripped by grief, his head bowed, unable to speak. At last he motioned with his hand for him to go.

"Go," he said. "I see what you have to tell me, but I have not the strength to hear it."

"I have nothing to tell you," the gentleman answered, "on which any certain judgment can be made. It is true that Monsieur de Nemours went into the garden in the forest two nights running, and that the day after he was at Coulommiers with Madame de Mercoeur."

"That is enough," replied Monsieur de Cleves, "that is enough," and he motioned him again to withdraw. "I have no need of any fuller account."

The gentleman was forced to leave his master abandoned to his despair. There has perhaps never been a more violent one. Few men of such high courage and such a passionate heart as Monsieur de Cleves have felt, at one and the same time, the grief that a lover feels at the infidelity of the woman he loves and the shame that a husband feels at being deceived by his wife.

Monsieur de Cleves could not withstand the blow. That same night a fever took hold of him, with such severe symptoms that from that moment his illness looked very dangerous. Word was sent to Madame de Cleves, and she came in haste. When she arrived he was worse still, and she found something so cold and so frozen in his manner toward her that she was extremely surprised and distressed. It even seemed to her that he accepted the care she gave him with reluctance; but in the end she thought this was perhaps an effect of his illness.

As soon as she was at Blois, where the court then was, Monsieur de Nemours could not help feeling joy at knowing she was in the same place as he. He tried to see her, and went every day to Monsieur de Cleves's lodging on the pretext of asking after him; but it was no use. She never left her husband's room, and the state she saw him in gave her violent grief. Monsieur de Nemours was in despair that she should be so stricken. He could easily judge how much this grief revived her affection for Monsieur de Cleves, and how dangerously that affection drew her away from the passion she carried in her heart. For a while this thought gave him a deadly sorrow; but the extremity of Monsieur de Cleves's illness opened new hopes to him. He saw that Madame de Cleves might perhaps be free to follow her heart, and that he might find in the future a future of lasting happiness and pleasure. He could not bear the thought, so much agitation and rapture did it give him, and he turned his mind away from it for fear of finding himself too wretched if he came to lose his hopes.

Meanwhile the doctors had almost given Monsieur de Cleves up. On one of the last days of his illness, after a very bad night, he said toward morning that he wanted to rest. Madame de Cleves stayed alone in his room. It seemed to her that instead of resting he was very restless. She went to him and knelt down beside his bed, her face covered with tears. Monsieur de Cleves had resolved not to show her the violent bitterness he felt against her. But the care she took of him, and her grief, which sometimes seemed to him real and which at other times he took for marks of pretense and treachery, gave him feelings so contradictory and so painful that he could not keep them inside him.

"You are shedding many tears, Madame," he said, "for a death that you are causing, and that cannot give you the grief you show. I am no longer in a condition to reproach you," he went on, in a voice weakened by illness and sorrow, "but I am dying of the cruel grief you have given me. Did an act as extraordinary as the one you performed in speaking to me at Coulommiers have to come to so little? Why open my eyes to your passion for Monsieur de Nemours, if your virtue had no more strength than that to resist it? I loved you so much that I was glad to be deceived; I confess it to my shame. I have missed that false peace of mind you took from me. Why did you not leave me in the untroubled blindness that so many husbands enjoy? I might perhaps have gone my whole life without knowing that you loved Monsieur de Nemours.

"I shall die," he added, "but know that you make death welcome to me, and that after taking from me the esteem and the tenderness I had for you, you have made life a horror. What would I do with life? Spend it with a woman I have loved so much and by whom I have been so cruelly deceived? Or live apart from that same woman, and come to a public scandal and to violence so contrary to my nature and to the passion I had for you? That passion went beyond anything you saw of it, Madame. I hid the greater part of it from you, for fear of troubling you, or of losing something of your esteem by behaving in ways that did not suit a husband. In short, I deserved your heart. I say it once more: I die without regret, since I could not have it, and can no longer wish for it.

"Farewell, Madame. One day you will miss a man who loved you with a true and lawful passion. You will feel the sorrow that reasonable people find in these entanglements, and you will know the difference between being loved as I loved you and being loved by men who, in professing their love for you, are looking only for the honor of seducing you. But my death will leave you free," he added, "and you will be able to make Monsieur de Nemours happy without its costing you any crime. What does it matter," he went on, "what happens when I am gone? And must I be weak enough to look at it?"

Madame de Cleves was so far from imagining that her husband could have any suspicion of her that she listened to all these words without understanding them. The only idea she had was that he was reproaching her for her feelings for Monsieur de Nemours. At last, coming out of her blindness all at once, she cried:

"I, crimes? The very thought of them is unknown to me. The strictest virtue could inspire no other conduct than mine has been, and I have never done a single thing that I would not have wished you to witness."

"Would you have wished," replied Monsieur de Cleves, looking at her with scorn, "that I had witnessed the nights you spent with Monsieur de Nemours? Ah, Madame, is it you I am speaking of, when I speak of a woman who has spent nights with a man?"

"No, Monsieur," she replied. "No, it is not me you are speaking of. I have never spent any nights, or any moments, with Monsieur de Nemours. He has never seen me in private; I have never allowed it, never listened to him, and I would swear any oath..."

"Say no more," Monsieur de Cleves interrupted. "False oaths or a confession would perhaps give me equal pain."

Madame de Cleves could not answer; her tears and her grief took away her power of speech. At last, with an effort, she said:

"Look at me, at least. Listen to me. If only my own interest were at stake, I would bear these reproaches; but your life is at stake. Listen to me for your own sake. It is impossible that with so much truth on my side I should fail to persuade you of my innocence."

"Would to God you could persuade me of it," he cried. "But what can you tell me? Was Monsieur de Nemours not at Coulommiers with his sister? And had he not spent the two nights before that with you in the garden in the forest?"

"If that is my crime," she replied, "it is easy for me to clear myself. I do not ask you to believe me; but believe all your servants. Ask them whether I went to the garden in the forest the evening before Monsieur de Nemours came to Coulommiers, and whether I did not leave it the evening before that two hours earlier than I usually did."

She then told him how she had thought she saw someone in the garden, and she admitted that she had believed it was Monsieur de Nemours. She spoke with such assurance, and the truth is so easily believed even when it is not probable, that Monsieur de Cleves was almost convinced of her innocence.

"I do not know," he said, "whether I ought to let myself believe you. I feel so close to death that I do not want to see anything that might make me regret leaving life. You have opened my eyes too late; but it will always be a comfort to me to carry away the thought that you are worthy of the esteem I had for you. I beg you to let me have the further consolation of believing that my memory will be dear to you, and that, if it had depended on you, you would have had for me the feelings you have for another."

He wanted to go on, but a faintness took his speech away. Madame de Cleves sent for the doctors, who found him almost without life. He lingered a few days more nonetheless, and died at last with admirable steadiness.

Madame de Cleves was left in a grief so violent that she nearly lost the use of her reason. The young Queen came to see her, with great kindness, and took her to a convent without her knowing where she was being taken. Her sisters-in-law brought her back to Paris before she was yet in a condition to feel her sorrow distinctly. When she began to have the strength to face it, and saw what a husband she had lost; when she considered that she was the cause of his death, and that it was through the passion she had felt for another man that she was the cause of it, the horror she felt for herself and for Monsieur de Nemours cannot be described.

In those first days Nemours did not dare to pay her any attentions beyond what propriety required. He knew Madame de Cleves well enough to believe that anything more pressing would be unwelcome to her. But what he learned next showed him clearly that he would have to keep to the same conduct for a long time.

One of his equerries told him that Monsieur de Cleves's gentleman, who was his close friend, had said to him, in his grief at the loss of his master, that Monsieur de Nemours's journey to Coulommiers was the cause of his death. Monsieur de Nemours was extremely surprised by this account; but after thinking it over he guessed part of the truth, and he could well judge what Madame de Cleves's feelings would be at first, and how she would turn away from him, if she believed that her husband's illness had been caused by jealousy. He decided that he must not even remind her of his name so soon, and he kept to this course, painful as he found it.

He made a journey to Paris, and still could not keep himself from going to her door to ask for news of her. He was told that no one saw her, and that she had even forbidden her people to tell her who had come asking for her. Perhaps these strict orders were given with Nemours in mind, so that she would never hear him spoken of. But Monsieur de Nemours was too much in love to be able to live so completely deprived of the sight of Madame de Cleves. He resolved to find some means, however difficult, of escaping from a state that seemed to him unbearable.

Her grief went beyond the bounds of reason. Her husband dying, and dying because of her, and with such tenderness for her, never left her mind. She went over and over everything she owed him, and she blamed herself as if it were a crime that she had never felt passion for him, as if that had been something in her power. Her only consolation was to think that she mourned him as much as he deserved to be mourned, and that for the rest of her life she would do only what he would have been glad to see her do, had he lived.

She had wondered several times how he had known that Monsieur de Nemours had come to Coulommiers. She did not suspect Nemours of having told it, and it even seemed to her a matter of indifference whether he had repeated it, so fully did she believe herself cured of the passion she had felt for him, and far removed from it. All the same, she felt a sharp pain at the thought that he was the cause of her husband's death, and she remembered with distress the fear Monsieur de Cleves had shown, as he lay dying, that she would marry him. But all these griefs were lost in the grief of losing her husband, and she believed she had no other.

After several months had passed she came out of that violent grief and passed into a state of sadness and languor. Madame de Martigues made a journey to Paris and visited her faithfully for as long as she stayed there. She talked to her about the court and everything that was happening in it; and though Madame de Cleves did not seem to take any interest, Madame de Martigues went on telling her about it all the same, to distract her.

She gave her news of the Vidame, of Monsieur de Guise, and of all the others who stood out for their person or their worth.

"As for Monsieur de Nemours," she said, "I don't know whether affairs of state have taken the place of love affairs in his heart, but he is far less cheerful than he used to be. He seems to have withdrawn almost entirely from the company of women. He often makes journeys to Paris, and I believe he is there even now."

The name of Monsieur de Nemours surprised Madame de Cleves and made her blush. She changed the subject, and Madame de Martigues did not notice her agitation.

The next day, looking for occupations that suited the state she was in, she went to see a man near her house who made silk work of an unusual kind, meaning to have some of the same made for herself. After she had been shown his pieces, she saw the door of a room where she thought there might be more, and asked for it to be opened. The master replied that he did not have the key, and that the room was taken by a man who came there sometimes during the day to draw the fine houses and gardens that could be seen from its windows.

"He is the handsomest man in the world," he added. "He hardly looks like someone reduced to earning his living. Every time he comes here, I see him looking at the houses and the gardens, but I never see him work."

Madame de Cleves listened to this with close attention. What Madame de Martigues had told her, that Monsieur de Nemours was sometimes in Paris, joined in her imagination with this handsome man who came so near her house, and gave her an idea of Monsieur de Nemours, of Monsieur de Nemours bent on seeing her, that threw her into a confused agitation whose cause she did not even know. She went to the windows to see where they looked out. She found that they looked over her whole garden and the front of her apartments; and when she was back in her room, she easily picked out the very window where she had been told this man came. The thought that it was Monsieur de Nemours changed the whole state of her mind. She no longer found herself in that sad calm she had begun to taste; she felt anxious and restless. At last, unable to bear her own company, she went out to take the air in a garden beyond the outskirts of the city, where she expected to be alone. When she arrived she thought she had not been mistaken: she saw no sign that anyone was there, and she walked for a good while.

After crossing a small wood, she saw at the end of an avenue, in the most secluded corner of the garden, a kind of summer-house open on every side, and she walked toward it. As she came near it she saw a man lying on the benches, who seemed buried in deep thought, and she recognized Monsieur de Nemours. The sight stopped her short. But her servants, who were following her, made some noise, and it drew Monsieur de Nemours out of his reverie. Without looking to see who had made the noise he had heard, he rose from his place to avoid the company coming toward him, and turned into another avenue, making a bow so low that it kept him from even seeing the people he was bowing to.

If he had known whom he was avoiding, how eagerly he would have turned back! But he went on along the avenue, and Madame de Cleves watched him go out by a back gate where his carriage was waiting. What an effect that moment's sight had on her heart! What a sleeping passion it rekindled there, and with what violence! She went and sat down in the very place Monsieur de Nemours had just left, and she stayed there as if crushed. He rose up in her thoughts, more lovable than anyone in the world: a man who had loved her for so long with a passion full of respect and faithfulness, who scorned everything for her sake, who respected even her grief, who thought of seeing her without a thought of being seen, who had left the court, where he was the delight of everyone, to come and look at the walls that shut her in, and to come and dream in places where he could not hope to meet her. In short, a man worthy to be loved for his devotion alone, and for whom her own feelings were so violent that she would have loved him even if he had not loved her; and more than that, a man of high rank, and of a rank that matched her own. There was no longer any duty, any virtue, to stand against her feelings. Every obstacle had been removed, and nothing remained of their past condition but Monsieur de Nemours's passion for her and hers for him.

All these ideas were new to her. Her grief at Monsieur de Cleves's death had occupied her enough to keep her from ever looking at them. The presence of Monsieur de Nemours brought them crowding into her mind. But when it was wholly filled with them, and she remembered as well that this same man whom she now thought of as able to marry her was the one she had loved while her husband was alive, and who was the cause of his death; that even as he died, her husband had shown his fear that she would marry him; then her strict virtue was so wounded by the thought that she found hardly less crime in marrying Monsieur de Nemours than she had found in loving him during her husband's life. She gave herself up to these reflections, so contrary to her happiness, and she strengthened them further with several reasons that concerned her peace of mind and the evils she foresaw if she married him. At last, after she had stayed two hours in that place, she went home, convinced that she must flee the sight of him as something wholly opposed to her duty.

But that conviction, which was the work of her reason and her virtue, did not carry her heart with it. Her heart stayed bound to Monsieur de Nemours with a violence that put her in a state deserving of pity, and that left her no peace of mind. She passed one of the cruelest nights she had ever passed. In the morning her first impulse was to go and see whether anyone was at the window that looked into her house. She went, and she saw Monsieur de Nemours there. The sight surprised her, and she drew back so quickly that he judged he had been recognized. He had often wished to be, ever since his passion had made him find these ways of seeing Madame de Cleves; and when he had no hope of that pleasure, he went to dream in the same garden where she had found him.
