Part Two

(Part 3 of 3)

A few days before the Duke of Alba arrived, the King played a game of tennis with Monsieur de Nemours, the Chevalier de Guise, and the Vidame de Chartres. The Queen and the Queen Dauphine went to watch them play, followed by all the ladies, Madame de Cleves among them. When the game was over and everyone was leaving the tennis court, Chastelart came up to the Queen Dauphine and told her that chance had just put into his hands a love letter that had fallen from Monsieur de Nemours's pocket. The Queen Dauphine, who was always curious about anything that concerned Nemours, told Chastelart to give it to her. She took it and followed the Queen, Catherine de' Medici, her mother-in-law, who was going with the King to watch the work on the lists for the tournament.

After they had been there a while, the King had some horses brought out that he had recently acquired. They were not yet broken in, but he wanted to ride them, and he had them given to everyone who had come with him. The King and Monsieur de Nemours found themselves on the two most spirited horses, and the two animals tried to go at each other. Monsieur de Nemours, afraid of hurting the King, pulled back sharply and drove his horse against a pillar of the riding ground so violently that the shock made him reel. People ran to him, and everyone thought he was badly hurt. Madame de Cleves thought him more badly hurt than anyone did. Her concern for him gave her a fear and an agitation that she did not think to hide. She went up to him with the Queen and the Queen Dauphine, and her face was so changed that a man with less at stake than the Chevalier de Guise would have noticed it. He noticed it easily, and he paid far more attention to the state Madame de Cleves was in than to the state of Monsieur de Nemours. The blow Nemours had taken left him so dazed that for some time he stayed with his head bowed over the men who were holding him up. When he raised it, the first person he saw was Madame de Cleves. He read in her face the pity she felt for him, and he looked at her in a way that let her judge how deeply it touched him. Then he thanked the Queen and the Queen Dauphine for the kindness they had shown him and apologized for the state they had seen him in. The King ordered him to go and rest.

Once Madame de Cleves had recovered from her fright, she soon began to reflect on the signs of it she had given. The Chevalier de Guise did not leave her long in the hope that no one had noticed. He gave her his hand to lead her out of the lists.

"I am more to be pitied than Monsieur de Nemours, Madame," he said to her. "Forgive me if I step outside the deep respect I have always had for you, and if I let you see the sharp pain I feel at what I have just witnessed. This is the first time I have ever been bold enough to speak to you, and it will also be the last. Death, or at least a lifelong absence, will take me away from a place where I can no longer live, since I have just lost the sad consolation of believing that every man who dares to look at you is as unhappy as I am."

Madame de Cleves answered only a few disjointed words, as though she had not understood what the Chevalier de Guise's words meant. At another time she would have been offended that he had spoken to her of his feelings for her; but at that moment she felt nothing except grief that he had seen her feelings for Monsieur de Nemours. The Chevalier de Guise was so convinced of them, and so pierced with sorrow, that from that day on he resolved never again to think of being loved by Madame de Cleves. But to give up an enterprise that had seemed to him so difficult and so full of honor, he needed another one great enough to fill his mind. He set his heart on capturing Rhodes from the Turks, an idea he had already toyed with; and when death took him from the world in the flower of his youth, just when he had won the reputation of one of the greatest princes of his age, the only regret he showed at leaving life was that he had not been able to carry out so fine a plan, whose success he believed certain because of all the care he had taken over it.

When she left the lists, Madame de Cleves went to the Queen's apartments, her mind full of what had happened. Monsieur de Nemours came in shortly afterward, magnificently dressed and looking like a man who felt nothing of the accident that had befallen him. He even seemed more cheerful than usual, and his joy at what he believed he had seen gave him an air that made him more attractive still. Everyone was surprised when he came in, and there was no one who did not ask how he was, except Madame de Cleves, who stayed by the fireplace and pretended not to see him. The King came out of a side room where he had been, and seeing Nemours among the others, called him over to talk about his accident. Monsieur de Nemours passed close to Madame de Cleves and said to her very quietly:

"I received marks of your pity today, Madame; but they are not the ones I most deserve."

Madame de Cleves had suspected that he had noticed the feeling she had shown for him, and his words proved that she had not been mistaken. It was a great grief to her to find that she was no longer able to hide her feelings, and to have let the Chevalier de Guise see them. It grieved her greatly, too, that Monsieur de Nemours knew them; but this last grief was not so complete, and it was mixed with a kind of sweetness.

The Queen Dauphine, who was extremely impatient to know what was in the letter Chastelart had given her, came over to Madame de Cleves.

"Go and read this letter," she said. "It is addressed to Monsieur de Nemours, and by all appearances it comes from the woman he loves, the one he has given up all the others for. If you can't read it now, keep it; come to my bedchamber tonight when I retire, give it back to me, and tell me whether you recognize the handwriting."

With these words the Queen Dauphine left Madame de Cleves, who was so stunned and so shaken that for some time she could not move from where she stood. The impatience and agitation she felt would not let her stay in the Queen's apartments. She went home, though it was not the hour at which she usually withdrew. She held the letter in a trembling hand. Her thoughts were so confused that none of them was clear, and she found herself in a kind of unbearable grief that she did not recognize and had never felt before. As soon as she was in her own room she opened the letter, and this is what she found:

"I have loved you too much to let you believe that the change you see in me comes from any fickleness of mine. I want you to know that your infidelity is the cause of it. You are surprised that I speak to you of your infidelity. You hid it from me with such skill, and I took such care to hide from you that I knew of it, that you have reason to be shocked that it is known to me. I am surprised myself that I managed to let nothing of it show. No grief has ever been like mine. I believed that you felt a violent passion for me; I no longer hid from you the passion I felt for you; and at the very time I was letting you see it whole, I learned that you were deceiving me, that you loved another woman, and that by every appearance you were sacrificing me to this new love. I learned it on the day of the riding at the ring; that is why I did not go. I pretended to be ill to hide the disorder of my mind; but I truly became ill, and my body could not bear so violent an agitation.

"When I began to feel better, I went on pretending to be very unwell, so as to have an excuse not to see you and not to write to you. I wanted time to decide how I ought to deal with you. Twenty times I made and unmade the same resolutions; but in the end I found you unworthy to see my grief, and I resolved not to let you see it at all. I wanted to wound your pride by showing you that my passion was fading away of its own accord. I thought that in this way I would lessen the value of the sacrifice you were making of it; I did not want you to have the pleasure of showing how much I loved you in order to seem more lovable for it. I resolved to write you lukewarm, listless letters, so as to put it into the mind of the woman you were giving them to that I had stopped loving you. I did not want her to have the pleasure of learning that I knew she had triumphed over me, nor to add to her triumph by my despair and my reproaches. I thought I would not punish you enough by breaking with you, and that I would give you only a slight pain if I stopped loving you when you no longer loved me. I decided that you had to love me in order to feel the misery of not being loved, which I was feeling so cruelly myself. I believed that if anything could rekindle the feelings you had once had for me, it was to let you see that mine had changed; but to let you see it while pretending to hide it from you, as if I had not had the strength to admit it.

"I settled on this resolution. But how hard it was to take! And when I saw you again, how impossible it seemed to carry out! A hundred times I was ready to burst out in reproaches and tears. The state my health was still in helped me to disguise my agitation and my grief from you. Afterward I was sustained by the pleasure of deceiving you as you were deceiving me. And yet it cost me such violence to tell you and to write to you that I loved you, that you saw sooner than I had intended that my feelings had changed. You were hurt; you complained of it. I tried to reassure you, but in such a forced way that you were only more convinced that I no longer loved you. In the end I did everything I had meant to do. The strangeness of your heart brought you back to me as you saw me drawing away from you. I have tasted all the pleasure that revenge can give. It has seemed to me that you loved me better than you ever had, and I have let you see that I no longer loved you. I have had reason to believe that you had entirely abandoned the woman for whom you left me. I have also had reasons to be sure that you never spoke to her of me. But your return and your discretion cannot make up for your fickleness. Your heart has been divided between me and another woman. You deceived me, and that is enough to take away the pleasure of being loved by you as I thought I deserved to be, and to leave me in the resolution I have taken never to see you again, which so surprises you."

Madame de Cleves read this letter, and read it again several times, without all the same knowing what she had read. She saw only that Monsieur de Nemours did not love her as she had thought, and that he loved other women, whom he deceived as he deceived her. What a sight, and what a discovery, for a woman of her temper, who felt a violent passion, who had just given signs of it to a man she judged unworthy of it, and to another man whom she was treating badly for his sake! No grief was ever so sharp and so keen. It seemed to her that what gave this grief its bitterness was what had happened that day, and that if Monsieur de Nemours had not had reason to believe she loved him, she would not have cared whether he loved another. But she was deceiving herself; and this pain she found so unbearable was jealousy, with all the horrors that can go with it. She saw from the letter that Monsieur de Nemours had been carrying on a love affair for a long time. She found that the woman who had written it had intelligence and worth; she seemed to her worthy of being loved; she found in her more courage than she found in herself, and she envied the strength the woman had had to hide her feelings from Monsieur de Nemours. She saw from the end of the letter that this woman believed herself loved; and she thought that the discretion Nemours had shown her, which had touched her so deeply, was perhaps only the effect of his passion for this other woman, whom he was afraid of displeasing. In short, she thought everything that could add to her grief and her despair.

How she searched her own heart! What reflections she made on the advice her mother had given her! How she repented of not having insisted on withdrawing from society, in spite of Monsieur de Cleves, or of not having followed the thought she had had of confessing to him her feelings for Monsieur de Nemours! She found that she would have done better to reveal them to a husband whose goodness she knew, and who would have had every interest in hiding them, than to let them be seen by a man who was unworthy of them, who deceived her, who was perhaps sacrificing her, and who wanted to be loved by her only out of pride and vanity. In the end she found that every misfortune that could befall her, and every extreme she might be driven to, was less than having let Monsieur de Nemours see that she loved him, and knowing that he loved another. All that consoled her was to think that at least, after this discovery, she had nothing more to fear from herself, and that she would be entirely cured of her feelings for him.

She hardly gave a thought to the Queen Dauphine's order to attend her when she retired. She went to bed and pretended to be unwell, so that when Monsieur de Cleves came back from the King's, he was told she was asleep. But she was very far from the calm that leads to sleep. She spent the night doing nothing but grieving and rereading the letter she held in her hands.

Madame de Cleves was not the only person whose peace of mind this letter had destroyed. The Vidame de Chartres, who had lost it, and not Monsieur de Nemours, was in extreme anxiety over it. He had spent the whole evening at the Duke of Guise's, who had given a great supper for his brother-in-law the Duke of Ferrara and for all the young people of the court. By chance, over supper, the talk turned to well-written letters. The Vidame de Chartres said he had one on him that was finer than any letter ever written. They pressed him to show it; he refused. Monsieur de Nemours maintained that he had no such letter and was talking out of vanity. The Vidame answered that Nemours was pushing his discretion to the limit; that he would not show the letter all the same, but he would read a few passages from it, which would make them judge that few men received letters like it. At the same moment he went to take out the letter and could not find it. He searched for it in vain; they teased him about it; but he looked so anxious that they stopped talking of it. He withdrew earlier than the others and went home in a hurry to see whether he had left the missing letter there. While he was still searching, one of the Queen's chief valets came to find him, to say that the Viscountess d'Uzès had thought it necessary to warn him at once: it was being said in the Queen's apartments that a love letter had fallen from his pocket while he was at the tennis court; that a great part of what was in it had been repeated; that the Queen had shown great curiosity to see it; that she had sent to ask one of her gentlemen in waiting for it; but that he had answered that he had left it in Chastelart's hands.

The valet told the Vidame de Chartres a great many other things besides, which completed his alarm. He went out that very moment to the house of a gentleman who was a close friend of Chastelart's. He had him roused, although it was an extraordinary hour, and sent him to ask for the letter, without saying who was asking for it or who had lost it. Chastelart, whose mind was already made up that the letter belonged to Monsieur de Nemours and that Nemours was in love with the Queen Dauphine, had no doubt that it was Nemours who was sending to ask for it back. He answered with malicious pleasure that he had put the letter into the hands of the Queen Dauphine. The gentleman brought this answer back to the Vidame de Chartres. It increased the anxiety he already felt and added new anxieties to it. After being long undecided about what to do, he concluded that only Monsieur de Nemours could help him out of the awkward position he was in.

He went to Nemours's house and entered his bedroom as day was just beginning to break. Nemours was sleeping peacefully; what he had seen of Madame de Cleves the day before had left him nothing but pleasant thoughts. He was very surprised to be woken by the Vidame de Chartres, and asked him whether it was to avenge what he had said at supper that he came to disturb his rest. The Vidame's face made it plain that the business that brought him was nothing but serious.

"I have come to confide to you the most important affair of my life," he said. "I know very well that you owe me no gratitude for it, since it comes at a time when I need your help; but I know too that I would have lost your esteem if I had told you all I am about to tell you without necessity forcing me to it. I dropped the letter I spoke of last night. It matters extremely to me that no one should know it is addressed to me. It was seen by a great many people who were at the tennis court, where it fell yesterday. You were there too, and I beg you as a favor to be willing to say that it was you who lost it."

"You must believe I have no lady of my own," Monsieur de Nemours answered with a smile, "to make me such a proposal, and to imagine there is no one I could fall out with by letting it be believed that I receive letters like that."

"I beg you," said the Vidame, "listen to me seriously. If there is a woman you love, as I have no doubt there is, though I don't know who she is, it will be easy for you to clear yourself, and I will give you sure means of doing it. And even if you did not clear yourself with her, it can cost you no more than a quarrel of a few moments. But I, through this affair, dishonor a woman who has loved me passionately and who is one of the most estimable women in the world; and on the other side I draw down on myself an implacable hatred that will cost me my fortune, and perhaps something more."

"I can't follow everything you say," replied Monsieur de Nemours, "but you give me a glimpse that the rumors about the interest a great princess took in you are not entirely false."

"They are not," replied the Vidame de Chartres, "and would to God they were! I would not be in the awkward position I am in. But I must tell you everything that has happened, to show you everything I have to fear.

"Ever since I came to court, the Queen has always treated me with great distinction and kindness, and I had reason to believe she thought well of me. Still, there was nothing particular in it, and I had never thought of having any feelings for her but respect. I was even deeply in love with Madame de Thémines. Anyone who sees her can easily judge that a man may love her very much when she loves him; and she loved me. Nearly two years ago, when the court was at Fontainebleau, I found myself two or three times in conversation with the Queen at hours when very few people were about. It seemed to me that my mind pleased her and that she took up everything I said. One day among others we began to talk about trust. I said there was no one I trusted completely; that in my view one always regretted trusting anyone; and that I knew a great many things I had never spoken of. The Queen told me she esteemed me the more for it; that she had found no one in France who could keep a secret, and that this was what had troubled her most, because it had taken from her the pleasure of giving her trust; that it was a necessary thing in life to have someone one could talk to, above all for people of her rank. In the days that followed she returned to the same conversation several times; she even told me some rather private things that were going on. In the end it seemed to me that she wished to be sure of my discretion, and that she wanted to confide her secrets to me. The thought attached me to her; I was touched by the distinction, and I paid my court to her far more assiduously than I had been used to.

"One evening when the King and all the ladies had gone riding in the forest, where she had not wanted to go because she was feeling a little unwell, I stayed with her. She went down to the edge of the pond and let go of her equerries' hands so as to walk more freely. After a few turns she came up to me and ordered me to follow her. 'I want to speak to you,' she said, 'and you will see, by what I am going to say, that I am your friend.' She stopped at these words, and looking at me steadily, went on: 'You are in love; and because you perhaps trust no one, you believe your love is not known. But it is known, and even by the people concerned. You are being watched; the places where you see the woman you love are known; there is a plan to surprise you there. I do not know who she is; I am not asking you; I only want to protect you from the misfortunes you may fall into.' Consider, I beg you, what a trap the Queen was setting for me, and how hard it was not to fall into it. She wanted to know whether I was in love; and by not asking me with whom, and by letting me see nothing but an intention to do me a kindness, she took away any thought that she was speaking out of curiosity or by design.

"And yet, against every appearance, I worked out the truth. I was in love with Madame de Thémines; but although she loved me, I was not fortunate enough to have private places to see her, nor to fear being surprised there; so I saw clearly that she could not be the woman the Queen meant. I knew, too, that I was carrying on an affair with another woman, less beautiful and less strict than Madame de Thémines, and that it was not impossible that the place where I saw her had been discovered; but since I cared little for her, it was easy for me to put myself out of reach of every danger by simply ceasing to see her. So I decided to admit nothing to the Queen, and to assure her, on the contrary, that I had long ago given up the wish to be loved by women whose love I could hope for, because I found nearly all of them unworthy of holding a gentleman, and that only something far above them could engage me. 'You are not answering me sincerely,' the Queen replied. 'I know the opposite of what you tell me. The way I am speaking to you ought to oblige you to hide nothing from me. I want you to be one of my friends,' she went on; 'but in giving you that place I do not want to be ignorant of your attachments. See whether you are willing to buy it at the price of telling me them. I give you two days to think it over; but after that, consider well what you will say to me, and remember that if I ever find afterward that you have deceived me, I will not forgive you as long as I live.'

"The Queen left me after saying these words, without waiting for my answer. You can believe that my mind was full of what she had just said. The two days she had given me to think it over did not seem too long for me to make up my mind. I saw that she wanted to know whether I was in love, and that she did not wish me to be. I saw the consequences of the course I was about to take. My vanity was not a little flattered by a private understanding with the Queen, and a queen whose person is still extremely attractive. On the other side, I loved Madame de Thémines; and although I was being unfaithful to her, in a way, with that other woman I have told you about, I could not bring myself to break with her. I saw too the danger I was exposing myself to by deceiving the Queen, and how hard it was to deceive her. Nevertheless I could not bring myself to refuse what fortune was offering me, and I took my chance on everything my bad conduct might bring down on me. I broke with the woman whose affair with me could be discovered, and I hoped to hide the one I had with Madame de Thémines.

"At the end of the two days the Queen had given me, as I came into the room where all the ladies were gathered for the circle, she said to me out loud, with a grave air that surprised me: 'Have you thought about that business I charged you with, and do you know the truth of it?' 'Yes, Madame,' I answered, 'and it is as I told Your Majesty.' 'Come this evening, at the hour when I write my letters,' she replied, 'and I will finish giving you my orders.' I made a deep bow without answering, and did not fail to be there at the hour she had named. I found her in the gallery, where her secretary was, and one or two of her women. As soon as she saw me she came to me and led me to the other end of the gallery. 'Well,' she said, 'is it after thinking it over carefully that you have nothing to tell me? And does the way I treat you not deserve that you should speak to me sincerely?' 'It is because I am speaking to you sincerely, Madame,' I answered, 'that I have nothing to tell you; and I swear to Your Majesty, with all the respect I owe her, that I have no attachment to any woman at court.' 'I am willing to believe it,' the Queen replied, 'because I wish it; and I wish it because I want you to be entirely devoted to me, and because it would be impossible for me to be content with your friendship if you were in love. One cannot trust people who are in love; one cannot be sure of their discretion. They are too distracted and too divided; the woman they love is their first concern, and that does not fit the way I want you to be attached to me. Remember, then, that it is on the word you give me that you have no engagement that I choose you to receive all my trust. Remember that I want yours entire; that I want you to have no friend, man or woman, but those who are agreeable to me, and to give up every care but that of pleasing me. I will not make you lose the care of your fortune; I will manage it more attentively than you would yourself; and whatever I do for you, I will think myself too well rewarded if I find you toward me what I hope. I choose you to confide all my sorrows to, and to help me soften them. You may judge that they are not slight. I seem to bear the King's attachment to the Duchess of Valentinois without much pain; but it is unbearable to me. She governs the King; she deceives him; she despises me; all my people are hers. The Queen my daughter-in-law, proud of her beauty and of her uncles' power, pays me no duty. The Constable de Montmorency is master of the King and of the kingdom; he hates me, and he has given me proofs of his hatred that I cannot forget. The Marshal de Saint-André is a brash young favorite who treats me no better than the rest. The full account of my misfortunes would move you to pity. Until now I have not dared to trust anyone; I trust you. See that I do not regret it, and be my only consolation.' The Queen's eyes reddened as she finished these words. I nearly threw myself at her feet, so truly was I touched by the kindness she showed me. From that day on she gave me her entire confidence; she no longer did anything without speaking to me of it; and I have kept up a connection with her that lasts to this day.
