The Princess of Montpensier

(Part 1 of 2)

While civil war was tearing France apart under the reign of Charles IX, love still found its place among so many disorders, and caused a good many of its own. The only daughter of the Marquis de Mézières was a very great heiress, both through her large fortune and through the illustrious house of Anjou from which she descended, and she was promised to the Duke of Maine, the younger brother of the Duke of Guise who was later called Le Balafré, the Scarred. Because this great heiress was still so young, the marriage was put off. In the meantime the Duke of Guise, who saw her often and saw in her the beginnings of a great beauty, fell in love with her and was loved in return. They hid their love with great care. The Duke of Guise, who did not yet have as much ambition as he has shown since, wanted passionately to marry her, but fear of the Cardinal of Lorraine, who stood in the place of a father to him, kept him from declaring himself.

That was how matters stood when the house of Bourbon, which could not watch the rise of the house of Guise without envy, saw the advantage the Guises would gain from this marriage and resolved to take it from them and profit by it themselves, by marrying the heiress to the young Prince of Montpensier. They worked at this plan with such success that Mademoiselle de Mézières's family, against the promises they had made to the Cardinal of Lorraine, resolved to give her in marriage to the young Prince. The whole house of Guise was deeply shocked by this behavior, but the Duke was crushed with grief, and because his love was at stake, he took the broken promise as an unbearable insult. His resentment soon broke out, despite the reprimands of his uncles, the Cardinal of Lorraine and the Duke of Aumale, who did not want to dig in over something they could see they were powerless to stop. He lost his temper so violently, even in the presence of the young Prince of Montpensier himself, that a hatred was born between the two of them which ended only with their lives.

Mademoiselle de Mézières was pressed by her family to marry the Prince. She saw, besides, that she could not marry the Duke of Guise, and her virtue told her how dangerous it would be to have as a brother-in-law a man she would have wished for a husband. So she resolved at last to follow her family's wishes, and she begged Monsieur de Guise to put no further obstacle in the way of her marriage. She married the Prince of Montpensier, then, and soon afterward he took her to Champigny, the usual residence of the princes of his house, to get her out of Paris, where the whole weight of the war seemed about to fall. That great city was threatened with a siege by the army of the Huguenots, the French Protestants, whose leader was the Prince of Condé, and who had just declared war on the King for the second time.

In his earliest youth the Prince of Montpensier had formed a very close friendship with the Count of Chabannes, a man a good deal older than himself and of extraordinary merit. The Count had been so touched by the young Prince's regard and trust that, against the commitments he had made to the Prince of Condé, who was holding out the hope of important posts for him on the Huguenot side, he declared for the Catholics; he could not bring himself to be opposed in anything to a man who was so dear to him. Since this change of sides had no other foundation, people doubted that it was sincere, and the Queen Mother, Catherine de' Medici, suspected him so strongly that, once the Huguenots had declared war, she meant to have him arrested. But the Prince of Montpensier prevented it, and took Chabannes with him to Champigny when he went there with his wife.

The Count had a very gentle and agreeable mind, and he soon won the regard of the Princess of Montpensier. Before long she had no less trust and affection for him than her husband the Prince had. Chabannes, for his part, looked with admiration on all the beauty, intelligence and virtue that showed in this young princess, and he used the friendship she showed him to inspire in her a virtue of an extraordinary kind, worthy of the greatness of her birth. In a short time he made her one of the most accomplished women in the world.

The Prince went back to court, where the continuing war called him, and the Count stayed behind alone with the Princess. He went on treating her with a respect and a friendship that matched her rank and her merit. The trust between them grew on both sides, and on the Princess of Montpensier's side it grew to such a point that she told him about the feelings she had once had for Monsieur de Guise. But she told him at the same time that those feelings were almost dead; that only enough of them remained to bar the door of her heart against any new attraction; and that, with her virtue added to this last trace of an old impression, she was capable of nothing but contempt for anyone who dared to be in love with her.

The Count knew how sincere this beautiful princess was, and he could see in her, besides, a nature so set against the weakness of love affairs that he did not doubt the truth of what she said. And yet he could not defend himself against so many charms, seen every day from so close. He fell passionately in love with her. Whatever shame he felt at letting himself be overcome, he had to give way, and to love her with the most violent and the most sincere passion there ever was. If he was not master of his heart, he was master of his actions. The change in his soul brought none in his conduct, and no one suspected his love. For a whole year he took exact care to hide it from the Princess, and he believed he would always want to hide it from her.

Love did in him what it does in everyone else: it gave him the urge to speak. After all the struggles that are usual on such occasions, he dared to tell her that he loved her, well prepared to weather the storms that the Princess's pride threatened him with. But what he found in her was a calm and a coldness a thousand times worse than all the severity he had expected. She did not take the trouble to be angry with him. In a few words she set before him the difference in their rank and their age, the particular knowledge he had of her virtue and of the feelings she had once had for the Duke of Guise, and above all what he owed to the friendship and the trust of her husband the Prince. The Count nearly died of shame and grief at her feet. She tried to console him by assuring him that she would never remember what he had just said to her, that she would never let herself believe a thing so much to her disadvantage, and that she would never look on him as anything but her best friend.

These assurances consoled the Count, as one may imagine. He felt the contempt in the Princess's words in its full extent, and the next day, when he saw her again with a face as open as ever, his grief doubled. The Princess's conduct did nothing to lessen it. She lived with him with the same kindness she had always shown. When the occasion brought up the subject, she spoke to him again of the feelings she had once had for the Duke of Guise, and since fame was then beginning to publish the great qualities that were showing in that prince, she confessed that she felt joy at it, and was glad to see that he deserved the feelings she had had for him. All these marks of trust, which had been so dear to the Count, became unbearable to him. He did not dare show this to the Princess, though he did sometimes dare to remind her of what he had been so bold as to tell her.

After two years of absence, when peace had been made, the Prince of Montpensier came back to his wife, covered in the honor he had won at the siege of Paris and at the battle of Saint-Denis. He was surprised to find her beauty in such perfection, and because jealousy was natural to him, he felt some vexation at it, foreseeing that he would not be the only man who found her beautiful. He was very glad to see the Count of Chabannes again; his friendship for him had not lessened at all. In confidence, he asked him what he could tell him of his wife's mind and temper, for she was almost a stranger to him, he had spent so little time with her. The Count, with a sincerity as exact as if he had not been in love, told the Prince everything he knew in her that could make him love her, and he also advised Madame de Montpensier of everything she should do to finish winning her husband's heart and esteem.

Indeed, the Count's passion led him so naturally to think of nothing but what could add to the happiness and the honor of this princess that he forgot without any trouble the interest a man in love has in keeping the woman he loves from a perfect understanding with her husband.

The peace had barely appeared before it was gone. War began again at once, through the King's plan to have the Prince of Condé and the Admiral de Châtillon, the Huguenot leader, arrested at Noyers. The plan was discovered, preparations for war started up afresh, and the Prince of Montpensier was forced to leave his wife to go where his duty called him. Chabannes followed him to court, having fully cleared himself with the Queen Mother. It was not without extreme grief that he left the Princess, who for her part remained very sad over the dangers the war was about to expose her husband to.

The Huguenot leaders had withdrawn to La Rochelle. Since Poitou and Saintonge were on their side, the war flared up fiercely there, and the King gathered all his troops in that region. The King's brother, the Duke of Anjou, who was afterward Henri III, won great honor there by several fine actions, among them the battle of Jarnac, where the Prince of Condé was killed. It was in this war that the Duke of Guise began to hold important commands, and to make it known that he far surpassed the great hopes people had formed of him. The Prince of Montpensier, who hated him both as his personal enemy and as the enemy of his house, could not watch the Duke's growing reputation without pain, nor the friendship the Duke of Anjou showed him.

After the two armies had worn themselves out in a great many small engagements, by common consent the troops were sent home for a time. The Duke of Anjou stayed at Loches to see to all the strongholds that might be attacked. The Duke of Guise stayed there with him, and the Prince of Montpensier, with the Count of Chabannes, went home to Champigny, which was not far away. The Duke of Anjou often went to inspect the places he was having fortified. One day, when he was returning to Loches by a road that few of his party knew, the Duke of Guise, who boasted of knowing it, put himself at the head of the troop to act as guide. But after they had ridden for some time he lost his way, and found himself on the bank of a small river that he did not recognize himself. The Duke of Anjou teased him for leading them so badly. While they were halted there, as ready for amusement as young princes usually are, they noticed a small boat stopped in the middle of the river. Since the river was not wide, they could easily make out three or four women in the boat, and one in particular who seemed very beautiful to them. She was magnificently dressed, and she was watching closely two men who were fishing beside her.

This incident gave fresh amusement to the young princes and to everyone in their party. It seemed to them like something out of a romance. Some told the Duke of Guise that he had lost the way on purpose, to show them this beautiful person; others, that after what chance had done, he was bound to fall in love with her; and the Duke of Anjou maintained that he was the one who ought to be her suitor. In the end, wanting to see the adventure through, they had some of their men ride into the river as far as they could, to call out to the lady that it was Monsieur d'Anjou, who would very much like to cross to the other side of the water, and who begged to be fetched.

The lady, who was the Princess of Montpensier, heard that the Duke of Anjou was there, and from the number of people she saw on the bank she had no doubt that it was he. She had her boat brought forward to the side where he was. His fine bearing soon let her pick him out from the others, but she picked out the Duke of Guise even sooner. The sight of him stirred a confusion in her that made her blush a little, and it made her appear to these princes in a beauty they thought more than natural. The Duke of Guise knew her at once, despite the change for the better that had come over her in the three years since he had seen her. He told the Duke of Anjou who she was, and the Duke was at first ashamed of the liberty he had taken. But seeing Madame de Montpensier so beautiful, and enjoying the adventure so much, he resolved to carry it through. After a thousand apologies and a thousand compliments he invented some important business that he claimed to have on the other side of the river, and accepted the offer she made to carry him across in her boat. He got in alone with the Duke of Guise, ordering everyone who was following them to cross the river at another spot and to join them at Champigny, which Madame de Montpensier told them was only about five miles away.

As soon as they were in the boat, the Duke of Anjou asked her to what they owed such a pleasant meeting, and what she was doing in the middle of the river. She answered that she had set out from Champigny with her husband the Prince, meaning to follow him to the hunt; that she had found herself too tired and had come down to the riverbank; and that curiosity to see a salmon taken, one that had run into a net, had brought her into this boat. Monsieur de Guise took no part in the conversation. But he felt everything this princess had once awakened in his heart wake again, sharply, and he thought to himself that he would have trouble getting out of this adventure without falling back into his old chains.

They soon reached the bank, where they found Madame de Montpensier's horses and equerries waiting for her. The Duke of Anjou and the Duke of Guise helped her to mount, and she sat her horse with admirable grace. All the way home she entertained them agreeably on all sorts of subjects. They were no less surprised by the charm of her mind than they had been by her beauty, and they could not keep from letting her know how extraordinarily surprised they were. She answered their praise with all the modesty imaginable, but a little more coldly to the Duke of Guise's, wanting to keep up a pride that would prevent him from building any hope on the feelings she had once had for him.

As they rode into the first courtyard at Champigny, they found the Prince of Montpensier, who had only just come back from the hunt. He was greatly shocked to see two men riding beside his wife, and his shock became extreme when, coming closer, he recognized the Duke of Anjou and the Duke of Guise. His hatred for the latter, joined to his natural jealousy, made him find something so disagreeable in seeing these princes with his wife, without knowing how they had come to be there or what they had come to do in his house, that he could not hide his annoyance. He cleverly laid the blame for it on his fear of being unable to receive so great a prince as his rank deserved and as he himself would have wished.

The Count of Chabannes was even more troubled than Monsieur de Montpensier himself to see Monsieur de Guise beside Madame de Montpensier. What chance had done to bring these two people together seemed to him so bad an omen that he could easily foresee that this beginning of a romance would not be without a sequel. That evening Madame de Montpensier did the honors of her house with the same grace she brought to everything. In short, she pleased her guests only too well. The Duke of Anjou, who was very much a ladies' man and very handsome, could not see a prize so worthy of him without wanting it ardently. He was struck with the same illness as Monsieur de Guise, and still pretending to have extraordinary business, he stayed two days at Champigny with nothing to keep him there but Madame de Montpensier's charms, for her husband the Prince did nothing at all to press him to stay.

The Duke of Guise did not leave without letting Madame de Montpensier understand that he was to her what he had always been. Since his passion had never been known to anyone, he told her several times, in front of everybody and heard by no one but her, that his heart had not changed. Then he and the Duke of Anjou left Champigny with great regret. The two of them rode for a long time in deep silence. But at last the Duke of Anjou, suddenly imagining that what filled his own thoughts might well be the cause of the Duke of Guise's, asked him abruptly whether he was thinking about the beauty of the Princess of Montpensier. The abruptness of the question, together with what the Duke of Guise had already noticed of the Duke of Anjou's feelings, showed him that the Duke would certainly be his rival, and that it mattered very much not to let this prince discover his love. To clear away any suspicion, he answered with a laugh that the Duke himself had seemed so taken up with the very daydream he was accusing him of that he had not thought it right to interrupt; that the Princess of Montpensier's beauty was nothing new to him; that he had grown used to bearing its brilliance in the days when she was meant to be his sister-in-law; but that he could see that not everyone was so little dazzled by it. The Duke of Anjou admitted that he had never yet seen anything that seemed to him comparable to this young princess, and that he could feel the sight of her might be dangerous to him if he were often exposed to it. He wanted the Duke of Guise to agree that he felt the same, but the Duke, who was beginning to make a serious matter of his love, would admit nothing. The princes went back to Loches, and the adventure that had brought them the Princess of Montpensier often supplied their pleasant conversation.

It was no such source of amusement at Champigny. The Prince of Montpensier was unhappy about everything that had happened, without being able to say why. He thought it wrong that his wife had been in that boat. It seemed to him that she had received these princes too agreeably; and what displeased him most was to have noticed that the Duke of Guise had looked at her attentively. From that moment he conceived a furious jealousy, which made him remember the rage the Duke had shown at the time of his marriage, and it crossed his mind that even then the Duke had been in love with her. The vexation all these suspicions caused him gave the Princess of Montpensier some bad hours.

The Count of Chabannes, as was his habit, took care to keep them from falling out completely, in order to persuade the Princess by this how sincere and unselfish his passion for her was. He could not keep himself from asking her what effect the sight of the Duke of Guise had had on her. She told him that it had unsettled her, out of shame at the memory of the feelings she had once shown him; that she had found him much handsomer than he was in those days; and that it had even seemed to her that he wanted to persuade her he loved her still. But she assured him at the same time that nothing could shake the resolution she had made never to become involved. The Count of Chabannes was very glad to learn of this resolution, but nothing could reassure him about the Duke of Guise. He told the Princess that he was extremely afraid her first impressions would soon return, and he made her understand the mortal grief he would feel, for the sake of them both, if he ever saw her change her mind. The Princess of Montpensier, keeping up her usual manner with him, hardly answered what he said about his passion, and went on seeing in him only the best friend in the world, without doing him the honor of noticing that he was also a man in love with her.

When the armies were raised again, all the princes went back to them, and the Prince of Montpensier thought it best for his wife to go to Paris, so as to be no longer so close to where the war was being fought. The Huguenots laid siege to the city of Poitiers. The Duke of Guise threw himself into it to defend it, and there he did things that would by themselves have been enough to make any other life than his glorious. Then the battle of Moncontour was fought. The Duke of Anjou, after taking Saint-Jean-d'Angély, fell ill and left the army at the same time, whether from the violence of his illness or from a wish to go back and taste the rest and the pleasures of Paris, where the presence of the Princess of Montpensier was not the least of the attractions. The army was left under the command of the Prince of Montpensier, and soon afterward, peace being made, the whole court was gathered in Paris.

The Princess's beauty eclipsed every beauty that had been admired until then. She drew everyone's eyes by the charm of her mind and her person. The Duke of Anjou had not changed, in Paris, the feelings he had conceived for her at Champigny. He took extreme care to let her know it by attentions of every kind, though he was careful not to make them too conspicuous, for fear of making her husband the Prince jealous. The Duke of Guise finished falling violently in love with her. For several reasons he wanted to keep his passion hidden, so he resolved to declare it to her straight away, to spare himself all those preliminaries that always give rise to talk and scandal.

One day he was at the Queen Mother's apartments at an hour when very few people were there, and the Queen Mother had withdrawn to talk business with the Cardinal of Lorraine, when the Princess of Montpensier arrived. He resolved to take this moment to speak to her, and going up to her, he said:

"I am going to surprise you, Madame, and displease you, by telling you that I have always kept the passion you once knew of. Seeing you again has increased it so much that neither your severity, nor the Prince of Montpensier's hatred, nor the rivalry of the first prince of the kingdom could take away one moment of its violence. It would have been more respectful to let you know it by my actions than by my words; but, Madame, my actions would have told others as well as you, and I want you alone to know that I am bold enough to adore you."

At first she was so surprised and so agitated by this speech that she did not think of interrupting him. But then, having recovered herself, she was beginning to answer him when the Prince of Montpensier came in. Her agitation and confusion were painted on her face, and the sight of her husband finished unsettling her, so that she let him see more than the Duke of Guise had just told her. The Queen Mother came out of her closet, and the Duke withdrew, to cure the Prince's jealousy.

That evening the Princess of Montpensier found her husband in every bad humor imaginable. He flew into terrible rages against her and forbade her ever to speak to the Duke of Guise. She withdrew to her rooms very sad, and very much taken up with everything that had happened to her that day. The next day she saw the Duke of Guise again at the Queen Mother's, but he did not approach her; he simply left a little after she did, to show her that he had no business there when she was not. Not a day went by without her receiving a thousand hidden signs of the Duke's passion, though he never tried to speak to her of it except when no one could see him. Since she was thoroughly persuaded of this passion, she began, in spite of all the resolutions she had made at Champigny, to feel in the depths of her heart something of what had once been there.

The Duke of Anjou, for his part, left nothing undone to show her his love wherever he could see her, and he followed her continually to the apartments of the Queen Mother, his mother. His sister, Madame Marguerite, who was in love with him, was treated by him with a harshness that would have cured any passion but hers. At about this time it was discovered that this princess, who was afterward Queen of Navarre, had some attachment for the Duke of Guise, and what made it plainer was the coolness that the Duke of Anjou began to show toward the Duke of Guise. The Princess of Montpensier heard this news. It was not a matter of indifference to her, and it made her feel that she took more interest in the Duke of Guise than she had thought. Her father-in-law, Monsieur de Montpensier, was then marrying Mademoiselle de Guise, the Duke's sister, and so she was forced to see him often at the places where the wedding ceremonies brought them both.

The Princess of Montpensier could no longer bear that a man whom all France believed in love with Madame Marguerite should dare to tell her that he was in love with her. She felt insulted, and almost grieved at having deceived herself. So one day, when the Duke of Guise met her at his sister's, a little apart from the others, and tried to speak to her of his passion, she cut him off abruptly and said, in a voice that showed her anger:

"I do not understand how anyone, on the strength of a weakness one was capable of at thirteen, can have the audacity to play the lover to a woman like me, and above all when he is in love with another in full view of the whole court."

The Duke of Guise, who had a great deal of intelligence and was very much in love, needed to consult no one to understand everything the Princess's words meant. He answered her with great respect:

"I admit, Madame, that I was wrong not to scorn the honor of being my King's brother-in-law, rather than let you suspect for one moment that I could want any heart but yours. But if you will do me the favor of hearing me out, I am sure I can justify myself to you."

The Princess of Montpensier did not answer, but she did not move away, and the Duke of Guise, seeing that she was giving him the hearing he wanted, told her that Madame Marguerite had honored him with her favor without his having done anything to win it; that, having no passion for her, he had answered the honor she did him very poorly, until she gave him some hope of marrying her; that it was true the greatness to which this marriage could raise him had obliged him to pay her more attention, and that this was what had given rise to the suspicions of the King and the Duke of Anjou; that the opposition of the one and the other did not turn him from his plan; but that if the plan displeased her, he would abandon it that very hour, and never think of it again as long as he lived.

The sacrifice the Duke of Guise was making for her made the Princess forget all the severity and all the anger with which she had begun speaking to him. She changed the subject, and began to talk with him about the weakness Madame Marguerite had shown in loving him first, and about the considerable advantage he would gain by marrying her. In the end, without saying anything kind to the Duke of Guise, she let him see again a thousand charming things he had once found in Mademoiselle de Mézières. Though they had not spoken to each other for a long time, they found themselves at ease together, and their hearts slipped easily back onto a road that was not unknown to them. They ended this pleasant conversation, which left a keen joy in the Duke of Guise's mind. The Princess's joy, at learning that he truly loved her, was not small either.

But once she was alone in her closet, what reflections she made on the shame of having let herself be softened so easily by the Duke of Guise's excuses, on the awkward position she was about to plunge herself into by entering on something she had looked on with such horror, and on the frightful misfortunes her husband's jealousy could bring down on her! These thoughts made her form new resolutions, but they melted away the next day at the sight of the Duke of Guise. He never failed to give her an exact account of what passed between Madame Marguerite and himself. The new alliance between their houses gave him occasion to speak to her often, but he had no small trouble curing her of the jealousy that Madame Marguerite's beauty gave her, against which no oath could reassure her. This jealousy served the Princess of Montpensier to defend what was left of her heart against the attentions of the Duke of Guise, who had already won the greater part of it.
