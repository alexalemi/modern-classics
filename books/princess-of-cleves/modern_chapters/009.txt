Part Three

(Part 3 of 3)

A few days before the day chosen for the wedding, the Queen Dauphine gave a supper for the King, her father-in-law, and for the Duchess of Valentinois. Madame de Cleves, who was busy dressing, went to the Louvre later than usual. On the way she met a gentleman whom the Queen Dauphine had sent to fetch her. As she came into the room, the Queen Dauphine called out from the bed where she was lying that she had been waiting for her with the greatest impatience.

"I think, Madame," Madame de Cleves answered, "that I have no reason to thank you for that impatience. I am sure it comes from something other than a wish to see me."

"You are right," the Queen Dauphine replied. "But you ought to be grateful to me all the same, because I am going to tell you a story that I am certain you will be very glad to hear."

Madame de Cleves knelt down beside the bed, and luckily for her, the light was not on her face.

"You know," the Queen Dauphine said, "how curious we have been to guess what has caused the change in the Duke of Nemours. I think I know, and it is something that will surprise you. He is madly in love with one of the most beautiful women at court, and she loves him in return."

Madame de Cleves could not take these words to herself, since she did not believe anyone knew she loved him, and they caused her a pain that is easy to imagine.

"I see nothing in that," she answered, "which should surprise anyone, in a man of Monsieur de Nemours's age and looks."

"That is not what should astonish you either," the Queen Dauphine went on. "What should astonish you is this: the woman who loves Monsieur de Nemours has never given him the slightest sign of it, and she was so afraid of not always being able to control her passion that she confessed it to her husband, so that he would take her away from court. And it is Monsieur de Nemours himself who told the story I am telling you."

If Madame de Cleves had first felt pain at the thought that she had no part in this story, the Queen Dauphine's last words threw her into despair, because now she was certain that she had far too much. She could not answer. She stayed with her head bent over the bed while the Queen Dauphine went on talking, so taken up with what she was saying that she did not notice her confusion. When Madame de Cleves had recovered a little, she said: "That story hardly seems likely to me, Madame, and I would very much like to know who told it to you."

"It was Madame de Martigues," the Queen Dauphine replied, "who heard it from the Vidame de Chartres. You know he is in love with her. He told it to her as a secret, and he has it from the Duke of Nemours himself. It is true that the Duke of Nemours did not give him the lady's name, and did not even admit that he was the man she loved, but the Vidame de Chartres has no doubt of it."

As the Queen Dauphine finished these words, someone came up to the bed. Madame de Cleves was turned in a way that kept her from seeing who it was, but she had no doubt when the Queen Dauphine cried out, with an air of gaiety and surprise: "Here he is himself, and I am going to ask him what the truth of it is."

Madame de Cleves knew it was the Duke of Nemours, and so it was. Without turning toward him, she leaned quickly toward the Queen Dauphine and whispered that she must take great care not to speak to him of this story. He had confided it to the Vidame de Chartres, and it was the kind of thing that could make them quarrel. The Queen Dauphine answered, laughing, that she was too cautious, and turned to Monsieur de Nemours. He was dressed for the evening's assembly, and he spoke with that grace which came so naturally to him.

"I think, Madame," he said, "that I may suppose, without presumption, that you were talking about me when I came in, that you meant to ask me something, and that Madame de Cleves objects."

"That is true," the Queen Dauphine replied. "But I will not oblige her the way I usually do. I want to know from you whether a story I have been told is true, and whether you are not the man who is in love with, and loved by, a woman of this court who hides her passion from him with great care, and who has confessed it to her husband."

Madame de Cleves's agitation and distress were beyond anything that can be imagined. If death had offered to release her from that state, she would have welcomed it. But Monsieur de Nemours was even more at a loss, if that is possible. Here was the Queen Dauphine, who he had reason to believe did not dislike him, saying this in front of Madame de Cleves, the person at court she trusted most, and who trusted her most in return. It threw his thoughts into such strange confusion that he could not keep control of his face. The awkward position he saw Madame de Cleves in through his own fault, and the thought of the good reason he had given her to hate him, struck him so hard that he could not answer. The Queen Dauphine, seeing how completely he was thrown, said to Madame de Cleves: "Look at him. Look at him, and judge whether this story is not his own."

Meanwhile Monsieur de Nemours recovered from his first confusion. He saw how important it was to get out of so dangerous a position, and all at once he took command of his wits and his face.

"I admit, Madame," he said, "that no one could be more surprised or more hurt than I am by the Vidame de Chartres's breach of faith, in repeating a story about one of my friends which I told him in confidence. I could take my revenge," he went on, smiling, with an air of calm that nearly took from the Queen Dauphine the suspicions she had just had, "for he has confided things to me that are of no small importance. But I do not know, Madame," he continued, "why you do me the honor of mixing me up in this story. The Vidame cannot say that it concerns me, because I told him the opposite. The part of a man in love may suit me well enough. But the part of a man who is loved, Madame, is one I do not think you can give me."

Nemours was glad to say something to the Queen Dauphine that matched what he had let her see of himself in earlier days, to turn her mind from the thoughts she might have had. She believed she understood what he meant. But without answering it, she went on teasing him about his confusion.

"I was agitated, Madame," he answered, "on my friend's account, and because of the just reproaches he could make me for having repeated something that is dearer to him than life. Still, he only half confided in me, and he did not name the woman he loves. All I know is that he is the most deeply in love, and the most to be pitied, of any man in the world."

"Do you find him so much to be pitied," the Queen Dauphine replied, "since he is loved?"

"Do you believe that he is, Madame?" he answered. "Do you believe that a woman with a real passion could reveal it to her husband? That woman surely does not know what love is. She has mistaken a faint gratitude for the devotion a man feels for her, and called it love. My friend cannot flatter himself with any hope. But unhappy as he is, he counts himself happy at least to have made her afraid of loving him, and he would not change his state for that of the happiest suitor in the world."

"Your friend has a passion that is very easily satisfied," said the Queen Dauphine, "and I am beginning to think it is not yourself you are speaking of. I am very nearly of Madame de Cleves's opinion," she went on. "She maintains that this story cannot be true."

"I do not believe it can be," said Madame de Cleves, who had not yet spoken. "And even if it were possible, how could anyone have found it out? It is not likely that a woman capable of something so extraordinary would be weak enough to tell it. Her husband, presumably, would not have told it either. Or if he did, he would be a husband very unworthy of the way he had been treated."

Monsieur de Nemours saw that Madame de Cleves's suspicions had fallen on her husband, and he was glad to confirm them. He knew that her husband was the most dangerous rival he had to destroy.

"Jealousy," he answered, "and the curiosity to learn more, perhaps, than he has been told, can lead a husband into a great many indiscretions."

Madame de Cleves was at the last limit of her strength and her courage. She could not keep up the conversation any longer, and she was about to say that she felt unwell when, luckily for her, the Duchess of Valentinois came in and told the Queen Dauphine that the King was on his way. The Queen Dauphine went into her dressing room to dress. Monsieur de Nemours came up to Madame de Cleves as she was about to follow.

"I would give my life, Madame," he said, "to speak to you for a moment. But of all the important things I would have to say to you, nothing seems to me more important than to beg you to believe this: if I said anything that the Queen Dauphine might take as meant for her, I did it for reasons that have nothing to do with her."

Madame de Cleves gave no sign of having heard him. She left him without a glance and went to follow the King, who had just come in. There was a great crowd, and she caught her foot in her gown and stumbled. She used that as her excuse to leave a place where she had not the strength to stay, and pretending that she could not stand, she went home.

Monsieur de Cleves came to the Louvre and was surprised not to find his wife there. He was told of the accident that had happened to her. He went home at once to find out how she was. He found her in bed, and he learned that her injury was nothing serious. But after he had been with her for a while, he saw that she was in a sadness so extreme that it surprised him.

"What is the matter, Madame?" he said. "It seems to me you have some other grief than the one you complain of."

"I have the sharpest grief I could ever have," she answered. "What use have you made of the extraordinary trust, or rather the mad trust, that I placed in you? Did I not deserve to have my secret kept? And even if I did not deserve it, did your own interest not demand it? Did the curiosity to learn a name that I must not tell you really drive you to confide in someone, in the hope of finding it out? Nothing but that one curiosity can have made you commit so cruel an indiscretion. And the consequences are as bad as they could possibly be. The story is known. It has just been told to me, by someone who did not know that I was the one it chiefly concerned."

"What are you saying, Madame?" he answered. "You accuse me of having told what passed between you and me, and you inform me that the thing is known. I will not defend myself against having repeated it; you could not believe that of me. You must surely have taken something said about some other woman as said about yourself."

"Ah, Monsieur," she replied, "there is no other story in the world like mine. There is no other woman capable of the same thing. Chance cannot have invented it. No one ever imagined such a thing, and the thought never entered any mind but my own. The Queen Dauphine has just told me the whole story. She had it from the Vidame de Chartres, who had it from Monsieur de Nemours."

"Monsieur de Nemours!" cried Monsieur de Cleves, with a movement that showed both fury and despair. "What! Monsieur de Nemours knows that you love him, and that I know it?"

"You always insist on choosing Monsieur de Nemours rather than anyone else," she replied. "I have told you that I will never answer you about your suspicions. I do not know whether Monsieur de Nemours knows the part I have in this story, or the part you have given him in it. But he told it to the Vidame de Chartres, and said that he had it from one of his friends, who had not named the woman. This friend of Monsieur de Nemours must be a friend of yours, and you must have trusted him in order to find out what you wanted to know."

"Is there a friend in the world to whom one would make such a confidence," Monsieur de Cleves answered, "and would anyone try to clear up his suspicions at the price of telling someone else what he would wish to hide even from himself? Think instead, Madame, of whom you have spoken to. It is more likely that this secret escaped through you than through me. You could not bear alone the awkward position you found yourself in, and you looked for the relief of complaining to some confidante, who has betrayed you."

"Do not finish crushing me," she cried, "and do not be so hard as to accuse me of a fault that you have committed. Can you suspect me of it? Since I was capable of speaking to you, am I capable of speaking to anyone else?"

The confession Madame de Cleves had made to her husband was so great a proof of her sincerity, and she denied so strongly that she had confided in anyone, that Monsieur de Cleves did not know what to think. On the other hand, he was sure he had repeated nothing. It was something no one could have guessed, and yet it was known. So it had to have come from one of the two of them. But what caused him violent pain was knowing that this secret was in someone's hands, and that in all likelihood it would soon be made public.

Madame de Cleves was thinking very nearly the same things. She found it equally impossible that her husband had spoken and that he had not. What Monsieur de Nemours had said, that curiosity could lead a husband into indiscretions, seemed to fit Monsieur de Cleves's state so exactly that she could not believe it had been said by chance. And that likelihood made her decide that Monsieur de Cleves had abused the trust she had placed in him. They were both so taken up with their own thoughts that they were a long time without speaking, and they broke the silence only to repeat the same things they had already said several times over. They were left further apart in heart and in mind, and more changed toward each other, than they had ever been.

It is easy to imagine what kind of night they passed. Monsieur de Cleves had used up all his firmness in bearing the misfortune of seeing a woman he adored in love with another man. He had no courage left. He even thought he had no duty to find any, in a matter where his pride and his honor were so deeply wounded. He no longer knew what to think of his wife. He could no longer see what conduct he ought to require of her, or how he ought to behave himself. On every side he saw nothing but cliffs and chasms. At last, after a very long agitation and uncertainty, and knowing that he would soon have to leave for Spain, he resolved to do nothing that might increase the suspicions or the knowledge of his unhappy state. He went to Madame de Cleves and told her that the question was not which of them had failed to keep the secret. The question was how to show that the story people were telling was a fable in which she had no part. It was in her power to convince Monsieur de Nemours and everyone else of that. She had only to treat him with the severity and coldness she owed to a man who showed her love. By that conduct she would easily rid him of any idea that she had feelings for him. So she need not distress herself over anything he might have thought, because if from now on she showed no weakness, all his thoughts would easily fall apart on their own. And above all, she must go to the Louvre and to the assemblies as usual.

With these words Monsieur de Cleves left his wife without waiting for her answer. She found a great deal of reason in everything he had said, and her anger against Monsieur de Nemours made her believe she would find it easy to carry out. But it seemed hard to her to be present at all the wedding ceremonies, and to appear there with a calm face and an untroubled mind. Still, she was to carry the Queen Dauphine's train, an honor for which she had been chosen over several other princesses, and there was no way to give it up without causing a great deal of talk and making people look for reasons. So she resolved to make an effort over herself. But she took the rest of the day to prepare for it, and to give way to all the feelings that were tormenting her. She shut herself up alone in her private room. Of all her sufferings, the one that pressed on her most violently was that she had reason to complain of Monsieur de Nemours, and could find no way to excuse him. She could not doubt that he had told this story to the Vidame de Chartres; he had admitted it. And from the way he had spoken, she could not doubt either that he knew the story was hers. How could so great an indiscretion be excused, and what had become of the extreme discretion which had touched her so deeply?

"He was discreet," she said to herself, "as long as he believed himself unhappy. But the thought of a happiness, even an uncertain one, put an end to his discretion. He could not imagine that he was loved without wanting it known. He said everything he could say. I did not confess that it was he I loved; he suspected it, and he let his suspicions show. If he had been certain, he would have done exactly the same. I was wrong to believe there was a man capable of hiding what flatters his pride. And yet it is for this man, whom I believed so different from the rest of men, that I find myself like other women, when I am so far from resembling them. I have lost the heart and the respect of a husband who should have been my happiness. Soon I will be looked on by everyone as a woman with a mad and violent passion. The man I have it for no longer doubts it. And it was to avoid these very misfortunes that I risked all my peace of mind, and even my life."

These sad reflections were followed by a flood of tears. But whatever grief weighed her down, she felt that she would have had the strength to bear it, if only she had been satisfied with Monsieur de Nemours.

Nemours was in no calmer state. The indiscretion he had committed in speaking to the Vidame de Chartres, and its cruel consequences, caused him a mortal grief. He could not picture, without being crushed, the awkwardness, the agitation and the distress in which he had seen Madame de Cleves. He was inconsolable at having said things about this story which, however gallant in themselves, now seemed to him coarse and ill-mannered, since they had let Madame de Cleves understand that he knew very well she was the woman with the violent passion, and that he was the man she had it for. All he could have wished for was a conversation with her. But he found that he ought to fear one rather than desire it.

"What could I say to her?" he cried. "Would I go and show her again what I have already made far too plain? Would I let her see that I know she loves me, I who have never even dared to tell her that I love her? Would I begin to speak openly of my passion, and look like a man made bold by his hopes? Can I so much as think of going near her, and would I dare put her through the ordeal of facing me? How could I excuse myself? I have no excuse. I am unworthy of a look from Madame de Cleves, and I have no hope that she will ever look at me again. By my own fault I have given her better weapons against me than any she was looking for, and might have looked for in vain. Through my indiscretion I lose the happiness and the honor of being loved by the most charming and most admirable woman in the world. But if I had lost that happiness without her suffering for it, without having given her a mortal grief, it would be some consolation to me. At this moment I feel the harm I have done her more than the harm I have done myself in her eyes."

Monsieur de Nemours went on grieving and thinking the same thoughts for a long time. The wish to speak to Madame de Cleves kept coming back to him. He thought of ways to manage it; he thought of writing to her. But in the end he decided that after the fault he had committed, and in the mood she was in, the best thing he could do was to show her a deep respect by his grief and his silence, to let her see that he did not even dare appear before her, and to wait for whatever time, chance and her feelings for him might do on his behalf. He also resolved not to reproach the Vidame de Chartres for his breach of faith, for fear of strengthening his suspicions.

The betrothal of Madame Elisabeth, which took place the next day, and the wedding, which took place the day after, so occupied the whole court that Madame de Cleves and Monsieur de Nemours easily hid their sadness and their agitation from the public. The Queen Dauphine mentioned only in passing to Madame de Cleves the conversation they had had with Monsieur de Nemours, and Monsieur de Cleves made a point of not speaking to his wife again about anything that had happened. So she found herself in a less awkward position than she had imagined.

The betrothal took place at the Louvre, and after the banquet and the ball, the whole royal household went to sleep at the Bishop's Palace, as was the custom. In the morning the Duke of Alba, who never dressed in anything but the plainest clothes, put on a coat of cloth of gold mixed with flame color, yellow and black, covered all over with jewels, and on his head he wore a closed crown. The Prince of Orange, just as magnificently dressed and with his liveried servants, and all the Spaniards followed by theirs, came to collect the Duke of Alba at the Hôtel de Villeroy, where he was lodged, and set out four abreast for the Bishop's Palace. As soon as he arrived, everyone went to the church in order of rank. The King led Madame Elisabeth, who also wore a closed crown, with her train carried by Mademoiselle de Montpensier and Mademoiselle de Longueville. The Queen walked next, but without a crown. After her came the Queen Dauphine, Madame Marguerite, the King's sister, Madame de Lorraine, and the Queen of Navarre, their trains carried by princesses. The queens and princesses all had their maids of honor magnificently dressed in the same colors they wore themselves, so that one could tell whose maids they were by the color of their clothes. They climbed onto the platform that had been built in the church, and the marriage ceremonies were performed. Then everyone went back to the Bishop's Palace to dine, and at about five o'clock they left for the Palais, the old royal palace where the Parlement sat, where the banquet was held, and where the Parlement, the sovereign courts and the city magistrates had been invited to attend. The King, the queens, the princes and princesses ate at the marble table in the great hall of the Palais, with the Duke of Alba seated beside the new Queen of Spain. Below the steps of the marble table, on the King's right, was a table for the ambassadors, the archbishops and the Knights of the Order, and on the other side a table for the gentlemen of the Parlement.

The Duke of Guise, in a robe of crimped cloth of gold, waited on the King as Grand Master of his household; the Prince of Condé served his bread, and the Duke of Nemours his wine. When the tables had been cleared, the ball began. It was interrupted by ballets and by extraordinary stage machinery, then taken up again, and at last, after midnight, the King and the whole court went back to the Louvre. Sad as Madame de Cleves was, she still appeared, in everyone's eyes and above all in Monsieur de Nemours's, incomparably beautiful. He did not dare speak to her, though the bustle of the ceremony gave him several chances. But he showed her so much sadness, and such a respectful fear of coming near her, that she no longer found him so guilty, though he had said nothing to excuse himself. He behaved the same way in the days that followed, and that behavior had the same effect on Madame de Cleves's heart.

At last the day of the tournament came. The queens took their places in the galleries and on the stands that had been set aside for them. The four champions appeared at the end of the lists, with such a quantity of horses and liveries that it was the most magnificent spectacle ever seen in France.

The King wore no colors but white and black, which he always wore for Madame de Valentinois, who was a widow. The Duke of Ferrara and all his train were in yellow and red. The Duke of Guise appeared in flesh pink and white. At first no one knew why he had chosen those colors, but then people remembered that they were the colors of a beautiful woman he had loved while she was still unmarried, and whom he loved still, though he no longer dared let her see it. Monsieur de Nemours wore yellow and black, and people looked for the reason in vain. Madame de Cleves had no trouble guessing it. She remembered having said in front of him that she liked yellow, and that she was sorry to be fair, because she could not wear it. Nemours thought he could appear in that color without indiscretion, since Madame de Cleves never wore it, and no one could suspect that it was hers.

Never was such skill shown as the four champions displayed. Though the King was the best horseman in his kingdom, no one knew to whom to give the prize. Monsieur de Nemours had a grace in everything he did that could win over people far less partial than Madame de Cleves. The moment she saw him appear at the end of the lists, she felt an extraordinary emotion, and at every one of his courses she had trouble hiding her joy when he had run it well.

Toward evening, when it was almost over and everyone was about to leave, to the misfortune of the kingdom the King wanted to break one more lance. He sent word to the Count of Montgomery, who was extremely skilled, to take the field against him. The Count begged the King to excuse him, and offered every excuse he could think of. But the King, almost angry, sent word that he absolutely insisted. The Queen sent to the King, begging him to run no more. He had done so well that he ought to be satisfied, and she implored him to come back to her side. He answered that it was for love of her that he was going to run again, and he entered the barrier. She sent the Duke of Savoy to beg him a second time to come back, but it was no use. He ran, the lances shattered, and a splinter from the Count of Montgomery's lance struck him in the eye and stayed there. The King fell from the blow. His equerries, and Monsieur de Montmorency, who was one of the marshals of the field, ran to him. They were stunned to see him so badly hurt. But the King was not alarmed. He said it was nothing much, and that he forgave the Count of Montgomery. One can imagine what agitation and grief such a fatal accident brought to a day meant for rejoicing. As soon as the King had been carried to his bed and the surgeons had examined his wound, they found it very serious. At that moment the Constable remembered the prediction that had been made to the King, that he would be killed in single combat, and he had no doubt that the prediction had come true.

The King of Spain, Philip II, who was then in Brussels, was told of the accident and sent his own physician, a man of great reputation. But he judged the King to be beyond hope.

A court so divided, and so full of opposed interests, was in no small agitation on the eve of so great an event. All the same, every movement was hidden, and people seemed concerned with nothing but their single anxiety for the King's health. The queens, the princes and the princesses hardly ever left his antechamber.

Madame de Cleves knew that she was obliged to be there, that she would see Monsieur de Nemours there, and that she could not hide from her husband the agitation the sight of him caused her. She knew too that his mere presence excused him in her eyes and destroyed all her resolutions. So she decided to pretend to be ill. The court was too busy to pay attention to her conduct, or to work out whether her illness was false or real. Her husband alone could know the truth of it, but she was not sorry for him to know it. So she stayed at home, little concerned with the great change that was coming, and full of her own thoughts, she was entirely free to give way to them. Everyone was at the King's bedside. Monsieur de Cleves came at certain hours to bring her news of him. He treated her exactly as he always had, except that when they were alone there was something a little colder and less open about him. He had not spoken to her again of anything that had happened, and she had not had the strength, and had not even thought it wise, to reopen that conversation.

Monsieur de Nemours, who had expected to find a few moments to speak to Madame de Cleves, was very surprised and very grieved not to have even the pleasure of seeing her. The King's wound proved so serious that on the seventh day the physicians gave him up. He received the certainty of his death with an extraordinary firmness, all the more admirable because he was losing his life by so unlucky an accident, and dying in the flower of his age, happy, adored by his people, and loved by a mistress whom he loved to distraction. On the eve of his death he had Madame Marguerite, his sister, married to the Duke of Savoy, without ceremony. One can imagine what state the Duchess of Valentinois was in. The Queen would not allow her to see the King, and sent to demand from her the King's seals and the crown jewels that she had in her keeping. The Duchess asked whether the King was dead. When she was told that he was not, she answered: "Then I have no master yet, and no one can oblige me to give back what his trust placed in my hands."

As soon as the King had died, at the palace of the Tournelles, the Duke of Ferrara, the Duke of Guise and the Duke of Nemours escorted the Queen Mother, the new King and the Queen his wife to the Louvre. Monsieur de Nemours led the Queen Mother. As they began to walk, she stepped back a few paces and told the Queen, her daughter-in-law, that it was for her to go first. But it was easy to see that there was more bitterness than courtesy in the compliment.
