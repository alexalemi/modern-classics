Part Four

(Part 1 of 3)

The Cardinal of Lorraine had made himself absolute master of the mind of the Queen Mother, Catherine de' Medici. The Vidame de Chartres no longer had any share in her good graces, and his love for Madame de Martigues and for his own freedom had even kept him from feeling the loss as keenly as it deserved to be felt. During the ten days of the King's illness the Cardinal had had time to lay his plans and to bring the Queen round to decisions that suited them. So as soon as the King was dead, the Queen ordered the Constable to stay at the Tournelles, beside the body of the late King, to carry out the customary ceremonies. That duty kept him away from everything and left him no freedom to act. He sent a courier to the King of Navarre, urging him to come at once, so that together they could oppose the great rise in power he saw coming for the Guises. The command of the armies was given to the Duke of Guise, and the treasury to the Cardinal of Lorraine. The Duchess of Valentinois was driven from court. The Cardinal de Tournon, a declared enemy of the Constable, was recalled, and so was the Chancellor Olivier, a declared enemy of the Duchess of Valentinois. In short, the court changed its face entirely. The Duke of Guise took the same rank as the princes of the blood in carrying the King's mantle at the funeral ceremonies. He and his brothers were wholly the masters, not only because of the Cardinal's hold on the Queen's mind, but because she believed she could remove them if they ever gave her cause for uneasiness, whereas she could never remove the Constable, who was backed by the princes of the blood.

When the ceremonies of mourning were over, the Constable came to the Louvre and was received by the young King with great coldness. He wanted to speak with him in private, but the King called the Guises in and told him, in front of them, that he advised him to take his rest; that the treasury and the command of the armies were already given; and that when he needed his advice, he would call him to his side. The Queen Mother received him even more coldly than the King had, and she even reproached him for having told the late King that his children did not look like him. The King of Navarre arrived and was no better received. The Prince of Condé, less patient than his brother, complained loudly, and his complaints were useless: he was sent away from court on the pretext of going to Flanders to sign the ratification of the peace. The King of Navarre was shown a forged letter from the King of Spain accusing him of designs on the Spanish strongholds; he was made to fear for his own lands; and in the end he was brought to the idea of going home to Béarn. The Queen gave him the means by putting him in charge of escorting Madame Elisabeth to Spain, and she even obliged him to set out ahead of her. And so there was no one left at court who could balance the power of the house of Guise.

It was a disappointment for Monsieur de Cleves not to be the one to escort Madame Elisabeth, but he could not complain of it, given the greatness of the man preferred to him. Yet what he regretted was less the honor he would have had from the post than the fact that it would have taken his wife away from court without his appearing to have planned it.

A few days after the King's death, it was decided to go to Reims for the coronation. As soon as the journey was spoken of, Madame de Cleves, who had stayed at home all this time pretending to be ill, asked her husband to agree that she should not follow the court, but should go to Coulommiers for the air and to look after her health. He answered that he did not wish to inquire whether it was really her health that kept her from making the journey, but that he consented to her staying behind. He had no trouble consenting to something he had already decided on himself. Whatever good opinion he had of his wife's virtue, he saw clearly that prudence did not allow him to expose her any longer to the sight of a man she loved.

Monsieur de Nemours soon learned that Madame de Cleves was not to follow the court. He could not bring himself to leave without seeing her, and on the eve of his departure he went to her house as late as propriety allowed, hoping to find her alone. Fortune favored his plan. As he came into the courtyard he met Madame de Nevers and Madame de Martigues on their way out, and they told him they had left her alone. He went up with an agitation and confusion that can only be compared to what Madame de Cleves felt when she was told that Monsieur de Nemours had come to see her. The fear that he would speak to her of his passion, the dread of answering him too kindly, the anxiety the visit might cause her husband, the pain of having to account for it to him or of hiding it all from him: everything came into her mind at once and put her in such an impossible position that she decided to avoid the one thing in the world she perhaps wanted most. She sent one of her women to Monsieur de Nemours, who was waiting in her antechamber, to tell him that she had just been taken ill and was very sorry that she could not receive the honor he wished to do her. What grief for him, not to see Madame de Cleves, and not to see her because she did not want him to. He was leaving the next day; he had nothing more to hope for from chance; he had said nothing to her since that conversation at the Queen Dauphine's, and he had reason to believe that his mistake in speaking to the Vidame had destroyed all his hopes. In short, he went away with everything that can embitter a sharp grief.

As soon as Madame de Cleves had recovered a little from the agitation the thought of his visit had given her, all the reasons that had made her refuse it disappeared. She even felt that she had made a mistake, and if she had dared, or if there had still been time, she would have had him called back.

Madame de Nevers and Madame de Martigues, on leaving her house, went to the Queen Dauphine's. Monsieur de Cleves was there. The Queen Dauphine asked them where they had come from. They said they had come from Monsieur de Cleves's house, where they had spent part of the afternoon with a great many people, and that they had left no one there but Monsieur de Nemours. These words, which they thought so harmless, were not harmless to Monsieur de Cleves, though he might well have imagined that Monsieur de Nemours could often find occasions to speak to his wife. Still, the thought that he was at her house, that he was there alone, and that he could speak to her of his love, seemed to him at that moment something so new and so unbearable that jealousy flared up in his heart more violently than it ever had before. It was impossible for him to stay at the Queen Dauphine's. He went home, not even knowing why he was going, or whether he meant to go and interrupt Monsieur de Nemours. As he came near his house he looked about for anything that might tell him whether Nemours was still there. He felt relief at seeing that he was gone, and found some comfort in thinking that he could not have stayed long. He told himself that perhaps it was not Monsieur de Nemours he ought to be jealous of; and though he had no doubt of it, he tried to doubt. But so many things would have convinced him that he did not stay long in the uncertainty he wished for. He went straight to his wife's room, and after talking with her for a while about ordinary things, he could not keep from asking her what she had done and whom she had seen. She gave him an account. When he saw that she did not name Monsieur de Nemours, he asked her, trembling, whether that was everyone she had seen, so as to give her the chance to name him, and to spare himself the pain of her hiding it from him. Since she had not seen him, she did not name him, and Monsieur de Cleves spoke again in a tone that showed his distress:

"And Monsieur de Nemours," he said. "Didn't you see him, or have you forgotten him?"

"I didn't see him, in fact," she answered. "I was feeling unwell, and I sent one of my women to make my excuses."

"So you were unwell only for him," said Monsieur de Cleves, "since you saw everyone else? Why these distinctions for Monsieur de Nemours? Why isn't he the same to you as any other man? Why must you be afraid of seeing him? Why do you let him see that you're afraid? Why do you let him know that you are using the power his passion gives you over him? Would you dare refuse to see him if you didn't know very well that he can tell your severity from rudeness? But why must you be severe with him at all? From a person like you, Madame, everything is a favor except indifference."

"Whatever suspicions you have about Monsieur de Nemours," said Madame de Cleves, "I didn't think you could reproach me for not having seen him."

"I do reproach you, Madame," he replied, "and with good reason. Why not see him, if he has said nothing to you? But, Madame, he has spoken to you. If his silence alone had told you of his passion, it would not have made so deep an impression on you. You could not tell me the whole truth; you hid the greater part of it from me; you even regretted the little you confessed, and you did not have the strength to go on. I am more unhappy than I thought, and I am the unhappiest of all men. You are my wife, I love you the way a man loves a woman he is courting, and I see you love another man. That other man is the most attractive man at court, and he sees you every day, and he knows that you love him. And I could believe," he cried, "that you would overcome your passion for him! I must have lost my reason to have thought it possible."

"I don't know," said Madame de Cleves sadly, "whether you were wrong to think well of conduct as extraordinary as mine. But I don't know whether I was wrong to believe that you would do me justice."

"Have no doubt of it, Madame," replied Monsieur de Cleves. "You were wrong. You expected things of me as impossible as the things I expected of you. How could you hope that I would keep my reason? Had you forgotten that I loved you desperately, and that I was your husband? Either one can drive a man to extremes; what can the two together not do? And to what extremes they are driving me," he went on. "I have nothing but violent and shifting feelings that I cannot master. I no longer find myself worthy of you; you no longer seem to me worthy of me. I adore you, I hate you; I offend you, I ask your pardon; I admire you, and I am ashamed of admiring you. In short, there is no calm left in me and no reason. I don't know how I have gone on living since you spoke to me at Coulommiers, and since the day you learned from the Queen Dauphine that your story was known. I cannot work out how it became known, or what passed between Monsieur de Nemours and you on the subject. You will never explain it to me, and I do not ask you to. I ask you only to remember that you have made me the unhappiest man in the world."

With these words Monsieur de Cleves left his wife's room, and he set out the next day without seeing her. But he wrote her a letter full of grief, of courtesy, and of tenderness. She answered it so touchingly, and with such assurances about her past conduct and the conduct she would keep in future, that the letter made an impression on Monsieur de Cleves and gave him some calm, for her assurances were founded on the truth and were truly what she felt. Besides, since Monsieur de Nemours was going to join the King just as he was, he had the comfort of knowing that Nemours would not be in the same place as Madame de Cleves. Every time she spoke with her husband, the passion he showed her, the honorableness of his conduct, the affection she felt for him, and what she owed him made impressions on her heart that weakened the thought of Monsieur de Nemours. But it was only for a time, and the thought soon came back more vivid and more present than before.

In the first days after Nemours left, she hardly felt his absence; then it seemed cruel to her. Since she had loved him, not a day had passed on which she had not feared or hoped to meet him, and it was a great pain to her to think that it was no longer in the power of chance to bring them together.

She went to Coulommiers, and on the way she took care to have some large paintings brought there, copies she had had made of the originals that Madame de Valentinois had commissioned for her beautiful house at Anet. All the remarkable deeds of the King's reign were in those paintings. Among them was the siege of Metz, and everyone who had distinguished himself there was painted to the life. Monsieur de Nemours was among them, and that was perhaps what had made Madame de Cleves want the paintings.

Madame de Martigues, who had not been able to leave with the court, promised to come and spend a few days at Coulommiers. The young Queen's favor, which they shared, had given them no envy or dislike of each other. They were friends, though they did not confide their feelings to one another. Madame de Cleves knew that Madame de Martigues loved the Vidame, but Madame de Martigues did not know that Madame de Cleves loved Monsieur de Nemours, or that he loved her. Being the Vidame's niece made Madame de Cleves dearer to Madame de Martigues, and Madame de Cleves loved her too, as someone who was in love as she was, and in love with the closest friend of the man she loved.

Madame de Martigues came to Coulommiers as she had promised, and found her living a very solitary life. Madame de Cleves had even looked for a way to be entirely alone, and to spend her evenings in the gardens without any of her household about her. She would go to the pavilion where Monsieur de Nemours had overheard her, and into the room that opened onto the garden. Her women and her servants stayed in the other room, or below the pavilion, and did not come to her unless she called them. Madame de Martigues had never seen Coulommiers. She was surprised by all the beauty she found there, and above all by the charm of the pavilion, and she and Madame de Cleves spent every evening in it. The freedom of being alone together at night, in the most beautiful place in the world, kept the conversation going endlessly between two young women who had violent passions in their hearts; and though they confided nothing to each other, they took great pleasure in talking. Madame de Martigues would have found it hard to leave Coulommiers if leaving it had not meant going where the Vidame was. She left for Chambord, where the court was then.

The coronation had been performed at Reims by the Cardinal of Lorraine, and the rest of the summer was to be spent at the château of Chambord, which was newly built. The young Queen showed great joy at seeing Madame de Martigues again, and after giving her several signs of it, she asked for news of Madame de Cleves and of what she was doing in the country. Monsieur de Nemours and Monsieur de Cleves were with the Queen at the time. Madame de Martigues, who had found Coulommiers wonderful, described all its beauties, and she dwelt at great length on the pavilion in the forest and on the pleasure Madame de Cleves took in walking there alone for part of the night. Monsieur de Nemours knew the place well enough to follow what Madame de Martigues was saying, and it occurred to him that it might not be impossible to see Madame de Cleves there without being seen by anyone but her. He asked Madame de Martigues a few questions to learn more. Monsieur de Cleves, who had watched him the whole time Madame de Martigues was speaking, thought he could see at that moment what was passing through his mind. The questions Nemours asked confirmed him in the thought, until he had no doubt that Nemours meant to go and see his wife. He was not mistaken in his suspicions. The plan took such hold of Monsieur de Nemours that after spending the night thinking of ways to carry it out, he asked the King's leave the very next morning to go to Paris, on some pretext he invented.

Monsieur de Cleves had no doubt of the purpose of the journey, but he resolved to find out the truth about his wife's conduct and not to remain in a cruel uncertainty. He wanted to leave at the same time as Monsieur de Nemours and to come himself, in secret, to discover how the journey would turn out. But he was afraid his departure would look strange, and that Monsieur de Nemours, once warned of it, would take other measures. So he decided to trust a gentleman in his service whose loyalty and intelligence he knew. He told him the awkward position he was in, told him how virtuous Madame de Cleves had always been until then, and ordered him to set out on Monsieur de Nemours's heels, to watch him closely, and to see whether he went to Coulommiers and whether he entered the garden at night.

The gentleman, who was very capable of such a commission, carried it out with all the exactness imaginable. He followed Monsieur de Nemours to a village a mile or so from Coulommiers, where Nemours stopped, and the gentleman easily guessed that he was waiting there for nightfall. He did not think it wise to wait there too. He went on through the village and into the forest, to the place where he judged Monsieur de Nemours would have to pass. He was right in everything he had supposed. As soon as night came he heard footsteps, and though it was dark, he easily recognized Monsieur de Nemours. He saw him walk all around the garden, as if listening for anyone inside and choosing the place where he could get in most easily. The fences were very high, and there were more behind them to keep anyone from entering, so it was quite difficult to make a way through. Monsieur de Nemours managed it all the same. Once he was in the garden, he had no trouble making out where Madame de Cleves was. He saw a great many lights in the room; all its windows were open; and slipping along the fences, he came up to it with an agitation and emotion that are easy to imagine. He placed himself behind one of the windows, which served as a door, to see what Madame de Cleves was doing. He saw that she was alone, but he saw her of such wonderful beauty that he could barely master the rapture the sight gave him. It was hot, and she had nothing on her head or her throat but her hair, loosely tied back. She was on a daybed, with a table before her on which stood several baskets full of ribbons. She was choosing some of them, and Monsieur de Nemours noticed that they were the very colors he had worn at the tournament. He saw that she was tying them in bows on a very unusual Indian cane, one he had carried for a time and had given to his sister, and which Madame de Cleves had taken from her without seeming to recognize that it had belonged to Monsieur de Nemours. When she had finished her work, with a grace and a sweetness that the feelings in her heart spread over her face, she took a candle and went over to a large table that stood opposite the painting of the siege of Metz, where the portrait of Monsieur de Nemours was. She sat down and began to look at that portrait with an attention and a dreaminess that only passion can give.

What Monsieur de Nemours felt at that moment cannot be expressed. To see, in the middle of the night, in the most beautiful place in the world, a person he adored; to see her without her knowing that he saw her; and to see her wholly taken up with things that had to do with him and with the passion she was hiding from him: this is something no other lover has ever tasted or imagined.

He was so far beside himself that he stood motionless, looking at Madame de Cleves, without a thought for how precious the moments were. When he had recovered a little, he thought he should wait to speak to her until she went out into the garden. He believed he could do it more safely there, because she would be farther from her women. But seeing that she stayed in the room, he resolved to go in. When he tried to do it, what agitation he felt, what fear of displeasing her, what dread of changing a face that held so much sweetness, and of seeing it fill with severity and anger.

He saw that there had been madness, not in coming to see Madame de Cleves without being seen, but in thinking of showing himself to her. He saw everything he had not yet considered. It seemed to him wild boldness to come and surprise, in the middle of the night, a woman to whom he had never yet spoken of his love. He thought he had no right to expect her to listen to him, and that she would be justly angry at the danger he exposed her to from the accidents that might happen. All his courage left him, and several times he was on the point of deciding to go back without showing himself. Still, driven by the desire to speak to her, and reassured by the hopes that everything he had seen gave him, he came forward a few steps; but with so much agitation that a scarf he was wearing caught on the window and made a noise. Madame de Cleves turned her head. Whether because her mind was full of him, or because he stood where the light fell well enough for her to make him out, she thought she recognized him. Without hesitating, and without turning toward where he was, she went into the room where her women were. She came in so agitated that, to hide it, she was forced to say she felt unwell; and she said it also to keep all her people busy, and to give Monsieur de Nemours time to withdraw. When she had thought it over a little, she decided she had been mistaken, and that it was an effect of her imagination to have believed she saw Monsieur de Nemours. She knew he was at Chambord; she could see no likelihood that he had attempted anything so reckless. Several times she wanted to go back into the room and look into the garden to see whether anyone was there. Perhaps she wished as much as she feared to find Monsieur de Nemours; but in the end reason and prudence won out over all her other feelings, and she decided it was better to stay in her doubt than to run the risk of settling it. It took her a long time to make up her mind to leave a place where she thought he might be so near, and it was almost daylight when she went back to the house.

Monsieur de Nemours had stayed in the garden as long as he could see a light. He had not been able to give up hope of seeing Madame de Cleves again, though he was convinced that she had recognized him and had gone out only to avoid him. But when he saw the doors being shut, he knew there was nothing more to hope for. He went back for his horse close to the spot where Monsieur de Cleves's gentleman was waiting. The gentleman followed him to the same village he had set out from that evening. Monsieur de Nemours decided to spend the whole day there, so that he could return to Coulommiers at night and see whether Madame de Cleves would again be so cruel as to flee from him, or to keep herself from being seen. Though he felt a keen joy at having found her so full of the thought of him, he was deeply distressed all the same to have seen her flee from him by so natural an impulse.

Passion has never been so tender and so violent as it was then in Monsieur de Nemours. He went off under some willows, along a little stream that ran behind the house where he was hiding. He went as far as he could, so as to be neither seen nor heard by anyone, and gave himself up to the transports of his love. His heart was so full that he was forced to let a few tears fall; but they were not the tears that grief alone draws out. They were mixed with sweetness, and with that charm which is found only in love.

He began to go back over everything Madame de Cleves had done since he had been in love with her: what honorable and modest severity she had always shown him, though she loved him.

"For she does love me," he said. "She loves me; I cannot doubt it. The greatest promises and the greatest favors are not such sure signs as the ones I have had from her. And yet I am treated with the same severity as if I were hated. I hoped that time would help me; I can expect nothing more from it. I see her always defending herself against me and against herself alike. If I were not loved, I would set about pleasing her; but I do please her, I am loved, and it is hidden from me. What can I hope for, then, and what change can I expect in my fate? What! I am to be loved by the most attractive woman in the world, and I am to have that excess of love which the first certainty of being loved gives, only to feel more sharply the pain of being ill-treated!

"Let me see that you love me, beautiful princess," he cried. "Let me see your feelings. If only I can learn them from you once in my life, I consent to your taking up forever the severity you have crushed me with. At least look at me with the same eyes I saw you look at my portrait with tonight. Can you have looked at it with such sweetness, and fled from me myself so cruelly? What are you afraid of? Why is my love so fearful to you? You love me; you hide it from me in vain; you yourself have given me signs of it without meaning to. I know my happiness; let me enjoy it, and stop making me unhappy.

"Is it possible," he went on, "that I am loved by Madame de Cleves, and that I am unhappy? How beautiful she was tonight! How could I resist the urge to throw myself at her feet? If I had done it, perhaps I would have kept her from fleeing; my respect would have reassured her. But perhaps she did not recognize me. I am grieving more than I should, and the sight of a man at so strange an hour simply frightened her."

These same thoughts occupied Monsieur de Nemours all day. He waited impatiently for night, and when it came, he took the road to Coulommiers again. Monsieur de Cleves's gentleman, who had disguised himself to be less noticeable, followed him to the place where he had followed him the evening before, and saw him go into the same garden. Nemours soon realized that Madame de Cleves had not wanted to risk his trying to see her again: every door was shut. He went around on every side to see whether he could find any light, but it was useless.

Madame de Cleves, suspecting that Monsieur de Nemours might come back, had stayed in her room. She had been afraid that she would not always have the strength to flee from him, and she had not wanted to put herself in danger of speaking to him in a way so unlike the conduct she had kept until then.

Though Monsieur de Nemours had no hope of seeing her, he could not bring himself to leave so soon a place where she was so often. He spent the whole night in the garden, and found some consolation in at least seeing the same things she saw every day. The sun was up before he thought of leaving; but at last the fear of being discovered forced him to go.
