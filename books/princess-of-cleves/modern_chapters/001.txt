Part One

(Part 1 of 3)

Magnificence and gallantry never shone in France so brilliantly as in the last years of the reign of Henri II. The King was a ladies' man, handsome, and in love. His passion for Diane de Poitiers, Duchess of Valentinois, had begun more than twenty years earlier, yet it was no less violent for that, and the proofs he gave of it were no less dazzling.

He excelled at every kind of physical exercise and made it one of his chief occupations. Every day there was hunting or tennis, ballets, riding at the ring, or some such amusement. The colors and the monogram of Madame de Valentinois were everywhere, and she herself appeared in all the finery that her granddaughter, Mademoiselle de la Marck, who was then of an age to marry, might have worn.

The Queen's presence made hers respectable. The Queen, Catherine de' Medici, was beautiful, though no longer in her first youth; she loved grandeur, magnificence, and pleasure. The King had married her while he was still Duke of Orléans and had an elder brother ahead of him, the Dauphin who died at Tournon, a prince whose birth and great qualities marked him out to be a worthy successor to his father, King François I.

The Queen's ambitious nature made reigning very sweet to her. She seemed to bear the King's attachment to the Duchess of Valentinois without pain, and she showed no jealousy of it; but she was so practiced at hiding her feelings that it was hard to tell what they were, and policy obliged her to keep the Duchess close to her, so as to keep the King close as well. The King enjoyed the company of women, even of those he was not in love with. Every day he went to the Queen's apartments at the hour of the circle, her evening reception, where the handsomest men and most beautiful women of the court were always to be found.

No court ever had so many beautiful women and so many admirably handsome men, and nature seemed to have taken pleasure in bestowing her finest gifts on the greatest princesses and the greatest princes. Madame Elisabeth of France, who was later Queen of Spain, was beginning to show a surprising intelligence and that incomparable beauty which was to prove so fatal to her. Mary Stuart, Queen of Scots, who had just married the Dauphin and whom the court called the Queen Dauphine, was perfect in mind and in person. She had been raised at the French court and had acquired all its polish, and she was born with such a gift for every fine thing that, young as she was, she loved and understood such things better than anyone. The Queen, her mother-in-law, and Madame Marguerite, the King's sister, also loved poetry, plays, and music. The taste that King François I had had for poetry and letters still reigned in France; and since his son, the present King, loved physical exercise, every kind of pleasure was to be found at court. But what made this court beautiful and majestic was the endless number of princes and great lords of extraordinary merit. The men I am about to name were, each in his own way, the ornament and the wonder of their age.

The King of Navarre commanded everyone's respect, both by the greatness of his rank and by the greatness that showed in his person. He excelled in war, and the Duke of Guise roused such rivalry in him that more than once he had left his post as general to go and fight beside the Duke, like a common soldier, in the most dangerous places. It is true, too, that the Duke had shown such admirable courage, and won such successes, that no great captain could look at him without envy. His courage was matched by every other great quality: a vast and profound mind, a noble and lofty soul, and an equal talent for war and for affairs of state. The Cardinal of Lorraine, his brother, had been born with boundless ambition, a quick mind, and admirable eloquence, and he had acquired deep learning, which he used to make himself a man of consequence by defending the Catholic religion, then beginning to come under attack. The Chevalier de Guise, who was later called the Grand Prior, was a prince loved by everyone: handsome, witty, skillful, and famous throughout Europe for his courage. The Prince of Condé, though nature had given him a small and unfavored body, had a great and proud soul, and a wit that made him attractive even to the most beautiful women. The Duke of Nevers, whose life had been glorious in war and in the great offices he had held, was the delight of the court, though he was getting on in years. He had three sons, all of them remarkably handsome. The second, known as the Prince of Cleves, was worthy to uphold the honor of his name; he was brave and generous, and he had a prudence rarely found in the young. The Vidame de Chartres, who bore the old title of vidame that had come down in his family, came of that ancient house of Vendôme whose name even the princes of the blood, the royal family's own branches and next in line to the throne, had not disdained to bear. He was as distinguished in love as in war: handsome, fine-looking, brave, bold, and generous, and all these good qualities in him were lively and brilliant. In short, he alone was worthy to be compared with the Duke of Nemours, if anyone could have been compared with him. But Monsieur de Nemours was a masterpiece of nature. The least admirable thing about him was that he was the handsomest and best-built man in the world. What set him above everyone else was an incomparable courage, and a charm in his mind, his face, and everything he did that has never been seen in anyone but him. He had a gaiety that pleased men and women alike, an extraordinary skill in every exercise, a way of dressing that everyone copied and no one could imitate, and, in short, an air about his whole person that made it impossible to look at anyone else wherever he appeared. There was no lady at court whose pride would not have been flattered to see him devoted to her. Few of those he had devoted himself to could boast of having resisted him, and even several to whom he had shown no sign of passion had not been able to keep from feeling one for him. He had so much gentleness and so natural a bent for love affairs that he could not refuse some attentions to the women who tried to please him; so he was courting several women at once, and it was hard to guess which one he truly loved. He often went to the Queen Dauphine's. Her beauty, her sweetness, the trouble she took to please everyone, and the particular regard she showed him had often led people to believe that he dared to raise his eyes to her. The Guises, whose niece she was, had greatly increased their influence and standing through her marriage; their ambition made them aspire to equal the princes of the blood and to share the power of the Constable de Montmorency. The King left most of the business of government in the Constable's hands, and treated the Duke of Guise and the Marshal de Saint-André as his favorites. But whether favor or business brought men close to him, they could keep their place only by submitting to the Duchess of Valentinois; and though she no longer had youth or beauty, she ruled him so absolutely that she may be said to have been mistress of his person and of the state.

The King had always loved the Constable, and as soon as he came to the throne he had recalled him from the exile to which King François I had sent him. The court was divided between the Guises and the Constable, who had the backing of the princes of the blood. Both sides had always tried to win over the Duchess of Valentinois. The Duke of Aumale, the Duke of Guise's brother, had married one of her daughters; the Constable wanted the same alliance. He was not content with having married his eldest son to Madame Diane, the King's own daughter by a lady of Piedmont who had become a nun as soon as the child was born. That marriage had met many obstacles, because of the promises this eldest son, Monsieur de Montmorency, had made to Mademoiselle de Piennes, one of the Queen's maids of honor; and though the King had overcome them with great patience and kindness, the Constable still did not feel secure unless he could win Madame de Valentinois and detach her from the Guises, whose greatness was beginning to make her uneasy. She had put off the Dauphin's marriage to the Queen of Scots as long as she could. She could not bear the beauty and the able, precocious mind of that young queen, or the rise the marriage gave the Guises. She especially hated the Cardinal of Lorraine; he had spoken to her bitterly and even with contempt, and she saw that he was forming ties with the Queen. So the Constable found her willing to join him and to seal their alliance by marrying her granddaughter, Mademoiselle de la Marck, to Monsieur d'Anville, his second son, who later succeeded to his office under Charles IX. The Constable did not expect to find Monsieur d'Anville as unwilling to marry as Monsieur de Montmorency had been; but though the reasons were hidden from him, the difficulties were hardly smaller. Monsieur d'Anville was desperately in love with the Queen Dauphine, and little hope as he had in that passion, he could not bring himself to make a commitment that would divide his attentions. The Marshal de Saint-André was the only man at court who had taken no side. He was one of the favorites, and his favor was his own: the King had loved him since the days when he was Dauphin, and later made him a Marshal of France at an age when men do not usually hope for even the smallest honors. His favor gave him a brilliance that he sustained by his merit and his personal charm, by the refinement of his table and his furnishings, and by the greatest magnificence ever seen in a private man. The King's generosity paid for all this; he was lavish to the point of extravagance with those he loved. He did not have every great quality, but he had several, above all a love and understanding of war, and so he had been fortunate in it. Apart from the battle of Saint-Quentin, his reign had been an unbroken series of victories. He had won the battle of Renty in person; Piedmont had been conquered; the English had been driven out of France; and the Emperor Charles V had seen his good fortune come to an end before the city of Metz, which he had besieged in vain with all the forces of the Empire and of Spain. Nevertheless, since the disaster of Saint-Quentin had dimmed our hopes of further conquest, and since fortune had seemed ever since to divide itself between the two kings, both of them had gradually come round to thinking of peace.

The Dowager Duchess of Lorraine had begun to make proposals for it at the time of the Dauphin's marriage, and ever since there had always been some secret negotiation under way. At last Cercamp, in Artois, was chosen as the place where the parties would meet. The Cardinal of Lorraine, the Constable de Montmorency, and the Marshal de Saint-André went for the King; the Duke of Alba and the Prince of Orange for Philip II; and the Duke and Duchess of Lorraine were the mediators. The chief terms were the marriage of Madame Elisabeth of France to Don Carlos, the Infante of Spain, and that of Madame Marguerite, the King's sister, to the Duke of Savoy.

Meanwhile the King stayed on the frontier, and there he received the news of the death of Mary Tudor, Queen of England. He sent the Count of Randan to Queen Elizabeth to congratulate her on coming to the throne, and she received him gladly: her claim was so shaky that it was to her advantage to be recognized by the King. The Count found her well informed about the interests of the French court and the merits of the people in it; but above all he found her so full of the Duke of Nemours's reputation, and she spoke to him of the Duke so often and so eagerly, that when Monsieur de Randan came back and reported on his journey, he told the King there was nothing Monsieur de Nemours might not hope for from that queen, and that he had no doubt she would be willing to marry him. The King spoke to Monsieur de Nemours about it that very evening. He had Monsieur de Randan tell him every conversation he had had with Elizabeth, and advised him to try for this great fortune. Monsieur de Nemours thought at first that the King was not speaking seriously; but when he saw that he was, he said:

"At least, Sire, if I embark on a wild venture on Your Majesty's advice and in your service, I beg you to keep it secret until success justifies me in the eyes of the public, and to be so good as not to make me look vain enough to imagine that a queen who has never seen me would want to marry me for love."

The King promised to speak of the plan to no one but the Constable; indeed, he thought secrecy necessary to its success. Monsieur de Randan advised Monsieur de Nemours to go to England on the simple pretext of traveling, but he could not bring himself to do it. Instead he sent Lignerolles, a clever young man who was his favorite, to sound out the Queen's feelings and try to open some connection with her. While he waited to hear how that journey turned out, he went to see the Duke of Savoy, who was then in Brussels with the King of Spain. Queen Mary's death put great obstacles in the way of peace. The conference broke up at the end of November, and the King returned to Paris.

Then a beauty appeared at court who drew every eye, and we must believe she was a perfect beauty, since she roused admiration in a place so used to seeing beautiful women. She was of the same family as the Vidame de Chartres, and one of the greatest heiresses in France. Her father had died young and left her in the care of his wife, Madame de Chartres, whose wealth, virtue, and merit were extraordinary. After losing her husband, she had spent several years away from court. During that time she had devoted herself to her daughter's upbringing; but she did not work only to cultivate her mind and her beauty. She also set out to give her virtue, and to make virtue attractive to her. Most mothers imagine that never speaking of love affairs in front of young girls is enough to keep them away from such things. Madame de Chartres held the opposite opinion. She often described love to her daughter. She showed her what was pleasant in it, the more easily to persuade her of what she taught her was dangerous in it. She told her how little sincerity men have, how they deceive and how they betray, and what domestic misery such entanglements bring a woman; and on the other side she showed her the peace that comes with the life of a virtuous woman, and how much brilliance and dignity virtue gives to a woman who has beauty and birth. But she also showed her how hard it was to keep that virtue, except by an extreme distrust of oneself and by holding fast to the one thing that can make a woman happy, which is to love her husband and to be loved by him.

This heiress was then one of the greatest matches in France, and though she was extremely young, several marriages had already been proposed for her. Madame de Chartres, who was extremely proud, found almost nothing good enough for her daughter. When the girl was in her sixteenth year, she decided to bring her to court. On her arrival the Vidame went out to meet her. He was struck by Mademoiselle de Chartres's great beauty, and with good reason. The fairness of her skin and her golden hair gave her a radiance that has never been seen in anyone but her; all her features were regular, and her face and her whole person were full of grace and charm.

The day after she arrived, she went to have some jewels matched at the house of an Italian who dealt in them all over the world. He had come from Florence with the Queen and had grown so rich in his trade that his house looked more like a great lord's than a merchant's. While she was there, the Prince of Cleves came in. He was so struck by her beauty that he could not hide it, and Mademoiselle de Chartres could not help blushing when she saw how astonished he was. She recovered herself all the same, and paid him no more attention than courtesy required toward a man of the rank he appeared to be. Monsieur de Cleves looked at her with admiration, unable to think who this beautiful person could be whom he did not know. He could tell from her bearing, and from all her attendants, that she must be of very high rank. Her youth made him think she was unmarried; but since he saw no mother with her, and heard the Italian, who did not know her either, call her "madame," he did not know what to think, and he kept looking at her in astonishment. He noticed that his gaze embarrassed her, unlike most young girls, who always enjoy seeing the effect of their beauty; it even seemed to him that he was the reason she was in a hurry to leave, and in fact she left quite soon. Monsieur de Cleves consoled himself for losing sight of her with the hope of finding out who she was; but he was very surprised to learn that no one knew her. He was so moved by her beauty, and by the modesty he had noticed in everything she did, that one may say he conceived from that moment an extraordinary passion and esteem for her. That evening he went to see Madame Marguerite, the King's sister. She was held in high regard because of her influence over her brother the King, and that influence was so great that in making peace the King agreed to give back Piedmont so that she could marry the Duke of Savoy. Though she had wanted to marry all her life, she had never been willing to marry anyone but a sovereign; for that reason she had refused the King of Navarre when he was Duke of Vendôme, and had always wished for the Duke of Savoy, for whom she had kept a fondness ever since she saw him at Nice, at the meeting of King François I and Pope Paul III. Because she had great intelligence and a fine judgment of beautiful things, she drew all the people of taste and breeding to her, and at certain hours the whole court was to be found in her apartments. Monsieur de Cleves came as usual. He was so full of the intelligence and beauty of Mademoiselle de Chartres that he could talk of nothing else. He told everyone what had happened to him, and never tired of praising this person he had seen and did not know. Madame Marguerite told him there was no one like the person he described, and that if there were, everyone would know her. Madame de Dampierre, her lady of honor and a friend of Madame de Chartres, overheard this conversation, went up to Madame Marguerite, and told her quietly that it must surely have been Mademoiselle de Chartres whom Monsieur de Cleves had seen. Madame Marguerite turned back to him and said that if he would come again the next day, she would show him the beauty who had made such an impression on him. Mademoiselle de Chartres did indeed appear the next day. The Queen and the Queen Dauphine received her with every imaginable mark of favor, and everyone admired her so much that she heard nothing but praise around her. She received it with such noble modesty that she seemed not to hear it, or at least not to be moved by it. Then she went to Madame Marguerite's. After praising her beauty, Madame Marguerite told her how astonished Monsieur de Cleves had been. He came in a moment later.

"Come," she said to him, "see whether I haven't kept my word, and whether, in showing you Mademoiselle de Chartres, I'm not showing you the beauty you were looking for. At least thank me for having told her how much you admired her already."

Monsieur de Cleves was overjoyed to see that the person he had found so charming was of a rank to match her beauty. He went up to her and begged her to remember that he had been the first to admire her, and that without even knowing her he had felt for her all the respect and esteem that were her due.

He and the Chevalier de Guise, who were friends, left Madame Marguerite's together. At first they praised Mademoiselle de Chartres freely; then they felt they were praising her too much, and both stopped saying what they thought of her. But in the days that followed they could not avoid talking about her wherever they met. This new beauty was long the subject of every conversation. The Queen praised her highly and treated her with extraordinary consideration; the Queen Dauphine made her one of her favorites and begged Madame de Chartres to bring her often; the King's daughters sent for her to join in all their amusements. In short, the whole court loved and admired her, except Madame de Valentinois. Not that this beauty gave her any uneasiness; long experience had taught her that she had nothing to fear where the King was concerned. But she hated the Vidame de Chartres so much, having wanted to bind him to herself by marrying him to one of her daughters only to see him attach himself to the Queen instead, that she could not look kindly on a girl who bore his name and to whom he showed great affection.

The Prince of Cleves fell passionately in love with Mademoiselle de Chartres and longed to marry her; but he was afraid that Madame de Chartres's pride would be wounded at giving her daughter to a man who was not the eldest son of his house. Yet that house was so great, and the Count of Eu, its eldest son, had just married a lady so close to the royal family, that his fears came from the timidity love brings rather than from any real reason. He had a great many rivals. The one who seemed most formidable to him was the Chevalier de Guise, because of his birth, his merit, and the brilliance that royal favor lent his house. The Chevalier had fallen in love with Mademoiselle de Chartres the first day he saw her; he had noticed Monsieur de Cleves's passion, just as Monsieur de Cleves had noticed his. Though they were friends, the coolness that comes of wanting the same thing had kept them from speaking openly to each other, and their friendship had cooled without either of them having the courage to have it out. Having been the first to see Mademoiselle de Chartres seemed to Monsieur de Cleves a happy omen, and seemed to give him some advantage over his rivals; but he foresaw great obstacles from his father, the Duke of Nevers. The Duke had close ties with the Duchess of Valentinois; she was the Vidame's enemy, and that alone was enough to keep the Duke of Nevers from ever consenting to his son's thinking of the Vidame's niece.

Madame de Chartres, who had taken such pains to instil virtue in her daughter, did not relax that care in a place where it was so necessary and where there were so many dangerous examples. Ambition and love affairs were the soul of this court, and occupied men and women alike. There were so many interests and so many different cabals, and the ladies played so large a part in them, that love was always mixed with politics and politics with love. No one was calm or indifferent. Everyone was thinking of rising, of pleasing, of helping or of harming; no one knew boredom or idleness, and everyone was always busy with pleasures or intrigues. The ladies had their particular loyalties: to the Queen, to the Queen Dauphine, to the Queen of Navarre, to Madame Marguerite, the King's sister, or to the Duchess of Valentinois. Personal liking, a sense of what was proper, or a likeness of temperament decided these loyalties. Those who were past their first youth and professed a stricter virtue attached themselves to the Queen. The younger ones, who were looking for gaiety and romance, paid court to the Queen Dauphine. The Queen of Navarre had her favorites too: she was young, and she had influence over her husband the King, who was allied with the Constable and had great credit through him. Madame Marguerite still had some beauty, and drew several ladies to her. The Duchess of Valentinois could have any woman she deigned to look at; but few women pleased her, and except for a few who were her intimates and confidantes, and whose temper resembled her own, she received ladies only on the days when she chose to hold a court like the Queen's.

All these cabals were rivals and envied one another. The ladies who made them up were also jealous of each other, over favor or over suitors; and the interests of rank and advancement were often bound up with those other interests, which mattered less but were felt no less keenly. So there was a kind of agitation without disorder at this court, which made it very pleasant, but also very dangerous for a young woman. Madame de Chartres saw the danger and thought of nothing but how to protect her daughter from it. She asked her, not as her mother but as her friend, to confide in her everything men said to court her, and she promised to help her find her way in matters where a young person is so often at a loss.

The Chevalier de Guise made his feelings and his intentions toward Mademoiselle de Chartres so plain that everyone knew of them. Yet he saw nothing but impossibility in what he wanted. He knew very well that he was no fit match for Mademoiselle de Chartres, with so little fortune to support his rank; and he knew too that his brothers would not want him to marry, for fear of the decline that the marriages of younger sons usually bring on great houses. The Cardinal of Lorraine soon showed him that he was not mistaken. He condemned the Chevalier's attachment to Mademoiselle de Chartres with extraordinary heat, but he did not tell him the real reasons. The Cardinal hated the Vidame, a hatred that was secret then and broke out later. He would rather have seen his brother make any other alliance than one with the Vidame, and he declared so publicly how far he was from consenting that Madame de Chartres was deeply offended. She took great pains to show that the Cardinal of Lorraine had nothing to fear and that she had no thought of this marriage. The Vidame did the same, and he resented the Cardinal of Lorraine's conduct even more than Madame de Chartres did, because he understood its cause better.
