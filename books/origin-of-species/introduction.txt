Charles Darwin published this book in November 1859, at fifty, having sat on its argument for twenty years. He had worked the idea out in the late 1830s, not long after coming home from five years as the naturalist aboard the Beagle; he wrote a short sketch of it in 1842 and a longer essay in 1844, showed the essay to one or two friends, and then went on collecting facts. What ended the delay was a letter. In 1858 Alfred Russel Wallace, who was collecting specimens in the Malay Archipelago and knew nothing of Darwin's manuscripts, sent him an essay laying out the same theory. Extracts from both men were read to the Linnean Society that summer, and Darwin wrote up what he called an abstract of his unfinished big book. The abstract is this volume of five hundred pages.

The argument is simple enough to state in a paragraph, and Darwin states it early. More individuals are born than can possibly survive. They vary, and some of the variation is inherited. Any variation that helps its owner survive and breed will therefore tend to spread, and over enough generations the accumulated changes will make a new kind of thing. The same process, run long enough and in enough directions, gives every living species a common ancestry.

What takes the other four hundred and ninety pages is evidence, and the evidence is the book. Darwin bred pigeons himself and devotes pages to their skeletons. He had spent eight years dissecting barnacles. He measures the cells of a honeycomb, watches ants that raid other nests for slaves, counts seedlings on a plot of turf, and floats seeds in salt water for weeks to see whether they will still germinate afterwards. He also raises every objection he can think of before his opponents can, and answers them at length: the eye, the missing intermediate forms, the gaps in the fossil record, the sterility of hybrids, instincts that look too complicated to have been assembled a piece at a time. That habit of arguing against himself, more than any single fact in the book, is why it persuaded people.

He barely mentions human beings. There is one sentence near the end saying that light will be thrown on the origin of man and his history, and that is all; he waited twelve years and wrote a separate book about it.

This is the sixth edition of 1872, the last Darwin revised. It is fuller and more defensive than the famous first edition: there is an extra chapter answering critics by name, a historical sketch at the front crediting the people who had got part of the way there before him, and Herbert Spencer's phrase "survival of the fittest" now used alongside natural selection. The closing page, about a tangled bank with birds and insects and worms in it, is the best-known thing Darwin wrote and is worth having read.
