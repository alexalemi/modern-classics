Appendix XV: Calculation of the Intensity of the Beats of Different Intervals

{¶415d}(See pp. {@187|187a} and note ⁎ and 193, note ⁎.)

We shall again employ the formulae for the strength of the sympathetic vibration developed in Appendix IX., equations (4a) and (4b), p. {@402c|402c}, and (5) and (5a), p. {@402d|402d}. For the tone of strongest resonance in one of Corti's elementary organs, let \(n\) be its number of vibrations in \(2\pi\) seconds, \(n_1\) and \(n_2\) the corresponding numbers of vibrations in \(2\pi\) seconds for two tones heard, and \(B'\), \(B''\) the greatest velocities of the vibrations which they superinduce in those Corti's organs which have the same pitch, and \(B_1\), \(B_2\) the greatest velocities which both attain in their {¶416}representation of the number of vibrations \(n\). Then by equation (5a) of Appendix IX., p. {@402d|402d}, we have \[B_1 = B' \sin\epsilon_1, \text{ and } B_2 = B'' \cdot \sin\epsilon_2\] where \[\pi \cdot \tan\epsilon_1 = \frac{\beta}{\frac{n}{n_1} - \frac{n_1}{n}}, \text{ and } \pi \cdot \tan\epsilon_2 = \frac{\beta}{\frac{n}{n_2} - \frac{n_2}{n}},\] and \(\beta\) is a magnitude which may be regarded as independent of \(n\). Hence the intensity of the vibrations of the organ for the number of vibrations \(n\), when both tones \(n_1\) and \(n_2\) affect it simultaneously, fluctuates between the values \[(B_1 + B_2)^2 \text{ and } (B_1 - B_2)^2.\]

The difference of these two magnitudes, which measures the strength of the {¶416b}beats, is \[4\,B_1\,B_2 = 4\,B'\,B'' \cdot \sin\epsilon_1 \cdot \sin\epsilon_2 \tag{7}\]

Hence for equal differences in the amount of pitch, the strength of the beats is dependent on the product \(B'\,B''\). For the *m*th partial tone of the compound tone of a violin, we may, by Appendix VI., p. 597c, put \(B'^2 = \frac{A'^2}{m^2}\), and hence if the *m₁*th and *m₂*th partial tone of two compound tones of a violin, beat, we may put the intensity of their beats for equal differences of interval \(= \frac{A'^2}{m_1 \cdot m_2}\).

This is the expression from which the numbers in the last column of the table on p. {@187b|187b} have been calculated. [They are therefore 100 times the reciprocals of the products of the two numbers which give the ratio of the pitch numbers in the corresponding line of its third column.]

For the calculation of the degree of roughness of the various intervals, mentioned in pp. 193, 332, and 333, the following abbreviations of notation are {¶416c}introduced: \[\begin{aligned} n_1 + n_2 &= 2N. \\ n_1 &= N(1 + \delta). \\ n_2 &= N(1 - \delta). \\ n &= N(1 + \nu). \end{aligned}\] So that \[\pi \cdot \tan\epsilon_1 = \frac{\beta}{\frac{1 + \nu}{1 + \delta} - \frac{1 + \delta}{1 + \nu}} \text{ and } \pi \cdot \tan\epsilon_2 = \frac{\beta}{\frac{1 + \nu}{1 - \delta} - \frac{1 - \delta}{1 + \nu}}\]

Since powerful sympathetic resonance ensues only when \(\nu\) and \(\delta\) are very small, we may assume that, approximatively, \[\tan\epsilon_1 = \frac{\beta}{2\pi(\nu - \delta)}, \text{ and } \tan\epsilon_2 = \frac{\beta}{2\pi(\nu + \delta)}.\]

{¶416d}Putting these values for \(\epsilon_1\) and \(\epsilon_2\), in equation (7) we have \[4\,B_1\,B_2 = 4\,B'\,B'' \cdot \frac{\beta^2}{\sqrt{[\beta^2 + 4\pi^2 \cdot (\nu - \delta)^2]} \cdot \sqrt{[\beta^2 + 4\pi^2 \cdot (\nu + \delta)^2]}} \tag{7a}\]

If then we consider \(\nu\), that is, the pitch of the Corti's organ which vibrates sympathetically, to be variable, \(4\,B_1\,B_2\) will reach its maximum when \(\nu = 0\), and hence \(n = N = \tfrac{1}{2}(n_1 + n_2)\), and if we call the value of this maximum \(s\) we have \[s = 4\,B'\,B'' \cdot \frac{\beta^2}{\beta^2 + 4\pi^2\delta^2} \tag{7b}\]

In calculating the degree of roughness arising from sounding two tones together which differ from each other by the interval \(2\delta\), I have thought it sufficient to consider this maximum value, which exists in those Corti's organs which are most favourably situated. Undoubtedly other beats of a weaker kind will be excited in {¶417}the neighbouring fibres, but their intensity rapidly diminishes. It might therefore appear to be a more exact process to integrate the value of \(4\,B_1\,B_2\) in equation (7a) with respect to \(\nu\), in order to obtain the sum of the beats in all Corti's organs. This would require an at least approximate knowledge of the density of Corti's organs for different values of \(\nu\), that is, for different parts of the scale, and of that we know nothing. In sensation, the highest degree of roughness is certainly more important than the distribution of a less degree of roughness over many sensitive organs. Hence I have preferred to take only the maximum of the vibrations from (7b) into account.

Finally we have to consider that very slow beats cause no roughness, and that when the intensity of the beats remains unaltered, and their number increases, the roughness reaches a maximum and then decreases. To express this, the value of \(s\) must be also multiplied by a factor, which vanishes when the number of beats is small, attains a maximum for about 30 beats in a second, and then diminishes, and again vanishes when the number of such beats is infinite. Suppose then that {¶417b}the roughness \(r_p\), due to the *p*th partial tone, is expressed by \[r_p = \frac{4\,\theta^2 \cdot \delta^2 \cdot p^2}{(\theta^2 + p^2 \cdot \delta^2)^2} \cdot s_p.\] The factor of \(s_p\) reaches its maximum value \(= 1\), when \(p\delta = \theta\); and becomes \(= 0\), when \(\delta\), that is, half the interval between the two tones in the scale, is \(= 0\) or \(\infty\). Since \(\delta\) may be indifferently positive or negative, the expression can only involve even powers of \(\delta\). The above is the simplest expression which satisfies the conditions, but it is of course to a certain extent arbitrary.

For \(\theta\) we must put half the extent of the interval which at the pitch of the lower beating tone causes 30 beats to be made in a second.

Since we have taken *c′* with 264 vibrations in a second, as the lower tone, \(\theta\) has been put \(= \frac{15}{264}\). Hence we have finally {¶417c}\[r_p = 16\,B'\,B'' \cdot \frac{\beta^2\theta^2\delta^2p^2}{(\beta^2 + 4\pi^2\delta^2)(\theta^2 + p^2\delta^2)}.\]

And from this formula I have calculated the roughness of the intervals, shewn graphically in the diagrams, fig. 60, A and B, p. {@193b|193b}, {@193c|c}, and fig. 61, p. {@333|333a}. The roughnesses due to the separate partial tones have been drawn separately and superimposed on one another in the drawing.

Although the theory leaves much to be desired in the matter of exactness, it at least serves to shew that the theoretical view we have proposed is really capable of explaining such a distribution of dissonances and consonances as actually occurs in nature.

Professor Alfred M. Mayer, of Hoboken, New Jersey,⁎ has instituted experiments on the duration of sensations of sound, and the number of audible beats. Between a vibrating tuning-fork and its resonator he interposed a revolving disc with openings of the same shape as that of the resonator, so that the sound was heard loudly when an opening in the disc came in front of that in the resonator, and faintly when the latter was covered. His results agree essentially with the {¶417d}assumptions I have made on pp. 143 to 145, and 183 to 185, but are more complete as they have been pursued throughout the whole scale.
