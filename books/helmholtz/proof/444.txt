Then by equation (5a) of Appendix IX., p. 402d, we have \[B_1 = B' \sin\epsilon_1, \text{ and } B_2 = B'' \cdot \sin\epsilon_2\] where \[\pi \cdot \tan\epsilon_1 = \frac{\beta}{\frac{n}{n_1} - \frac{n_1}{n}}, \text{ and } \pi \cdot \tan\epsilon_2 = \frac{\beta}{\frac{n}{n_2} - \frac{n_2}{n}},\] and \(\beta\) is a magnitude which may be regarded as independent of \(n\). Hence the intensity of the vibrations of the organ for the number of vibrations \(n\), when both tones \(n_1\) and \(n_2\) affect it simultaneously, fluctuates between the values \[(B_1 + B_2)^2 \text{ and } (B_1 - B_2)^2.\]

The difference of these two magnitudes, which measures the strength of the ¶ beats, is \[4\,B_1\,B_2 = 4\,B'\,B'' \cdot \sin\epsilon_1 \cdot \sin\epsilon_2 \tag{7}\]

Hence for equal differences in the amount of pitch, the strength of the beats is dependent on the product \(B'\,B''\). For the *m*th partial tone of the compound tone of a violin, we may, by Appendix VI., p. 597c, put \(B'^2 = \frac{A'^2}{m^2}\), and hence if the *m₁*th and *m₂*th partial tone of two compound tones of a violin, beat, we may put the intensity of their beats for equal differences of interval \(= \frac{A'^2}{m_1 \cdot m_2}\).

This is the expression from which the numbers in the last column of the table on p. 187b have been calculated. [They are therefore 100 times the reciprocals of the products of the two numbers which give the ratio of the pitch numbers in the corresponding line of its third column.]

For the calculation of the degree of roughness of the various intervals, mentioned in pp. 193, 332, and 333, the following abbreviations of notation are ¶ introduced: \[\begin{aligned} n_1 + n_2 &= 2N. \\ n_1 &= N(1 + \delta). \\ n_2 &= N(1 - \delta). \\ n &= N(1 + \nu). \end{aligned}\] So that \[\pi \cdot \tan\epsilon_1 = \frac{\beta}{\frac{1 + \nu}{1 + \delta} - \frac{1 + \delta}{1 + \nu}} \text{ and } \pi \cdot \tan\epsilon_2 = \frac{\beta}{\frac{1 + \nu}{1 - \delta} - \frac{1 - \delta}{1 + \nu}}\]

Since powerful sympathetic resonance ensues only when \(\nu\) and \(\delta\) are very small, we may assume that, approximatively, \[\tan\epsilon_1 = \frac{\beta}{2\pi(\nu - \delta)}, \text{ and } \tan\epsilon_2 = \frac{\beta}{2\pi(\nu + \delta)}.\]

¶ Putting these values for \(\epsilon_1\) and \(\epsilon_2\), in equation (7) we have \[4\,B_1\,B_2 = 4\,B'\,B'' \cdot \frac{\beta^2}{\sqrt{[\beta^2 + 4\pi^2 \cdot (\nu - \delta)^2]} \cdot \sqrt{[\beta^2 + 4\pi^2 \cdot (\nu + \delta)^2]}} \tag{7a}\]

If then we consider \(\nu\), that is, the pitch of the Corti's organ which vibrates sympathetically, to be variable, \(4\,B_1\,B_2\) will reach its maximum when \(\nu = 0\), and hence \(n = N = \tfrac{1}{2}(n_1 + n_2)\), and if we call the value of this maximum \(s\) we have \[s = 4\,B'\,B'' \cdot \frac{\beta^2}{\beta^2 + 4\pi^2\delta^2} \tag{7b}\]

In calculating the degree of roughness arising from sounding two tones together which differ from each other by the interval \(2\delta\), I have thought it sufficient to consider this maximum value, which exists in those Corti's organs which are most favourably situated.
