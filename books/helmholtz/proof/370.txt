This position of the chord of the Seventh, deduced from the closest positions of the triads, is regarded as fundamental or primary. The intervals between the individual tones appear as Thirds, and when chords of the Seventh are formed from the consonant triads of the scale, these Thirds will be alternately major and minor, because consonant triads always unite a major with a minor Third. Hauptmann calls these chords of the Seventh which occur spontaneously in the natural ¶ series of Thirds of a key \[f + a_1 - c + e_1 - g + b_1 - d\] the *chords of the direct system* or simply *direct chords*. There are two kinds of these chords. In one a minor Third lies between two major Thirds, as in the tetrad *c* + *e₁* − *g* + *b₁* already cited, and similarly in *f* + *a₁* − *c'* + *e₁'* in *C* major, and *A¹*♭ + *c* − *e¹*♭ + *g* in *C* minor. In the other a major Third lies between two minor Thirds, as in

B. *Chords of the Seventh formed of Dissonant Triads.*

Other chords of the Seventh may be formed from the dissonant triads of the key, each united with one consonant triad, and also from the two dissonant triads themselves. By thus uniting the limiting tones of the series of chords in the key, \[\begin{array}{ll} \text{major}: & c + e_1 - g + b_1 - d \mid f + a_1 - c \\ \text{and minor}: & c - e^1\flat + g + b_1 - d \mid f - a^1\flat + c \end{array}\] we obtain the following *Chords of the Seventh in the reverted system*, or *indirect* ¶ *tetrads*:
