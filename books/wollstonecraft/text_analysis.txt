A VINDICATION OF THE RIGHTS OF WOMAN (1792) — TRANSLATION STRATEGY
Mary Wollstonecraft. Source: Standard Ebooks, from the second edition.
21 files, 84,349 source words, 13 chapters plus the Dedication and the
Introduction.

READ FIRST: mill/text_analysis.txt and subjection/text_analysis.txt.
Same decade of English, same publisher, same pipeline, and the whole
class-3 diagnosis carries over. This book is three years older than
On Liberty is younger, and it is the book Mill's is the sequel to.


1. WHY THIS BOOK, AND WHAT KIND OF DIFFICULT IT IS
---------------------------------------------------------------------
The measured screening put it at calque 35.0 with 42% of sentences over
35 words, against an archaism score low enough that on that axis alone
it would have been struck from the roadmap. That is the hume/mill/burke
profile exactly: NOTHING IN IT SOUNDS OLD. There is no thou, no hath,
almost no vocabulary a reader would think to look up. What blocks it is

  (a) eighteenth-century periodic sentence architecture — a claim, three
      qualifications, a metaphor and a conclusion, all inside one full
      stop; and
  (b) a technical vocabulary used with total consistency, every word of
      which is still current English meaning something else.

The false friends are the real danger, and there are more of them than
in either Mill:

    SEXUAL CHARACTER   the book's central term, and it does NOT mean
                       what a modern reader will assume. It means a
                       character proper to one's sex — the doctrine
                       that women have a nature of their own, distinct
                       from human nature. Chapter Two's title is about
                       this and nothing else. Get it wrong and the book
                       becomes unreadable in a way the reader will
                       blame on the author.
    SENSIBILITY        capacity for feeling, treated as a faculty. Not
                       sensibleness, not sensitivity in the modern
                       therapeutic sense.
    MANNERS            the whole moral texture of a society (as Burke).
                       "A revolution in female manners" is not about
                       etiquette.
    ACCOMPLISHMENTS    the finishing-school skills — music, drawing,
                       French, needlework. A technical term of the
                       period's education debate.
    UNDERSTANDING      the intellect, as a faculty.
    DELICACY           refined feeling, and, of a woman, sexual
                       propriety. She uses both senses and plays them
                       against each other.
    PREJUDICE          inherited opinion (Burke's sense), not bigotry.
    VIRTUE             moral excellence generally, and NOT, when used
                       of a woman, chastity — except where she is
                       reporting other people's usage, which she does
                       often and pointedly.
    PROPRIETY / DECORUM  what is fitting. Keep.
    CONDESCENSION      graciousness from a superior. Not contempt.
    ARTIFICIAL         made by art, contrived. Not fake-looking.
    OBTAIN            (occasionally) hold good, prevail.
    SUFFER            allow.
    WANT              lack.

AND THERE IS A SECOND, STRONGER JUSTIFICATION, the one that makes this
worth doing even though Mill's is on the shelf. This book has a
reputation that stands in for reading it. Everyone knows it is the
first great feminist argument; far fewer know that it is furious,
funny, personal, sometimes unfair, and that its central claim is not
"women are equal" but something harder and more interesting: THE
CHARACTER OF WOMEN AS IT EXISTS IS AN ARTEFACT OF THEIR EDUCATION, and
nobody has any idea what women are like because no one has ever let
them find out. In 1792 prose a reader files that as a period document.
That misreading is what the retelling exists to prevent.


2. THE GOVERNING RULE, WHICH IS THE REVERSE OF THIS PROJECT'S USUAL ONE
---------------------------------------------------------------------
IF A SENTENCE IS ALREADY CLEAR, LEAVE IT ALONE (the hume rule).

Wollstonecraft is a far better writer than her reputation, and her
famous sentences need nothing:

    "Taught from their infancy, that beauty is woman's sceptre, the
     mind shapes itself to the body, and, roaming round its gilt cage,
     only seeks to adorn its prison."

There is nothing to modernise there and every change makes it worse.
Most must_contain pins are pinned UNCHANGED for exactly this reason.

AND SHE ASKS FOR IT HERSELF, which no other author in this collection
does. The Introduction states her own style rule:

    "I shall not waste my time in rounding periods, nor in fabricating
     the turgid bombast of artificial feelings... I shall be employed
     about things, not words!"

She wrote the book in six weeks and refused to polish it. So the work
here is NOT to make her elegant. It is to unstack the sentences where
the stack has collapsed — and she does lose her thread, especially in
Chapters Four and Five, where a sentence will run 120 words and change
its subject twice. Unstack those. Leave the rest.

A file in which every sentence has been rewritten has been damaged.


3. VOICE
---------------------------------------------------------------------
She is arguing with several people at once and the register shifts with
the target:

  - TO TALLEYRAND (the Dedication): formal, but not deferential for a
    sentence. She is a woman with no standing writing to a French
    statesman to tell him his education plan is a disgrace. "I plead
    for my sex, not for myself."
  - TO MEN IN GENERAL: sardonic, and much ruder than expected. Men who
    write conduct books are "sensualists", "tyrants of every
    denomination", and the attentions they pay women are "insultingly
    supporting their own superiority".
  - TO WOMEN: this is the surprising one, and it must not be softened.
    She is often savage about women — vain, cunning, "insignificant
    objects of desire; mere propagators of fools" — and the whole force
    of the argument depends on it, because her point is that this is
    what the education PRODUCED, not what women are. Flatten her
    contempt into sympathy and you delete the argument.
  - TO ROUSSEAU: personal. She admires him and is betrayed by him, and
    Chapter Five Section One is a lover's quarrel conducted in public.
  - TO HERSELF: the book is full of sudden first-person breaks — "A
    wild wish has just flown from my heart to my head, and I will not
    stifle it though it may excite a horse laugh." KEEP EVERY ONE.
    They are the best thing in the book.

KEEP THE METAPHORS. They are compressed argument, not ornament: the
gilt cage, the flowers planted in too rich a soil, the rattle that must
jingle in his ears, the "iron sceptre of tyranny". Losing them costs
more than losing a clause.


4. QUOTED MATTER — TWO RULES, AND THE LINE BETWEEN THEM IS DELIBERATE
---------------------------------------------------------------------
She quotes constantly, and mostly in order to attack.

  VERSE IS VERBATIM. Eleven blockquotes — Milton, Dryden, Pope,
  Shakespeare. It is the property of the language, half of it is what
  she is arguing with, and a reader must be able to see exactly what
  she was given to answer. Milton's "God is thy law, thou mine" is
  pinned in must_contain for that reason. Keep the attributions.

  QUOTED PROSE IS MODERNISED WITH HER OWN, in the same register, inside
  her quotation marks and with the attribution untouched. This is the
  one place this book departs from the lecture-volumes rule that quoted
  matter stays as printed, and the reason is that here the quotation is
  not decoration but the thing under examination. Chapter Five is 16,000
  words of Rousseau, Fordyce, Gregory and Chesterfield quoted at length
  and then taken apart line by line; if the quotations stay in 1762
  prose while her replies are modern, the reader can follow the reply
  and not the charge, which is the exact failure the retelling exists
  to prevent. Two further reasons it is safe: Rousseau's Emile is
  quoted in an eighteenth-century ENGLISH TRANSLATION, so the archaism
  there is the translator's costume and not the author's (the nights/
  and boethius reasoning); and she is often quoting to ridicule, which
  requires the reader to understand the words.
  NOTHING IS SOFTENED IN THE QUOTATIONS. Rousseau on women existing to
  please men, Gregory advising a daughter to conceal her learning and
  to dissemble even good health, Fordyce's "sermons" — all at full
  length and at full strength. They are the evidence.


5. STRUCTURE, AND WHAT IS NEW WRITING
---------------------------------------------------------------------
CHAPTER TITLES ARE HERS. Standard Ebooks prints a bare Roman numeral,
but the 1792 book titles every chapter, and the titles were recovered
from Gutenberg #3420's contents list (cross-checked, not remembered).
They are therefore MODERNISED like any other sentence of hers — the
ball/ rule for a source that captions its own plates — and every
original is logged beside its modern form in running_notes.txt.

SECTION TITLES ARE PART NEW WRITING. Chapters Five and Thirteen are
divided into numbered sections with no titles at all ("Section 5.4").
Each keeps its number and gains a short descriptive tail, written from
the section's own opening sentence. In Chapter Five this is nearly free,
because each section takes one writer.

ALL 37 FOOTNOTES ARE HERS, inlined as "Footnote: ..." after the citing
paragraph, IN HER VOICE — several are jokes and one is the best line in
its chapter.

ELEVEN OF THE TWENTY-ONE FILES ARE CHAPTER PARTS, so the quixote trap is
live: heading line, "(Part n of k)", blank, body. Parts 2+ never
re-introduce the chapter and non-final parts never conclude it.


6. SENSITIVE CONTENT — THE VERNE RULE, WITH ONE CLASS DECIDED THE OTHER
   WAY AND THE DISTINCTION WRITTEN DOWN
---------------------------------------------------------------------
KEPT ENTIRE AND UNHEDGED, with no note anywhere in the volume:

  - THE PHYSICAL-INFERIORITY CONCESSION. In the Introduction she grants
    that "in the government of the physical world... the female, in
    general, is inferior to the male", calls it a noble prerogative,
    and builds her argument on top of the concession. Modern readers
    find this the most surprising thing in the book. It is load-bearing
    — the whole structure is "grant that, and nothing else follows" —
    and softening it would replace her argument with one she did not
    make.
  - HER CONTEMPT FOR WOMEN AS THEY ARE. See VOICE above. "Mere
    propagators of fools", "only fit for the seraglio", the standing
    charge of cunning. It stands.
  - THE TWO "MUHAMMADAN" PASSAGES, where she uses the European
    commonplace that Islam denies women souls as her stick to beat
    Milton with. The CLAIM is hers and is part of her subject, so it
    stands (the Verne rule); the WORD is dated vocabulary and is
    modernised, as mill/ does with Mahomedans -> Muslims.
  - THE RELIGION. She is a Dissenting Christian arguing from immortal
    souls and a rational God, and she means it. Do not secularise her.
  - HER CLASS POSITION. She addresses "those in the middle class,
    because they appear to be in the most natural state", and is
    withering about the rich and frequently condescending about the
    poor. Both stand.
  - THE SLAVERY VOCABULARY, which is everywhere and is not decorative:
    slaves, bondage, tyrants, "convenient slaves", the seraglio. She is
    writing in the middle of the British abolition campaign and the
    comparison is deliberate. It is never softened, and the reader is
    left to notice what she is claiming.

There is no passage here in the class that made mill/'s and burke/'s
one exception — no racial noun standing in for a relationship. The
sweep for one found nothing.
