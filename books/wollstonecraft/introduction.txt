Mary Wollstonecraft wrote this book in six weeks at the beginning of 1792, in London, at thirty-two. She had run a school at Newington Green, worked as a governess, and was by then earning her living by her pen, writing and reviewing for the publisher Joseph Johnson — a rare position for a woman of her time. Two years earlier she had published a reply to Edmund Burke's Reflections on the Revolution in France, which is also in this collection. This book carries that argument on to the other half of the population. She dedicated it to Talleyrand, whose recent report on national education in France had proposed that girls be taught at home and stop at eight, and the dedication tells him to his face that he handled the subject too quickly.

One phrase will trip a modern reader in silence. Wollstonecraft writes about "sexual character" constantly, and it is the subject of her second chapter. It does not mean what it now means; it means a character proper to one's sex — the set of qualities a woman was held to have by nature, as distinct from the qualities of a human being. The modern reading is not merely wrong, it is obscene, and it will quietly wreck whole pages. A few of her other words have also moved: sensibility is the capacity for feeling, accomplishments are the drawing and music and scraps of French that a girl was finished with, manners means the whole moral texture of a society, and want means lack.

Her actual claim is harder than her reputation. It is not that women are equal to men. It is that nobody knows what women are like, because nobody has ever let them find out. What passes for the nature of women is the product of an upbringing that tells a girl from infancy that her business is to please, denies her serious work, and then points at the result as proof. Her demand is not for privilege but for the thing that makes privilege unnecessary: an education in reason, work that pays, a civil existence in the state, and national schools where girls and boys are taught together.

About a third of the book is quotation. She sets out Rousseau on the education of Sophia, Dr. Gregory advising his daughters to hide any learning they have, and Dr. Fordyce's sermons, and then takes them apart line by line. Those quotations are modernized alongside her own prose, so the charge stays as followable as the reply; quoted verse is left as printed. The chapter titles here are hers, recovered from the 1792 printing. She wrote fast and would not polish, and says so: she will be employed about things, not words.

Her argument did not stop here. John Stuart Mill's On Liberty of 1859 and his The Subjection of Women, written in 1861 and published in 1869, are both in this collection, and Mill's case for opening every occupation to women begins where hers does.
