Introduction

I have looked back over the pages of history and watched the living world with anxious care, and the result has been the most melancholy kind of sorrowful indignation. It has weighed my spirits down. I have sighed as I was forced to admit that either nature has made a great difference between one human being and another, or else the civilization the world has achieved so far has been very partial indeed. I have turned over book after book written on education, and patiently observed how parents behave and how schools are run. And what has come of it? A deep conviction that the neglected education of my fellow creatures is the great source of the misery I am deploring — and that women in particular are made weak and wretched by a whole set of causes working together, all of them springing from one hasty conclusion.

The conduct and manners of women are proof enough that their minds are not in a healthy state. Like flowers planted in soil that is too rich, they have had strength and usefulness sacrificed to beauty, and the showy leaves, having pleased a fastidious eye, fade unnoticed on the stalk long before the season when they should have come to maturity. One cause of this barren blooming I put down to a false system of education, gathered out of books written on the subject by men who, treating women as women rather than as human creatures, have been more anxious to make them alluring mistresses than rational wives. Women's understanding has been so taken in by this plausible flattery that the civilized women of this century, with a few exceptions, are anxious only to inspire love, when they ought to be cherishing a nobler ambition and demanding respect for their abilities and their virtues.

In a book about the rights and manners of women, then, the works written specifically to improve them cannot be passed over — particularly when the charge is stated in so many words: that women's minds are weakened by false refinement; that books of instruction written by men of genius have had the same tendency as far more frivolous productions; and that, in the true Muslim style, women are considered only as females and not as part of the human species, at a time when improvable reason is allowed to be the dignified distinction that raises men above the animals and puts a natural sceptre into a feeble hand.

Still, because I am a woman, I would not have my readers suppose that I mean to stir up the disputed question of the equality or inferiority of the sexes. But the subject lies in my path, and I cannot step over it without laying the main drift of my reasoning open to misunderstanding, so I shall stop a moment to give my opinion in a few words. In the government of the physical world it is observable that the female is generally inferior to the male. The male pursues, the female yields — that is the law of nature, and it does not appear to have been suspended or repealed in favour of woman. This physical superiority cannot be denied, and it is a noble prerogative! But men, not content with this natural pre-eminence, work to sink us lower still, merely to make us alluring objects for a moment; and women, intoxicated by the adoration that men pay them under the influence of their senses, do not try to gain any lasting hold on their hearts, or to become the friends of the fellow creatures who find them amusing company.

I am aware of the obvious retort. From every quarter I have heard people exclaiming against masculine women — but where are they to be found? If by that name men mean to attack women's enthusiasm for hunting, shooting and gambling, I shall join in the cry most heartily. But if it is meant against women imitating manly virtues — or, to put it more properly, acquiring those talents and virtues whose exercise ennobles the human character, and which raise females in the scale of living beings when they are given the comprehensive name of mankind — then everyone who looks at the matter with a philosophical eye must, I should think, wish with me that they may every day grow more and more masculine.

This discussion divides the subject naturally. I shall first consider women in the grand light of human creatures who, like men, are placed on this earth to unfold their faculties; and afterwards I shall point out more particularly what is peculiar to them.

I want also to steer clear of a mistake that many respectable writers have fallen into. The instruction addressed to women so far has really been meant for *ladies* — with the exception of the little indirect advice scattered through Sandford and Merton. Speaking to my own sex in a firmer tone, I pay particular attention to those in the middle class, because they seem to me to be in the most natural condition. Perhaps the seeds of false refinement, immorality and vanity have always been scattered by the great. Weak, artificial beings, raised above the common wants and affections of their kind in a premature and unnatural way, undermine the very foundation of virtue and spread corruption through the whole mass of society. As a class they have the strongest claim on our pity: the education of the rich tends to make them vain and helpless, and the unfolding mind is not strengthened by practising the duties that give the human character its dignity. They live only to amuse themselves, and by the same law that produces certain effects everywhere in nature, they very soon become merely barren amusement.

But since I intend to look separately at the different ranks of society and at the moral character of women in each, this hint is enough for the present. I have touched on the subject only because it seems to me the very essence of an introduction to give some quick account of what the book it introduces contains.

My own sex, I hope, will forgive me if I treat them as rational creatures instead of flattering their *fascinating* graces and viewing them as though they were in a state of perpetual childhood, unable to stand alone. I want in earnest to point out where true dignity and human happiness lie. I want to persuade women to try to acquire strength, of mind and of body, and to convince them that the soft phrases — susceptibility of heart, delicacy of sentiment, refinement of taste — are very nearly synonyms for weakness, and that beings who are only objects of pity, and of the kind of love that has been called pity's sister, will soon become objects of contempt.

Dismissing, then, those pretty feminine phrases that men use so condescendingly to soften our slavish dependence, and despising that weak elegance of mind, that exquisite sensibility and sweet docility of manners supposed to be the marks of the weaker vessel, I want to show that elegance is inferior to virtue; that the first object of laudable ambition is to obtain a character as a human being, whatever one's sex; and that every secondary aim should be brought to that simple test.

This is a rough sketch of my plan. And if I express my conviction with the energy I feel whenever I think about the subject, then what experience and reflection have taught me will be felt by some of my readers. With that important object in view, I shall not stoop to pick over my phrases or polish my style. I aim at being useful, and sincerity will keep me unaffected. Wishing to persuade by the force of my arguments rather than dazzle by the elegance of my language, I shall not waste my time rounding off periods, or manufacturing the swollen bombast of artificial feelings, which comes from the head and never reaches the heart. I shall be employed about things, not words! And, anxious to make my sex more respectable members of society, I shall try to avoid that flowery diction which has slid out of essays into novels, and out of novels into ordinary letters and conversation.

These pretty nothings, these caricatures of the real beauty of feeling, drop so glibly off the tongue that they spoil the taste and create a kind of sickly delicacy which turns away from plain unadorned truth. A flood of false sentiment and overstretched feeling stifles the natural emotions of the heart and makes insipid those domestic pleasures which ought to sweeten the exercise of the severe duties that educate a rational and immortal being for a nobler field of action.

The education of women has been more attended to lately than it used to be; yet they are still reckoned a frivolous sex, and are ridiculed or pitied by the very writers who try, by satire or by instruction, to improve them. Everyone admits that they spend the first years of their lives acquiring a smattering of accomplishments — a little music, a little drawing, a little French, a little needlework. Meanwhile strength of body and mind is sacrificed to libertine notions of beauty and to the desire of establishing themselves in the only way a woman can rise in the world, which is by marriage. And this desire makes mere animals of them, so that when they do marry they behave as such children may be expected to behave: they dress; they paint, and give God's creatures new names. Surely such weak beings are fit for nothing but the harem! Can they govern a household, or take care of the poor babies they bring into the world?

If, then, it can be fairly concluded from the present conduct of my sex — from the prevailing fondness for pleasure, which takes the place of ambition and of those nobler passions that open and enlarge the soul — that the instruction women have received has only worked, along with the constitution of civil society, to render them insignificant objects of desire, mere breeders of fools; if it can be proved that in aiming to give them accomplishments without cultivating their understandings we take them out of their proper sphere of duties and make them ridiculous and useless once the short-lived bloom of beauty is over, then I presume that *rational* men will excuse me for trying to persuade women to become more masculine and respectable.

Indeed the word masculine is only a bogey. There is little reason to fear that women will acquire too much courage or fortitude. Their apparent inferiority in bodily strength must make them to some degree dependent on men in the various relations of life; but why should that dependence be increased by prejudices which give a sex to virtue and confuse simple truths with sensual daydreams?

Women have in fact been so degraded by mistaken notions of female excellence that I am not adding a paradox when I say that this artificial weakness produces a tendency to tyrannize, and gives birth to cunning, the natural opponent of strength, which leads them to play off those contemptible childish airs that undermine esteem even while they excite desire. Stop fostering these prejudices and women will fall naturally into their subordinate, but still respectable, place in life.

It hardly needs saying that I am speaking here of the sex in general. Many individual women have more sense than their male relatives; and since nothing outweighs anything else in a constant struggle for balance unless it naturally has more gravity, some women govern their husbands without degrading themselves, because intellect will always govern.
