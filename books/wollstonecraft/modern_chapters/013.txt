Chapter Eight: How Morality Is Undermined by Ideas About a Woman's Reputation

It occurred to me long ago that the advice about behaviour, and all the various ways of preserving a good reputation, which have been so strenuously drummed into the female world, were plausible poisons that encrusted morality and ate away its substance — and that this measuring of shadows produced a false calculation, since a shadow's length depends so much on the height of the sun and on other accidental circumstances.

Where does a courtier's easy, deceptive manner come from? From his situation, undoubtedly; for, needing dependants, he is obliged to learn the art of refusing without giving offence, and of feeding hope evasively on the chameleon's diet of air. So politeness plays with truth, and, eating away the sincerity and humanity natural to a man, produces the fine gentleman.

Women in the same way acquire, from a supposed necessity, an equally artificial way of behaving. Yet truth is not to be played with unpunished, for the practised dissembler ends by becoming the dupe of his own arts. He loses that shrewdness which has justly been called common sense — a quick perception of the common truths that an unsophisticated mind always accepts as truths, even if it would not have had energy enough to discover them for itself when they were obscured by local prejudice. Most people take their opinions on trust to save themselves the trouble of exercising their own minds, and these indolent beings naturally cling to the letter rather than the spirit of a law, divine or human. "Women," says an author whose name I cannot recall, "do not mind what only heaven sees." And why indeed should they? It is the eye of man they have been taught to dread; and if they can lull their Argus to sleep they seldom think about heaven or about themselves, because their reputation is safe. And it is reputation, not chastity with all its fair train, that they are employed in keeping spotless — not as a virtue, but to preserve their station in the world.

To prove the truth of this remark I need only point to the intrigues of married women, particularly in high life, and in countries where women are married off suitably to their respective ranks by their parents. If an innocent girl falls prey to love, she is degraded forever, though her mind has not been polluted by the arts that married women practise under the convenient cloak of marriage, and though she has violated no duty except the duty of respecting herself. The married woman, on the contrary, breaks a most sacred engagement, and becomes a cruel mother when she becomes a false and faithless wife. If her husband still has any affection for her, the arts she must practise to deceive him will make her the most contemptible of human beings; and in any case the contrivances necessary to keep up appearances will keep her mind in that childish or vicious turmoil which destroys all its energy. Besides, in time, like people who habitually take cordials to raise their spirits, she will need an intrigue to give life to her thoughts, having lost all relish for pleasures that are not highly seasoned with hope or fear.

Sometimes married women behave still more audaciously. I will mention an instance.

A woman of quality, notorious for her gallantries, though nobody chose to place her in the class where she belonged since she still lived with her husband, made a point of treating with the most insulting contempt a poor timid creature who was abashed by the memory of her own former weakness, and whom a neighbouring gentleman had seduced and afterwards married. This woman had actually confused virtue with reputation; and I do believe she prided herself on the propriety of her behaviour before marriage, though once she was settled to her family's satisfaction, she and her lord were equally faithless — so that the half-alive heir to an immense estate came from heaven knows where.

To look at the subject in another light.

I have known a number of women who, if they did not love their husbands, loved nobody else either, giving themselves up entirely to vanity and dissipation and neglecting every domestic duty — even squandering money that should have been saved for their helpless younger children — and who yet plumed themselves on an unsullied reputation, as though the whole compass of their duty as wives and mothers were simply to preserve it. Other indolent women, neglecting every personal duty, have thought they deserved their husbands' affection because in this one respect they behaved with propriety.

Weak minds are always fond of resting in the ceremonies of duty, but morality offers much simpler motives; and it would have been better if superficial moralists had said less about behaviour and outward observances. For unless virtue of any kind is built on knowledge, it will produce nothing but a kind of insipid decency. Respect for the opinion of the world has, however, been called a woman's chief duty in so many words, for Rousseau declares "that reputation is no less indispensable than chastity". "A man," he adds, "secure in his own good conduct, depends only on himself and may brave public opinion; but a woman in behaving well performs only half her duty, since what is thought of her is as important to her as what she really is. It follows that the system of a woman's education should in this respect be directly contrary to ours. Opinion is the grave of virtue among men, but its throne among women." It is strictly logical to infer that a virtue resting on opinion is merely worldly, and that it is the virtue of a being to whom reason has been denied. But even as far as the opinion of the world goes, I am convinced that this class of reasoners is mistaken.

This regard for reputation, quite apart from being one of the natural rewards of virtue, took its rise from a cause I have already deplored as the grand source of female depravity: the impossibility of regaining respectability by returning to virtue, while men keep theirs throughout the indulgence of vice. It was natural, then, for women to try to preserve what, once lost, was lost forever, until this one care swallowed up every other, and a reputation for chastity became the one thing needful. But the scrupulousness of ignorance is vain; for neither religion nor virtue, when they live in the heart, requires such childish attention to mere ceremonies, since behaviour must on the whole be proper when the motive is pure.

I can produce very respectable authority in support of my opinion; and the authority of a cool reasoner ought to carry weight enough to enforce consideration, if not to establish a sentiment. Speaking of the general laws of morality, Dr. Smith observes: "By some very extraordinary and unlucky circumstance a good man may come to be suspected of a crime he was altogether incapable of, and may on that account be most unjustly exposed for the rest of his life to the horror and aversion of humankind. By an accident of this kind he may be said to lose everything, in spite of his integrity and justice, in the same way that a cautious man may be ruined by an earthquake or a flood in spite of the utmost circumspection. Accidents of the first kind, however, are perhaps rarer still, and still more contrary to the common course of things, than those of the second; and it remains true that the practice of truth, justice and humanity is a certain and almost infallible method of acquiring what those virtues chiefly aim at, the confidence and love of the people we live among. A person may easily be misrepresented with regard to a particular action; but it is scarcely possible that he should be misrepresented with regard to the general tenor of his conduct. An innocent man may be believed to have done wrong; this, however, will rarely happen. On the contrary, the established opinion of the innocence of his conduct will often lead us to acquit him where he really has been at fault, in spite of very strong presumptions."

I entirely agree with this writer, for I truly believe that few people of either sex have ever been despised for certain vices without deserving to be. I am not speaking of the slander of the moment, which hangs over a character like one of the dense November fogs over this city until it gradually clears in the common light of day. I only maintain that the daily conduct of most people stamps their character with the impression of truth. Quietly the clear light, shining day after day, refutes the ignorant guess or the malicious tale that has thrown dirt on a pure character. A false light distorted its shadow, reputation, for a short time; but the shadow seldom fails to become true again once the cloud that caused the trick of vision has dispersed.

Many people, undoubtedly, obtain a better reputation in various respects than, strictly speaking, they deserve, for unremitting industry will generally reach its goal in every kind of race. Those who strive only for this paltry prize, like the Pharisees who prayed at the street corners to be seen by men, do truly get the reward they were after; for the heart of one human being cannot be read by another. Still, the fair fame that is naturally reflected by good actions, when a man is only concerned to direct his own steps rightly and pays no attention to onlookers, is generally not only truer but surer.

There are trials, it is true, in which a good man must appeal to God against the injustice of men, and, amid the whining candour or the hissing of envy, must build a pavilion in his own mind to retire into until the rumour has passed over. The darts of undeserved censure may indeed pierce an innocent, tender heart through with many sorrows. But these are all exceptions to the general rule, and it is by the common laws that human behaviour ought to be regulated. The eccentric orbit of a comet never affects astronomical calculations about the unvarying order established in the motion of the principal bodies of the solar system.

I will venture to say, then, that once a man has reached maturity, the general outline of his character in the world is a just one, allowing for the exceptions I have mentioned. I do not say that a prudent, worldly-wise man with nothing but negative virtues and qualities may not sometimes get a smoother reputation than a wiser or a better man. So far from it, that I am inclined to conclude from experience that where two people's virtue is roughly equal, the more negative character will be better liked by the world at large, while the other may have more friends in private life. But the hills and valleys, the clouds and sunshine so conspicuous in the virtues of great men set each other off; and though they give envious weakness a fairer mark to shoot at, the real character will still work its way into the light, however bespattered by weak affection or ingenious malice.

As for the anxiety to preserve a hard-earned reputation, which leads shrewd people to analyse it, I shall not make the obvious comment; but I am afraid that morality is very insidiously undermined in the female world by attention being turned to the show instead of the substance. A simple thing is thus made strangely complicated; indeed, virtue and its shadow are sometimes set at odds. We should probably never have heard of Lucretia if she had died to preserve her chastity instead of her reputation. If we really deserve our own good opinion we shall generally be respected in the world; but if we long for higher improvement and higher attainments, it is not enough to see ourselves as we suppose others see us, though this has been ingeniously argued to be the foundation of our moral sentiments — because every bystander has his own prejudices, besides the prejudices of his age and country. We should rather try to see ourselves as we suppose that Being sees us who watches each thought ripen into action, and whose judgment never swerves from the eternal rule of right. Righteous are all his judgments, and as just as they are merciful.

The humble mind that seeks to find favour in his sight, and calmly examines its conduct when only his presence is felt, will seldom form a very mistaken opinion of its own virtues. In the still hour of self-collection, the angry brow of offended justice will be fearfully deprecated, or else the tie that draws a human being to the Deity will be recognized in that pure sentiment of reverential adoration which swells the heart without stirring up any tumultuous emotion. In these solemn moments a man discovers in himself the germ of the vices that, like the Java tree, shed a pestilential vapour all round them — death is in the shade! — and he sees them without abhorrence, because he feels himself drawn by some cord of love to all his fellow creatures, whose follies he is anxious to find every excuse for in their nature, and in his own. If I, he may argue, who exercise my own mind and have been refined by tribulation, find the serpent's egg in some fold of my own heart and crush it only with difficulty, shall I not pity those who were stamped with less vigour, or who heedlessly nursed the insidious reptile until it poisoned the vital stream it fed on? Can I, conscious of my own secret sins, cast off my fellow creatures and watch calmly while they drop into the chasm of perdition that yawns to receive them? No, no. The agonized heart will cry out with suffocating impatience: I too am human, and have vices, hidden perhaps from every human eye, that bend me to the dust before God, and that tell me loudly when everything else is silent that we are formed of the same earth and breathe the same air. Humanity rises naturally out of humility in this way, and twists the cords of love that wind about the heart in so many convolutions.

This sympathy reaches further still, until a man is well pleased to see force in arguments that carry no conviction to his own breast, and gladly puts in the fairest light for himself the shows of reason that have led others astray, glad to find some reason in all the errors of humankind, though he was convinced already that He who rules the day makes his sun shine on everyone. Yet even while shaking hands, as it were, with corruption, with one foot on the earth, the other mounts with bold strides to heaven and claims kinship with higher natures. Virtues unnoticed by men drop their balmy fragrance at this cool hour, and the thirsty land, refreshed by the pure streams of comfort that suddenly gush out, is crowned with smiling green. This is the living green on which that eye may look with pleasure which is too pure to look on iniquity.

But my spirits are flagging, and I must silently indulge the reverie these reflections lead to, unable to describe the feelings that have calmed my soul when, watching the rising sun, a soft shower drizzling through the leaves of the neighbouring trees seemed to fall on my languid but tranquil spirits, cooling a heart that had been heated by the passions reason was labouring to tame.

The leading principles running through all my discussions would make it unnecessary to enlarge on this subject, if a constant attention to keeping the varnish of the character fresh and in good condition were not so often taught as the sum total of a woman's duty, and if rules for regulating the behaviour and preserving the reputation did not so frequently supersede moral obligations. But where reputation is concerned, all the attention is confined to a single virtue: chastity. If a woman's honour, as it is absurdly called, is safe, she may neglect every social duty, and even ruin her family by gambling and extravagance, and still present a shameless front — for truly she is an honourable woman.

Mrs. Macaulay has justly observed that "there is only one fault a woman of honour may not commit with impunity." She then adds, justly and humanely: "This has given rise to the trite and foolish observation that a woman's first fault against chastity has a radical power to deprave her character. But no such frail beings come out of the hands of nature. The human mind is built of nobler materials than to be corrupted so easily; and with all their disadvantages of situation and education, women seldom become entirely abandoned until they are thrown into a state of desperation by the venomous rancour of their own sex."

But in proportion as this regard for a reputation of chastity is prized by women, it is despised by men; and the two extremes are equally destructive to morality.

Men are certainly more under the influence of their appetites than women, and their appetites are more depraved by unbridled indulgence and by the fastidious contrivances of satiety. Luxury has introduced a refinement in eating that destroys the constitution, and a degree of gluttony so beastly that all sense of seemliness must be worn away before one human being could eat immoderately in the presence of another and afterwards complain of the oppression his own intemperance naturally produced. Some women, and Frenchwomen particularly, have also lost their sense of decency here, for they will talk very calmly about an indigestion. It is to be wished that idleness were not allowed to breed on the rank soil of wealth those swarms of summer insects that feed on putrefaction; then we should not be disgusted by the sight of such brutal excesses.

There is one rule about behaviour that I think ought to govern every other, and it is simply to cherish such a habitual respect for humankind as will keep us from disgusting a fellow creature for the sake of some present indulgence. The shameful indolence of many married women, and of others a little advanced in life, often leads them to sin against delicacy. For though they are convinced that the person is the bond of union between the sexes, how often, out of sheer indolence or to enjoy some trifling indulgence, do they disgust?

The depravity of the appetite that brings the sexes together has had a still more fatal effect. Nature must always be the standard of taste, the gauge of appetite — and yet how grossly the voluptuary insults nature. Leaving the refinements of love out of the question, nature, by making the gratification of this appetite, like every other, a natural and imperious law for preserving the species, exalts the appetite and mixes a little mind and affection with a sensual relish. The feelings of a parent, mingling with an instinct that is merely animal, give it dignity; and, since the man and the woman often meet on the child's account, a mutual interest and affection is excited by the exercise of a common sympathy. Women, then, having some duty to fulfil nobler than adorning their persons, would not be content to be the slaves of a casual appetite, which is the present situation of a very considerable number of them who are, quite literally, standing dishes that any glutton may help himself from.

I may be told that, great as this enormity is, it affects only a devoted portion of my sex — devoted for the salvation of the rest. But quite apart from the falsity, easily proved, of any assertion that recommends sanctioning a small evil to produce a greater good, the mischief does not stop there. The moral character and peace of mind of the chaster part of the sex is undermined by the conduct of the very women they allow no refuge from guilt; whom they inexorably consign to practising the arts that lure their husbands from them, debauch their sons, and force them — let no modest woman start at this — to take on something of the same character themselves. For I will venture to assert that all the causes of female weakness, and of female depravity, that I have already enlarged on branch out of one grand cause: the want of chastity in men.

This intemperance, so prevalent, depraves the appetite to such a degree that a wanton stimulus is needed to rouse it; but nature's parental design is forgotten, and the mere person, and that only for a moment, engrosses the thoughts. Indeed the lustful prowler often grows so voluptuous that he refines on female softness.

To satisfy this taste in men, women are made systematically voluptuous; and though they may not all carry their libertinism to the same length, this heartless intercourse that men allow themselves with the sex depraves both sexes, because men's taste is vitiated, and women of every class naturally square their behaviour to gratify the taste through which they obtain pleasure and power. Women, consequently becoming weaker in mind and body than they ought to be if one of the grand ends of their being were taken into account, the bearing and nursing of children, have not strength enough to discharge a mother's first duty; and, sacrificing to lasciviousness the parental affection that ennobles instinct, they either destroy the embryo in the womb or cast it off once it is born. Nature demands respect in everything, and those who violate her laws seldom violate them with impunity. The weak, enervated women who particularly catch a libertine's attention are unfit to be mothers though they may conceive; so that the rich sensualist who has rioted among women, spreading depravity and misery, receives from his wife, when he wishes to perpetuate his name, nothing but a half-formed being that inherits both its father's and its mother's weakness.

In contrasting the humanity of the present age with the barbarism of antiquity, great stress has been laid on the savage custom of exposing children whom their parents could not maintain; while the man of sensibility who perhaps makes the complaint produces by his promiscuous amours a most destructive barrenness and a contagious viciousness of manners. Surely nature never intended that women, by satisfying an appetite, should defeat the very purpose that appetite was implanted for.

I have observed before that men ought to maintain the women they have seduced; this would be one means of reforming female manners and stopping an abuse that has an equally fatal effect on population and on morals. Another, no less obvious, would be to turn women's attention to the real virtue of chastity. For a woman has little claim to respect on the score of modesty, though her reputation may be white as driven snow, if she smiles on the libertine while she spurns the victims of his lawless appetites and their own folly.

Besides, pure as she thinks herself, she has a taint of the same folly when she studiously adorns her person only to be looked at by men, to excite respectful sighs and all the idle homage of what is called innocent gallantry. If women really respected virtue for its own sake, they would not look for compensation in vanity for the self-denial they must practise to preserve their reputation, nor would they keep company with men who defy reputation altogether.

The two sexes mutually corrupt and mutually improve each other. I believe this to be an indisputable truth, and I extend it to every virtue. Chastity, modesty, public spirit and all the noble train of virtues on which social virtue and happiness are built should be understood and cultivated by everyone, or they will be cultivated to little effect. And instead of giving the vicious or the idle a pretext for violating some sacred duty by calling it a duty of one sex only, it would be wiser to show that nature has made no such difference — that the unchaste man doubly defeats the purpose of nature, by making women barren and destroying his own constitution, though he escapes the shame that pursues the same crime in the other sex. Those are the physical consequences; the moral ones are more alarming still. For virtue is only a name when the duties of citizens, husbands, wives, fathers, mothers and heads of families become nothing but the selfish ties of convenience.

Why, then, do philosophers look for public spirit? Public spirit must be nourished by private virtue, or it will be like that artificial sentiment which makes women careful of their reputation and men careful of their honour — a sentiment that often exists without any virtue to support it, and without that sublime morality which makes the habitual breach of one duty a breach of the whole moral law.
