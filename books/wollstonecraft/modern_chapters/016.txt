Chapter Eleven: Duty to Parents

There seems to be an indolent tendency in us to let long custom always take the place of reason, and to set every duty on an arbitrary foundation. The rights of kings are traced in a direct line from the King of kings, and the rights of parents from our first parent.

Why do we go back like this for principles that ought always to rest on the same base, and that have exactly the weight today they had a thousand years ago, and not a jot more? If parents discharge their duty they have a strong hold and a sacred claim on their children's gratitude; but few parents are willing to receive their children's respectful affection on those terms. They demand blind obedience because they do not deserve a reasonable service; and to make these demands of weakness and ignorance more binding, a mysterious sanctity is spread round the most arbitrary principle in the world — for what else can you call the blind duty of obeying vicious or feeble people merely because they obeyed a powerful instinct?

The reciprocal duty that naturally exists between parent and child can be defined simply, in a few words. The parent who pays proper attention to helpless infancy has a right to require the same attention when the feebleness of age comes on. But to subject a rational being to the mere will of another, once he is old enough to answer to society for his own conduct, is a most cruel and improper stretch of power, and perhaps as damaging to morality as those religious systems which allow right and wrong no existence except in the Divine will.

I have never known a parent who paid more than common attention to his children to be disregarded. On the contrary, the early habit of relying almost implicitly on the opinion of a respected parent is not easily shaken, even when mature reason convinces the child that his father is not the wisest man in the world. This weakness — for a weakness it is, though the epithet *amiable* is often tacked onto it — a reasonable man must steel himself against. For the absurd duty, too often taught, of obeying a parent merely because he is a parent shackles the mind and prepares it for slavish submission to any power except reason.

I distinguish between the natural duty owed to parents and the accidental one.

The parent who assiduously works to form the heart and enlarge the understanding of his child has given a duty common to the whole animal world the dignity that only reason can give. This is the parental affection of a human being, and it leaves instinctive natural affection far behind. Such a parent acquires all the rights of the most sacred friendship, and his advice, even when his child is well advanced in life, deserves serious consideration.

As for marriage, though after the age of twenty-one a parent seems to have no right to withhold consent on any account, still twenty years of solicitude call for some return; and the son ought at least to promise not to marry for two or three years if the object of his choice does not entirely meet with the approval of his first friend.

But respect for parents is generally a much more debasing principle than that; it is only a selfish respect for property. The father who is blindly obeyed is obeyed out of sheer weakness, or from motives that degrade the human character.

A great proportion of the misery that wanders about the world in hideous shapes is allowed to arise from the negligence of parents; and still these are the very people who cling hardest to what they call a natural right, though it undermines the birthright of every human being, the right of acting by the direction of one's own reason.

I have already had frequent occasion to observe that vicious or indolent people are always eager to profit by enforcing arbitrary privileges, and generally in exactly the proportion in which they neglect the duties that alone make those privileges reasonable. At bottom this is a dictate of common sense, or the instinct of self-defence peculiar to ignorant weakness — like the instinct that makes a fish muddy the water it swims in to escape its enemy, instead of facing him boldly in the clear stream.

From the clear stream of argument the supporters of long custom, of every description, do indeed fly; and, taking refuge in the darkness that sublime poetry has supposed to surround the throne of Omnipotence, they dare to demand the implicit respect that is due only to his unsearchable ways. But let me not be thought presumptuous: the darkness that hides God from us concerns only speculative truths. It never obscures moral ones. Those shine clearly, for God is light, and by the constitution of our nature he never requires the discharge of a duty whose reasonableness does not shine on us the moment we open our eyes.

The indolent parent of high rank may, it is true, extort a show of respect from his child; and on the continent women are particularly subject to the plans of their families, who never think of consulting their inclinations or providing for the comfort of the poor victims of their pride. The consequence is notorious. These dutiful daughters become adulteresses and neglect the education of their own children, from whom they in their turn exact the same kind of obedience.

Women in every country are, it is true, too much under the dominion of their parents; and few parents think of addressing their children in the following way, though this is the reasonable way in which Heaven itself seems to command the whole human race. It is in your interest to obey me until you can judge for yourself; and the Almighty Father of all has planted an affection in me to serve as a guard for you while your reason is unfolding. But when your mind reaches maturity you must obey me, or rather respect my opinions, only so far as they agree with the light that is breaking in on your own mind.

A slavish bondage to parents cramps every faculty of the mind; and Mr. Locke very judiciously observes that "if the mind is curbed and humbled too much in children, if their spirits are abased and broken by too strict a hand over them, they lose all their vigour and industry." This strict hand may to some degree account for the weakness of women; for girls, from various causes, are kept down by their parents more than boys, in every sense of the phrase. The duty expected of them, like all the duties arbitrarily imposed on women, comes more from a sense of propriety, more out of respect for decorum, than from reason; and so, taught to submit slavishly to their parents, they are prepared for the slavery of marriage. I may be told that a great many women are not slaves in the married state. True; but then they become tyrants, for what they get is not rational freedom but a lawless kind of power, resembling the authority wielded by the favourites of absolute monarchs, and obtained by debasing means. Nor do I dream of hinting that boys or girls are always slaves. I only insist that when they are obliged to submit to authority blindly, their faculties are weakened and their tempers made either imperious or abject. I lament too that parents, lazily availing themselves of a supposed privilege, damp the first faint glimmer of reason, and at the same time make the very duty they are so anxious to enforce an empty name, because they will not let it rest on the only basis a duty can rest on securely. For unless it is founded on knowledge it cannot gain strength enough to resist the squalls of passion, or the silent sapping of self-love. But it is not the parents who have given the surest proof of their affection for their children — or, to speak more properly, who by fulfilling their duty have let a natural parental affection take root in their hearts, the child of exercised sympathy and reason and not the overweening offspring of selfish pride — who insist most vehemently on their children submitting to their will merely because it is their will. On the contrary, the parent who sets a good example patiently lets that example work, and it seldom fails to produce its natural effect: filial respect.

Children cannot be taught too early to submit to reason, which is the true definition of that necessity Rousseau insisted on without ever defining it; for to submit to reason is to submit to the nature of things, and to the God who formed them so in order to promote our real interest.

Why should children's minds be warped just as they begin to expand, merely to suit the indolence of parents who insist on a privilege without being willing to pay the price nature has fixed for it? I have observed before that a right always includes a duty, and I think it may fairly be inferred as well that those who do not fulfil the duty forfeit the right.

It is easier, I grant, to command than to reason; but it does not follow that children cannot understand why they are made to do certain things habitually. For from a steady adherence to a few simple principles of conduct flows the wholesome power that a judicious parent gradually gains over a child's mind. And that power becomes strong indeed when it is tempered by a steady display of affection brought home to the child's heart. For I believe it must be allowed as a general rule that the affection we inspire always resembles the affection we cultivate; so that the natural affections, which have been supposed almost separate from reason, may turn out to be more closely connected with judgment than is commonly allowed. Indeed, as another proof of the necessity of cultivating the female understanding, it is only fair to observe that the affections seem to have a kind of animal capriciousness when they live in the heart alone.

It is the irregular exercise of parental authority that first injures the mind, and girls are more subject to these irregularities than boys. The will of people who never allow their will to be disputed, unless they happen to be in a good humour and relax proportionately, is almost always unreasonable. To get round this arbitrary authority, girls learn very early the lessons they afterwards practise on their husbands; for I have often seen a sharp-faced little miss rule a whole family, except when now and then mamma's anger bursts out of some accidental cloud — either her hair was badly dressed, or she had lost more money at cards the night before than she cared to admit to her husband, or some such moral cause of anger.

Footnote: I once heard a little girl say to a servant: "My mamma has been scolding me finely this morning, because her hair was not dressed to please her." The remark was pert, but it was just. And what respect could a girl feel for such a parent, without doing violence to reason?

After watching outbursts of this kind I have been led into a melancholy train of reflection about women, concluding that when their first affection must lead them astray, or set their duties clashing until those duties rest on nothing but whim and custom, little can be expected of them as they advance in life. And how is any instructor to remedy the evil? For to teach them virtue on any solid principle is to teach them to despise their parents. Children cannot, and ought not to, be taught to make allowances for their parents' faults, because every such allowance weakens the force of reason in their minds and makes them more indulgent still to their own. It is one of the most sublime virtues of maturity that leads us to be severe with ourselves and forbearing to others; but children should be taught only the simple virtues, for if they begin too early to make allowances for human passions and manners, they wear the fine edge off the standard by which they ought to regulate their own, and grow unjust in exactly the proportion in which they grow indulgent.

The affections of children, and of weak people, are always selfish: they love others because others love them, and not on account of their virtues. Yet until esteem and love are blended together in the first affection, and reason made the foundation of the first duty, morality will stumble on the threshold. But until society is very differently constituted, parents, I am afraid, will still insist on being obeyed because they will be obeyed, and will keep trying to settle on a Divine right a power that will not bear the investigation of reason.
