Chapter Four: On the Degraded State to Which Various Causes Have Reduced Women

(Part 1 of 2)

That woman is either naturally weak or has been degraded by a combination of circumstances seems to me clear. But I shall simply set that proposition beside a conclusion I have often heard sensible men draw in favour of aristocracy: that the mass of humankind cannot amount to anything, or else the obsequious slaves who patiently let themselves be penned up would feel their own importance and kick off their chains. Men submit to oppression everywhere, they add, when they have only to lift their heads to throw off the yoke; yet instead of asserting their birthright they quietly lick the dust and say, let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we die. Women, I argue by analogy, are degraded by the same tendency to enjoy the present moment, and end by despising a freedom they have not virtue enough to struggle for. But I must be more explicit.

As far as cultivating the heart goes, everyone agrees that sex does not come into it; but the line of subordination in mental powers is never to be crossed. Being merely "absolute in loveliness", the portion of rationality granted to women is a very scanty one indeed; for once you deny her genius and judgment it is hard to guess what is left to constitute an intellect at all.

The very stuff of immortality, if I may put it that way, is the perfectibility of human reason. If man had been created perfect, or if a flood of knowledge broke in on him at maturity that made error impossible, I should doubt whether his existence continued after the body dissolved. But as things are, every difficulty in morals that escapes human discussion, and that baffles profound thought and the lightning glance of genius alike, is an argument on which I build my belief in the immortality of the soul. Reason is accordingly the simple power of improvement — or, more properly, of discerning truth. In this respect every individual is a world in itself. More of it may show in one being than in another, but the nature of reason must be the same in all, if it is an emanation of divinity, the tie connecting the creature with the Creator. For can a soul be stamped with the heavenly image which is not perfected by exercising its own reason? Yet, outwardly ornamented with elaborate care and adorned to delight a man, "that with honour he may love", the soul of woman is not allowed this distinction; and, with a man always placed between her and reason, she is always represented as created only to see through a coarse medium and to take things on trust. But dismissing these fanciful theories, and considering woman as a whole — whatever that whole may be — rather than as a part of man, the question is whether she has reason or not. If she has, which I will assume for the moment, then she was not created merely to be man's comfort, and what belongs to her sex should not be allowed to destroy what belongs to her humanity.

Men have probably been led into this error by looking at education in a false light: not as the first step in forming a being who is advancing gradually towards perfection, but only as a preparation for life. On this sensual error, for I must call it that, the whole false system of female manners has been raised, which robs women of their dignity and classes the dark and the fair alike with the smiling flowers that merely decorate the landscape. This has always been the language of men, and the fear of departing from a supposed character proper to their sex has made even women of superior sense adopt the same sentiments. So understanding, strictly speaking, has been denied to woman, and instinct, refined into wit and cunning for the purposes of life, has been put in its place.

The power of generalizing ideas, of drawing broad conclusions from individual observations, is the only acquirement for an immortal being that really deserves the name of knowledge. Merely to observe without trying to account for anything may serve, very incompletely, as the common sense of daily life; but where is the store being laid up that is to clothe the soul when it leaves the body?

This power has not only been denied to women; writers have insisted that, with a few exceptions, it is inconsistent with the character of their sex. Let men prove that, and I shall grant that woman exists only for man. First, though, I must remark that the power of generalizing ideas to any great extent is not very common among men or women. But this exercise is the true cultivation of the understanding — and everything conspires to make cultivating the understanding harder for women than for men.

That assertion leads me naturally to the main subject of this chapter, and I shall now try to point out some of the causes that degrade my sex and prevent women from generalizing their observations.

I shall not go back into remote antiquity to trace the history of woman. It is enough to grant that she has always been either a slave or a despot, and to remark that both conditions retard the progress of reason equally. The great source of female folly and vice has always seemed to me to be narrowness of mind; and the very constitution of civil governments has put nearly insuperable obstacles in the way of cultivating the female understanding — yet virtue can be built on no other foundation. The same obstacles are put in the way of the rich, and the same consequences follow.

Necessity has proverbially been called the mother of invention; the saying may be extended to virtue. Virtue is an acquirement, and one for which pleasure must be sacrificed — and who sacrifices pleasure when it lies within reach, if their mind has not been opened and strengthened by adversity, or their pursuit of knowledge goaded on by necessity? It is a happy thing when people have the cares of life to struggle with, for the struggle keeps them from falling prey to enervating vices out of sheer idleness. But if men and women are placed from birth in a tropical zone with the noonday sun of pleasure beating straight down on them, how can they brace their minds enough to discharge the duties of life, or even to relish the affections that carry them out of themselves?

Pleasure is the business of a woman's life as society is at present arranged, and while that goes on being true, little is to be expected of such weak beings. Inheriting in a direct line from the first fair defect in nature the sovereignty of beauty, they have, in order to keep that power, given up the natural rights that exercising their reason might have won them, choosing to be short-lived queens rather than labour for the sober pleasures that come from equality. Exalted by their inferiority — this sounds like a contradiction — they constantly demand homage as women, though experience should teach them that the men who pride themselves on paying this arbitrary, insolent respect to the sex with the most scrupulous exactness are the likeliest to tyrannize over and despise the very weakness they cherish. Often they repeat Mr. Hume's remark, where, comparing the French and Athenian character, he alludes to women. "But what is more singular in this whimsical nation, I say to the Athenians, is that a frolic of yours during the Saturnalia, when the slaves are waited on by their masters, is seriously kept up by them all year round, and all their lives, and with some further circumstances that make the absurdity and the ridicule greater still. Your sport only raises up for a few days those whom fortune has cast down, and whom she may in her own sport really raise above you forever. But this nation gravely exalts those whom nature has made subject to them, and whose inferiority and infirmities are absolutely incurable. The women, though without virtue, are their masters and sovereigns."

Why do women — I write with affectionate concern — condescend to accept from strangers a degree of attention and respect quite different from the reciprocal civility that humanity and the politeness of civilization allow between one man and another? And why do they not discover, while they are "in the noon of beauty's power", that they are treated like queens only so that hollow respect may delude them until they resign, or never claim, their natural prerogatives? Shut up in cages like birds, they have nothing to do but preen themselves and stalk with mock majesty from perch to perch. It is true they are provided with food and clothing for which they neither toil nor spin — but health, liberty and virtue are given in exchange. And where among humankind has there ever been found strength of mind enough to let a creature resign these adventitious privileges: someone who, rising with the calm dignity of reason above opinion, dared to be proud of the privileges inherent in being human? It is vain to expect it while hereditary power chokes the affections and nips reason in the bud.

The passions of men have thus set women on thrones; and until humankind grows more reasonable it is to be feared that women will avail themselves of the power that costs them least exertion and is least disputed. They will smile — yes, they will smile — even when they are told that

	"In beauty's empire is no mean,
	And woman either slave or queen,
	Is quickly scorn'd when not ador'd."

But the adoration comes first, and the scorn is not looked for.

Louis XIV in particular spread artificial manners and caught the whole nation in his toils by a plausible method: in establishing an artful chain of despotism, he made it every individual's interest to respect his station and support his power. And women, whom he flattered by a childish attention to the whole sex, obtained in his reign that prince-like distinction so fatal to reason and virtue.

A king is always a king, and a woman always a woman: his authority and her sex stand forever between them and rational conversation. With a lover, I grant, she should be a woman, and her sensibility will naturally lead her to try to stir emotion, not to gratify her vanity but her heart. That I do not call coquetry; it is the artless impulse of nature. I protest only against the sexual desire of conquest when the heart is not involved at all.

This desire is not confined to women. "I have tried," says Lord Chesterfield, "to win the hearts of twenty women whose persons I would not have given a fig for." The libertine who in a gust of passion takes advantage of unsuspecting tenderness is a saint compared with this cold-hearted scoundrel — and I like to use words that mean something. Yet, taught only to please, women are always on the watch to please, and with truly heroic ardour set about winning hearts merely in order to give them up, or spurn them, once the victory is decided and conspicuous.

I must come down to the small details of the subject.

I am sorry to see women systematically degraded by receiving the trivial attentions that men think it manly to pay them, when in fact those attentions are an insulting way of propping up their own superiority. It is not condescension to bow to an inferior. Indeed these ceremonies look so ludicrous to me that I can hardly control my face when I see a man start up with eager and serious concern to pick up a handkerchief or shut a door which the *lady* could have managed herself if she had only moved a pace or two.

A wild wish has just flown from my heart to my head, and I will not stifle it, though it may raise a horse laugh. I earnestly wish to see the distinction of sex confounded in society, except where love animates the behaviour. For that distinction is, I am firmly persuaded, the foundation of the weakness of character attributed to women; it is why the understanding is neglected while accomplishments are acquired with painstaking care; and the same cause explains why women prefer the graceful virtues to the heroic ones.

Everybody, of every description, wishes to be loved and respected for *something*, and the common herd will always take the shortest road to what it wants. The respect paid to wealth and beauty is the most certain and unmistakable, and so it will always catch the vulgar eye of ordinary minds. Ability and virtue are absolutely necessary to raise a man out of the middle rank of life into notice, and the natural consequence is notorious: the middle rank contains most of the virtue and most of the ability. Men have, in that station at least, a chance of exerting themselves with dignity and of rising by exertions that really improve a rational creature. But women as a whole are, until their characters are formed, in the same condition as the rich: they are born — I am speaking of a state of civilization — with certain privileges of sex; and while those are granted them for nothing, few will ever think of doing more than is required in order to earn the esteem of a small number of superior people.

When do we hear of women who, starting out of obscurity, boldly claim respect for their great abilities or their daring virtues? Where are they to be found? "To be observed, to be attended to, to be taken notice of with sympathy, complacency and approval, is all the advantage they look for." True, my male readers will probably exclaim. But before they draw any conclusion, let them recollect that this was not originally written to describe women, but to describe the rich. In Dr. Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments I have found a general character of people of rank and fortune which might, in my opinion, be applied with the greatest propriety to women. I refer the shrewd reader to the whole comparison, but I must be allowed to quote a passage in support of the argument I mean to press as the most conclusive of all against the notion of a character proper to each sex. For if, warriors excepted, no great men of any description have ever appeared among the nobility, may we not fairly infer that their situation swallowed up the man, and produced a character like that of women, who are *localized*, if I may use the word, by the rank they are placed in — by *courtesy*? Women, commonly called ladies, are not to be contradicted in company, are not allowed to exert any physical strength, and are expected to show only the negative virtues, when any virtues are expected of them at all: patience, docility, good humour and pliancy, virtues incompatible with any vigorous exertion of intellect. Besides, living more with each other and being seldom absolutely alone, they are more under the influence of sentiments than of passions. Solitude and reflection are needed to give wishes the force of passions, and to let the imagination enlarge an object until it becomes the most desirable thing in the world. The same may be said of the rich: they do not deal enough in general ideas, collected by dispassionate thinking or calm investigation, to acquire the strength of character on which great resolutions are built. But hear what an acute observer says of the great.

"Do the great seem unaware how cheaply they may acquire the public's admiration? Or do they imagine that for them, as for other men, it must be bought with sweat or with blood? By what important accomplishments is the young nobleman taught to support the dignity of his rank, and to make himself worthy of that superiority over his fellow citizens which the virtue of his ancestors won for them? Is it by knowledge, by industry, by patience, by self-denial, by any virtue at all? Since all his words and all his movements are attended to, he acquires a habitual regard for every circumstance of ordinary behaviour, and studies to perform all those small duties with the most exact propriety. Conscious of how closely he is watched, and how ready people are to indulge all his inclinations, he acts on the most trivial occasions with the freedom and elevation this naturally inspires. His air, his manner, his bearing all mark that elegant and graceful sense of his own superiority which those born to a lower station can hardly ever arrive at. These are the arts by which he means to make people submit more easily to his authority and govern their inclinations according to his own pleasure — and in this he is seldom disappointed. These arts, supported by rank and pre-eminence, are enough on ordinary occasions to govern the world. Louis XIV, during most of his reign, was regarded not only in France but all over Europe as the most perfect model of a great prince. But what were the talents and virtues by which he acquired that great reputation? Was it the scrupulous and inflexible justice of everything he undertook, the immense dangers and difficulties attending his enterprises, or the unwearied and unrelenting application with which he pursued them? Was it his extensive knowledge, his exquisite judgment, his heroic valour? It was none of these. He was, first of all, the most powerful prince in Europe and consequently held the highest rank among kings; and then, says his historian, 'he surpassed all his courtiers in the gracefulness of his figure and the majestic beauty of his features. The sound of his voice, noble and affecting, won the hearts that his presence intimidated. He had a step and a bearing that could suit nobody but him and his rank, and that would have been ridiculous in anyone else. The embarrassment he caused those who spoke to him flattered the secret satisfaction with which he felt his own superiority.' These frivolous accomplishments, supported by his rank and no doubt by some degree of other talents and virtues — which seem, however, to have been not much above mediocrity — established this prince in the esteem of his own age, and have drawn even from posterity a good deal of respect for his memory. Compared with these, in his own time and in his own presence, no other virtue seemed to have any merit at all. Knowledge, industry, valour and beneficence trembled, were abashed, and lost all their dignity before them."

Woman too, being thus "in herself complete" by possessing all these *frivolous* accomplishments, so changes the nature of things that

	—"That what she wills to do or say
	Seems wisest, virtuousest, discreetest, best;
	All higher knowledge in *her presence* falls
	Degraded. Wisdom in discourse with her
	Loses discountenanc'd, and like folly shows;
	Authority and reason on her wait."—

And all of it is built on her loveliness!

In the middle rank of life, to carry the comparison on, men are prepared in youth for a profession, and marriage is not considered the great event of their lives; whereas women have no other scheme to sharpen their faculties on. It is not business, or extensive plans, or any of the roving flights of ambition that engage their attention; their thoughts are not employed in raising such noble structures. To rise in the world and win the liberty of running from one pleasure to another, they must marry advantageously; and to that object their time is sacrificed and their persons often legally prostituted. A man entering a profession has his eye steadily fixed on some future advantage — and the mind gains great strength from having all its efforts directed to one point — and, full of his business, he thinks of pleasure as mere relaxation; whereas women look for pleasure as the main purpose of existence. In fact, from the education society gives them, the love of pleasure may be said to govern them all. But does that prove that there is a sex in souls? It would be just as reasonable to declare that the courtiers of France, when a destructive system of despotism had formed their character, were not men, because liberty, virtue and humanity had been sacrificed to pleasure and vanity. Fatal passions, which have always domineered over the *whole* race!

The same love of pleasure, fostered by the whole tendency of their education, gives a trifling turn to women's conduct in most situations. They are forever anxious about secondary things, and on the lookout for adventures instead of being occupied with duties.

A man setting out on a journey generally has the end of it in view; a woman thinks more about the incidental occurrences, the strange things that may happen on the road, the impression she may make on her fellow travellers — and above all she is anxiously intent on looking after the finery she has brought with her, which is more than ever a part of herself when she is going to appear in a new scene, when, to borrow an apt French turn of phrase, she is going to produce a sensation. Can dignity of mind survive such trivial cares?

In short, women in general, like the rich of both sexes, have acquired all the follies and vices of civilization and missed its useful fruit. I need not keep repeating that I am speaking of the condition of women as a whole and leaving exceptions out of the question. Their senses are inflamed and their understandings neglected; so they fall prey to their senses, delicately named sensibility, and are blown about by every momentary gust of feeling. They are therefore in a much worse condition than they would be in a state nearer to nature. Forever restless and anxious, their over-exercised sensibility makes them not only uncomfortable in themselves but troublesome, to use a soft phrase, to others. All their thoughts turn on things calculated to excite emotion; and, feeling where they should reason, their conduct is unstable and their opinions wavering — not the wavering produced by deliberation or by a widening view, but by contradictory emotions. By fits and starts they are warm about many pursuits, but that warmth, never concentrated into perseverance, soon exhausts itself, evaporated by its own heat, or else it meets some other fleeting passion to which reason has never given any weight, and neutrality follows. Miserable indeed must that being be whose cultivation of mind has only inflamed its passions! A distinction should be drawn between inflaming them and strengthening them. What can be expected to follow when the passions are pampered in this way while the judgment is left unformed? Undoubtedly a mixture of madness and folly.

This observation should not be confined to the *fair* sex; but for the present I mean to apply it only to them.

Novels, music, poetry and gallantry all tend to make women creatures of sensation, and their character is formed in this way during the years when they are acquiring accomplishments — the only improvement their station in society encourages them to seek. This over-stretched sensibility naturally slackens the other powers of the mind and stops the intellect from attaining the sovereignty it ought to have if a rational creature is to be useful to others and content in its own station; for exercising the understanding, as life goes on, is the only method nature points out for calming the passions.

Satiety has a very different effect, and I have often been forcibly struck by a vivid description of damnation in which the spirit is shown hovering forever with futile eagerness round the defiled body, unable to enjoy anything without the organs of sense. And yet women are made slaves to their senses, because it is through their sensibility that they get their present power.

And will moralists pretend to say that this is the condition in which one half of the human race should be encouraged to stay, in listless inactivity and stupid acquiescence? Kind instructors! What were we created for? To remain innocent, it may be said — by which they mean, in a state of childhood. We might as well never have been born, unless it were necessary that we should be created to enable man to acquire the noble privilege of reason, the power of telling good from evil, while we lie down in the dust we were taken from, never to rise again.

It would be an endless task to trace all the varieties of meanness, care and sorrow into which women are plunged by the prevailing opinion that they were created to feel rather than to reason, and that all the power they can get must be got by their charms and their weakness —

	"Fine by defect, and amiably weak!"

And, made entirely dependent by this amiable weakness — dependent on men for advice as well as protection, except for what they gain by illicit sway — is it surprising that, neglecting the duties reason alone points out and shrinking from any trial that might strengthen their minds, they exert themselves only to give their defects a graceful covering, which may heighten their charms in the voluptuary's eye though it sinks them below the scale of moral excellence?

Fragile in every sense of the word, they are obliged to look up to a man for every comfort. In the most trifling danger they cling to their support with parasitic tenacity, piteously begging for help; and their *natural* protector extends his arm or raises his voice to defend the lovely trembler — from what? Perhaps the frown of an old cow, or the jump of a mouse; a rat would be a serious danger. In the name of reason, and even of common sense, what can save such beings from contempt, however soft and fair they may be?

These fears, when they are not put on, may be very pretty; but they show a degree of feebleness that degrades a rational creature in a way women do not realise — for love and esteem are very different things.

I am fully persuaded we should hear nothing of these infantile airs if girls were allowed enough exercise and were not shut up in close rooms until their muscles are slack and their digestion ruined. To carry the remark further: if fear in girls, instead of being encouraged and perhaps even created, were treated the way cowardice in boys is treated, we should soon see women with more dignified faces. It is true they could not then be called with equal propriety the sweet flowers that smile along man's path; but they would be more respectable members of society and would discharge the important duties of life by the light of their own reason. "Educate women like men," says Rousseau, "and the more they resemble our sex the less power will they have over us." That is precisely the point I am aiming at. I do not wish them to have power over men, but over themselves.

I have heard men argue in exactly the same strain against instructing the poor, for aristocracy takes many forms. "Teach them to read and write," they say, "and you take them out of the station nature assigned them." An eloquent Frenchman has answered them, and I will borrow his sentiments. They do not know that when they make a man into a brute they may expect at any instant to see him transformed into a ferocious beast. Without knowledge there can be no morality.

Ignorance is a frail base for virtue! Yet that ignorance is the condition woman was made for has been insisted on by the very writers who have argued most vehemently for the superiority of man — a superiority not of degree but of essence; though, to soften the argument, they have laboured to prove with chivalrous generosity that the sexes ought not to be compared at all: man was made to reason, woman to feel, and together, flesh and spirit, they make the most perfect whole by happily blending reason and sensibility into one character.

And what is sensibility? "Quickness of sensation; quickness of perception; delicacy." So Dr. Johnson defines it, and the definition gives me no idea except of an exquisitely polished instinct. I can discern no trace of the image of God in either sensation or matter. Refined seventy times seven, they are still material; intellect does not live there; nor will fire ever turn lead into gold.

I come round to my old argument. If woman is allowed to have an immortal soul, she must have an understanding to improve as the employment of her life. And when, to make the present state more complete — though everything proves it to be only a fraction of a mighty sum — she is tempted by present gratification to forget her grand destination, then nature is thwarted, or else she was born only to breed and rot. Or, if we grant animals of every description a soul, though not a rational one, the exercise of instinct and sensibility may be the step they are to take in this life towards attaining reason in the next; so that through all eternity they will lag behind man, who, for reasons we cannot tell, was given the power of attaining reason in his first mode of existence.

When I come to the duties peculiar to women, as I should come to the duties peculiar to a citizen or a father, it will be seen that I do not mean to hint that women should be taken out of their families — speaking of the majority. "He who has a wife and children," says Lord Bacon, "has given hostages to fortune; for they are impediments to great enterprises, whether of virtue or of mischief. Certainly the best works, and those of greatest merit to the public, have come from unmarried or childless men." I say the same of women. But the welfare of society is not built on extraordinary exertions; and if society were more reasonably organized there would be even less need of great abilities or heroic virtues.

In the regulation of a family and the education of children, understanding in the plainest sense is particularly required, and strength of body and mind as well. Yet the men who have laboured hardest in their writings to keep women at home have tried, with arguments dictated by a coarse appetite that satiety had made fastidious, to weaken their bodies and cramp their minds. If even by these underhand methods they really did *persuade* women, by working on their feelings, to stay at home and fulfil the duties of a mother and mistress of a family, I should be cautious about opposing opinions that led women to right conduct, by getting them to make the discharge of a duty the business of life, even though reason was insulted in the process. Yet — and I appeal to experience — if by neglecting the understanding women are as much detached from those domestic duties as they could be by the most serious intellectual pursuit, and indeed more so, then, though it may be observed that most people will never vigorously pursue any intellectual object, I may be allowed to infer that reason is absolutely necessary to enable a woman to perform any duty properly. And I must repeat once more that sensibility is not reason.

The comparison with the rich keeps occurring to me; for when men neglect the duties of humanity, women will do the same, since a common stream hurries them both along with thoughtless speed. Riches and honours prevent a man from enlarging his understanding and enervate all his powers by reversing the order of nature, which has always made true pleasure the reward of labour. Pleasure — enervating pleasure — is likewise within a woman's reach without her earning it. But until hereditary possessions are spread more widely, how can we expect men to be proud of virtue? And until they are, women will govern them by the most direct means, neglecting their dull domestic duties to catch the pleasure that is on the wing of time.

"The power of a woman," some author says, "is her sensibility"; and men, not seeing the consequence, do everything they can to make that power swallow up every other. Those who constantly employ their sensibility will have most of it — poets, painters and composers, for example. Yet when sensibility is increased in this way at the expense of reason, and even of imagination, why do philosophical men complain of women's fickleness? The sexual attentions of men act particularly on female sensibility, and this sympathy has been exercised in them from their youth up. A husband cannot go on paying those attentions with the passion needed to excite lively emotions, and a heart used to lively emotions turns to a new lover, or pines in secret, the prey of virtue or of prudence. I mean where the heart really has been made susceptible and the taste formed; for from what I have seen of fashionable life I am inclined to conclude that the mode of education, and the intercourse between the sexes that I have been condemning, foster vanity oftener than sensibility, and that coquetry comes more often from vanity than from the inconstancy that overstrained sensibility naturally produces.
