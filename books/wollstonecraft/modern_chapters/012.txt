Chapter Seven: Modesty, Considered in Full, and Not as a Virtue Belonging to One Sex

Modesty! Sacred offspring of sensibility and reason! True delicacy of mind! May I presume, without blame, to investigate your nature, and trace to its hiding place that mild charm which mellows every harsh feature of a character and makes lovely what would otherwise inspire nothing but cold admiration? You that smooth the wrinkles of wisdom, and soften the tone of the more sublime virtues until they all melt into humanity; you that spread the ethereal cloud which, surrounding love, heightens every beauty it half shades, breathing those coy sweets that steal into the heart and charm the senses — modulate for me the language of persuasive reason, until I have roused my sex from the flowery bed where they lie sleeping their lives away.

In speaking of the association of our ideas I noticed two distinct modes; and in defining modesty it seems to me equally proper to distinguish that purity of mind which is the effect of chastity from the simplicity of character that leads us to form a just opinion of ourselves — equally far from vanity and from presumption, though by no means incompatible with a lofty consciousness of our own dignity. Modesty in the second sense of the word is that soberness of mind which teaches a man not to think more highly of himself than he ought; and it should be distinguished from humility, because humility is a kind of self-abasement.

A modest man often conceives a great plan and holds to it tenaciously, conscious of his own strength, until success gives it a sanction that settles its character. Milton was not arrogant when he let slip a suggestion of his judgment that turned out to be a prophecy; nor was General Washington when he accepted command of the American forces. Washington has always been described as a modest man; but if he had been merely humble he would probably have shrunk back irresolute, afraid to trust himself with the direction of an enterprise on which so much depended.

A modest man is steady, a humble man timid, and a vain one presumptuous: that is the judgment which observing many characters has led me to form. Jesus Christ was modest, Moses was humble, and Peter was vain.

Having distinguished modesty from humility in the one case, I do not mean to confound it with bashfulness in the other. Bashfulness is in fact so distinct from modesty that the most bashful girl, or the rawest country lout, often becomes the most impudent; for their bashfulness being merely the instinctive timidity of ignorance, custom soon turns it into assurance.

Footnote: "Such is the country maiden's fright, when first a redcoat is in sight; behind the door she hides her face, next time at distance eyes the lace: she now can all his terrors stand, nor from his squeeze withdraws her hand, she plays familiar in his arms, and every soldier has his charms; from tent to tent she spreads her flame; for custom conquers fear and shame."

The shameless behaviour of the prostitutes who infest the streets of London, rousing pity and disgust by turns, may serve to illustrate the remark. They trample on virgin bashfulness with a sort of bravado and, glorying in their shame, become more audaciously lewd than men ever appear to be, however depraved — men to whom the quality has not been gratuitously granted along with their sex. But these poor ignorant wretches never had any modesty to lose when they consigned themselves to infamy, for modesty is a virtue and not a quality. No: they were only bashful, shamefaced innocents, and when they lost their innocence their shamefacedness was rudely brushed off. A virtue would have left some traces in the mind, even if it had been sacrificed to passion, to make us respect the grand ruin.

Purity of mind, or that genuine delicacy which is the only virtuous support of chastity, is near kin to the refinement of humanity that never lives in any but a cultivated mind. It is something nobler than innocence: it is the delicacy of reflection, and not the coyness of ignorance. This reserve of reason, which, like habitual cleanliness, is seldom seen to any great degree unless the soul is active, is easily distinguished from rustic shyness or wanton skittishness; and so far from being incompatible with knowledge, it is knowledge's fairest fruit. What a gross idea of modesty the writer of the following remark had! "The lady who asked whether women may be instructed in the modern system of botany, consistently with female delicacy, was accused of ridiculous prudery. Nevertheless, if she had put the question to me, I should certainly have answered: they cannot." So the fair book of knowledge is to be shut with an everlasting seal. On reading passages like that I have reverently lifted my eyes and my heart to Him who lives forever and ever, and said: O my Father, have you, by the very constitution of her nature, forbidden your child to seek you in the fair forms of truth? And can her soul be sullied by the knowledge that calls her so awfully towards you?

Then I have philosophically pursued these reflections until I concluded that the women who have improved their reason most must have the most modesty, though a dignified sedateness of manner may have taken the place of the playful, bewitching bashfulness of youth.

Footnote: Modesty is the graceful calm virtue of maturity; bashfulness is the charm of vivacious youth.

And this is how I have argued. To make chastity the virtue from which an unaffected modesty naturally flows, the attention must be drawn away from employments that only exercise the sensibility, and the heart must be made to beat time to humanity rather than throb with love. The woman who has devoted a considerable part of her time to purely intellectual pursuits, and whose affections have been exercised by humane plans of usefulness, must as a natural consequence have more purity of mind than the ignorant creatures whose time and thoughts have been taken up with gay pleasures or with schemes for conquering hearts. Regulating the behaviour is not modesty, though the women who study the rules of decorum are generally called modest. Make the heart clean, let it expand and feel for everything human instead of being narrowed by selfish passions; let the mind often contemplate subjects that exercise the understanding without heating the imagination; and an artless modesty will put the finishing touches to the picture.

The woman who can discern the dawn of immortality in the streaks shooting across the misty night of ignorance, promising a clearer day, will respect as a sacred temple the body that houses such an improvable soul. True love likewise spreads this kind of mysterious sanctity round the beloved object, making the lover most modest in her presence. Affection is so reserved that in giving or receiving personal endearments it wishes not only to avoid the human eye, as a kind of profanation, but to draw a cloudy obscurity all round to shut out even the saucy sparkling sunbeams. Yet no affection deserves the name of chaste that does not take on a sublime gloom of tender melancholy, allowing the mind to stand still for a moment and enjoy the present satisfaction, when a consciousness of the Divine presence is felt — for that must always be the food of joy.

Since I have always been fond of tracing any prevailing custom back to its source in nature, I have often thought that what gave rise to the respect for relics, so much abused by selfish priests, was a feeling of affection for whatever has touched the person of an absent or a lost friend. Devotion, or love, may be allowed to hallow the clothes as well as the person; for a lover must be short of fancy who has no sort of sacred respect for his mistress's glove or slipper. He could not confuse them with common things of the same kind.

This fine sentiment, perhaps, would not bear analysis by the experimental philosopher — but this is the stuff human rapture is made of. A shadowy phantom glides in front of us, obscuring every other object; yet when the soft cloud is grasped, the form melts into common air, leaving a solitary void, or a sweet perfume stolen from the violet, which memory holds dear for a long time afterwards. But I have wandered onto fairy ground without meaning to, and feel the balmy breeze of spring stealing over me though November is frowning.

As a sex, women are more chaste than men; and since modesty is the effect of chastity, they may deserve to have the virtue ascribed to them in a rather special sense. Yet I must be allowed to add a hesitating if. For I doubt whether chastity will produce modesty, though it may produce propriety of conduct, when it is merely a respect for the opinion of the world, and when coquetry and the lovelorn tales of novelists occupy the thoughts. Indeed, experience and reason would lead me to expect more modesty among men than among women, simply because men exercise their understandings more.

But as far as propriety of behaviour goes, one class of women excepted, women plainly have the advantage. What can be more disgusting than that impudent dross of gallantry, thought so manly, which makes so many men stare insultingly at every woman they meet? Is this respect for the sex? Such loose behaviour shows so habitual a depravity, such a weakness of mind, that it is useless to expect much public or private virtue until both men and women grow more modest — until men, curbing a sensual fondness for women, or an affectation of manly assurance, or, more properly speaking, impudence, treat each other with respect, instead of letting appetite or passion set the tone of their behaviour. I mean personal respect: the modest respect of humanity and fellow feeling, not the libidinous mockery of gallantry, nor the insolent condescension of protection.

To carry the observation further still, modesty must heartily disown and refuse to live with that debauchery of mind which lets a man coolly produce indecent allusions or obscene witticisms, without blushing, in the presence of a fellow creature. Women are out of the question here, for in their presence it is brutality. Respect for a man as a man is the foundation of every noble sentiment. How much more modest is the libertine who obeys the call of appetite or fancy than the lewd joker who sets the table in a roar.

This is one of many instances in which the distinction of the sexes with regard to modesty has proved fatal to virtue and to happiness. It is carried further still, for woman — weak woman, made by her education the slave of sensibility — is required on the most trying occasions to resist that very sensibility. "Can anything," says Knox, "be more absurd than keeping women in a state of ignorance, and yet insisting so vehemently on their resisting temptation?" So when virtue or honour makes it proper to check a passion, the burden is thrown on the weaker shoulders, contrary to reason and to true modesty, which should at least make the self-denial mutual — to say nothing of the generosity of bravery, which is supposed to be a manly virtue.

Rousseau's and Dr. Gregory's advice about modesty, strangely so called, runs in the same strain; for they both want a wife to leave it in doubt whether it was sensibility or weakness that brought her into her husband's arms. The woman is immodest who can let the shadow of such a doubt stay in her husband's mind for a moment.

But to put the subject in a different light. The want of modesty that I chiefly deplore as subversive of morality arises from that state of warfare which voluptuous men so strenuously maintain to be the very essence of modesty, though it is in fact modesty's bane; for it is a refinement on sensual desire that men fall into who have not virtue enough to relish the innocent pleasures of love. A man of delicacy carries his notions of modesty further, for neither weakness nor sensibility will satisfy him: he is looking for affection.

Again: men boast of their triumphs over women. What are they boasting of? Truly, that a creature of sensibility was surprised by her own sensibility into folly, into vice; and the dreadful reckoning falls heavily on her own weak head when reason wakes. For where are you to find comfort, forlorn and disconsolate creature? The man who ought to have directed your reason and supported your weakness has betrayed you. In a dream of passion you consented to wander through flowery meadows, and, stepping heedlessly over the precipice to which your guide lured you instead of guarding you, you start out of your dream only to face a sneering, frowning world and find yourself alone in a wilderness; for the man who triumphed in your weakness is off pursuing new conquests. But for you there is no redemption this side of the grave. And what resource do you have, with an enfeebled mind, to lift a sinking heart?

But if the sexes really must live in a state of warfare, if nature has marked it out for them, then let men act nobly, or let their pride whisper to them that it is a mean victory merely to overcome a woman's sensibility. The real conquest is over an affection that was not taken by surprise — when, like Heloisa, a woman deliberately gives up the whole world for love. I am not considering the wisdom or the virtue of such a sacrifice now; I only maintain that it was a sacrifice to affection and not merely to sensibility, though sensibility had its share. And I must be allowed to call her a modest woman before I leave this part of the subject, saying only that until men are more chaste, women will be immodest. Where indeed could modest women find husbands they would not be continually turning from in disgust? Modesty must be cultivated equally by both sexes, or it will always be a sickly hothouse plant, while the affectation of it, that fig leaf borrowed by wantonness, gives a zest to voluptuous enjoyments.

Men will probably still insist that a woman ought to have more modesty than a man; but it is not the dispassionate reasoners who will oppose my opinion most earnestly. No: it is the men of fancy, the favourites of the sex, who outwardly respect and inwardly despise the weak creatures they play with. They cannot bring themselves to give up the highest sensual gratification, or even to relish the epicurism of virtue, which is self-denial.

To take another view of the subject, confining my remarks to women.

The ridiculous falsehoods told to children out of mistaken notions of modesty tend very early to inflame their imaginations and set their little minds working on subjects nature never intended them to think about until the body had reached some degree of maturity — when the passions naturally begin to take the place of the senses as instruments for unfolding the understanding and forming the moral character.

I am afraid it is in nurseries and boarding schools that girls are first spoiled, and particularly in boarding schools. A number of girls sleep in the same room and wash together. And though I should be sorry to contaminate an innocent creature's mind by instilling a false delicacy, or those indecent prudish notions that early cautions about the other sex naturally breed, I should be very anxious to prevent their acquiring indelicate or immodest habits; and since a great many girls have learned very indelicate tricks from ignorant servants, mixing them together indiscriminately in this way is very improper.

To tell the truth, women are in general too familiar with one another, and this leads to the gross familiarity that so often makes married life unhappy. Why in the name of decency are sisters, or female friends, or ladies and their maids, to be so grossly familiar as to forget the respect one human creature owes another? The squeamish delicacy that shrinks from the most disgusting offices when affection or humanity calls us to sit up beside a sickbed is contemptible. But why women in health should be more familiar with each other than men are, when they boast of their superior delicacy, is a puzzle in manners I have never been able to solve.

To preserve health and beauty I would earnestly recommend frequent washing — to dignify my advice so that it may not offend a fastidious ear. And girls ought to be taught by example to wash and dress alone, whatever their rank; and if custom has made them need a little assistance, let them not need it until that part of the business is over which ought never to be done in front of a fellow creature, because it is an insult to the majesty of human nature. Not on the score of modesty, but of decency: for the care some modest women take not to let their legs be seen, while making a display of taking it, is as childish as it is immodest.

Footnote: I remember coming across a sentence in a book on education that made me smile: "It would be needless to caution you against putting your hand, by chance, under your neck handkerchief; for a modest woman never did such a thing."

I could go further, and criticize customs more indelicate still, which men never fall into. Secrets are told where silence ought to reign; and that regard for cleanliness which some religious sects have perhaps carried too far — the Essenes among the Jews particularly, by making an insult to God of what is only an insult to humanity — is violated in a brutal fashion. How can *delicate* women force on other people's notice that part of the animal economy which is so very disgusting? And is it not quite reasonable to conclude that women who have not been taught to respect the human nature of their own sex in these particulars will not long respect the mere difference of sex in their husbands? Once their maidenly bashfulness is lost, I have generally observed that women fall back into old habits and treat their husbands as they used to treat their sisters or their female acquaintance.

Besides, women, because their minds are not cultivated, have recourse very often to what I familiarly call bodily wit, and their intimacies are of the same kind. In short, in mind and in body alike, they are too intimate. That decent personal reserve which is the foundation of dignity of character must be kept up between women, or their minds will never gain either strength or modesty.

On this account too I object to numbers of women being shut up together in nurseries, schools or convents. I cannot recall without indignation the jokes and hoyden tricks that knots of young women indulged in when, in my youth, accident threw me, an awkward country girl, in their way. They were almost on a level with the double meanings that shake a convivial table once the glass has gone round freely. But it is useless to try to keep the heart pure unless the head is furnished with ideas and set to work comparing them, so as to acquire judgment by generalizing the simple ones, and modesty by making the understanding damp down the sensibility.

It may be thought that I lay too much stress on personal reserve; but reserve is always the handmaid of modesty. So that if I were to name the graces that ought to adorn beauty, I should exclaim at once: cleanliness, neatness and personal reserve. It is obvious, I suppose, that the reserve I mean has nothing to do with sex, and that I think it *equally* necessary in both. So necessary indeed is that reserve, and that cleanliness which indolent women too often neglect, that I will venture to say that where two or three women live in the same house, the one who is most respected by the men of the family living with them — leaving love entirely out of the question — will be the one who pays this kind of habitual respect to her own person.

When friends who live together meet in the morning, an affectionate seriousness naturally prevails, especially if each is looking forward to the duties of the day. It may be reckoned fanciful, but this feeling has often risen unbidden in my mind. After breathing the sweet bracing morning air I have been pleased to see the same freshness in the faces I particularly loved; I was glad to see them braced, as it were, for the day, and ready to run their course with the sun. The greetings of affection in the morning are in this way more respectful than the familiar tenderness that so often prolongs the evening's talk. Indeed I have often felt hurt, not to say disgusted, when a friend I parted from fully dressed the evening before appeared with her clothes huddled on, because she had chosen to indulge herself in bed until the last moment.

Domestic affection can only be kept alive by these neglected attentions. Yet if men and women took half as much trouble to be habitually neat as they take to ornament, or rather to disfigure, their persons, a great deal would be done towards attaining purity of mind. But women only dress to gratify men of gallantry; a lover is always best pleased with the simple garment that sits close to the shape. There is an impertinence about ornament that repels affection, because love always clings round the idea of home.

As a sex, women are habitually indolent, and everything conspires to make them so. I do not forget the starts of activity that sensibility produces; but as these flights of feeling only increase the evil, they must not be confused with the slow, orderly walk of reason. So great in reality is their indolence of body and mind that until the body is strengthened and the understanding enlarged by active exertion there is little reason to expect modesty to take the place of bashfulness. They may find it prudent to assume the appearance of it; but the fair veil will only be worn on gala days.

Perhaps there is no virtue that mixes so kindly with every other as modesty. It is the pale moonbeam that makes every virtue it softens more interesting, giving a mild grandeur to a narrow horizon. Nothing could be more beautiful than the poetic fiction that makes Diana, with her silver crescent, the goddess of chastity. I have sometimes thought that as she wandered with sedate step in some lonely place, a modest lady of antiquity must have felt a glow of conscious dignity when, after contemplating the soft shadowy landscape, she invited with placid fervour the mild reflection of her sister's beams to turn towards her own chaste breast.

A Christian has still nobler motives for preserving her chastity and acquiring modesty, for her body has been called the temple of the living God — of that God who requires more than a modest bearing. His eye searches the heart; and let her remember that if she hopes to find favour in the sight of purity itself, her chastity must be founded on modesty and not on worldly prudence, or truly a good reputation will be her only reward. For that awful intercourse, that sacred communion which virtue establishes between a human being and its Maker must give rise to the wish to be as pure as he is pure.

After these remarks it is almost superfluous to add that I consider all those feminine airs of maturity that succeed bashfulness to be immodest — the airs to which truth is sacrificed in order to secure a husband's heart, or rather to force him to go on being a lover when nature, if she had not been interrupted, would have made love give place to friendship. The tenderness a man feels for the mother of his children is an excellent substitute for the ardour of unsatisfied passion; but to prolong that ardour it is indelicate, not to say immodest, for a woman to feign an unnatural coldness of constitution. Women, like men, ought to have the common appetites and passions of their nature; those are only brutal when they are unchecked by reason, and the obligation to check them is a human duty, not a duty of one sex. In these respects nature may safely be left to herself. Let women only acquire knowledge and humanity, and love will teach them modesty. There is no need of falsehoods as disgusting as they are futile, for studied rules of behaviour only impose on shallow observers; a man of sense soon sees through the affectation and despises it.

The behaviour of young people towards each other as men and women is the last thing that should be thought about in education. In fact behaviour is now so much thought about in most circumstances that simplicity of character is rarely to be seen. Yet if men were only anxious to cultivate each virtue and let it take firm root in the mind, the grace that results from it, which is its natural outward mark, would soon strip affectation of its flaunting plumes; for conduct that is not founded on truth is as fallacious as it is unstable.

They seem anxious never to let their husbands forget the privilege of marriage, and to find no pleasure in his company unless he is playing the lover. The reign of love must indeed be short when the flame is constantly blown up like that without being given any solid fuel.

If you would really possess modesty, O my sisters, you must remember that the possession of virtue of any kind is incompatible with ignorance and vanity. You must acquire that soberness of mind which only the exercise of duties and the pursuit of knowledge can inspire, or you will still be left in a doubtful, dependent situation, and be loved only while you are beautiful. The downcast eye, the rosy blush, the retiring grace are all proper in their season; but modesty, being the child of reason, cannot exist for long alongside a sensibility untempered by reflection. Besides, when love, even innocent love, is the whole employment of your lives, your hearts will be too soft to give modesty that tranquil retreat where she loves to live, in close union with humanity.
