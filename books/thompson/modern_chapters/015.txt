Lecture Three: The Polarisation of Light

(Part 4 of 4)

Suppose polarised light falls on a thin slice of crystal whose axis is set obliquely across the beam. Then the vibrations, in going through the crystal, are split, as I have explained, into two parts, one vibrating parallel to the axis and the other at right angles to it; and they do not take the same time to cross the crystal film, because of the difference in rigidity in the two directions. And since the wave-lengths of different colours differ, waves of different colours, though they cross the same actual thickness, come out in different states. When the two components of a wave of any given colour recombine on emerging, they form a vibration in some new direction, and that resultant direction — whether oblique or elliptical — will be different for different colours. It follows that the analyser will cut off more of one colour than of another, and that the light coming through the analyser will be the sum of the resolved parts of each kind of light. If, for instance, the crystal's thickness is such that yellow light, on emerging, recombines into a nearly vertical vibration, then a horizontal analyser will cut off the yellow, and the light that gets through — the sum of all the other parts — will be a dark violet. Just as the colours of Newton's rings depend on the thickness of the air film between the glasses, so these colours from the crystal film in the polariscope depend on the thickness of the film (see p. 139).

The next object to be shown you is a slice of selenite exceedingly thin at one end and thick at the other, tapered like a very thin wedge. It shows the Newton tints most magnificently, right up to the end of the third order, and it will serve as a standard for comparing other slices. For instance, there is now placed in the apparatus a uniform piece of crystal. In the dark field it shows the red of the second order. I therefore know that it is exactly as thick as the wedge is at the place where the wedge shows that same red. This red turns to a vivid green when the analyser is turned to give the bright field. Likewise a slice that in the dark field gives a violet of the second order changes in the bright field to the complementary primrose.

I now take two prepared pieces of mica, which will be shown to you first separately and then together. One shows the blue of the second order, a tint that by the table is the same as that produced by an air film 13 millionths of an inch thick. The other shows a yellow of the second order, corresponding to an air film 18 millionths of an inch thick. Now guess what will happen if both are put in together. Will blue and yellow make green? Not in the least. Laid one on the other, with their axes both at 45° to the right, they will have the same effect as a single piece of mica whose thickness equals the two added together — that is, they will act as an air film in Newton's rings 31 millionths of an inch thick, giving a tint which, by the table, you see is a rose red. My assistant slides one crystal over the other (Fig. 97), and you see in this case the unexpected — though predicted — result that blue and yellow added in this way make pink.

Now let one of the crystals be turned so as to put its axis 45° to the left, so that it acts negatively, giving the same result as if we had subtracted one thickness from the other. What tint ought it to give? Subtracting 13 millionths (blue) from 18 millionths (yellow), we get the answer that it ought to give the same tint as an air film 5 millionths of an inch thick, which is a greyish white. Look for yourselves and see how, on the screen, where the reversed blue crystal overlaps the yellow one, the resulting tint is a greyish white.

[Figure 97: The two prepared micas being slid one over the other. Where the blue of the second order overlaps the yellow of the second order, the thicknesses add and the patch comes out rose red.]

The next object is a wedge made of twenty-four very thin pieces of mica set overlapping one another, so as to form a wedge in steps. Like the smooth selenite wedge, it gives the Newton tints of the first three orders; but in this case they do not shade finely into one another — they jump suddenly from the tint of one thickness to the tint of the next. Where the crystal comes nearest to white, halfway along the first order, it corresponds to an air film whose thickness is half the length of a wave of yellow light. Such a crystal is therefore called a half-wave plate. If it is laid, with its axis also at 45°, on one of the other crystal slices in the polariscope, it raises all the tints by an amount corresponding to adding 11 millionths of an inch to the equivalent air film, and changes each almost exactly to its complementary. Whitish in the dark field, it is nearly black — a very dark purple — in the bright field.

The next slide to go into the polariscope illustrates this principle. In the dark field you see a white swan which, when I turn the analyser to give the bright field, changes into a black swan. The space inside the outline of the swan on the slide is covered with a piece of half-wave selenite. I have another slide in which a baker's boy in white clothes, with a sack of flour on his back, changes into a chimney sweep carrying a bag of soot.

Footnote: Not precisely exactly, since it cannot be an exact half-wave plate for all the different colours. It is chosen so as to be an exact half-wave plate for the tint that is brightest to the eye — yellow, or yellow-green.

A slice of crystal half as thick as the half-wave plate is called a quarter-wave plate. It produces a retardation of a quarter of a wave, for yellow light, between the two components of the vibration that cross the slice.

Here is a beautiful object. A thin slice of selenite has been ground hollow on one face, like a concave lens — thin in the middle, thicker at the edges. As a result it shows Newton's rings far more splendidly than Newton's own delicate film of air ever showed them. Along with this concave selenite I now put in a slide made up of twelve sectors of quarter-wave crystal, set with their axes alternately at 45° to the right and 45° to the left. They seem to dislocate the Newton's rings, pushing alternate segments in or out by a quarter of a whole "order" of tints. Beyond these objects, next to the analyser, I now put a quarter-wave plate set upright. And now, on turning the analyser, we get the strange sight of all these dislocated rings of colour marching inwards to vanish at the centre, each in turn succeeded by others. Turning the analyser the other way makes the rings seem to grow at the centre and march outwards.

Footnote: The quarter-wave plate, if set with its axis at 45° so as to produce, as mentioned, a difference of phase of a quarter between the two components, produces circularly polarised light. Light still comes through in all positions of the analyser, nearly equally; there is no dark field. Also, if a quarter-wave plate is used along with other polarising objects — such as the concave selenite described next — but is set with its axis vertical instead of at 45°, and is put between the polarising object and the analyser, it produces great varieties of tint, each tint passing through an intermediate series of tints on its way to its complementary as the analyser is turned.

Here is another object of great beauty: a butterfly cut out of selenite. And here is a heart's-ease; and here some daisies which, though pale yellow in the dark field, turn into purple Michaelmas daisies in the bright field. Of pretty devices like these there is no end.

We may now apply what we know to a further study of complementary and supplementary tints. A few minutes ago I showed you (Fig. 87, p. 125) how the double-image prism, used as an analyser, gives us two polarised images which, when the polarised light passes through a circular aperture of the right size, overlap one another. If, with the same arrangement, I cover the aperture with a thin slice of mica or selenite and add a vertical quarter-wave plate, our two overlapping disks on the screen are seen to give two complementary colours, as though we had two analysers, one of them turned through 90°. As we turn the double-image analyser, the two images revolve round one another exactly as they did before. But as they revolve they change colour in regular succession. And in every position, whatever tint one image shows, the other shows its complementary; while in every position the patch of light where they overlap is white. That is because we are taking the white light of the electric lamp and resolving it into two complementary constituents. Now if I put in a piece of coloured glass to colour the beam, we shall resolve that coloured light into two constituents that we may call "supplementary" to one another. Blue glass, as we found last lecture, lets through some green and violet as well as blue — and here again you see that fact revealed. Red glass, on the other hand, is fairly monochromatic; for though we split its light into two supplementary beams, both are red, scarcely differing in hue at all.

[Figure 87: The double-image prism used as analyser. The polarised light coming through a round aperture gives two overlapping disks, each showing the complementary tint of the other, with white where they cross.]

Natural crystal patterns, made by pouring a crystallisable solution onto glass and letting it dry, form objects of great beauty. Here, for example, is a plate prepared from a solution of antipyrin. It gives an effect like frost on a window pane. But the delicate traceries and plumes, when put in the polariscope, show the most gorgeous play of colours, as you see. And here are some crystals of sulphate of copper, and some of pyrogallic acid, which are just as curious.

Now, there are in nature various substances besides crystals that have different rigidities in different directions — thin slices of wood, for example, and bone, and horn, and many other animal and vegetable structures. Here is a thin slice of horn. It is nearly transparent and colourless. But put it into the polariscope with its grain at 45° to the vertical and you see at once the remarkable streak of colour it produces. Here again is a quill pen flattened out and mounted as a polariscope object. It is really gorgeous in its hues.

Here is a very interesting object: the natural lens from the eye of a codfish. Having a fibrous, radial structure, it shows a black cross in the dark field.

Glass, in ordinary circumstances, has no difference of rigidity between one direction and another. Nevertheless, if it is suddenly heated or suddenly cooled, the unequal expansion of its parts produces differences of rigidity that make themselves visible in the polariscope. Here, for example, is a small square piece of glass, which at present shows no colour or any other effect in the polariscope. But if it is dropped into a heated brass frame that quickly warms its edges before the central part has time to expand, its structure is put under unequal stresses, and the resulting strains show themselves in the strange patterns of colour you now see growing on the screen.

If a hot piece of glass is suddenly chilled, so that the outside cools and contracts before the inside has time to cool, the piece may take on — and keep — a state of permanent strain. Such glass, usually described as "unannealed," is very liable to break with almost explosive violence. Here is a square piece of glass, colourless in itself, that has been suddenly chilled. Its state of permanent strain is at once revealed by the peculiar pattern and the dull tints that seem to gather round a distorted cross radiating from its centre. A second square of glass, cooled even more suddenly, shows the same black cross (Fig. 98), but the tints in the corners run up into the second order. And here is a short cylinder of glass that was suddenly chilled all round its outside. The outer surface has contracted onto the inner part and squeezed it with enormous force. As a result you see not only the black cross that shows a radial arrangement of the axes of elasticity, but also a number of concentric rings coloured with the now familiar succession of Newton's tints, right up to the fourth order.

[Figure 98: Strained glass in the polariscope. The square that was chilled too quickly shows a dark cross radiating from its centre, with the tints in the corners running up into the second order.]

Footnote: The extreme case is the "Rupert's drop" — a drop of melted glass suddenly cooled by dropping it into water. Examined in polarised light, best of all when immersed in a small glass tank of oil to get rid of surface reflections, they show fine colours.

And now I am going to squeeze a piece of glass mechanically, by gripping it in a strong brass frame and forcing a point against its side by turning a strong screw. In the dark field the glass shows neither light nor colour until I put on the screw. But the moment compression is applied, a luminous pattern seems to grow, stretching out in two patches at about 45° on each side of the point where the screw has been forced against the glass. Tightening the screw makes the internal strain greater and the pattern more brilliant. Loosening it releases the strain, and the glass returns to its ordinary colourless state.

So you see you can use polarised light not only to tell false gems from real, and not only to tell glass from crystal, but also to find out whether a piece of glass is likely to break. Any piece of glass that has been cooled too suddenly — that is, not properly annealed by slow cooling down from the furnace heat — can always be detected by the colours it shows in the polariscope between polariser and analyser.

For such purposes a very simple polariscope, such as any handy young person might build, is quite sufficient. Here (Fig. 99) is one made entirely of glass. The polariser is simply a flat piece of window glass, 9 inches long by 5 inches wide, blackened on its underside with black varnish and laid on a simple wooden frame. Two other pieces of glass are cut to the size 8¼ by 5 inches, one of clear window glass and the other of ground glass. Across the lower part of the clear piece, 1⅝ inch from its edge, a strip of glass 5 inches long by 1 inch broad is cemented, to serve as a ledge to rest the objects on while they are being looked at. These two pieces of glass are joined at the top by a hinge of paper or cloth cemented to them, and they stand up like a roof over the piece of blackened glass, held in place on the baseboard by two strips of wood fastened 9¾ inches apart. The baseboard should be 17 inches long by 5 inches wide. Daylight or lamplight is allowed to fall on the ground glass, passes down to the blackened glass, is reflected there at an angle of about 57° to its surface, and so passes as a polarised beam through the clear glass on its way to the eye.

As an analyser — since Nicol prisms are expensive — a cheap substitute must be found. One good enough for many purposes can be made by taking a bundle of eight or ten very thin slips of glass, each about 1½ inch long and ⅝ inch wide, and fixing them with sealing wax obliquely across a small wooden tube or box open at both ends. They should be fixed in the tube so that the glass slips lie at about 33° to the direction the light passes through it.

[Figure 99: The home-made polariscope. A sheet of blackened glass lies on the baseboard as polariser; over it, hinged like a roof, stand a clear pane with a glass ledge for the object and a ground-glass pane to catch the daylight.]

With quite simple apparatus you can check and repeat many of the experiments that have been shown to you here. There are many others, equally beautiful, that I have not shown; for in a single lecture one can only deal very incompletely with this fascinating and complicated subject of polarisation. I have not shown you how quartz crystals have the special property of rotating polarised light, nor have I told you that solutions of sugar and various other liquids are also found to produce an optical rotation. Indeed, the regular way of measuring the amount of sugar in a watery syrup in sugar factories is to put some of it into a polariscope and measure how far it turns the direction of the vibrations. Lastly, I have said nothing about the remarkable discovery with which Faraday crowned his researches in this very place: that the polarised waves of light can be rotated by a magnet. Let me hope that some day you may learn about these marvellous discoveries, to which the things you have seen today are a first step.
