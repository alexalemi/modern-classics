Lecture Five: The Invisible Spectrum — the Infra-Red Part

(Part 2 of 3)

The next experiment is with the same thermopile, only instead of shining a flame on it I shall put a lump of ice in front of it. The spot of light on the wall now retreats to the left, right past the zero mark, showing that the face of the thermopile has been chilled. Perhaps you will say this proves that the ice is radiating cold. It may seem so. But what is really happening is this. "Cold" is a relative term meaning, in truth, "less hot." Everything not in that unattainable state of absolute zero of temperature is more or less hot — hot things more, cold things less. And everything tends to radiate its heat away, the hotter it is the more strongly. Ice is less hot than the other things in this room; the ice is colder than the thermopile. The thermopile is radiating heat, some of which goes to the ice; the ice is also radiating heat, though less. So here we have two things, a warm thermopile and colder ice, radiating to one another unequally. The result of this unequal exchange is that the thermopile parts with more heat than the ice does, and is therefore cooled. But the effect is exactly as if cold were being radiated.

Now let us go to an experiment which I believe began nearly a century ago in this very Royal Institution. Here the emission of heat and the properties of heat waves have always been favourite subjects of study. The founder of the Royal Institution, Count Rumford, himself began many experiments on the radiation of heat, and it was he who discovered that heat can be radiated across a vacuum. Sir Humphry Davy, while Professor here, showed a most beautiful experiment in which heat waves were reflected from one point of space to another by two parabolic mirrors of silvered metal. That experiment I propose to repeat, using the two mirrors that are believed to be the very pair Sir Humphry used, and that Professor Tyndall used often since.

One of the two curved mirrors hangs mouth downwards some fifteen feet above the lecture table. The other stands mouth upwards on the table, exactly beneath the first. The upper mirror is lowered for a moment; a red-hot iron ball is slung by a hook at its focus; and it is hoisted back into position above the table. The ball being at the focus, the mirror gathers the diverging waves and reflects them straight down in a parallel beam. It is a beam of invisible infra-red waves, accompanied by a few waves of visible red. This beam falls on the second mirror (Fig. 113), which gathers them again and brings them to a focus at F. I put my hand where the waves converge: it is intolerably hot. I hold a bit of black paper at the focus; at once it smokes and bursts into visible flame. Other things can be lit too. Here is a cigar. Held at the focus, it absorbs enough waves to warm through — and the rising wreath of smoke proves it has been lit by the reflected and concentrated waves.

[Figure 113: Davy's two parabolic mirrors, one hung mouth downwards high above the table with the red-hot ball at its focus, the other standing mouth upwards below. The invisible beam travels down between them and comes to a focus at F hot enough to light a cigar.]

Our experiment has proved that heat waves can be reflected. Our earlier experiments with the rock-salt prism proved that they can be refracted. Let us confirm both points with other experiments.

The red-hot ball, fast fading to dullness as it gives up its store of energy, has now been put on a stand on the table. Taking up the thermopile that did such useful service just now, let us see what it can tell us about that ball. The distance between them is some seven or eight feet. I have only to turn the conical mouthpiece of the thermopile straight towards the ball, and at once the spot of light on the wall shows that the pile has received some of the radiation. Waves our eyes cannot see, this thermopile can see, if only we turn it to look straight at their source. It acts as a kind of eye sensitive not to visible light but to infra-red waves of invisible light. I turn the aperture of the pile aside so that no heat waves can enter, and the spot of light settles back to zero. Then, taking up a simple piece of tin plate as a mirror, I reflect some of the heat waves from the iron ball back into the mouth of the thermopile. As soon as the mirror is set at the right angle the spot moves to the right, showing that we have reflected heat waves into the pile.

While we have a hot ball — better still if it were brightly red-hot — I can show some interesting experiments that turn on certain heat-indicating paints. Here is a specimen of a scarlet heat-indicating paint that turns black when heated. Here is another, of a pale yellow tint, that turns red when only gently warmed. And here is a paper screen mounted in a convenient frame. Its front is painted with the yellow heat-indicating paint; its back has been blackened so as to absorb the heat waves better.

Footnote: These heat-indicating paints are double iodides of mercury with other metals. They were discovered nearly thirty years ago by Dr. Meusel. The scarlet paint, which turns almost black at about 87° C, is the double iodide of mercury and copper. The yellow paint, which turns red at about 45° C, is the double iodide of mercury and silver. To prepare the former, a solution of potassium iodide is added to a solution of copper sulphate until the precipitate redissolves, when a concentrated solution of mercuric chloride is added, precipitating the red double iodide. To prepare the more sensitive yellow paint, add a solution of potassium iodide to a solution of silver nitrate until the precipitate (silver iodide) redissolves; then add a concentrated solution of mercuric chloride until a bright yellow precipitate forms. The precipitates are collected on filter paper and should be washed with cold water. They may be mixed with very dilute gum water so that they can be used as paint. Many interesting experiments can be done with these paints, illustrating the spread of heat by conduction and convection as well as by radiation. One very simple one is worth mentioning: it shows how hot water will float on cold. A strip of paper painted with the yellow paint is pasted vertically down the outside of a tall glass beaker. The beaker is half filled with cold water, a floating disk of wood is put in to prevent too much stirring, and then it is filled up by pouring in boiling water from a kettle. Only the top half of the strip turns red.

Holding this screen a little way from the hot ball — an ordinary coal fire answers even better — I put my hand between the ball and the screen, against its back. In a few seconds the screen turns red all over, except where my hand protects it; and there a shadow, a sort of heat shadow, is temporarily photographed — or rather thermographed — on the screen in yellow. As the screen cools it returns to its former yellow.

Here is another screen of paper painted with the scarlet heat-indicating paint. Its back has been gilded all over, and on the gilt surface a big letter S has been painted. I hold it with its gilded back to the hot ball, and the gilding reflects away most of the heat waves. Now you might suppose that the part where black paint has been put on top of the gold would be doubly protected against heat. But no! It absorbs the waves and grows warm; and the heat, conducted through the gold film, turns the scarlet paint on the front of the screen black. The letter painted on the back becomes visible on the front.

Here is a variation on one of Professor Tyndall's observations. You will find it recorded in his book on heat how, on one occasion when a fire broke out in a street, the heat radiated across from the burning house charred the window frames and burned and blistered the paint on the signboards. But where the number of the house stood in gilt letters on the signboard, the mere film of metal reflected the waves away and protected the paint and wood behind it from charring. To illustrate this, here is a blackened sheet of paper with a triangle of gold leaf pasted on it. The other side of the paper has been coated with the scarlet heat-indicating paint. Exposing it to the radiation of the hot ball, you see how the triangle protected by the gold leaf stays cool, while the rest absorbs heat and turns the scarlet to black.

You will probably admit that we now have plenty of proof that these invisible heat waves really are a kind of invisible light, and that the difference is one of degree rather than of kind. Consider once again the process of incandescence, in which such waves are given out. Heat a body, beginning by warming it gently. At first its particles vibrate only moderately; the waves they send out into the surrounding ether are few and of relatively great wave-length. As you warm the body more and more, its particles vibrate more actively and jostle together; it gives out more waves, and waves of shorter length and higher frequency. The long waves are still there — indeed there are more of them than before — but now there are some shorter ones as well. Heat it hotter still: the lower kinds of waves go on being given out, and given out more copiously, but some waves of a still higher kind now accompany them.

Here is a thin platinum wire stretched between two supports. By leading an electric current into it through a thicker copper wire, I can heat it as little or as much as I please. It is now warm, giving out a few dull waves. Increasing the current raises its temperature, and now it gives out much more heat, and with the heat a few waves of visible red. Every solid body when heated shows red as its first colour. The first glow is never blue or yellow. Increasing the temperature of the wire, it gives out orange as well as red, and so looks bright red. The next increase brings in yellow along with orange and red; then green joins the yellow, orange and red. As soon as the wire is hot enough to give out all the different visible kinds, we call the state a white heat. But no solid ever gets blue-hot, because in every case the emission begins at the bottom of the spectrum with red, the other colours chiming in until white is reached. Nor is white reached until a certain proportion of the still higher ultra-violet waves is being given out too.

So it appears that the process by which visible light-waves are emitted is only a continuation of the process by which the invisible infra-red waves are emitted. What further proof do you need of the essentially kindred nature of the visible and the invisible waves? I have shown you that these infra-red waves behave as visible light-waves do in a number of respects. You have seen that we can refract them with a lens, disperse them with a prism, reflect them with a mirror, and absorb them with a black surface. Further, they travel across space at the same rate as the visible waves. We know this from what happens during a total eclipse of the sun. At the moment the sun's light stops being visible, his heat stops reaching us too. When the light reappears, so do the heat waves. This one fact proves these heat waves to be simply light of an invisible kind. But if you are not satisfied, I will give you one further proof yet. You shall see that they can be polarised.

Footnote: Captain Abney has shown, however, that because the eye is so insensitive to red light and so much more sensitive to green, the tint of minimum visible brightness in any hot body — indeed in any feebly lit body in a perfectly dark room — is greenish. This holds even for a light seen through ruby glass, if the eye has been kept in darkness for some time.

Footnote: The whitest known artificial light is that of the arc-lamp, the light of carbon incandescent at about 3500° C. This is considerably lower than the temperature of the sun's surface, which gives a light with a relatively higher proportion of blue and violet and of ultra-violet waves. In fact, seen in full sunlight, the light of the arc-lamp is decidedly dull and reddish. No accurate definition of any standard of whiteness has ever been given.

Here, as in my third lecture (Fig. 93, p. 132), stands a pair of Nicol prisms, one as polariser and one as analyser. The lantern sends its beams through them. Catching the visible light on a paper screen, we notice that when the analyser is set with its principal plane parallel to the polariser's, light comes through; but on turning the analyser through a right angle, all the light is cut off. That is a purely optical experiment.

Now let me take my thermopile — which I described to you as a sort of eye sensitive to the invisible heat waves — and put it where the paper screen was. At present the polariser and analyser are crossed, giving the dark field (p. 119). No light falls on the thermopile, and no heat waves either, for, see, the spot of light from the galvanometer that shows the state of the pile is at its zero. Now I turn the analyser back and restore the bright field. At once the spot swings over to the right, telling us that polarised heat, as well as polarised light, is coming through the analyser. Turn the analyser back again: the visible waves are cut off, and so are the invisible ones, for the spot of light has returned.

Now let me clinch the proof by working entirely with invisible waves and excluding the visible ones altogether. Here is a sheet of opaque hard black indiarubber — ebonite, in fact. No visible light will come through it. And yet, you see, having filtered out the visible waves and let the invisible ones through, there still come through the polariser waves that can warm the thermopile, and that can be cut off by turning the analyser to the crossed position.

This material, ebonite, is a most interesting one, because it can act in this way as a wave filter, transmitting only the longer waves. Wave filters — or ray filters — were used extensively by Professor Tyndall in his lectures on radiant heat, but I do not think he knew the properties of ebonite. Here is one of the Crookes radiometers (Fig. 110, p. 199). I place it in front of the lantern but screen it at first with a thick sheet of metal, so that it is all but at rest; the diffused light of the room is enough to make it turn slowly. Now I put a sheet of ebonite in place of the metal, which is just as opaque to ordinary light — yet the little vanes now run merrily round.

Tyndall's filter for heat waves was a cell containing a dark solution of iodine in carbon disulphide. Here is one of the cells, kept cool by an outer jacket with cold water circulating in it. Behind the wall at the back of the theatre is a powerful electric arc-lamp whose beams come into the theatre through an opening. This beam I propose to concentrate with a rock-salt lens, bringing it to a focus after it has passed through the cell that filters out the visible waves and lets the invisible ones through. First we make the experiment without the cell. All the waves, visible and invisible, come to a focus. Holding a bit of black paper at the focus, it smoulders and then catches fire. Now put in the cell. The visible light is cut off; but hold the bit of paper in the invisible focus and it again begins to smoulder, and finally breaks into visible flame.

And now I must pass to the most important recent discoveries — discoveries dating only from 1888 — of some larger waves that are exactly like light-waves in the following respects: they can be reflected, refracted, absorbed, polarised and diffracted. Yet they differ in the most striking way from any waves of light we have considered so far. Their wave-lengths, instead of being measured in a few millionths of an inch, may be several inches, several yards, or even several hundred yards. I refer to the electric waves predicted in 1864 by the late Professor Clerk Maxwell and discovered experimentally in 1888 by the late Professor Hertz.

Hertz was occupied with researches on electric sparks, which in certain circumstances were known to be oscillatory. That is to say, each spark might, under certain conditions, consist of a series of sparks flying backwards and forwards along the same path with great regularity and extreme rapidity. If, for instance, there were twenty successive oscillations, each lasting only one hundred-millionth of a second, the whole series would last only one five-millionth of a second, and would of course look to the eye like a single instantaneous spark. Working with these oscillatory sparks, Hertz was led to investigate the disturbances they set up in the surrounding medium, which travel outwards as waves.

To illustrate Hertz's work I must resort to a few diagrams. Fig. 114 shows the apparatus set up on the table. To produce the sparks we use an induction coil. The electric discharges the coil produces are sent into the simple apparatus called the Hertzian oscillator. As you see, it consists of two square sheets of metal fixed to two metal rods that nearly meet, ending in two well-polished metal balls. There is a small gap between them, across which the sparks are seen to pass. But each such spark is really a series of oscillations, the electric discharge swinging backwards and forwards not merely across the gap where you see the spark, but from one end of the apparatus to the other. Suppose the coil makes one of these metal wings — say A in Fig. 114 — positive, while the other, B, is negative. When the electric state has risen high enough, the air in the gap is pierced by a spark.
