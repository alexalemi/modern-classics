The charge rushes from A to B, and in doing so overcharges B, making it positive while leaving A negative. At once the charge surges back from B to A, and back again to B, each oscillation lasting only about one hundred-millionth of a second. The frequency of these oscillations depends on the size of the apparatus. At every oscillation an electric wave is sent out from the apparatus into the surrounding space and travels away at the speed of light. The wave is strongest in the directions at right angles to the metal rods along which the electricity is oscillating, and at right angles to the plane of the metal wings.

Fig. 115 gives a front view of the oscillator, and also of the apparatus Hertz used for detecting the waves, called the resonator. The model on the table is made to the same size as one of Hertz's smaller pieces of apparatus, the two metal wings being 40 centimetres square with 60 centimetres between them. The wave-length of the waves it sends out is rather less than 300 centimetres, or nearly 10 feet. The resonator, or detector, is a simple wire bent into a ring so that its two ends nearly meet. Hertz demonstrated that waves pass out from the oscillator by holding the resonator some distance away and watching the minute electric sparks they set up in the small gap between the ends of the wire.

But the resonator ring must be of the right size and held in the right position. Its size — 70 centimetres across, in this example — must be such that the natural period of an electric current oscillating round the ring agrees with the period of the waves the oscillator sends out. Its position must be such that the waves, as they reach it, set up secondary oscillations in the ring. If the resonator is stood upright, edgeways to the oscillator, no sparks appear: the waves simply stream past it. But if the resonator is held horizontally, and on the base line shown in Fig. 114, sparks can be seen in the gap.

Hertz put a great sheet of metal at the far end of the room he was working in, to reflect the waves back, and then moved about the room exploring to find where sparks were produced. He found that when the waves are reflected back on themselves there are nodal points, just as there are nodal points in sound-waves and light-waves reflected back. These nodal points were spaced exactly half a wave-length apart, so the wave-length could be measured precisely.

Before Hertz's time it was known that there were oscillating sparks. Fig. 116 illustrates some experiments I made myself on the subject in 1876. I had an induction coil connected so as to send sparks across a small air gap, A, to a condenser made of a dielectric, D, between two metal plates, P and Q. I found that with this spark gap in the coil's circuit I could draw secondary sparks at B from the outer plate of the condenser; and by means of a small vacuum tube and a rotating mirror I proved these sparks to be oscillatory. With this arrangement in place I could get sparks from insulated metal objects anywhere in the room. I had only to hold a knife or a pencil case to the

A word more about the electric oscillations themselves. Each sudden discharge of the induction coil — and to make them sudden the discharge balls must be well polished — sets up a train of oscillations, shown diagrammatically in Fig. 118 by the upper curve, which die away as time goes on. A mechanical analogy may be found in the vibrations of a spring. Bend it to one side and suddenly release it.

Footnote: A run of waves diminishing steadily from left to right, drawn beneath the text as a picture of a dying oscillation.

It flies backwards and forwards, the motion dying out after a certain number of swings. In the same way trains of waves are set up which die away as they travel across space. But suppose they fall on a proper resonator or detector: then, by their timed impulses, they will set up a sympathetic electrical vibration in it, the oscillations beginning and growing stronger as wave after wave arrives. This is shown graphically by the lower curve in Fig. 118. It corresponds, in fact, to the way sound-waves from one tuning fork, falling on another, will set it vibrating in sympathy, provided it is tuned to the same note.

[Figure 118: Above, the train of electric oscillations set up by a single discharge, dying away as it goes. Below, the answering train built up in the resonator, growing stronger as wave after wave arrives.]

Fig. 119 shows the parabolic mirrors, each about 6 feet high, with which Hertz demonstrated the reflection of electric waves. At the focus of one an oscillator was placed upright — an arrangement producing sparks that vibrated up and down. The waves that resulted were of course waves of up-and-down motion, polarised in a vertical plane, and were reflected in a beam straight across the room to the second mirror, which gathered them and brought them to a focus on a detector. This detector was straight, not circular, with a small spark gap at its middle where the minute sparks could be seen.

[Figure 119: Hertz's two parabolic mirrors, each about six feet high, facing one another across the room — the oscillator standing at the focus of one, the straight detector with its little spark gap at the focus of the other.]

Many forms of oscillator, or vibrator, have been used by different experimenters to produce electric waves. Some are shown in Fig. 120. The first is one Hertz himself used: instead of flat wings of metal he used cylindrical metal conductors in this case. In another form, described as a dumb-bell oscillator, there were two large metal balls. In every case the spark gap was arranged between two small, highly polished metal balls midway along the length of the oscillator. The third shape is one devised by Professor Righi for making short waves. Here there are three gaps, the central one between two balls immersed in a vessel of oil to prevent premature discharge. The lowest form in Fig. 120 is also Professor Righi's: his apparatus for producing exceedingly short waves — less than an inch long, in fact — by the oscillations set up between two spheres to which sparks are given from two smaller balls outside them.

[Figure 120: Four forms of oscillator. At the top, Hertz's cylindrical conductors; below it, the dumb-bell oscillator with two large balls; then Righi's three-gap form with its central pair in oil; and at the bottom Righi's arrangement for waves under an inch long.]

The next diagram (Fig. 121) shows two forms of oscillator used by Professor Oliver Lodge of Liverpool. Here is a well-polished metal ball supported between two smaller balls that nearly touch it, one on each side. When a discharge is passed through this central ball, an electric charge surges from side to side in it with great vigour; but the motion dies out after only three or four such surges, since the ball radiates its energy into space readily. It does not send out a long train of waves: the effect dies out after about 1½ or 2 complete oscillations. The wave-length of the waves it emits is about 1½ times the diameter of the ball. The other form, Fig. 121b, shows a metal cylinder sparked into at opposite ends of a diameter by two balls inside it. This oscillator parts with its energy less rapidly and its oscillations last longer. It gives rise to a train of waves that travel chiefly straight out of the mouth of the cylinder.

Fig. 122 shows one of the simplest ways of detecting such electric waves, and one that makes them evident to a whole audience at once. An ordinary gold-leaf electroscope is fitted with a by-pass of wire carrying a minute gap, adjustable by a screw, to break the continuity. If the gold leaves are carefully charged they will stay apart, because their electric potential has not been raised enough to discharge across the gap. But if an electric wave from a Hertz oscillator — especially from one set upright, to produce vertically polarised waves — now falls on the electroscope, it sets up in the wire by-pass an electric surge that will leap the gap with a minute spark. And while the spark bridges the gap, the gold leaves discharge themselves and fall together.

A still more sensitive detector, used by Lodge, consists of a bit of glass tube loosely filled with iron filings, joined with a weak voltaic cell in circuit with a galvanometer. Loose metal filings or powders are a very bad and incoherent conductor: hardly any current passes through them. But let an electric wave fall on the tube and instantly the filings become — as M. Branly discovered — an excellent conductor. The galvanometer moves, and with it the spot of light it reflects, proving that an electric wave has been detected by the tube.

[Figure 124: Lodge's apparatus. The sender is an oscillator in a copper box with a perforated diaphragm in front; the detector D, a tube of coarse iron filings behind a copper hat, sends its indication to the galvanometer G through screened wires. A paraffin prism and a wire grid stand ready on the table.]

Fig. 124 shows a set of the apparatus with which Professor Lodge repeated and verified Hertz's observations of the optical properties of these electric waves. Hertz had reflected them with parabolic mirrors 6 feet high and refracted them with huge prisms of pitch. He found they could pass through wooden floors and stone walls. He polarised them and diffracted them. Lodge was able to repeat these results with apparatus of less heroic dimensions. The sender consists of an oscillator like Fig. 121b, having a 5-inch ball emitting 7-inch waves, enclosed in a copper box with a diaphragm in front pierced with holes of various sizes, so that the radiation can be moderated to any degree wanted. As detector, D, a tube full of coarse iron filings is put at the back of a copper hat whose open end can be turned in whatever direction the waves are to come from. Wires run from the detector to the galvanometer, G, enclosed in a metal tube to shield them from stray radiation. If the receiver is set askew to the sender so that no waves from the sender enter it, the galvanometer shows nothing. But hold a sheet of metal in front at the proper angle to reflect the waves into the mouth of the receiver, and at once the detector responds and the galvanometer says so. In the same way, as the diagram shows, a prism of paraffin wax may be used to refract the electric waves into the mouth of the receiver. The picture also shows a grid of metal wires that can be used to polarise them.

More recent than Professor Lodge's researches are those of Professor Righi, whose apparatus is shown in Fig. 125. It consists of two parts, a sender and a receiver. The sender, on the left, is a small oscillator — the one at the bottom of Fig. 120 — with three spark gaps, the central one finely adjustable. In Fig. 125 this gap lies between the ball marked V and another below it, not shown, enclosed in a small leather capsule filled with vaseline. This oscillator stands at the focus of a parabolic mirror, M, which reflects the waves straight out to the right across the central table, C. The detector, D, also fitted with a parabolic mirror, is an optical one: a film of silver on a slip of glass, divided in two across the middle by a diamond cut. Minute sparks pass across this narrow gap and are viewed through an eyepiece at D. The apparatus is quite small enough to stand on an ordinary table, and looks for all the world like a piece of optical apparatus. On the central table can be mounted reflectors, prisms, lenses, grids, or anything else.

With these devices Professor Righi has tracked down the optical properties of electric waves varying from 8 inches down to 1 inch in length. He has shown interference fringes with Fresnel's mirrors, with the biprism, with thin plates, and by diffraction. He has verified the laws of refraction, reflection, total reflection, polarisation, and of elliptical and circular oscillation. He has also investigated the transparency of various media, and the selective transparency of wood according to its grain — a property that makes wood polarise electric waves just as tourmaline polarises light. In short, he has completed the proof that these waves have all the known properties of ordinary light.

Other workers have been busy in the same field. We are shortly to hear a discourse here by Professor J. Chunder Bose of Calcutta on the polarisation of the electric wave as he has studied it, with an exceedingly elegant apparatus producing shorter waves still.

But before I close I must show you at least some of these effects in actual experiment. Here, in a metal box, is a small induction coil worked by a couple of battery cells. The spark it makes is carried to a small oscillator closely resembling Lodge's (Fig. 121). It is, in fact, a short piece of polished platinum tube 4 millimetres across, between two small beads of platinum. It emits waves about ½ inch long. I surround it with a metal bonnet, or tube, to direct the waves straight out. The detector is simply a tube loosely filled with iron filings, in circuit with a galvanometer and a cell made of a bit of iron and a bit of copper dipping into salt water. It too has an outer metal tube to screen it from stray radiation. If I set the sender and receiver facing one another, the receiver responds whenever a spark passes at the sender. After each response I have to give the detector a gentle tap, to shake up the filings and send the galvanometer index back to zero.

[Figure 128: The lecture-table version of the experiment — the little platinum-tube oscillator in its metal bonnet facing the filings-tube detector, each screened in its own metal sleeve, with the galvanometer alongside.]

Now I set the receiver askew, so that none of the waves from the sender can reach the detector. Spark after spark is discharged at the oscillator, but there is no response. Then I hold a plate of metal as a mirror to reflect the waves, or I put a small prism of paraffin wax in at the proper angle. At once the detector responds, proving that the waves have been turned round the corner, refracted by the prism.

If, then, it can be proved that these electric waves, though invisible, can be reflected, refracted, polarised and absorbed exactly as the visible waves of ordinary light can, have we not good reason to regard them as one and the same phenomenon? By every test, in every physical property, save only the accident that our eyes are not sensitive to them, they are nothing else than waves of light. But if that is so, are we not logically entitled to draw the converse conclusion — that if ordinary light behaves in the same way and has all the same properties on the small scale as these electric waves have on the large, then the little waves of ordinary light are electric waves too? That, indeed, was the brilliant speculation, the daring theory put forward in 1864 by the late Professor Clerk Maxwell. Basing his ideas on the investigations pursued in this institution by Faraday, who was himself the first to venture into this enchanted domain of electro-optics, Maxwell predicted the properties of electric waves in that famous memoir where he set out the doctrine that light consists of electric vibrations in space. And the brilliant success of Hertz, and of those who have followed him, in demonstrating the optical properties of these waves by experiment is the abundant justification of Maxwell's prediction.
