There is a gem called tourmaline which, cut into thin slices, has the property of polarising light. This gem is often found dark green, but also brown, dark blue, and even ruby-tinted. Into the beam of ordinary white light now falling on the screen I introduce a thin slice of brown tourmaline (Fig. 77). It looks dark, because it cuts off more than half the light. But the light that does get through is polarised, the vibrations being parallel to the longer dimension of the slice. A second thin slice of tourmaline is now brought in and laid over the first. When the two are parallel, light comes through both (Fig. 78). But if one is now turned so that they are crossed, as in Fig. 79, no light can get through the crossed crystals at all. One cuts off all horizontal vibrations and all horizontal components of vibration; the other cuts off all vertical vibrations and components. So when crossed they produce a dark field. One acts as polariser, the other as analyser.

Let us go back to the big polariser (Fig. 74) that we used in the earlier experiment, the one that was as clear as glass. It is made of Iceland spar, a natural crystal that was once common but is now rare and expensive. As it comes from the mine in Iceland, this spar has the peculiar property known as double refraction: when you look through it you see everything double. Here is a fine specimen mounted in a tube. Look at your finger through it and you will see two fingers. It is a substance that splits the waves of light into two parts, giving two images — and, moreover, polarises the light in the act of splitting it, so that each part comes out polarised.

Selecting a piece of Iceland spar of suitable proportions, we cut it across in an oblique direction from one blunt corner to the other — using a piece of copper wire as a saw, with some emery powder — and polish the surfaces, dividing the prism into two wedges. These are then cemented back together with Canada balsam, a resinous cement, and the polarising prism is complete. It works on light as follows. When the waves enter through one end face they are split into two parts, which take slightly different directions and strike the film of balsam at different angles. As a result, one of the two beams is reflected off sideways when it meets the balsam, as from a slanted mirror, while the other passes through the prism and comes out at the far end face. Only one of the two beams therefore gets through; the other is suppressed, reflected out of the way.

Let us give a little more attention to this phenomenon of double refraction, which gives us two beams of light polarised in different planes. Here is another wave-motion model (Fig. 86), built to show two sets of waves polarised in planes at right angles to one another. Here are two waves of silvered beads, both of which march along when I turn the handle. Both have the same wave-length; both march at the same pace and towards the same end. But there is this difference between them: in one the displacements are polarised at 45° one way, in the other they are at 45° the other way. Of course there is some mechanism inside the box to make them move like that; but they illustrate what is meant by saying that there can be two waves polarised at right angles to one another.

But the question still remains: why should the spar act on light so as to split it into two oppositely polarised beams? Let us first prove that it really does. Here is a piece of spar mounted as a double-image prism. In front of the lantern is placed a metal diaphragm with a round hole in it, whose image is focused on the screen as a circular white spot. Now, putting in the double-image prism, we see that it splits the light in two, diverting half of it away from the original spot and producing a second one which — with this size of aperture — overlaps the first. On turning the double-image prism, the ordinary image stays put while the extraordinary image revolves round it.

Now, to prove that each of them is polarised, I put in a Nicol prism. On turning it, we see that it cuts off first one of the two spots and then the other. If the Nicol prism is set to transmit only vertical vibrations, then as the double-image prism is turned we see that when the two images stand vertically one above the other (as in Fig. 88), the ordinary image is cut off while the extraordinary is transmitted. On turning the double-image prism further, the ordinary image gradually appears while the extraordinary fades away, until when the prism has been turned a quarter round the ordinary image is transmitted and the extraordinary cut off, as Fig. 89 shows. If the prism is turned to 45°, both images are equally bright, the directions of the resolved vibrations being shown by the fine lines in Fig. 90.

Now, having proved that the spar does split light into two beams whose vibrations move in different ways, we still have to consider how it does it. The resolution of oblique movements into two components at right angles to one another is an important principle in mechanics, and one best illustrated for our purpose by a model. Suppose a displacement is happening obliquely: it can always be resolved into two parts, one up-and-down and one right-and-left. The model (Fig. 91) is fixed on a board with a little diagram drawn in the corner. Suppose the oblique motion runs from A to B; you can resolve that oblique motion into two parts, a vertical part marked AV and a horizontal part marked AH.

Every schoolchild knows the problem called the parallelogram of forces, by which two forces acting at one point at the same time combine into a single oblique resultant along the diagonal of the parallelogram whose sides are the two component forces. Our present problem is simply the converse of that. Here in the model are two wooden slides with crossheads. One can slide up and down only; the other right and left only. Running in grooves in the two crossheads is a roller fixed to the end of a third wooden bar, which can be set at any angle and then slid along by hand. If I set this third bar horizontal and slide it, all the movement it gives is horizontal — there is no vertical component. If I set it vertical and slide it up and down, it simply moves the vertical slide up and down. But if I set it obliquely and slide it along, then part of its motion moves the vertical slide, while the horizontal component of its motion moves the horizontal slide. How much of the motion is vertical and how much horizontal obviously depends on the angle at which the original motion is given. Now, there is one particular angle at which the two resolved parts would be equal. What is that angle? If I set the bar exactly midway, at 45°, the displacement is resolved into two equal components. You will remember that at 45° the double-image prism gave two images of equal brightness.

[Figure 91: The resolution model. Two wooden slides, one running up and down and one side to side, are driven through their crossheads by a third bar that can be set at any angle. Set it at 45° and it moves both slides equally.]

Such a resolution into two parts can be produced in oblique waves of light by any of the crystals here — Iceland spar, quartz, mica, selenite — provided the crystal has a particular kind of structure. The condition is that the crystal must have a greater optical rigidity, a greater stiffness, in one direction than in another. You know what is meant by the grain in a piece of wood, and how much more easily it splits one way than another. But this grain, which comes from the fibrous structure, shows itself in other ways besides ease of splitting. A piece of wood is harder to bend along the grain than across it. And so with some crystals: they have an invisible structure, a grain so fine that we cannot see the fibres or lines of it, but a grain that shows itself in various ways. There are differences in the ease of cleavage, and differences of rigidity in different directions too. This particular crystal, Iceland spar, has a greater rigidity in one direction than in another, and as a result any wave of light passing obliquely into it is split into two portions: one with its vibrations parallel to the axis of greatest rigidity, and one with its vibrations parallel to the axis of least rigidity, and therefore at right angles to the first. That is why it splits light in two, and why each part comes out polarised. Some crystals — diamonds and garnets among them — are equally rigid in all directions, and therefore show no double refraction and do not polarise light at all.

Footnote: The word rigidity is preferred here, though in many treatises it would be called "elasticity." To say the crystal has different elasticities in different directions — which is quite correct — would give many people a wrong idea, because of the loose way the word "elastic" is often used. Elastic does not mean that a thing can easily be stretched. The hardest steel is more perfectly elastic than a piece of indiarubber, but it is certainly not as stretchable. In saying that it has elasticity we mean that however little we manage to compress or stretch it, it returns, when released, to its former shape and size. In scientific treatises a substance is regarded as having the highest coefficient of elasticity when it needs the greatest stress to produce a given deformation, or strain. When we are dealing, as with transverse displacements, with motions that tend to shear the medium (see p. 107 above), the particular elasticity to consider is the one that resists shearing, and for that the term rigidity is entirely appropriate. In all this optical work we are of course dealing not with mechanical elasticity but with optical elasticity — the elasticity of the ether within the substance. It is this which, in the crystals in question, is regarded as greater in one direction than in another. The greater the rigidity, the higher the speed at which a wave whose displacements lie in that direction travels. See the Appendix to Lecture Three, p. 156.

Here again is a model to illustrate the splitting of the waves. Two thin flexible strips of ebonite, O and E (Fig. 92), are pushed into saw-cuts in the end of a wooden cylinder. In the other end I can fix a third strip, A. Notice, if you please, that O is set with its edge vertical, so it can vibrate right and left; while E is set with its edge horizontal, and can vibrate up and down only. Holding the wooden cylinder in my left hand, I use my right to give the strip A at the other end a vibrating movement. If I waggle A from right to left, then O vibrates right and left. If I waggle A up and down, then E vibrates up and down while O stays quiet. And if I now give A an oblique motion, both O and E vibrate, the oblique motion being resolved into a vertical part and a horizontal part.

Returning now to the waves of light themselves, let us see some of the beautiful effects that come out of these operations — splitting the vibrations in two, recombining them after they have passed through a slice of crystal, and then analysing, that is to say resolving, the light that falls on the screen.
