TEXT ANALYSIS — Dante's Paradiso
Read this AND inferno/text_analysis.txt AND purgatorio/text_analysis.txt
before translating. The consistency ledger is running_notes.txt.


WHAT THIS EDITION IS
---------------------------------------------------------------------
The third and last cantica, complete: 33 cantos, 4,758 lines, from the
Italian (Gutenberg #999, the Petrocchi text), with Longfellow's 1867
English as a per-canto crib under reference/. USE THE CRIB TO CHECK A
LINE, NEVER TO BORROW A PHRASE — he is the most archaic English in the
collection's screening pass after Pusey, and check.py's sweep exists to
catch the drift.


THE FORM DOES NOT CHANGE
---------------------------------------------------------------------
  - VERSE STAYS VERSE, every line its own line, tab-indented.
  - TERCETS STAY TERCETS: same number, same order, three lines each and
    one line closing every canto.
  - THE LINE COUNT PER CANTO IS EXACT (142, 148, 130, 142, ...) and the
    total is 4,758 in 33 cantos. Canto III, at 130 lines, is the
    shortest in the whole Comedy: that number is not a bug.
  - NO RHYME and no imposed metre.
  - ENJAMBMENT ACROSS A TERCET BOUNDARY IS DANTE'S; keep it.


THE PROBLEM OF THIS CANTICA, STATED PLAINLY
---------------------------------------------------------------------
The Inferno has bodies and the Purgatorio has weather. The Paradiso has
neither: the souls are lights, the settings are spheres of moving
light, and nothing that could be called a plot happens after canto I.
Its reputation for being unreadable comes almost entirely from
translations that meet that difficulty by going vague and grand.

THE ANSWER IS THE OPPOSITE OF GRAND. Dante is at his most CONCRETE
exactly where the subject is least physical: souls come towards him
like fish rising to something dropped in a still tank, like a falcon
unhooded, like sparks in molten metal, like a clock's wheels; a soul
hides itself in its own light like a silkworm in silk. KEEP EVERY ONE
OF THESE COMPARISONS AND KEEP THEM DOMESTIC. They are the whole
technique: the reader is held by things he has actually seen while
being told about things nobody has.

THE INEFFABILITY PASSAGES ARE NOT PADDING. Dante says over and over
that he cannot remember, cannot say, is running out of language:
"trasumanar" cannot be put into words; his sight was greater than his
speech; the vision melts like snow, like Sibyl's leaves in the wind.
These are load-bearing — the poem is ABOUT the failure of language at
the top of it — and every one of them is rendered in full and plainly.
Do not smooth them into an apology and do not inflate them.


LIGHT IS A TECHNICAL VOCABULARY AND IS KEPT DISTINCT
---------------------------------------------------------------------
Dante does not use one word for light, and a translation that does
turns a hundred pages into a blur:

  luce            "light" — the thing itself.
  lume            "light" as it is received or shed, often a source.
  raggio          "ray".
  splendore       "splendour" (reflected light, in his own optics).
  fulgore         "brightness", "blaze".
  lampo / lampeggiare  "flash", "to flash".
  favilla / scintilla  "spark".
  faville         "sparks" — kept plural and concrete.
  candor          "whiteness".
  chiarezza       "brightness" (degree, not glare).
  vivo lume       "living light", kept as a unit.

Likewise the graded vocabulary of BLESSEDNESS AND SIGHT: beatitudine,
letizia, gaudio, giocondo are happiness/joy/gladness/joyful and are not
interchangeable; vista, veduta, viso mean sight and face and Dante puns
on both; disio and voglia stay "longing" and "will" as in the
Purgatorio.


VOICE
---------------------------------------------------------------------
As before: THE hume RULE GOVERNS. Dante's famous lines here are famous
for being plain — "and in his will is our peace" is exactly that, and
no ornament improves it.

Three registers to keep apart:
  1 THE FLIGHT. Beatrice explains and Dante rises. The explanations are
    real arguments — on the moon's markings, on vows, on the angelic
    orders — and their JOINTS MUST STAY VISIBLE: they are followable,
    and making them misty is the standard way this cantica is ruined.
  2 THE ENCOUNTERS. Piccarda, Justinian, Cacciaguida, Peter Damian,
    Aquinas, Bonaventure, Adam, Peter, Bernard. Each is a person with a
    temper and a subject, and each sounds different. Cacciaguida in
    particular (XV-XVII) is the emotional centre of the whole poem and
    is talking about Dante's own exile.
  3 THE INVECTIVE. Peter's face goes red and the whole heaven blushes
    with him (XXVII); Beatrice on preachers who sell wind; Folco on
    Florence's cursed flower. The anger is as hot as anything in the
    Inferno and must not be dulled by the surrounding light.


LATIN, LITURGY AND SONG
---------------------------------------------------------------------
LATIN STAYS LATIN, as in the other two cantiche: "Osanna", "Sperent in
te", "Te Deum", "Ave Maria", "Dio, Sabaòth" and the rest are heard as
what they are. Where Dante gives an Italian paraphrase in the next
line, so does this edition. Bernard's great prayer at XXXIII.1 is
ITALIAN ("Vergine Madre, figlia del tuo figlio") and is rendered in
English like everything else Dante wrote in Italian.


NAMES, REFERENCES, NO NOTES
---------------------------------------------------------------------
  - FAMILIAR ENGLISH FORMS where they exist: Beatrice, Bernard, Thomas
    Aquinas, Bonaventure, Francis, Dominic, Justinian, Trajan, Solomon,
    Peter, James, John, Adam. Italian forms for Italians who have no
    English one: Piccarda, Cacciaguida, Cunizza, Folco, Romeo.
  - NO FOOTNOTES ON THE PAGE. Where a reference is unfollowable, let
    the line carry the smallest identification it can. Every such case
    is logged in running_notes.txt.
  - THE PERIPHRASES FOR PLACES AND PLANETS ARE THE POINT and are kept:
    "the star that woos the sun" is Venus and stays that.
  - THE ASTRONOMY IS HIS. Ptolemaic spheres, the crystalline heaven,
    the influence of the planets on character: all stated as fact,
    unhedged, with no wink (the Verne rule, as with Ball's astronomy).


THE VERNE RULE, AND THIS CANTICA NEEDS IT AS MUCH AS THE INFERNO
---------------------------------------------------------------------
No note anywhere in the volume:
  - PREDESTINATION AND THE UNBAPTIZED. Canto XIX is a sustained
    argument that a man born on the Indus who never heard of Christ may
    be closer to salvation than baptized Christians, and canto XX puts
    two pagans in heaven (Trajan and Ripheus); canto XXXII sorts
    infants who died before they could choose. Dante's position is
    difficult, he knows it is difficult, and he says the difficulty is
    the point: render the whole argument and do not soften either end.
  - THE ATTACKS ON THE PAPACY are savage and specific — Boniface named
    as a usurper who has made Peter's tomb a sewer of blood and stench,
    the prophecy of Clement's fall, the friars preaching jokes.
  - THE HIERARCHY. Women, marriage, vows, the natural order of the
    spheres and the ranking of souls all stand as he writes them.
  - The claim that the poem itself is a prophecy given to Dante to
    write down (XVII.127-142) is his, and stands unhedged.


THE ENDING
---------------------------------------------------------------------
Canto XXXIII is the most famous close in European poetry and is the
place where the temptation to embroider is strongest. Do not. The last
tercets are almost monosyllabic in Italian; the geometer who cannot
square the circle, the wheel turned by love, and the last line —
"l'amor che move il sole e l'altre stelle" — need nothing but exactness
and the same last word all three cantiche end on: stars.
