Canto Twenty-Six

	While I was in doubt because my sight was put out,
	a breath came out of the blazing flame
	that had put it out, and made me attentive,

	saying: "Until you get back the sense
	of the sight you have used up on me,
	it is right to make up for it by talking.

	Begin, then; and say where your soul
	is aimed, and take it that your sight
	is bewildered in you and not dead:

	because the lady who is leading you through
	this godlike region has in her look
	the power Ananias had in his hand, when he gave Paul back his sight."

	I said: "At her pleasure, early or late,
	let the cure come to my eyes, which were the gates
	she came in by, with the fire I burn with always.

	The good that satisfies this court
	is the beginning and the end, the Alpha and Omega, of every text
	that Love reads to me, softly or aloud."

	That same voice which had taken away
	my fear at the sudden dazzling
	set me to talking again,

	and said: "Certainly you must clear yourself
	through a finer sieve: you must say
	who aimed your bow at such a target."

	And I: "By philosophical arguments
	and by authority that comes down from here,
	such a love has to be stamped on me:

	for the good, as good, so far as it is understood,
	kindles love, and it kindles the more
	the more goodness it holds inside itself.

	So towards that being, God, which is so far above all others
	that every good found outside it
	is nothing but a beam of its own light,

	the mind of everyone who makes out the truth
	on which this proof is founded must move
	in love, more than towards anything else.

	This truth is laid out for my understanding
	by the philosopher Aristotle, who shows me that the everlasting beings
	are moved by the first love.

	It is laid out by the voice of God himself, who cannot lie,
	when he says to Moses, speaking of himself:
	'I will make you see all goodness.'

	You lay it out for me too, John, in the opening
	of your Gospel, which cries the secret
	of this place down there more loudly than any other announcement."

	And I heard: "By human understanding
	and by authorities that agree with it,
	the highest of your loves looks to God.

	But say as well whether you feel other cords
	drawing you towards him, so that you may sound out
	with how many teeth this love bites you."

	The holy purpose of John, Christ's eagle, was not hidden from me;
	on the contrary, I saw
	where he wanted my confession to go.

	So I began again: "All those bites
	that can turn a heart towards God
	have come together in my charity:

	for the being of the world and my own being,
	the death he suffered so that I might live,
	and what every believer hopes for, as I do,

	together with the living knowledge I spoke of,
	have drawn me out of the sea of crooked love
	and set me on the shore of the straight love.

	The leaves that the whole garden of the eternal
	gardener is green with, all his creatures, I love just as much
	as the good he has given to each of them."

	As soon as I fell silent, a sweetest song
	rang through the heaven, and my lady
	was saying with the others: "Holy, holy, holy!"

	And as a man is woken out of sleep by a sharp light,
	because the spirit of sight runs to meet
	the brightness that passes through the coats of the eye one by one,

	and the man who wakes shrinks from what he sees,
	his sudden waking is so uncomprehending,
	until his judgment comes to his help:

	so Beatrice chased every mote out of my eyes
	with the ray of hers,
	which shone more than a thousand miles;

	so that I saw better after than before,
	and, almost stupefied, I asked
	about a fourth light which I saw among us.

	And my lady: "Inside those rays
	the first soul that the first Power ever created
	gazes lovingly at its maker."

	As a branch bends its top
	at the wind's going by, and then lifts
	by its own strength, which raises it again,

	so I did while she was speaking,
	astonished; and then a longing to speak,
	which I was burning with, made me sure again.

	And I began: "O Adam, the one apple
	that was made ripe from the start, O ancient father
	to whom every bride is both daughter and daughter-in-law,

	as devoutly as I can I beg you
	to speak to me: you see what I want,
	and to hear you sooner I do not say it."

	Sometimes an animal stirs under a cover,
	so that its feeling has to show
	by the way the wrapping follows it;

	and in the same way the first soul
	made it clear to me through his covering
	how gladly he was coming to please me.

	Then he breathed: "Without your telling it to me,
	I make out your wish better
	than you make out whatever is surest to you;

	because I see it in the truthful mirror, God,
	which reflects all other things exactly,
	and which nothing else reflects.

	You want to hear how long it is since God put me
	in the high garden, where this lady
	made you ready for so long a stair,

	and how long that garden was a delight to my eyes,
	and the true cause of the great anger,
	and the language I used and made.

	Now, my son, the tasting of the tree
	was not in itself the cause of so long an exile,
	but only the overstepping of the mark.

	In Limbo, the place your lady sent Virgil out of,
	I longed for this company
	for four thousand three hundred and two turnings of the sun;

	and while I was on earth I saw him come back
	to all the lights of his own road
	nine hundred and thirty times.

	The language I spoke was altogether dead
	before Nimrod's people set themselves
	to their unfinishable work, the tower of Babel:

	for no product of reason was ever lasting,
	because of the human liking that keeps renewing itself
	as the heavens turn.

	That a man speaks is nature's doing;
	but whether it is this way or that, nature
	leaves to you, to do as it pleases you.

	Before I went down to the anguish of Hell,
	the highest good, from which comes the gladness
	that wraps me, was called I on earth;

	and afterwards he was called El: and that is fitting,
	for the habits of mortals are like leaves
	on a branch, one going and another coming.

	In Eden, on the mountain that rises highest out of the sea,
	I stayed, with a life first pure and then shamed,
	from the first hour of the day to the hour that follows

	the sixth, about seven hours, when the sun passes noon."
