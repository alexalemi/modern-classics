Canto Nineteen

	The beautiful image of the eagle, which the interwoven souls
	made, glad in their sweet enjoyment,
	appeared before me with its wings open;

	each of them seemed a little ruby in which
	a ray of the sun was burning so kindled
	that it threw the sun back into my eyes.

	And what I have to draw now
	no voice ever carried, nor ink ever wrote,
	nor was ever taken in by the imagination;

	for I saw, and also heard, the beak speak,
	and its voice said "I" and "mine"
	where the meaning was 'we' and 'ours'.

	And it began: "For being just and merciful
	I am exalted here to that glory
	which does not let itself be beaten by any longing;

	and on earth I left such a memory of myself
	that the wicked people there
	praise it, but do not follow the example."

	As one warmth is felt from many coals,
	so out of that image
	came one sound out of many loves.

	At which I said: "O everlasting flowers
	of the eternal gladness, who make all your scents
	seem only one to me,

	breathe on me and loose the long fast
	which has kept me hungry all this while,
	finding no food for it on earth.

	I know well that if divine justice
	uses any other part of heaven as its mirror,
	your part sees it with no veil between.

	You know how attentively I make myself ready
	to listen; you know what that doubt is
	which has been so old a fast for me."

	Like a falcon coming out of the hood,
	which moves its head and claps itself with its wings,
	showing its will and making itself handsome,

	so I saw that sign become, woven as it was
	out of the praises of the divine grace,
	with such songs as only those who are glad up there can know.

	Then it began: "The one who turned the compasses
	round the edge of the world, and inside that circle
	marked out so much that is hidden and so much that is shown,

	could not print his power so far
	on the whole universe that his Word
	did not remain in infinite excess.

	And this makes it certain that the first proud one, Lucifer,
	who was the summit of all creation,
	fell unripe because he would not wait for the light;

	and from this it appears that every lesser nature
	is too small a vessel for that good
	which has no end and measures itself by itself.

	So your sight, which must be
	one of the rays of that Mind
	with which all things are filled,

	cannot be so powerful by its own nature
	that it can make out its own source
	far beyond what appears to it.

	So the sight which your world receives
	goes into the everlasting justice
	as an eye goes into the sea:

	for although it sees the bottom from the shore,
	in the open water it does not; and yet the bottom
	is there, and the depth is what hides it.

	There is no light except what comes from the calm
	that is never troubled; everything else is darkness,
	or the shadow the flesh casts, or its poison.

	Now the hiding place which kept living justice
	from you, about which you kept putting
	your question, has been opened wide enough;

	for you used to say: 'A man is born on the bank
	of the Indus, and there is nobody there to talk
	about Christ, and nobody to read or write;

	and all his wishes and his acts are good,
	as far as human reason sees,
	without sin in his living or in his speaking.

	He dies unbaptized and without faith:
	where is this justice that condemns him?
	Where is his fault, if he does not believe?'

	Now who are you, to want to sit on the bench
	and judge a thousand miles away
	with sight that reaches a hand's breadth?

	Certainly, for a man who wanted to split hairs with me,
	if you did not have the Scriptures over you,
	there would be marvellous room for doubt.

	Oh earthly animals! Oh dull minds!
	The first will, God's, which is good of itself,
	never moved away from itself, which is the highest good.

	Everything is just so far as it chimes with that:
	no created good draws it to itself,
	but it, by shining out, is the cause of that good."

	As a stork wheels above her nest
	when she has fed her young,
	and as the one that has been fed looks up at her,

	so did the blessed image, and so I lifted my eyes,
	while it moved its wings, driven
	by so many wills together.

	Wheeling, it sang, and said: "As my notes
	are to you, who do not understand them,
	so is the eternal judgment to you mortals."

	Then, when those shining fires
	of the Holy Spirit had grown quiet, still in the shape of the eagle,
	the sign that made the Romans revered by the world,

	it began again: "Nobody ever climbed
	to this kingdom who did not believe in Christ,
	either before or after he was nailed to the wood.

	But look: many cry 'Christ, Christ!'
	who at the judgment will be far less near to him
	than someone who never knew Christ;

	and the Ethiopian, who never heard of him, will condemn such Christians
	when the two companies separate at the last judgment,
	the one rich for ever and the other beggared.

	What will the Persians be able to say to your kings
	when they see that book opened
	in which all their contemptible deeds are written?

	There will be seen, among the doings of the emperor Albert,
	the one that will soon set the pen going,
	his laying waste of the kingdom of Bohemia.

	There will be seen the grief the king of France brings on the Seine
	by debasing the coinage,
	the man who will die when a wild boar throws his horse.

	There will be seen the thirsty pride
	which makes the Scot and the Englishman mad,
	so that neither can bear to stay inside his own border.

	There will be seen the lechery and the soft living
	of the man of Spain and the man of Bohemia,
	who never knew worth and never wanted it.

	For Charles the Lame, who calls himself king of Jerusalem, there will be seen
	his good deeds marked with an I, for one,
	while an M, for a thousand, will mark the opposite.

	There will be seen the greed and the cowardice
	of Frederick, who holds Sicily, the island of the burning mountain,
	where Anchises finished his long life;

	and to give some idea of how small he is,
	what is written about him will be in clipped letters
	which will note a great deal in a little room.

	And the filthy doings of his uncle and his brother,
	the kings of Majorca and Aragon, will be plain to everyone: they who have made
	so fine a family, and two crowns, into a disgrace.

	The kings of Portugal and of Norway
	will be known there, and the king of Serbia,
	who copied the coin of Venice to his cost.

	O happy Hungary, if she lets herself
	be misgoverned no longer! And happy Navarre,
	if she armed herself with the mountains that belt her!

	And everybody ought to believe that already,
	as an earnest of this, Nicosia and Famagusta
	in Cyprus are complaining and crying out at the beast who rules them,

	who does not go one step out of line with the others."
