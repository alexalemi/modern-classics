The Paradiso is the last third of the poem Dante Alighieri wrote in exile and finished shortly before he died at Ravenna in 1321. The other two parts, the Inferno and the Purgatorio, are separate volumes in this collection; this one can be read alone, but it begins where the second left off, at the top of the mountain of Purgatory, with Beatrice taking over as guide from Virgil. From there Dante rises through the spheres of the moon, the planets, the fixed stars, and out beyond them.

It has a reputation for being the difficult one, and the reason is worth stating plainly. There are no bodies here and no weather. The souls are points of light, the settings are spheres of light, and after the first canto nothing happens that could be called a plot. Most translations answer that by going vague and grand, which is what actually makes it unreadable. Dante's own answer is the opposite. Whenever the subject gets least physical, he reaches for the smallest thing in the room: fish rising to something dropped in a tank, a bird on the nest waiting for dawn, a baby flinging its face towards the milk, jackdaws warming their feathers, wheels turning in a clock, a tailor cutting the gown to fit the cloth he has. Those comparisons are the technique, and they are kept here.

Two other things a reader should know going in. The light vocabulary is technical: Dante uses a dozen different words for light and means different things by them, and this edition keeps them apart instead of smoothing them into one word. And he says over and over that he cannot remember what he saw and cannot say it. That is not throat-clearing. The failure of language is one of the poem's subjects, and every one of those passages is here in full.

It is also angrier and more personal than its reputation. Saint Peter turns red in the face over what has become of his office and the whole heaven changes colour with him. Dante's great-great-grandfather, a crusader killed on campaign, tells him he is going to be exiled and that he will learn how salt another man's bread tastes. Two pagans turn up among the saved, and Dante knows exactly how startling that is. A whole canto argues that a good man born on the Indus who never heard of Christ has no claim anyone can measure, and it is not resolved in the reader's favour. None of that is softened here, and there are no notes.

The translation is from the Italian, complete: thirty-three cantos, 4,758 lines, three-line stanzas in the same number and order, without the rhyme, which cannot be carried into English without padding. The poem ends, after thirty-three cantos of saying it cannot be said, on a single line about a wheel turning evenly.
