For example, on the assumption that '*p* ⊃ *p*' has already been established, we can substitute for the variable '*p*' the formula '*q*' to get '*q* ⊃ *q*'; or we can substitute the formula '*p* ∨ *q*' to get '(*p* ∨ *q*) ⊃ (*p* ∨ *q*)'. Or, if we substitute actual English sentences for *'p',* we can obtain each of the following from '*p* ⊃ *p*': 'Frogs are noisy ⊃ Frogs are noisy'; '(Bats are blind ∨ Bats eat mice) ⊃ (Bats are blind ∨ Bats eat mice)'.⁹ The second Transformation Rule is the *Rule of Detachment* (or *Modus Ponens).* This rule says that from two formulas having the form S₁ and S₁ ⊃ S₂ it is always permissible to derive the formula S₂. For example, from the two formulas '*p* ∨ ∼ *p*' and '(*p* ∨ ∼ *p*) ⊃ (*p* ⊃ *p*)', we can derive '*p* ⊃ *p*'.

Finally, the axioms of the calculus (essentially those of *Principia)* are the following four formulas:

> 1. (*p* ∨ *p*) ⊃ *p*, or, in ordinary English, if either *p* or *p*, then *p* | 1. If (either Henry VIII was a boor or Henry VIII was a boor) then Henry VIII was a boor

[FN 9] On the other hand, suppose the formula '(*p* ⊃ *q*) ⊃ (∼ *q* ⊃ ∼ *p*)' has already been established, and we decide to substitute '*r*' for the variable '*p*' and '*p* ∨ *r*' for the variable '*q*'. We cannot, by this substitution, obtain the formula '(*r* ⊃ (*p* ∨ *r*)) ⊃ (∼ *q* ⊃ ∼ *r*)', because we have failed to make the same substitution for *each* occurrence of the variable '*q*'.
