In 1825 Michael Faraday started a series of science lectures at the Royal Institution in London given over the Christmas holidays and addressed to children. They are still running. This book is the course given in 1889 and 1890 by Charles Vernon Boys, a physicist best known for making the finest threads anyone had produced — he fired a crossbow bolt with molten quartz attached to it and drew out fibres too thin to see, which he then used to build instruments sensitive enough to weigh the Earth.

His subject here is soap films, and his method is to do everything in front of the audience. Nothing in these three lectures is described rather than shown. He blows a bubble and drops it onto a wire frame, lowers a hot wire into a film, sets a bubble oscillating in front of a lamp so its shadow shakes on the wall, floats an iron boat on water and pulls it about with a lump of camphor. The text keeps him in the present tense throughout, because he is talking about something that is happening on the table while he speaks.

The single idea underneath it all is that a liquid surface behaves like a stretched elastic skin. Boys introduces the term surface tension once, early, and then almost never uses it again, preferring the skin he can point at. That is a deliberate teaching choice and it is preserved here. Once you have the skin, everything else in the course follows from it: why a bubble is round, why two joined bubbles meet at the angle they do, why a small bubble blows itself into a bigger one, why water climbs a narrow tube, and why a raindrop in a thunderstorm is the size it is.

A third of the book is an appendix called Practical Hints, and it is the reason the lectures existed. It is a set of recipes for doing every experiment yourself, with the glassware, the wire, the soap and the proportions. It is translated here in full. Nine short bracketed notes have been added where Boys calls for something now known to be dangerous or now impossible to buy — he is cheerfully casual about carbon disulfide, ether and mercury, none of which anyone flagged in 1890 — and one gives a soap mixture that works with modern detergent.

Where his physics is dated it has been left as he gives it, including his account of why thunderstorm raindrops are large, which is not the explanation now accepted.
