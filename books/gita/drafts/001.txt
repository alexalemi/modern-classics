Chapter 2: Understanding and Action

Sanjaya said:

{1} Arjuna sat there overwhelmed by pity, sunk in despair, his eyes so
full of tears he could not see. And Krishna spoke to him.

Krishna said:

{2} Arjuna, where has this faintness of heart come from, at a crisis
like this? It is not how a noble man behaves. It does not lead to
heaven, and it brings disgrace. {3} Don't give in to this unmanliness,
Arjuna. It isn't worthy of you. Throw off this petty weakness of heart
and stand up, you who make your enemies tremble!

Arjuna said:

{4} Krishna, you who killed the demon Madhu, you who destroy your
enemies: how can I fight Bhishma and Drona in battle, shooting arrows at
men I ought to worship? {5} Surely it is better to live in this world on
a beggar's scraps than to kill my teachers, these great-souled men. If I
kill my teachers, even if they are greedy for gain, then every pleasure
I enjoy here, in this world, will be smeared with blood. {6} We don't
even know which is better for us, that we should win or that they should
win. The very men we would not care to live after killing are standing
there facing us: Dhritarashtra's sons.

{7} This flaw of weakness, this pity, has crippled my own nature, and my
mind is confused about what is right. So I am asking you: tell me for
certain what is best. I am your student. I have come to you for refuge.
Teach me! {8} For I can see nothing that would drive away this grief
that is drying up my senses, not even if I won a rich kingdom on earth
without a rival, or lordship over the gods themselves.

Sanjaya said:

{9} When he had said this to Krishna, my king, Arjuna told him, "I will
not fight," and fell silent. {10} And as he sat there in despair between
the two armies, my king, Krishna, with what looked almost like a smile,
spoke these words to him:

Krishna said:

{11} You are grieving for people who need no grief, and yet you talk
like a wise man. The wise grieve neither for the dead nor for the
living. {12} There was never a time when I did not exist, nor you, nor
these kings; and there will never be a time from now on when any of us
ceases to be. {13} Just as the one who lives in this body passes through
childhood, youth and old age, so it passes on to another body. A steady
person is not confused by this.

{14} When your senses meet the things of the world, Arjuna, they bring
cold and heat, pleasure and pain. These come and go; they do not last.
Bear them patiently. {15} The person they cannot shake, the steady
person who stays the same in pain and pleasure, finest of men, is fit
for immortality.

{16} What is unreal never comes to be; what is real never stops being.
Those who see things as they truly are have seen to the bottom of both.
{17} Know that what spreads through this whole world cannot be
destroyed. No one can destroy what is imperishable. {18} These bodies
come to an end, but the one who lives in them is said to be eternal,
indestructible, beyond all measure. So fight, Arjuna!

{19} Whoever thinks this self is a killer, and whoever thinks it is
killed, neither one understands: it does not kill, and it is not killed.
{20} It is never born, and it never dies. It did not come into being,
and it will never cease to be. Unborn, eternal, everlasting, ancient
beyond all time, it is not killed when the body is killed. {21} If
someone knows that it is indestructible and eternal, unborn and
unchanging, Arjuna, how can that person kill anyone, or make anyone
kill?

{22} Just as a man throws off worn-out clothes and puts on new ones, so
the one who lives in the body throws off worn-out bodies and enters
others that are new. {23} Weapons do not cut it, fire does not burn it,
water does not wet it, and wind does not dry it. {24} It cannot be cut,
it cannot be burned, it cannot be wetted or dried. It is eternal,
present everywhere, steady, unmoving, everlasting. {25} It is called
unmanifest, beyond the reach of the senses; it is called unthinkable and
unchanging. So, now that you know it is like this, you should not
grieve.

{26} And even if you think it is forever being born and forever dying,
even then, Arjuna, you should not grieve for it. {27} For death is
certain for whatever is born, and birth is certain for whatever has
died. So you should not grieve over what cannot be avoided. {28} Living
beings are unmanifest at their beginning, manifest in their middle,
Arjuna, and unmanifest again at their end. What is there to mourn in
that?

{29} Someone sees it as a wonder. Someone else speaks of it as a wonder.
Another hears of it as a wonder. And even having heard of it, no one
knows it at all. {30} The one who lives in the body of every being is
eternal and can never be killed, Arjuna. So you should not grieve for
any creature at all.

{31} Look at your own duty too, and you will not waver. For a kshatriya,
someone born to the warrior class, there is nothing better than a just war. {32} Happy
are the warriors, Arjuna, who are handed a war like this one: it comes
to them unsought, a door to heaven standing open. {33} But if you will
not fight this just war, then you will have thrown away your own duty
and your good name, and you will bring sin on yourself.

{34} People will tell the story of your disgrace forever, and for a man
who has been honored, disgrace is worse than death. {35} The great
chariot-fighters will think you ran from the battle out of fear, and
those who thought so highly of you will think little of you. {36} Your
enemies will say many unspeakable things about you and sneer at your
strength. What could be more painful than that? {37} If you are killed,
you will win heaven; if you win, you will enjoy the earth. So stand up,
Arjuna, determined to fight! {38} Treat pleasure and pain alike, gain
and loss alike, victory and defeat alike, and then get ready for battle.
That way you will bring no sin on yourself.

{39} What I have told you so far is the understanding that Sankhya
teaches, the way of reasoned knowledge. Now hear it as yoga teaches it:
yoga, the discipline that yokes the whole mind to its work. Once you are
yoked to this understanding, Arjuna, you will throw off the chains that
action binds you with. {40} On this path no effort is ever wasted, and
nothing can turn it against you. Even a little of this practice saves
you from great fear. {41} Here, Arjuna, the understanding of a resolute
person is single and focused, but the thoughts of the irresolute branch
out endlessly in every direction.

{42} There are people without insight, Arjuna, who love to argue about
the words of the Vedas and say there is nothing beyond them. They speak
a flowery language, {43} people whose very selves are desire, whose
highest aim is heaven: a language that promises rebirth as the reward of
what they do, and that lays down all kinds of special rites for gaining
pleasure and power. {44} Those who cling to pleasure and power, whose
minds are carried away by that language, never reach the resolute
understanding that stays fixed in deep concentration.

{45} The Vedas deal with the three gunas, the three strands that all of
nature is woven from. Rise above the three gunas, Arjuna. Be free of the
pairs of opposites. Stand firm always in goodness, don't worry about
getting things and keeping them, and be self-possessed. {46} For a
Brahmin who truly knows, all the Vedas are of as much use as a well when
the whole country is under water.

{47} Your right is to action alone, never to its fruits. Don't let the
fruit of action be your motive, and don't let yourself get attached to
doing nothing. {48} Do your work steady in yoga, Arjuna, letting go of
attachment, the same in success and failure. Evenness of mind is called
yoga. {49} Action on its own is far below the discipline of
understanding, Arjuna. Seek refuge in understanding. Those who act for
the sake of the fruit are to be pitied.

{50} A person whose understanding is disciplined leaves behind, here and
now, both good deeds and bad. So yoke yourself to yoga. Yoga is skill in
action. {51} The wise, disciplined in understanding, give up the fruit
that action brings; freed from the chains of birth, they go to the place
where there is no more suffering. {52} When your understanding has made
its way out of this thicket of delusion, you will have had enough of all
the scripture you have heard and all you have still to hear. {53} When
your understanding, now bewildered by everything it has heard, stands
firm and unmoving, steady in deep concentration, then you will reach
yoga.

Arjuna said:

{54} Krishna, how would you describe someone whose wisdom is steady, who
stands firm in deep concentration? How does a person of steady mind
talk? How does he sit? How does he walk?

Krishna said:

{55} When a person gives up every desire that rises in the mind, Arjuna,
and is content in the self, by the self alone, then he is called a
person of steady wisdom. {56} Someone whose mind is not shaken by
sorrows, who has no craving for pleasures, from whom longing and fear
and anger have gone, is called a sage of steady mind. {57} Whoever has
no clinging attachment to anything, who neither rejoices nor resents it
when good or bad comes his way: his wisdom stands firm. {58} And when,
like a tortoise drawing in its limbs, he draws his senses in from the
things of the senses on every side, his wisdom stands firm.

{59} When someone living in a body stops feeding the senses, the things
of the senses fall away from him, but the taste for them stays; and even
the taste falls away once he has seen the highest. {60} For the senses
are violent, Arjuna. Even when a wise man is struggling against them,
they carry his mind off by force. {61} He should restrain them all and
sit disciplined, with me as his highest goal. Whoever has his senses
under control: his wisdom stands firm.

{62} When a man dwells on the things of the senses, attachment to them
is born. From attachment comes desire, and from desire comes anger.
{63} From anger comes confusion; from confusion, the loss of memory;
from the loss of memory, the ruin of understanding; and from the ruin of
understanding, he is lost. {64} But a person who moves among the things
of the senses with senses free of longing and loathing, held under the
self's control, a person whose self is disciplined, reaches serenity.
{65} In that serenity all his sorrows come to an end, for when the heart
is serene, the understanding soon stands steady.

{66} The undisciplined have no understanding, and the undisciplined have
no inner focus. Without focus there is no peace, and without peace, how
can there be happiness? {67} When the mind runs after the wandering
senses, it carries off a person's wisdom, as the wind carries off a ship
on the water. {68} So, Arjuna, the one whose senses are held back
completely from the things of the senses: his wisdom stands firm.

{69} What is night for all beings is the time when the self-controlled
person is awake, and when all beings are awake, that is night for the
sage who sees. {70} The waters pour into the ocean, and it is filled and
filled, and still it never stirs from its steady bed: the person into
whom all desires pour in the same way finds peace, and not the one who
hungers after his desires. {71} The person who gives up all desires and
moves through life without longing, without any thought of "mine,"
without ego, the sense of "I," finds peace.

{72} This, Arjuna, is the state of standing firm in Brahman, the
ultimate reality behind everything. Whoever reaches it is never confused
again. Whoever stays in it, even at the hour of death, reaches the
nirvana of Brahman, its perfect peace.
