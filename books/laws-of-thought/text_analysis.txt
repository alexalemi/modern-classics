TEXT ANALYSIS — GEORGE BOOLE, AN INVESTIGATION OF THE LAWS OF THOUGHT
(1854), THE WHOLE BOOK: THE LOGIC (I–XV) AND PROBABILITY (XVI–XXII)

THE BOOK
Boole, a self-taught schoolmaster's son who became Professor of
Mathematics at Queen's College, Cork, set out to find the laws of the
mind's reasoning and to write them as an algebra. Letters stand for
classes (x = "sheep"), multiplication for "and" (xy = "white sheep"),
1 for the universe of discourse, 0 for nothing, 1 − x for "not-x". The
one law ordinary algebra lacks is x² = x, which only 0 and 1 obey; so
the algebra of logic is the algebra of 0 and 1. From there: every
proposition is an equation, reasoning is solving and eliminating, and
any logical function can be "developed" into its constituents (what we
now call the disjunctive normal form). Chapter XI turns the same algebra
on whole propositions ("secondary propositions"), the ancestor of
propositional logic and truth values. Chapters XIII–XIV test the method
on real arguments (Clarke's and Spinoza's proofs about God), and XV
re-derives Aristotle's syllogisms. Chapters XVI–XXII apply all this to
probability (retold 2026-10-16, after the logic half had shipped; see
THE PROBABILITY HALF below).

This edition is a retelling for a reader of fourteen and up who is
willing to follow algebra. Alex's brief (2026-10-14): make Boole
comprehensible, with brief modern notes.

STYLE AND VOICE
Grave, careful, mid-Victorian scientific prose: very long periods,
heavily qualified ("it may be remarked", "it is evident that", "in the
strictest sense"), philosophical in the opening chapters, then dense
worked mathematics. He is never playful, but he is earnest and often
moving about what the mind is (the end of Chapter I and XXII's spirit).
Keep his seriousness; lose the scaffolding. Claim first, then the
reason, then the example. Short sentences. "We" stays his "we".

THE THREE LAYERS OF THIS EDITION — KEEP THEM APART
1. BOOLE, RETOLD. His argument in plain modern English, every step, in
   his order. His symbols, equations, numbering and article numbers
   UNCHANGED. Every \( \) and \[ \] formula is copied BYTE FOR BYTE —
   check.py --modern fails the file otherwise.
2. GLOSSES: reading the equation aloud. Where Boole writes an equation
   and moves on, the retelling may add a short phrase IN THE SENTENCE
   saying what it means in words: "\(x(1-y) = 0\), that is, nothing is an
   x that is not a y". A gloss is a READING of what is written. Never a
   correction, never a step he did not take, never a different method.
   Use them where a fourteen-year-old would otherwise stall; don't gloss
   what Boole has just explained in words himself.
3. EDITOR'S NOTES: the modern view. Only the notes assigned in
   running_notes.txt, each at its assigned passage, as a separate
   paragraph beginning "Editor's note: " (precedent: candle, burke),
   two to five sentences, after the paragraph it comments on. They say
   where modern Boolean algebra differs or where an idea went. No agent
   adds an editor's note that is not on the list.

WHAT TO KEEP EXACTLY
- Every formula (layer 1). Including equation numbers "\qquad (n)".
- Article numbers ("12.") at paragraph start; "Proposition I." labels
  with their periods; "Rule.", "Definitions.", "Axioms.", "Examples.",
  "Class I.", "Form I." labels as printed.
- Cross-references in his system: "(II. 9)" = Chapter II, article 9;
  "Chap. V. Prop. 4". Keep them as printed — they are the book's own
  navigation and the article numbers survive.
- His technical vocabulary, LOCKED (where first defined in brackets):
  literal symbols, signs of operation, sign of identity (II.4);
  "fundamental law of thought" and "law of duality" for x² = x
  (III.15–16); universe of discourse (III.4); primary and secondary
  propositions (IV.1–2); the symbol v and "indefinite class" (IV.11);
  development, expansion (V.9–10); constituents (V.11); moduli (if used);
  interpretation (VI.1); elimination (VII.1–2); reduction (VIII.1);
  "logical function", "logical equation"; "law of commutation",
  "distributive law" or however he names them. Terms of traditional
  logic (subject, predicate, copula, syllogism, mood, figure,
  conversion) as in jevons-logic.
- Quotations from other writers (Aristotle in Greek, Clarke, Spinoza's
  Latin, Whately, Thomson, Mill...) verbatim. Greek and Latin copied
  exactly; a short English rendering may follow if he gives none.

WHAT TO MODERNISE
- Syntax above all: unstack the periods. The opening chapters are
  philosophy of mind in 1850s idiom; that is where the readability is
  won or lost.
- "Symbolical" -> "symbolic"; "viz." -> "namely"; "i.e." -> "that is";
  "whence" -> "so"; "hence" -> "so" (keep "hence" only inside a formal
  derivation if it reads naturally); "the same is" -> "this is".
- Mental-philosophy vocabulary that has drifted: "conceptions" can stay;
  "the faculties", "the intellect" can stay; "operations of the mind"
  stays (it is his subject).

SENSITIVE CONTENT
Chapters XIII–XIV analyse Samuel Clarke's "Demonstration of the Being
and Attributes of God" and Spinoza's Ethics as LOGICAL ARGUMENTS. This is
scholarly modernization of a canonical text: render Boole's analysis of
their reasoning in a measured register, as his argument about logical
form; do not editorialise on the theology either way. Boole's own
religious remarks (end of Chapter I and elsewhere) are his and stay.

RATIO
Formulas are copied unchanged; prose is unstacked; glosses and notes
add words. Expect 1.0–1.3 per file. Below 0.9 means something was cut:
check the worked examples first.

THE PROBABILITY HALF, XVI–XXII (added 2026-10-16)
What it does. Boole's claim is that any question in probability can be
turned into a question in his logic: the events become propositions,
"and", "or", "not" become his algebra, and the probability of a
compound event follows from the probabilities of the simple ones once
the logical connexion between them is worked out. XVI surveys the
received theory (Pascal, Laplace, Poisson, De Morgan, Donkin) and its
principles. XVII states the GENERAL METHOD in four Propositions; its
key step treats the simple events as independent unless the data say
otherwise, under the conditions the data impose. (Boole's NOTE, printed
before Chapter I and now set after the Preface, corrects the urn
example of XVII. 10 and restates that principle.) XVIII works seven
elementary examples. XIX finds "statistical conditions": the limits
(major and minor) within which data must lie to be consistent, and the
limits of an answer. XX applies the method to causes and effects:
general problems of several causes, then Michell's argument from the
clustering of the stars and Laplace's rule of succession (the sunrise
problem), where Boole argues that a definite answer from insufficient
data is an illusion. XXI treats the probability of judgments (juries,
witnesses), against Laplace and Poisson. XXII is philosophy again: the
nature of science, the laws of thought as mathematical in form but not
the whole of the mind, the Greek philosophers' search for "the One",
and the book's moving close.

Where it is hard. XVII and XX are the heaviest algebra in the book:
long systems of equations in Prob. x, arbitrary constants c, auxiliary
quantities mu and nu, roots to be chosen. The reader needs to be told
at each step WHAT is being found and WHY, in words, without adding a
step: glosses do that work. XIX's limits are best read aloud ("the
number of things that are both x and y is at least the number of x's
plus the number of y's minus the whole universe").

Vocabulary, LOCKED for XVI–XXII:
- "Prob. x" stays in formulas; in prose "the probability of x".
- simple / compound events; conditioned / unconditioned; independent;
  "absolute probability" (the probability apart from any condition).
- data, and quaesita -> "what is sought" (gloss "quaesita" once if
  Boole uses the word).
- the general method; the general rule; final logical equation.
- arbitrary constant(s) (c, c1 ...): "a constant whose value the data
  do not fix"; glossed once.
- statistical conditions (locked, Boole's term); "major and minor
  limits" kept (the formulas print them), glossed once as "the largest
  and smallest values possible"; "superior / inferior limit" -> "upper
  / lower limit".
- "a posteriori" / "a priori" were glossed in Chapter 1; do not re-gloss.
- "phaenomenon" -> "phenomenon"; "connexion" -> "connection" (keep
  "Connexion" in the Chapter 20 title); "the doctrine of chances" stays
  when he quotes it.
- Names stay: Laplace, Poisson, Pascal, De Morgan, Donkin, Michell,
  Herschel, Forbes, Cournot, Bishop Terrot, Ritter.

XXII: Boole's own philosophy and religion, in his voice. Render his
view of the mind, of Plato, Aristotle, the Neoplatonists, the
schoolmen, Spinoza and Hegel as HIS argument, in a measured register;
neither endorse nor mock (the XIII ruling). His notes quote Greek,
Latin and Italian: copy them exactly (the Greek is set in Unicode by
prep.py from the received texts, checked on the 1854 page); a short
English rendering in brackets may follow a quotation Boole leaves
untranslated, marked as ours only if the sense needs it. His verse
renderings ("Be mine, to partial views...") are his and stay verbatim.
