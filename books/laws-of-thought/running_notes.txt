ASSIGNED EDITOR'S NOTES — the ONLY ones in the book
Each is a separate paragraph "Editor's note: ..." placed right after the
paragraph named. 2-5 sentences, plain, no formulas and NO SYMBOLS (≠ ∨ ∧ ∃ ¬ are
missing from Alex's e-reader fonts; say it in words).
N1  003, after article 11's account of "+" (aggregation) — Boole's "+"
    joins classes that have NO MEMBERS IN COMMON ("either ... or", one
    excluding the other), and "x − y" is allowed only when the y's are
    all among the x's. Modern Boolean algebra, following Jevons (1864)
    and later Peirce, makes "+" an inclusive "or" and drops
    subtraction for the complement "not-y". Boole's results still come
    out right, but the reader who knows modern notation should not read
    his + as "or".
N2  004, after article 15 (the algebra of 0 and 1) — this is the step
    that made Boolean algebra the algebra of two values. In 1937 Claude
    Shannon showed that switching circuits obey these same laws, which
    is why every digital computer calculates in Boole's algebra.
N3  005, after the paragraph introducing v (IV.11, Prop. II) — v, an
    indefinite class, is Boole's way of saying "some". Later logic
    stopped using it: today "Some X are Y" says that the class of things
    both X and Y is not empty, and the notion of
    "some" became the existential quantifier.
N4  006, after article 11 (constituents) — the "development" of a
    function into constituents is what is now called its disjunctive
    normal form, and the constituents are what computer scientists call
    minterms. Listing all 2, 4, 8 ... constituents and keeping the ones a
    premise allows is the method Jevons teaches by hand in Lesson 23 of
    his Elementary Lessons in Logic (in this collection), and the
    ancestor of the truth table.
N5  007, after article 10 (interpreting 0/0 and 1/0) — Boole's
    division and the symbols 0/0 and 1/0 are formal devices, not
    arithmetic: a coefficient 0/0 marks an indefinite portion of a class
    (the v of Chapter IV), 1/0 a constituent that must vanish. His
    contemporaries found this the hardest part of the book; Jevons,
    Venn and Peirce rebuilt the algebra without division, and modern
    Boolean algebra has none. His conclusions are correct; only the
    route was abandoned.
N6  012, after article 9 (x, y, z as the TIMES a proposition is true) —
    Boole reads a secondary proposition's symbol as the portion of time
    in which it is true. Modern logic drops time and gives each
    proposition one of two truth values, true or false, 1 or 0 — which
    is exactly where Boole's algebra lands; this chapter is the ancestor
    of propositional logic.
N7  017, after the paragraph where Boole first expresses "All Y's are
    X's" with v and draws the particular conclusion (the existential
    import question; the agent places it at the first passage where
    Boole's treatment of a particular conclusion from universal premises
    appears) — modern logic does not let "All X are Y" imply that any X
    exist, so some of Aristotle's moods (Darapti, Felapton, Bramantip,
    Fesapo, and the weakened moods) need an extra premise. Boole's v
    quietly supplies it.
THE PROBABILITY HALF (assigned 2026-10-16):
N8  018, after article 6 (events replaced by the propositions that
    assert them) — this is the idea modern probability theory is built
    on. In 1933 Andrei Kolmogorov set probability on events treated as
    sets, combined by "and", "or" and "not" exactly as Boole combines
    classes; in today's terms a probability is a measure on a Boolean
    algebra of events.
N9  019, after the paragraph that ends "its numerical value varies with
    varying information" (the end of the urn example, XVII. 10) — Boole
    later saw that this example was wrong. In the Note printed at the
    front of the book (after the Preface in this edition) he corrected
    it and restated the principle behind his method: treat the simple
    events as independent, with whatever probabilities make them agree
    with the data. That principle was the most criticised step in his
    theory; Henry Wilbraham attacked it in 1854, and Boole defended it
    in print for years. Theodore Hailperin later showed (1976) that
    Boole's problems are best read as finding the range of values the
    data allow, which is a problem in what is now called linear
    programming.
N10 022, after article 1 (the definition of statistical conditions) —
    Boole's statistical conditions are the inequalities that any set
    of observed frequencies must satisfy if the data are consistent.
    He later called them "conditions of possible experience". In 1989
    the philosopher Itamar Pitowsky showed that the Bell inequalities
    of quantum physics are conditions of exactly this kind: measurements
    on certain pairs of particles break them, which is why those results
    cannot be explained by any account in which the particles simply
    carry their properties with them.
N11 026, after article 26 (the close of the sunrise discussion) — the
    rule Boole examines here, that after m sunrises the chance of
    another is (m + 1)/(m + 2), is Laplace's "rule of succession". The
    question behind his objection, whether ignorance can be expressed
    by spreading probability evenly over the possibilities, is the
    question of "prior probabilities" that still divides statisticians:
    Bayesian methods assign them, often in Laplace's way, while others,
    like Boole, refuse unless the evidence supplies them. (Write the
    fraction in words: "m plus one over m plus two".)
That is all. No agent adds a note not on this list. XXI and XXII take
no editor's note.
