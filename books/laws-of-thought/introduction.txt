George Boole published this book in 1854, when he was thirty-nine and Professor of Mathematics at Queen's College, Cork. He had not been to university. He was the son of a shoemaker in Lincoln, taught himself Latin, Greek and then mathematics, and was supporting his family as a schoolmaster from the age of sixteen. By his late twenties he was publishing original mathematics in the journals and had won a medal from the Royal Society, and in 1849 the new college in Cork made him its first professor of mathematics without his ever having taken a degree.

His idea was that reasoning has laws as definite as the laws of algebra, and that they can be written down as an algebra. Let a letter stand for a class of things: x for sheep, y for white things. Then xy is the class of things that are both, the white sheep; 1 stands for everything under discussion and 0 for nothing; 1 − x is everything that is not a sheep. "All x are y" becomes an equation, and reasoning becomes the solving of equations. Almost every rule of ordinary algebra carries over. The one that does not is the law Boole put at the centre of the book, x² = x: a class combined with itself is the same class. Of all numbers, only 0 and 1 obey it, and so the algebra of logic turns out to be the algebra of 0 and 1. He had sketched the system in a pamphlet of 1847, The Mathematical Analysis of Logic; this is the full statement.

It was admired and little used in his lifetime. Logicians found the algebra forbidding, and mathematicians found parts of it strange, above all his habit of working through steps that have no logical meaning, such as dividing by zero, and trusting the answer at the end. Jevons, Venn and Peirce rebuilt it in the following decades into a simpler form, and in that form it became the foundation of modern logic. In 1937 Claude Shannon showed that it also describes electrical switching circuits, which is why every digital computer now calculates in Boole's algebra.

Boole's title names a second subject, and the last seven chapters take it up. If reasoning is an algebra, then probability, which reasons about events, should follow from it: turn the events into the propositions that assert them, work out how those propositions are connected, and the probability of any compound event can be calculated from the probabilities of the simple ones. Chapters XVI to XXII build that general method and try it on dice and urns, on causes and effects, on Laplace's question of whether the sun will rise tomorrow, and on the reliability of witnesses and juries; the last chapter turns back to what all of this says about the human mind. This half was received more coldly. Its key step, treating events as independent unless the data say otherwise, was attacked in the year the book appeared, and Boole added a note correcting one of his own examples, which this edition sets after the Preface. But his idea that events combine as classes do is the one modern probability theory rests on, and his conditions for consistent data turned up again, more than a century later, in quantum physics.

This edition retells the whole book, logic and probability. His prose has been put into plain modern English. His symbols, equations and equation numbers are exactly his, except for a handful of printer's errors in the 1854 edition and the transcription it was set from, corrected against the original and listed in the source files. His numbered articles are kept, so that his own cross-references, such as "(II. 9)", meaning Chapter II, article 9, still work. Where an equation would stop a reader, a few words in the sentence say what it means. And eleven short notes marked "Editor's note" say where modern Boolean algebra does things differently, or where an idea of his went next; those are ours, not his.
