Chapter 13: Clarke and Spinoza
(Part 1 of 2)

1. In this chapter and the next I mean to follow a definite plan. I shall examine the actual premises involved in proving some of the general propositions of the two treatises named above, whether those premises are stated outright or only implied. By the actual premises I mean every proposition that the argument assumes without proving and uses as part of the foundation on which its final conclusion is built. Once I have found these premises, I shall express them in the language of symbols. Then, by the methods developed in the earlier chapters of this work, I shall deduce from them the most important inferences they contain, beyond the particular inferences the authors themselves drew. In some cases I shall change the premises: I shall leave out some fact or principle they contain, or add or substitute some new proposition, and then work out how the final conclusions are affected by the change. In all this it is not my task to ask, except in passing, how far the metaphysical principles laid down in these famous works deserve our trust. My task is only to find out what conclusions may rightly be drawn from given premises. And in doing that, I want to show the complete freedom we have in choosing both the elements of the final propositions and the order in which they appear: that is, in deciding which elementary propositions to declare true or false, and which to declare true or false under given restrictions, or in given combinations.

2. The main practical difficulty of this inquiry will not lie in applying the method once the premises are fixed. It will lie in finding out what the premises are. Even in what are regarded as the most rigorous examples of reasoning on metaphysical questions, we shall sometimes find that different trains of thought are blended together. We shall find that some particular but essential parts of a proof are given in parentheses, or off to the side of the main argument; that the meaning of a premise is to some degree ambiguous; and, not rarely, that arguments judged by the strict laws of formal reasoning are incorrect or inconclusive. In such cases it may be very hard to determine the true premises of a proof and set them out clearly. But this is a difficulty that everyone must overcome who wants to know whether a particular conclusion is proved or not, whatever form they intend to give the reasoning that follows. So it is not a difficulty peculiar to the method of this work, though it shows itself more plainly with this method than with any other. The connection is in fact so close that, using the method of this treatise, we cannot even guess whether a conclusion is valid until we have clearly grasped and exactly stated every premise it rests on. In the more usual way of proceeding, on the other hand, nothing is more common than to examine a few steps of a train of argument and from them form a vague general impression of the whole, without any such preliminary and thorough analysis of the premises it involves.

This need to pin down exactly the real premises of a proof should not be thought of as a drawback, especially because, once it is done, every source of doubt or ambiguity is gone. With the method of this treatise, the order in which the premises are arranged, the way they are connected, and every similar circumstance may be treated as a matter of indifference. The process of inference is then carried out with a precision that might almost be called mechanical.

3. The "Demonstration of the Being and Attributes of God" consists of a series of propositions, or theorems. Each is proved from premises that can mostly be sorted into two distinct kinds. The first kind is facts of observation, such as the existence of a material world, the phenomenon of motion, &c. The second kind is hypothetical principles, whose authority and universal reach are supposed to be recognized _à priori_. It is of course on the truth of the second kind that the validity of the proof really depends, assuming the reasoning is correct. But whatever may be thought of its claims on that score, there is no question that, as an intellectual achievement, the work stands very high. Its trains of argument are not generally very clearly arranged, but they are almost always examples of correct logic, and they show a subtlety of understanding and a force of reasoning that have seldom been equalled and perhaps never surpassed. In them we see the culmination of the intellectual efforts that were stirred up in the field of metaphysical inquiry at a time when the rule of hypothetical principles was less questioned than it is now, and when the rigorous proofs of the newly risen school of mathematical physics seemed to offer a model for such efforts to follow. For this reason, not to mention the dignity of their subject, they seem to me to deserve serious attention, and I do not think it a vain or pointless task to spend a careful analysis on some of them.

4. The Ethics of Benedict Spinoza is a treatise whose object is to prove that God and the universe are identical, and to build on that doctrine a system of morals and of philosophy. Its main argument is extremely hard to analyse. The difficulty is not that its separate propositions are complicated. It is that it uses vague definitions, and axioms so unclear that it is hard to decide whether we ought to accept them or reject them. The reasoning of Dr. Samuel Clarke is partly verbal; that of Spinoza is verbal to a much greater degree. Perhaps that is why, to some minds, Spinoza's reasoning has seemed to have a formal force to which in reality it has no fair claim. These points, however, will be considered in their proper place.

Clarke's Demonstration.

Proposition I.

5. "_Something has existed from eternity_."

The proof is as follows:—

"For since something now is, 'tis manifest that something always was. Otherwise the things that now are must have risen out of nothing, absolutely and without cause. Which is a plain contradiction in terms. For to say a thing is produced, and yet that there is no cause at all of that production, is to say that something is effected when it is effected by nothing, that is, at the same time when it is not effected at all. Whatever exists has a cause of its existence, either in the necessity of its own nature, and thus it must have been of itself eternal: or in the will of some other being, and then that other being must, at least in the order of nature and causality, have existed before it."

Let us now analyse this proof. Its first sentence breaks down into the following propositions:

1st. Something is.

2nd. If something is, either something always was, or the things that now are must have risen out of nothing.

The next part of the proof argues that the second of these alternatives, namely "The things that now are have risen out of nothing," is impossible. Formally, it breaks down as follows:

3rd. If the things that now are have risen out of nothing, something has been effected, and at the same time that something has been effected by nothing.

4th. If that something has been effected by nothing, it has not been effected at all.

This second part of the argument seems to be simply an assumption of the point to be proved, or an attempt to make that point clearer by stating it in different words.

The third and last part of the proof gives a separate proof of one of two things: either of the original proposition to be proved, namely "Something always was," or of the point argued in the second part, namely that the hypothesis "the things that now are have risen out of nothing" cannot stand. It breaks down as follows:—

5th. If something is, either it exists by the necessity of its own nature, or it exists by the will of another being.

6th. If it exists by the necessity of its own nature, something always was.

7th. If it exists by the will of another being, then the proposition, that the things which exist have arisen out of nothing, is false.

This last proposition is not stated in this form in Dr. Clarke's text. But his stated conclusion, that another Being existed first, is plainly meant to amount to a denial of the proposition that the things which now are have risen out of nothing.

So the proof consists of two distinct trains of argument. One is made up of what I have called the _first_ and _second_ parts of the proof; the other is made up of the _first_ and _third_ parts. Let us consider the second of these trains.

The premises are:—

1st. Something is.

2nd. If something is, either something always was, or the things that now are have risen out of nothing.

3rd. If something is, either it exists in the necessity of its own nature, or it exists by the will of another being.

4th. If it exists in the necessity of its own nature, something always was.

5th. If it exists by the will of another being, then the hypothesis, that the things which now are have risen out of nothing, is false.

We must now express these propositions in symbols.

	Let | \(x\) = | Something is.
	 | \(y\) = | Something always was.
	 | \(z\) = | The things which now are have risen from nothing.
	 | \(p\) = | It exists in the necessity of its own nature (that is, the _something_ spoken of above).
	 | \(q\) = | It exists by the will of another Being.

It should be understood that writing Let \(x =\) "Something is" means no more than that \(x\) is the representative symbol of that proposition (XI. 7). The equations \(x = 1\) and \(x = 0\) then declare, respectively, that it is true and that it is false.

The equations of the premises are:—

	1st. | \(x\) = 1;
	2nd. | \(x=v\{y(1-z)+z(1-y)\}\);
	3rd. | \(x=v\{p(1-q)+q(1-p)\}\);
	4th. | \(p=vy\);
	5th. | \(q=v(1-z)\);

and when we eliminate the various indefinite symbols \(v\), we get
\[\begin{gathered} 1-x=0; \qquad (1) \\ x[yz+(1-y)(1-z)]=0; \qquad (2) \\ x[pq+(1-p)(1-q)]=0; \qquad (3) \\ p(1-y)=0; \qquad (4) \\ qz=0. \qquad (5) \end{gathered}\]

6. First, I shall examine whether any conclusions can be deduced from these equations about the truth or falsehood of the single propositions represented by the symbols \(y\), \(z\), \(p\), \(q\), namely of the propositions "Something always was;" "The things which now are have risen from nothing;" "The something which is exists by the necessity of its own nature;" "The something which is exists by the will of another being."

To do this, we must eliminate in turn every symbol except \(y\), then every symbol except \(z\), &c. The equation that results each time will show whether any such separate relation exists.

To eliminate \(x\) from (1), (2), and (3), we need only put the value of \(x\) given by (1) into (2) and (3). The results are
\[\begin{gathered} yz+(1-y)(1-z)=0\text{.} \qquad (6) \\ pq+(1-p)(1-q)=0\text{.} \qquad (7) \end{gathered}\]
That is, of the two propositions in each pair, exactly one is true.

To eliminate \(p\), we add (4) and (7), which gives
\[p(1-y)+pq+(1-p)(1-q)=0\text{;} \qquad (8)\]
and from this we find
\[(1-y)(1-q)=0\text{.} \qquad (9)\]
that is, it is not the case both that nothing always was and that the something does not exist by the will of another Being.

To eliminate \(q\) from (5) and (9), we have
\[qz + (1-y)(1-q) = 0\text{;}\]
and from this we find
\[z(1-y) = 0\text{.} \qquad (10)\]
Only the two equations (6) and (10) now remain. Adding them gives
\[yz + 1 - y = 0\text{.}\]
Eliminating \(z\) from this equation, we have
\[1-y = 0, \text{or, } y = 1\text{.} \qquad (11)\]
Eliminating \(y\) from the same equation, we have
\[z = 0\text{.} \qquad (12)\]
The interpretation of (11) is:

Something always was.

The interpretation of (12) is:

The things which are have not risen from nothing.

Next let us go back to the system (6), (7), together with the two equations (4) and (5), and find the two equations that involve \(p\) and \(q\) respectively.

To eliminate \(y\), we have from (4) and (6)
\[p(1-y) + yz + (1-y)(1-z) = (0)\text{;}\]
and from this
\[(p + 1 - z) z = 0, \text{or, } pz = 0\text{.} \qquad (13)\]
that is, it is not the case both that the something exists in the necessity of its own nature and that the things which now are have risen from nothing.

To eliminate \(z\) from (5) and (13), we have
\[qz + pz = 0\text{;}\]
and from this we get
\[0=0\text{.}\]
So there remains only equation (7). Eliminating \(q\) from it, we get \(0=0\) as the final equation in \(p\).

So no conclusion can be drawn from the premises asserting simply that the proposition "The something which is exists in the necessity of its own nature" is true, or that it is false. And since eliminating \(p\) gives the same result, \(0=0\), as the final equation in \(q\), it follows also that no conclusion can be drawn from the premises about the simple truth or falsehood of the proposition "The something which is exists by the will of another Being."

As for relations connecting more than one of the propositions represented by the elementary symbols, we need only consider the one expressed by equation (7), which connects \(p\) and \(q\). The propositions represented by the other symbols are absolutely true or false on their own, independent of any connection of this kind. Put into the form
\[p(1-q) + q(1-p) = 1\text{, is,}\]
the interpretation of (7) is:

The something which is, either exists in the necessity of its own nature, or by the will of another being.

I have set out the details of this analysis at a length that is perhaps needlessly full and wordy. I did so because in the examples that follow I mean rather to indicate the steps by which the results are reached than to risk repeating myself tiresomely. The conclusions the method has produced here are easily checked by ordinary reasoning.

The reader will have no difficulty in applying the method to the other train of premises in Dr. Clarke's first Proposition, and deducing from them the first two of the conclusions this analysis has reached.

Proposition II.

7. Some one unchangeable and independent Being has existed from eternity.

The premises from which this proposition is proved are the following:

1st. Something has always existed.

2nd. If something has always existed, either there has existed some one unchangeable and independent being, or the whole of existing things has been comprehended in a succession of changeable and dependent beings.

3rd. If the universe has consisted of a succession of changeable and dependent beings, either that series has had a cause from without, or it has had a cause from within.

4th. It has not had a cause from without (because it includes, by hypothesis, all things that exist).

5th. It has not had a cause from within (because no part is necessary, and if no part is necessary, the whole cannot be necessary).

Leaving out, purely for brevity, the supporting proofs given in parentheses in the fourth and fifth premises, we may represent the premises as follows:
\[\begin{aligned} \text{Let } x &= \text{ Something has always existed.} \\ y &= \text{There has existed some one unchangeable and independent being.} \\ z &= \text{There has existed a succession of changeable and dependent beings.} \\ p &= \text{That series has had a cause from without.} \\ q &= \text{That series has had a cause from within.} \end{aligned}\]
Then we have the following system of equations, namely:
\[\begin{aligned} \text{1st. } x &= 1 ; \\ \text{2nd. } x &= v \{y(1 - z) + z(1 - y)\} ; \\ \text{3rd. } z &= v \{p(1 - q) + (1 - p)q\} ; \\ \text{4th. } p &= 0; \\ \text{5th. } q &= 0 : \end{aligned}\]
which, when we eliminate each indefinite symbol \(v\) separately, gives
\[\begin{gathered} 1-x = 0; \qquad (1) \\ x\{yz+(1-y)(1-z)\}=0; \qquad (2) \\ z\{pq+(1-p)(1-q)\}=0; \qquad (3) \\ p = 0; \qquad (4) \\ q = 0. \qquad (5) \end{gathered}\]
Eliminating \(x\), \(p\), \(q\), and \(y\) from this system leads to the equation
\[z=0.\]
And eliminating \(x\), \(p\), \(q\), and \(z\) leads in the same way to the equation
\[y=1.\]
The interpretations of these equations are, respectively:

1st. _The whole of existing things has not been comprehended in a succession of changeable and dependent beings_.

2nd. _There has existed some one unchangeable and independent being_.

The second of these is the proposition Dr. Clarke proves. This analysis has settled every one of the propositions represented by the literal symbols \(x\), \(y\), \(z\), \(p\), \(q\) as absolutely true or absolutely false. So there is no need to look for any further relations connecting them.

Clarke gives another proof of Prop. II., which for brevity I pass over. It is worth noticing that the "impossibility of infinite succession," which Clarke proves as part of his argument, has commonly been assumed as a fundamental principle of metaphysics and carried over to questions other than causation. Aristotle uses it to establish that there must be first principles of demonstration, and that there must be an end (the good) in human actions, &c. There is perhaps no principle he appeals to more often in his writings. The schoolmen (the philosophers of the medieval universities) used it in the same way to prove that genera and species cannot be ranked one under another without end, and so that universals must exist. It seems that our inability to form a definite and complete conception of an infinite series, that is, to grasp it as a _whole_, has been confused with a logical inconsistency, or a contradiction in the idea itself.

8. The analysis of the next argument depends on the theory of Primary Propositions.

Proposition III.

That unchangeable and independent Being must be self-existent.

The premises are:—

1. Every being must either have come into existence out of nothing, or it must have been produced by some external cause, or it must be self-existent.

2. No being has come into existence out of nothing.

3. The unchangeable and independent Being has not been produced by an external cause.

To express these in symbols, let us take
\[\begin{aligned} x &= \text{Beings which have arisen out of nothing.} \\ y &= \text{Beings which have been produced by an external cause.} \\ z &= \text{Beings which are self-existent.} \\ w &= \text{The unchangeable and independent Being.} \end{aligned}\]
Then we have
\[\begin{gathered} x(1-y)(1-z) + y(1-x)(1-z) + z(1-x) (1-y) = 1, \qquad (1) \\ x = 0, \qquad (2) \\ w = v(1-y), \qquad (3) \end{gathered}\]
and eliminating \(v\) from the last of these,
\[wy = 0. \qquad (4)\]
that is, the unchangeable and independent Being is not among the beings produced by an external cause.

Whenever, as here, a symbol's value is given as 0 or 1, the best way to eliminate it is by simple substitution. So eliminating \(x\) gives
\[\begin{aligned} y(1-z) + z(1-y) &= 1; \qquad (5) \\ \text{or, } yz + (1-y)(1-z) &= 0. \qquad (6) \end{aligned}\]
Now adding (4) and (6), and eliminating \(y\), we get
\[\begin{gathered} w(1-z) = 0, \\ \therefore w = vz; \end{gathered}\]
and the interpretation of this is,—_The unchangeable and independent being is necessarily self-existing_.

The interpretation of (5), in the form it has as it stands, is,—_Every being has either been produced by an external cause, or it is self-existent_.

9. In Dr. Samuel Clarke's remarks on this proposition there is a remarkable argument meant to prove that the material world is not the self-existent being just spoken of. The passage I mean is the following:

"If matter be supposed to exist necessarily, then in that necessary existence there is either included the power of gravitation, or not. If not, then in a world merely material, and in which no intelligent being presides, there never could have been any motion; because motion, as has been already shown, and is now granted in the question, is not necessary of itself. But if the power of gravitation be included in the pretended necessary existence of matter: then, it following necessarily that there must be a vacuum (as the incomparable Sir Isaac Newton has abundantly demonstrated that there must, if gravitation be an universal quality or affection of matter), it follows likewise, that matter is not a necessary being. For if a vacuum actually be, then it is plainly more than possible for matter not to be."—(pp. 25, 26).

A careful examination shows that the actual premises involved in this proof are the following:

1st. If matter is a necessary being, either the property of gravitation is necessarily present, or it is necessarily absent.

2nd. If gravitation is necessarily absent, and the world is not subject to any presiding Intelligence, motion does not exist.

3rd. If the property of gravitation is necessarily present, the existence of a vacuum is necessary.

4th. If the existence of a vacuum is necessary, matter is not a necessary being.

5th. If matter is a necessary being, the world is not subject to a presiding Intelligence.

6th. Motion exists.

Of these premises, the first four are stated in the proof. The fifth is implied in the way its first and second sentences are connected. The sixth states a fact that the author does not seem to have thought it necessary to state, but which is clearly part of the ground of his reasoning. Let us represent the elementary propositions as follows:
\[\begin{aligned} \text{Let } x &= \text{Matter is a necessary being.} \\ y &= \text{Gravitation is necessarily present.} \\ t &= \text{Gravitation is necessarily absent.} \\ z &= \text{The world is merely material, and not subject to any presiding Intelligence.} \\ w &= \text{Motion exists.} \\ v &= \text{A vacuum is necessary.} \end{aligned}\]
Then the system of premises is represented by the following equations, in which \(q\) is used as the symbol of an indefinite time:
\[\begin{aligned} x &= q\{y(1-t) + (1-y)t\}. \\ tz &= q(1-w). \\ y &= qv. \\ v &= q(1-x). \\ x &= qz. \\ w &= 1. \end{aligned}\]
If we eliminate the symbols \(q\) from these, we get the following system, namely:
\[\begin{gathered} x\{yt+(1-y)(1-t)\}=0. \qquad (1) \\ tzw=0. \qquad (2) \\ y(1-v)=0. \qquad (3) \\ vx=0. \qquad (4) \\ x(1-z)=0. \qquad (5) \\ 1-w=0. \qquad (6) \end{gathered}\]
Now if we eliminate \(w\), \(v\), \(z\), \(y\), and \(t\) from these equations, we obtain the equation
\[x = 0,\]
which expresses the proposition, _Matter is not a necessary being_. This is Dr. Clarke's conclusion. If we try to eliminate any other set of five symbols (except the set \(v\), \(z\), \(y\), \(t\), and \(x\), which would give \(w = 1\)), we get a result of the form \(0=0\). So there are no other conclusions stating that any of the elementary propositions marked by single symbols is absolutely true or absolutely false.

Of conclusions expressed by equations in two symbols, there is only the following one, namely:— _If the world is merely material, and not subject to a presiding Intelligence, gravitation is not necessarily absent_. This conclusion is expressed by the equation
\[tz =0, \text{ whence } z = q (1 - t).\]
Suppose that in this analysis we drop the last premise, the one stating the fact that motion exists, and leave untouched the hypothetical principles contained in the other premises. Then some remarkable conclusions follow. I shall turn to these in the next chapter.

10. Of the rest of Dr. Clarke's argument I shall give only the substance and the connections, briefly. I shall dwell only on certain parts of it that are more complex than the others and better illustrate the method of this work.

In Prop. IV. it is shown that the substance or essence of the self-existent being is incomprehensible. The drift of the reasoning is that we do not know the essential nature of anything else, and so, much less, the essence of the self-existent being.

In Prop. V. it is argued that "though the substance or essence of the self-existent being is itself absolutely incomprehensible to us, yet many of the essential attributes of his nature are strictly demonstrable, as well as his existence."

In Prop. VI. it is argued that "the self-existent being must of necessity be infinite and omnipresent;" and it is maintained that his infinity must be "an infinity of fulness as well as of immensity." The ground the proof rests on is that an absolute necessity of existence must be independent of time, place, and circumstance, free from limitation, and so exclude all imperfection. And from this it is inferred that the self-existent being must be "a most simple, unchangeable, incorruptible being, without parts, figure, motion, or any other such properties as we find in matter."

The premises actually used may be set out as follows:

1. If a finite being is self-existent, it is a contradiction to suppose it not to exist.

2. A finite being may, without contradiction, be absent from one place.

3. That which may without contradiction be absent from one place may without contradiction be absent from all places.

4. That which may without contradiction be absent from all places may without contradiction be supposed not to exist.

Let us take
\[\begin{aligned} x &= \text{Finite beings.} \\ y &= \text{Things self-existent.} \\ z &= \text{Things which it is a contradiction to suppose not to exist.} \\ w &= \text{Things which may be absent without contradiction from one place.} \\ t &= \text{Things which without contradiction may be absent from every place.} \end{aligned}\]
Expressing the premises and eliminating the indefinite symbols, we have
\[\begin{aligned} &xy(1-z) = 0. \qquad (1) \\ &x(1-w) = 0. \qquad (2) \\ &w(1-t) = 0. \qquad (3) \\ &tz = 0. \qquad (4) \end{aligned}\]
Eliminating \(t\), \(w\), and \(z\) in turn, we get
\[xy = 0,\]
\[\therefore y = \frac{0}{0}(1-x);\]
and the interpretation of this is,—_Whatever is self-existent is infinite_.

In Prop. VII. it is argued that the self-existent being must of necessity be One. The proof runs in this order: the self-existent being is "necessarily existent"; "necessity absolute in itself is simple and uniform, and without any possible difference or variety"; all "variety or difference of existence" implies dependence; and therefore "whatever exists necessarily is the one simple essence of the self-existent being."

The conclusion is also made to follow from these premises:—

1. If there are two or more necessary and independent beings, either of them may be supposed to exist alone.

2. If either may be supposed to exist alone, it is not a contradiction to suppose the other not to exist.

3. If it is not a contradiction to suppose this, there are not two necessary and independent beings.

Let us represent the elementary propositions as follows:—
\[\begin{aligned} x &= \text{there exist two necessary independent beings.} \\ y &= \text{either may be supposed to exist alone.} \\ z &= \text{it is not a contradiction to suppose the other not to exist.} \end{aligned}\]
Proceeding as before, we then have
\[\begin{aligned} x(1-y) &= 0. \qquad (1) \\ y(1-z) &= 0. \qquad (2) \\ zx = 0. \qquad (3) \end{aligned}\]
Eliminating \(y\) and \(z\), we have
\[x=0.\]
So, _There do not exist two necessary and independent beings_.

11. It is well known that objections to the premises on which these last two propositions rest were raised by Bishop Butler, in some celebrated letters. When the "Demonstration" was published, Butler was a student at a nonconformist academy (a college for Protestants outside the Church of England). His letters, with Dr. Clarke's replies, are usually printed at the back of editions of the work. The real question at issue is whether the principle holds that "whatsoever is absolutely necessary at all is absolutely necessary in every part of space, and in every point of duration." Clarke's reasoning assumes this principle, and he states it outright in his reply to Butler's first letter. In his second letter Butler says: "I do not conceive that the idea of ubiquity is contained in the idea of self-existence, or _directly follows from it_, any otherwise than as whatever exists must exist _somewhere_." That is to say, necessary existence implies existence in some part of space, but not in every part. Dr. Clarke never seems to have managed to answer this objection effectively. The whole correspondence is extremely curious and interesting. Butler's objections are exactly the ones that would occur to a sharp mind convinced that the successful search for truth depends mainly on sifting first principles, not on any mechanical skill in reasoning. And Dr. Clarke's replies, though they cannot be accepted as satisfactory, show to a remarkable degree the particular intellectual power that is evident in the work from which the discussion arose.

12. In Prop. VIII. it is argued that the self-existent and original cause of all things must be an Intelligent Being.

The main argument offered for this proposition runs as follows. The cause is more excellent than the effect. So the self-existent being, as the cause and origin of all things, must contain in itself the perfections of all things; and intelligence is one of the perfections shown in a part of the creation. It is further argued that this perfection is not a modification of shape, divisibility, or any of the known properties of matter, for these are not perfections but _limitations_. To this is added the _à posteriori_ argument, the argument from the evidence of design in the structure of the universe.

There is also attached, however, a separate argument for the existence of an intelligent self-existent being, based on the observed existence of motion in the universe. I shall set out this proof briefly and apply the method of this treatise to it.

Leaving out unimportant explanations, the argument is as follows:—

"’Tis evident there is some such a thing as motion in the world; which either began at some time or other, or was eternal. If it began in time, then the question is granted that the first cause is an intelligent being.... On the contrary, if motion was eternal, either it was eternally caused by some eternal intelligent being, or it must of itself be necessary and self-existent, or else, without any necessity in its own nature, and without any external necessary cause, it must have existed from eternity by an endless successive communication. If motion was eternally caused by some eternal intelligent being, this also is granting the question as to the present dispute. If it was of itself necessary and self-existent, then it follows that it must be a contradiction in terms to suppose any matter to be at rest. And yet, at the same time, because the determination of this self-existent motion must be every way at once, the effect of it would be nothing else but a perpetual rest.... But if it be said that motion, without any necessity in its own nature, and without any external necessary cause, has existed from eternity merely by an endless successive communication, as Spinoza inconsistently enough seems to assert, this I have before shown (in the proof of the second general proposition of this discourse) to be a plain contradiction. It remains, therefore, that motion must of necessity be originally caused by something that is intelligent."

The premises of this argument may be arranged as follows:

1. If motion began in time, the first cause is an intelligent being.

2. If motion has existed from eternity, either it has been eternally caused by some eternal intelligent being, or it is self-existent, or it must have existed by endless successive communication.

3. If motion has been eternally caused by an eternal intelligent being, the first cause is an intelligent being.

4. If it is self-existent, matter is at rest and not at rest.

5. That motion has existed by endless successive communication, and that at the same time it is not self-existent, and has not been eternally caused by some eternal intelligent being, is false.

To express these propositions, let us take—
\[\begin{aligned} x &= \text{Motion began in time (and therefore)} \\ 1-x &= \text{Motion has existed from eternity.} \\ y &= \text{The first cause is an intelligent being.} \\ p &= \text{Motion has been eternally caused by some eternal intelligent being.} \\ q &= \text{Motion is self-existent.} \\ r &= \text{Motion has existed by endless successive communication.} \\ s &= \text{Matter is at rest.} \end{aligned}\]
The equations of the premises are then—
\[\begin{aligned} &x = vy.\\ 1-&x = v \left\{p\left(1-q\right)\left(1-r\right) +q\left(1-p\right) \left(1-r\right) +r\left(1-p\right)\left(1-q\right)\right\}.\\ &p = vy.\\ &q = vs\left(1-s\right) = 0.\\ &r\left(1-q\right)\left(1-p\right) = 0. \end{aligned}\]
The fourth equation gives \(q = 0\), since nothing can be both at rest and not at rest. Putting this value for \(q\) into the other equations, we get the system
\[\begin{aligned} x &= vy, \\ p &= vy, \end{aligned} \qquad \begin{gathered} 1-x = v\left\{p\left(1-r\right) +r\left(1-p\right)\right\}, \\ r\left(1-p\right) = 0, \end{gathered}\]
and eliminating the indefinite symbols \(v\) from this, we have the final reduced system,
\[\begin{gathered} x\left(1-y\right)=0, \qquad (1) \\ \left(1-x\right)\left\{pr + \left(1-p\right)\left(1-r\right)\right\}=0, \qquad (2) \\ p\left(1-y\right)=0. \qquad (3) \\ r\left(1-p\right)=0. \qquad (4) \end{gathered}\]
We shall first look for the value of \(y\), the symbol that appears in Dr. Clarke's conclusion. First, eliminating \(x\) from (1) and (2), we have
\[(1-y) \lbrace pr + (1-p)(1-r) \rbrace = 0. \qquad (5)\]
Next, to eliminate \(r\) from (4) and (5), we have
\[\begin{gathered} r(1-p) + (1-y) \lbrace pr + (1-p)(1-r) \rbrace = 0, \\ \therefore \lbrace 1 - p + (1-y)p \rbrace \times (1-y)(1-p) = 0; \end{gathered}\]
and so
\[(1-y)(1-p) = 0. \qquad (6)\]
Last, eliminating \(p\) from (3) and (6), we have
\[\begin{aligned} 1 - y &= 0, \\ \therefore y &= 1, \end{aligned}\]
which expresses the conclusion we were looking for, _The first cause is an intelligent being_.

Let us now examine what other conclusions can be deduced from the premises.

If we put the value just found for \(y\) into equations (1), (2), (3), (4), they reduce to the following pair of equations, namely,
\[(1-x) \lbrace pr + (1-p)(1-r) \rbrace = 0,\quad r(1-p) = 0. \qquad (7)\]
Eliminating \(x\) from these equations, we have
\[r(1-p) = 0, \text{ whence }r = vp,\]
which expresses the conclusion, _If motion has existed by endless successive communication, it has been eternally caused by an eternal intelligent being_.

Again, eliminating \(r\) from the same pair, we have
\[\begin{gathered} (1-x)(1-p) = 0, \\ \text{or, } 1-x = vp, \end{gathered}\]
which expresses the conclusion, _If motion has existed from eternity, it has been eternally caused by some eternal intelligent being_.

Last, eliminating \(p\) from the same original pair, we get
\[(1-x)r = 0,\]
which, solved in the form
\[1-x = v(1-r),\]
gives the conclusion, _If motion has existed from eternity, it has not existed by an endless successive communication_.

Solved in the form
\[r = vx,\]
the same equation leads to the equivalent conclusion, _If motion exists by an endless successive communication, it began in time_.
