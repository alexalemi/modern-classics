Preface

This book is not a reissue of an earlier treatise of mine, “The Mathematical Analysis of Logic.” Its first part does pursue the same object, and it begins by laying down the same system of fundamental laws. But its methods are more general, and it applies them far more widely. It gives the results of some years of study and reflection on a way of investigating the operations of the intellect. My earlier account of that idea was written within a few weeks of first conceiving it.

The part of this book that deals with logic assumes that the reader knows the most important terms of the subject as it is usually taught, and what the subject is for. On these points there is no better guide than Archbishop Whately’s “Elements of Logic” or Mr. Thomson’s “Outlines of the Laws of Thought.” The present revival of interest in studies of this kind seems largely due to the first of these. The reader also needs some algebra, but no more than how to solve simple equations. The chapters on the theory of probability call for rather more algebra, especially the method of elimination and the solution of equations with more than one unknown. For background on that subject, see the treatises on probability in “Lardner’s Cabinet Cyclopædia,” by Professor De Morgan, and in the “Library of Useful Knowledge,” by Sir John Lubbock, and an interesting series of letters translated from the French of M. Quetelet. Other references are given in the book itself. On a first reading the reader may skip, as he sees fit, Chapters X., XIV., and XIX., along with any of the applications he finds uninviting or beside his purpose.

In various places, and especially in the notes to the last chapter, the reader will find references to writers ancient and modern. They are meant mainly to illustrate a certain view of the history of philosophy. On these I should add one thing. I have never given a citation that I did not believe, after careful examination, to be supported either by other authorities or by the general drift of the work it came from. I would gladly have avoided anything that might be taken for a parade of learning. But I felt I owed it both to my subject and to the truth to give the reader the means of checking the statements in the text.

I now bring to a close a labour whose extent few people will be able to judge from its visible fruits. If I may speak for a single moment of the feelings with which I have pursued this task, and with which I now lay it aside, I would say this. I never doubted that it was worthy of my best efforts. I felt that whatever truth it might bring to light was not a private or arbitrary thing, and did not depend, in its essence, on anyone’s opinion. I knew well that learned and able men held views on logic directly opposed to those on which the whole argument and method of this book rest. I believed their views to be mistaken, but I knew that my own might be bent, without my noticing, by an influence of another kind. I felt especially the danger of the bias that comes from long attention to one particular aspect of the truth. But I trust that out of this conflict of opinions the truth will only emerge the freer from anything merely personal. I trust that its different parts will be seen in their proper proportion, and that none of them will in the end be valued too highly or too lightly because of prejudices attached to the mere form in which it was presented.

To my valued friend the Rev. George Stephens Dickson, of Lincoln, I wish to record my thanks for much kind help in revising this work, and for some important suggestions.

5, Grenville-place, Cork,

Nov. 30th. 1853.

Note

In Prop. II. (XVII. 10), the “absolute probabilities” of the events \(x, y, z ..\) mean simply this: the probabilities those events would need to have so that, if we treat them as independent and take their probabilities as our only data, the probabilities we calculate for the same events under the condition \(V\) come out as \(p, q, r ..\) The problem of the urn added to that proposition must be restated in the same way. The true solution of that problem, as it is actually stated, is \(p' = cp, q' = cq\), where \(c\) is the arbitrary probability of the condition that the ball drawn is white, or made of marble, or both. (See XVII. 17, Case II.)

So, since logical reduction brings every question in the theory of probability to one form (from the probabilities of simple events, \(s\), \(t\), and so on, under a given condition \(V\), we must find the probability of some combination \(A\) of those events under the same condition), the principle behind the proof of Prop. IV. is really this: “The probability of such a combination \(A\) under the condition \(V\) must be calculated as if the events \(s\), \(t\), and so on were independent, and had such probabilities as would make the probabilities of those events, derived under the same condition \(V\), equal to the ones given in the data.” I regard this principle as an axiom. At the same time it can be checked without limit, both directly and through the results of the method that rests on it. I should add that this is the form in which the principle first came to me, and the way I have always understood it. The error in the particular problem I referred to came from carelessness in choosing a physical illustration.
