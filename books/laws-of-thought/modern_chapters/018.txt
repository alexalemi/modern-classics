Chapter 16: On the Theory of Probabilities

1. Before another year is out, exactly two centuries will have passed since Pascal solved the first known question in the theory of probability. In solving it he laid the foundations of a science with an unusual share of the charm that belongs to the more abstract kinds of mathematical thought. The problem came from the Chevalier de Méré, a man known as a gambler, who put it to the recluse of Port Royal. Pascal had not yet been drawn away from the interests of science by the more absorbing contemplation of "the greatness and the misery of man." That problem was the first of a long series. The series was destined to call new methods of mathematical analysis into being, and to give real service in the practical business of life. Nor does the interest of the subject lie only in its links with mathematics, or in its usefulness. The theory of probability repays the attention we give it as an object of study in its own right. It repays study of the basic ways in which it has been conceived; of the great secondary principles that have gradually been added to it, as they were to mechanics, a science of the same age; and, last, of how near it has actually come to perfection. By perfection I mean unity of conception and harmony of methods. In this chapter I mean to consider a few of these points, very briefly.

Footnote: See in particular a letter Pascal addressed to Fermat, who had asked for his attention to a mathematical problem (Port Royal, par M. de Sainte Beuve); also various passages in the collection of Fragments published by M. Prosper Faugère.

2. A distinguished writer has stated the basic definitions of the science like this:

Footnote: Poisson, Recherches sur la Probabilitè des Jugemens.

"The probability of an event is the reason we have to believe that it has taken place, or that it will take place."

"The measure of the probability of an event is the ratio of the number of cases favourable to that event, to the total number of cases favourable or contrary, and all equally possible" (that is, all equally likely to happen).

It follows from these definitions that the word _probability_, in its mathematical sense, refers to the state of our knowledge about the circumstances under which an event may happen or fail to happen. How much reason we have to think an event will occur depends on how much we know about its circumstances. Call that reason, in a single word, our _expectation_ of the event: it varies with what we know. Probability is expectation founded on partial knowledge. If we knew _all_ the circumstances that affect whether an event occurs, expectation would turn into certainty, and there would be neither room nor need for a theory of probability.

3. Our expectation of an event grows stronger as the number of known cases favourable to it grows, compared with the whole number of equally possible cases, favourable or not. Even so, it would be unphilosophical to claim that the strength of that expectation, taken as a feeling in the mind, can be measured against any numerical standard. The hopeful man builds high hopes where the timid man despairs and the undecided man is lost in doubt. As subjects for scientific study, _opinion_ and _sensation_ are somewhat alike. A thermometer, or a carefully prepared photographic plate, does not show how intense our sensations of heat and light are. It shows certain physical circumstances that go along with producing those sensations. In the same way, the theory of probability studies the numerical measure of the circumstances on which expectation is founded, and that aim covers the whole range of its proper uses. The rules we use in life insurance, and in the other statistical uses of the theory, have nothing to do with expectation as a _mental_ phenomenon. They rest on the assumption that the future will resemble the past: that under the same circumstances the same event will tend to recur with a definite numerical frequency. They do not rest on any attempt to calculate the strength of human hopes and fears.

Now experience really does show that events of a given kind, under given circumstances, tend to recur with a definite frequency, whether or not we know their true causes. Of course this tendency usually shows itself only when the field of observation is large enough. The court records of a great nation, and its registers of births and deaths by age and sex, and so on, are remarkably uniform from year to year. In a given language, or family of languages, the same sounds and sequences of sounds recur with fixed frequencies; and if the language is written, so do the same letters and sequences of letters. The key to the rough Ogham inscriptions found in various parts of Ireland, in which at first no division into words could even be made out, was recovered by strictly applying this principle. The same method, I understand, has been applied to deciphering the cuneiform records recently dug out of the ruins of Nineveh through the enterprise of Mr. Layard.

Footnote: The discovery is due to the Rev. Charles Graves, Professor of Mathematics in the University of Dublin. (See the Proceedings of the Royal Irish Academy, Feb. 14, 1848.) Professor Graves tells me that he has tested the principle by constructing sequence tables for all the European languages.

Footnote: By the learned Orientalist, Dr. Edward Hincks.

4. Let us try, from these statements and definitions, to form an idea of the proper aim of the theory of probability.

Probability, we have said, is expectation founded on a particular kind of knowledge, namely the knowledge of how often events occur relative to one another. So the probabilities of events, or of combinations of events, are always our data. It makes no difference whether we work them out from knowing how things are made, in the circumstances where the events happen, or get them from long observation of a past series of the events' occurrences and failures. The probability of some connected event, or combination of events, is then the _quæsitum_, the thing sought. Now in the most general, yet still strict, meaning of the word "event," every combination of events is also an event. Two or more events happening together is still an event. So is an event happening under given conditions, or in any connection we can imagine with other events. Using the word with this freedom, we could define the aim of the theory of probability like this. Given the probabilities of any events, of whatever kind, to find the probability of some other event connected with them.

5. Events can be divided into simple and compound. A compound event is one that consists of a combination of simple events (I. 13). In these terms, we could say that the practical aim of the theory is this: to find the probability of some event, simple or compound, from the given probabilities of other events, simple or compound, to which it is connected by the way it is defined.

For example, suppose we know from the way a die is made that the probability that any particular throw will give an ace (a one) is measured by the fraction \(\displaystyle\frac{1}{6}\). And suppose we are asked for the probability of getting one ace, and only one, in two throws in a row. We can set the problem out in the order of its _data_ and its _quæsitum_, like this:

First Datum.—The probability of the event that the first throw gives an ace \(\displaystyle =\frac{1}{6}\).

Second Datum.—The probability of the event that the second throw gives an ace \(\displaystyle =\frac{1}{6}\).

Quæsitum.—The probability of the event that either the first throw gives an ace and the second does not; or the first does not give an ace and the second does.

Here the two data are the probabilities of simple events: the first throw giving an ace, and the second throw giving an ace. The quæsitum is the probability of a compound event, a particular "either-or" combination of the simple events contained or implied in the data. When the numbers in a problem can be worked out, as they are here, from the way things are made, I suspect it will usually turn out that the data are the probabilities of _simple_ events and the _quæsitum_ is the probability of a _compound_ event that depends on them. That is the case with one class of problems that has taken up, perhaps, more than its fair share of the attention of students of probability, namely games of chance and games of skill. In a game of chance some physical circumstance, such as the way a die is made, fixes the probability of each possible step in the game, and the outcome is some definite combination of those steps. In a game of skill the players' relative ability, assumed to be known _à priori_, fixes that same element. But in statistical problems our knowledge comes not from studying how things are made but from recorded observations of Nature or of human society, and then there is no reason why the data these observations give us should be the probabilities of simple events. On the contrary, when events or conditions turn up together in striking combinations, which hints at some hidden causal connection, that very fact suggests that we should keep a numerical record of how often such combinations occur, so that they can teach us something for the future. And the data that observations of this kind give us are the probabilities of compound events. So a general method for solving problems with data like these is needed for more than the complete development of the theory of probability. It is also, perhaps, a necessary condition for applying the theory to a large and practically important class of questions.

6. Before we ask how far known methods can solve problems like these, it will help to notice that there is another way of looking at every question in the theory of probability. It is this. In place of _events_, we put the propositions that assert that those events have occurred, or will occur. And we take the numerical probability to refer to the _truth_ of those _propositions_, not to the _occurrence_ of the _events_ they make assertions about. So instead of taking the fraction \(p\) to express the probability that an event \(E\) occurs, let us take it to stand for the probability that the proposition \(X\) is true, the proposition that asserts that the event \(E\) will occur. In the same way, suppose some probability \(q\) refers to a compound event, such as the events \(E\) and \(F\) happening together. Instead, let it stand for the probability that the proposition is true which asserts that \(E\) and \(F\) will both occur. And let the same exchange be made everywhere: "either-or" combinations and "if" combinations of events become disjunctive and conditional propositions. This new use of probability goes necessarily hand in hand with the old one. But adopting it brings a practical advantage, because we have already worked out the theory of propositions. We have defined their main kinds, and we have set up methods for finding, in every case, how far and in what way they depend on one another. Any general theory of probability must rest on this foundation, or on something equivalent to it. I do not say that other considerations may not sometimes be needed when the theory is applied. The data may not be enough for a definite solution, and someone may think it necessary to _supply_ the gap by a hypothesis. Or, where large numbers are involved, difficulties of that origin may arise _after the solution_, and they need special methods of their own. But in every case some form of the general problem stated above (Art. 4) is involved, and the real, distinctive work of the theory lies in dealing with that problem. I want it noticed that the following chapters are devoted mainly to this aim. I do not intend to take up questions that involve extra hypotheses, except in passing. Even for those questions (I. 17), the first thing that matters is to establish a general method, founded on a solid and sufficient basis of theory.

Editor's note: This is the idea modern probability theory is built on. In 1933 the Russian mathematician Andrei Kolmogorov set probability on events treated as sets, combined by "and", "or" and "not" exactly as Boole combines classes. In today's terms, a probability is a measure on a Boolean algebra of events: a rule that gives each event a number between zero and one, and adds the numbers of events that cannot happen together.

7. Here is a summary, taken mainly from Laplace, of the principles that have been used to solve questions of probability. They follow from the basic definitions already given, and they show how far it has proved possible to put those definitions to use.

Principle 1st. If \(p\) is the probability that an event occurs, \(1-p\) is the probability that it does not occur.

2nd. The probability that two independent events both occur is the product of their probabilities.

3rd. The probability that two dependent events both occur is the probability of one of them multiplied by the probability that, if that one occurs, the other will occur too.

4th. The probability that, if an event \(E\) takes place, an event \(F\) will also take place, equals the probability that \(E\) and \(F\) both occur, divided by the probability that \(E\) occurs.

5th. The probability that one or the other of two events occurs, when the two cannot both occur, equals the sum of their separate probabilities.

6th. Suppose an observed event can only have come from one of \(n\) different causes, which are equally probable _à priori_. Then the probability of any one of those causes is a fraction. Its numerator is the probability of the event, assuming that cause is present. Its denominator is the sum of the same probabilities for all the causes.

7th. The probability of a future event is found by taking each cause, multiplying its probability by the probability that, if that cause is present, the future event will take place, and adding up all these products.

8. About how far these principles reach, and how far they are enough, we can make the following remarks.

First, by combining these principles properly, we can always express the probability of a compound event that depends in any way on independent simple events whose separate probabilities are given. A very large share of the problems that have actually been solved are of this kind. The difficulty in solving them has not come from any gap in what the theory of probability tells us to do. It has come from the need for an analysis that can carry out those instructions when they bring in functions of large numbers, or series with many complicated terms. So it can be fully granted that every problem whose data are the probabilities of independent simple events falls within the reach of the existing methods.

Second, some of the principles listed above, especially the sixth and seventh, do not assume that all the data are probabilities of simple events. But when they are applied, as they particularly are, to questions of causes, they do assume that the causes they take into account exclude one another, so that no two of them can combine to produce an effect. Now let us move the numerical probabilities, as explained above, from the events to the propositions that express those events. Then the most general problem to which these principles apply can be set out in the following order of _data_ and _quæsita_ (things sought).

Data.

First, the probabilities of the \(n\) conditional propositions:

	If the cause | \(A_1\) | exists, the event | \(E\) | will follow;
	" | \(A_2\) | " | \(E\) | "
	 | \(\vdots\) |  | \(\vdots\) | ""
	" | \(A_n\) | " | \(E\) | "

Second, the condition that the antecedents of those propositions (their "if" parts) conflict with one another, so that no two can be true together.

Requirements.

The probability that the proposition is true which declares that the event \(E\) occurs; and also, when that proposition is known to be true, the probabilities that the several propositions are true which assert that each of the causes \(A_1, A_2 \dotsc A_n\) is present.

Here we see that the data are the probabilities of a series of compound events, expressed by _conditional_ propositions. But the system is plainly a very limited and special one. Its antecedents must exclude one another, and there is only one consequent in the whole system, the event \(E\). Being able to handle a system like this does not mean we can solve problems whose data are the probabilities of _any_ system of conditional propositions. Still less does it mean we can solve problems whose data are the probabilities of _any system of propositions whatever_. And if we look at the subject from the side of its content rather than its form, it is clear that the hypothesis of _exclusive_ causes, causes that never act together, is seldom true of the actual world. The phenomena of the world seem usually to be the products of complex causes, working together to an extent and in a way we do not know. That is surely the case in nearly every field of natural or social inquiry where the theory of probability promises any new and useful applications.

9. To these principles we may add one more, stated in the following terms by the Savilian Professor of Astronomy at the University of Oxford.

Footnote: On certain Questions relating to the Theory of Probabilities; by W. F. Donkin, M. A., F. R. S., &c. Philosophical Magazine, May, 1851.

"Principle 8. If there be any number of mutually exclusive hypotheses, \(h_1, h_2, h_3,\dotsc\) of which the probabilities relative to a particular state of information are \(p_1, p_2, p_3,\dotsc\) and if new information be given which changes the probabilities of some of them, suppose of \(h_{m+1}\) and all that follow, without having otherwise _any reference to the rest_; then the probabilities of these latter have the same ratios to one another, after the new information, that they had before, that is,
\[p'_1\colon p'_2\colon p'_3\ldots\colon p'_m = p_1 \colon p_2 \colon p_3 \ldots\colon p_m,\]
where the accented letters denote the values after the new information has been acquired."

In plain terms: if new information bears only on some of the hypotheses, the others keep their old proportions to one another. This principle seems more basic than most of those listed before it, and perhaps, as Professor Donkin has suggested, it could be treated as an axiom. Indeed it seems to be founded in the very definition of the measure of probability, as "the ratio of the number of cases favourable to an event to the total number of cases favourable or contrary, and all equally possible." For on this definition it is clear that if the number of equally possible cases is cut down in some proportion, while the number of favourable cases stays the same, then the probabilities of any events those cases bear on go up in exactly that proportion. The new hypothesis, namely that the number of possible cases shrinks without changing how many of them favour the events in question, raises the probabilities of all those events by the same factor. So their sizes relative to one another stay the same. If, then, the principle is bound up in the very definition of probability, as it appears to be, it can hardly, _by itself_, take us any further in solving problems than careful study of the definition alone would. From these considerations it seems doubtful whether we can hope for any real advance, either in the theory of probability as a branch of knowledge or in the practical solving of its problems, without help of a different kind from anything we have looked at so far. And it seems impossible to build, _solely_ on a set of principles like those above, a method that applies to every problem, whatever the number or the nature of the propositions that express its data. Reaching that goal needs other elements, and they will be the subject of the next chapter.
