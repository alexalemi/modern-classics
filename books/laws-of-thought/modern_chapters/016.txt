Chapter 14: Example of Analysis

1. Let us go back to the remarkable system of premises used in the previous chapter to prove that "Matter is not a necessary being." This time we will drop the sixth premise, namely "Motion exists," and look at some of the consequences that follow from the premises that remain. In effect, we accept Dr. Clarke's hypothetical principles as true, but suppose ourselves ignorant of the fact that motion exists. There may be cases where picking out part of the premises of an argument in this way leads to interesting consequences. That is not, however, my reason for taking up this example again. The premises we will actually use are these—

1. If matter is a necessary being, either the property of gravitation is necessarily present, or it is necessarily absent.

2. If gravitation is necessarily absent, and the world is not subject to any presiding intelligence, motion does not exist.

3. If gravitation is necessarily present, a vacuum is necessary.

4. If a vacuum is necessary, matter is not a necessary being.

5. If matter is a necessary being, the world is not subject to a presiding intelligence.

As before, we represent the elementary propositions by letters, as follows:
\[\begin{aligned} x &= \text{Matter is a necessary being.} \\ y &= \text{Gravitation is necessarily present.} \\ w &= \text{Motion exists.} \\ t &= \text{Gravitation is necessarily absent.} \\ z &= \text{The world is merely material, and not subject to a presiding intelligence.} \\ v &= \text{A vacuum is necessary.} \end{aligned}\]

When we express the premises in symbols and eliminate the indefinite class symbols (\(q\)), we get the following system of equations:
\[\begin{aligned} xyt + x\bar{y}\bar{t} &= 0, \\ tzw &= 0, \\ y\bar{v} &= 0, \\ vx &= 0, \\ x\bar{z} &= 0; \end{aligned}\]
Here, to save space, \(\bar{y}\) stands for \(1-y\), \(\bar{t}\) for \(1-t\), and so on; so also \(1-\bar{t} = t\), \(1-\bar{y} = y\), and so on. Read aloud, the five equations say: matter is never a necessary being while gravitation is both necessarily present and necessarily absent, or while it is neither; gravitation is never necessarily absent in a merely material world where motion exists; gravitation is never necessarily present without a vacuum being necessary; a vacuum is never necessary while matter is a necessary being; and matter is never a necessary being without the world being merely material.

The left-hand sides of these equations contain only positive terms, so we can combine them into a single equation by adding them together (VIII. Prop. 2), namely:
\[xyt + x\bar{y}\bar{t} + y\bar{v} + vx + x\bar{z} + tzw = 0,\]
What remains is to reduce the left-hand side so that it satisfies the condition \(V(1-V) = 0\), the law of duality, which holds when the left-hand side is a sum of constituents with no constituent counted twice.

To do this, we first develop the left-hand side with respect to the symbols \(x\) and \(y\). The result is—
\[\begin{aligned} (t + \bar{v} + v + \bar{z} + tzw) xy + (\bar{t} + v + \bar{z} + tzw) x\bar{y} \\ + (\bar{v} + tzw) \bar{x}y + tzw\bar{x}\bar{y} = 0. \end{aligned}\]
Our goal will be reached if we reduce the four coefficients of this development to equivalent forms that themselves satisfy the required condition.

Take the first coefficient. Since \(v + \bar{v} = 1\), it is
\[1 + t + \bar{z} + tzw,\]
and this reduces to unity (IX. Prop. 1), because a sum of class terms that contains the whole universe is the whole universe.

The second coefficient is
\[\bar{t} + v + \bar{z} + tzw;\]
and its reduced form (X. 3) is
\[\bar{t} + tv + t\bar{v}\bar{z} + t\bar{v}zw.\]
Here each term after the first has been cut down to the part not already covered by the terms before it.

The third coefficient, \(\bar{v} + tzw\), reduces by the same method to \(\bar{v} + tzwv\). The last coefficient, \(tzw\), needs no reduction. So the development becomes
\[xy + \left(\bar{t} + tv + t\bar{v}\bar{z} + t\bar{v}zw\right)x\bar{y} + \left(\bar{v} + tzwv\right) \bar{x}y + tzw\bar{x}\bar{y} = 0; \qquad (1)\]
and this is the reduced form we were looking for.

2. Now by the principle stated in Prop. III., Chap. X., we can find the whole relation connecting any particular set of the symbols in this equation. We develop the equation with respect to the symbols in question, and keep in the result only those constituents whose coefficients are unity. So if we choose the symbols \(x\) and \(y\), we are led at once to the equation
\[xy=0,\]
that is, matter is never a necessary being while gravitation is necessarily present. From this we have
\[y=\frac{0}{0}(1-x),\]
that is, the cases in which gravitation is necessarily present are some of the cases in which matter is not a necessary being. In words: _If gravitation is necessarily present, matter is not a necessary being_.

Next let us look for the relation between \(x\) and \(w\). Developing (1) with respect to those symbols, we get
\[\begin{gathered} \left(y + \bar{t}\bar{y} + tv\bar{y} + t\bar{v}\bar{z}\bar{y} + t\bar{v}z\bar{y}\right)xw + \left(y + \bar{t}\bar{y} + tv\bar{y} + t\bar{v}\bar{z}\bar{y}\right)x\bar{w} \\ + \left(\bar{v}y + tzvy + tz\bar{y}\right)\bar{x}w + \bar{v}y\bar{x}\bar{w} = 0. \end{gathered}\]
The coefficient of \(xw\), and that one alone, reduces to unity. For \(t\bar{v}\bar{z}\bar{y} + t\bar{v}z\bar{y} = t\bar{v}\bar{y}\), and \(tv\bar{y} + t\bar{v}\bar{y} = t\bar{y}\), and \(\bar{t}\bar{y} + t\bar{y} = \bar{y}\), and lastly, \(y + \bar{y} = 1\). Each step joins two terms that differ only in one letter being present in one and absent in the other, and that letter drops out. This is always how such reductions go. So we get
\[\begin{gathered} xw=0, \\ \therefore w = \frac{0}{0}(1-x), \end{gathered}\]
that is, the cases in which motion exists are some of the cases in which matter is not a necessary being. In words: _If motion exists, matter is not a necessary being_.

If we develop (1) in the same way with respect to \(x\) and \(z\), we get the equation
\[\begin{aligned} x\bar{z} &= 0, \\ \therefore x &= \frac{0}{0}z, \end{aligned}\]
which says: _If matter is a necessary being, the world is merely material, and without a presiding intelligence_.

This, admittedly, is just the fifth premise over again. But it shows that there is no other relation connecting the two elements it involves.

If we look for the whole relation connecting the elements \(x\), \(w\), and \(y\), we find, by developing (1) with respect to those symbols and proceeding as before,
\[xy + xw\bar{y} = 0.\]
That is, matter is never a necessary being while gravitation is necessarily present; nor is it ever a necessary being while motion exists and gravitation is not necessarily present. Suppose we are asked to find from this what follows from the hypothesis "Motion does not exist," as it bears on two questions: whether matter is necessary, and whether gravitation is necessarily present. We find
\[\begin{gathered} w=\frac{-xy}{x\bar{y}}, \\ \therefore 1-w=\frac{x}{x\bar{y}} =\frac{1}{0}xy+x\bar{y}+\frac{0}{0}\bar{x}; \\ \text{or, } 1-w=x\bar{y}+\frac{0}{0}\bar{x},\text{ with }xy=0. \end{gathered}\]
The direct interpretation of the first equation is this: If motion does not exist, then either matter is a necessary being and gravitation is not necessarily present, or matter is not a necessary being.

The reverse interpretation is: _If matter is a necessary being, and gravitation not necessary, motion does not exist_.

In exactly the same way, if we looked for the full relation between \(x\), \(z\), and \(w\), we would find
\[xzw + x\bar{z} = 0.\]
That is, matter is never a necessary being in a merely material world where motion exists, nor in a world that is not merely material. From this we can deduce
\[z=x\bar{w}+\frac{0}{0}\bar{x},\text{ with }xw=0.\]

So: If the world is merely material, and not subject to any presiding intelligence, then either matter is a necessary being and motion does not exist, or matter is not a necessary being.

And, _reversely, If matter is a necessary being, and there is no such thing as motion, the world is merely material_.

3. We could of course extend the same method to find the consequences of any complex hypothesis \(u\). Take, for instance, "The world is merely material, and without any presiding intelligence (\(z\)), but motion exists" (\(w\)), and ask what it implies about any other open questions raised in the original premises, such as "Matter is a necessary being" (\(x\)), or "Gravitation is a necessary quality of matter" (\(y\)). To do this we would join to the general equation (1) a new equation,
\[u = wz,\]
which says that _u_ stands for the cases where the world is merely material and motion exists. We would then reduce the system thus formed to a single equation, \(V=0\), in which \(V\) satisfies the condition \(V(1-V) = 0\). Then we would proceed as above to find the relation between \(u\), \(x\), and \(y\), and finally \(u\) as a developed function of \(x\) and \(y\). But it is much better to use the methods of Chapters VIII. and IX. Here I will simply point out a few results, with the main steps of how they are deduced, and leave checking them to the reader, if the reader wishes.

In the problem just mentioned, we find the relation connecting \(x\), \(y\), \(w\), and \(z\) to be
\[xw + x\bar{w}y + x\bar{w}\bar{y}\bar{z} = 0.\]
That is, matter is never a necessary being while motion exists; nor while motion does not exist and gravitation is necessarily present; nor while motion does not exist, gravitation is not necessarily present, and the world is not merely material. If we now write \(u = wz\), and then eliminate the symbols \(w\) and \(z\) by the general problem of Chap. IX., we find
\[xu + xy\bar{u} = 0,\]
and from this
\[u=\frac{1}{0}xy+0x\bar{y}+\frac{0}{0}\bar{x};\]
and so
\[wz=\frac{0}{0}\bar{x}\text{ with }xy=0.\]
That is: If the world is merely material, and without a presiding intelligence, and at the same time motion exists, matter is not a necessary being.

Now we have already shown that _if motion exists, matter is not a necessary being_. So this conclusion actually tells us less than we had already found (by inference) to be true. All the same, it is the proper and complete answer to the question that was asked, which was simply to find the consequences of a certain complex hypothesis.

4. So even from the limited set of premises before us, it would be easy to deduce a great variety of further inferences, taking as the given condition any combination of the elementary propositions we like. If the condition is inconsistent with the premises, the form of the solution will show it: the method will assign the value 0 to the combination of symbols that expresses the proposed condition. If, on the other hand, the condition puts no restriction at all on the propositions whose relation we are looking for, so that every combination of those propositions is equally possible, the form of the solution will show that too. Here are examples of each case.

Suppose we look, in the usual way, for the consequences of the condition that _matter is a necessary being_ and at the same time _motion exists_, as they bear on the propositions _The world is merely material, and without a presiding intelligence_, and _Gravitation is necessarily present_. We get the equation
\[xw = 0,\]
which shows that the proposed condition is inconsistent with the premises, and so can never be met.

Now suppose we look for the consequences of the condition that _Matter is not a necessary being_ and at the same time _Motion does exist_, with respect to the same elements as above, namely _the absence of a presiding intelligence_ and _the necessity of gravitation_. We get the following result,
\[(1-x)w =\frac{0}{0}yz +\frac{0}{0}y(1-z) +\frac{0}{0}(1-y)z +\frac{0}{0}(1-y)(1-z),\]
in which every one of the four constituents carries the indefinite coefficient, so that each of them may or may not hold. Read literally, it says this:

If matter is not a necessary being, and motion exists, then either the world is merely material and without a presiding intelligence, and gravitation is necessary; or one of these two holds without the other; or both fail to be true. So of the four possible combinations, one of which must be true and only one of which can be true, we are told that any one may be true. Such a result is a truism, a merely _necessary_ truth. Still, it is the only answer that can be given to the question asked.

I do not think I need to defend these applications against the charge of laborious trifling. To build confidence in general principles and methods, it may be necessary to go fairly fully into details that are useless in themselves. When that end has been reached in the subject of this inquiry, let everything that helped to reach it, but later proved unnecessary, be forgotten.
