Chapter 2: Signs and Their Laws

1. Language is a tool of human reason, not just a way of expressing thought. That much is generally agreed. In this chapter I want to ask what it is that makes language able to serve the most important of our intellectual powers in this way. Along the way we will look at how language is built, treating it as a system designed for a purpose. We will pick out its elements, try to settle how they relate to and depend on one another, and ask how each of them, as a working part of the system, helps it reach the end it serves.

We need not, for these inquiries, enter the famous old debate of the schools: is language an _essential_ tool of reasoning, or could we reason without it? I take that question to lie outside the plan of this book, and here is why. The business of science is to investigate laws. We may regard signs as standing for things and their relations, or as standing for the conceptions and operations of the human intellect. Either way, in studying the laws of signs we are in effect studying the laws of reasoning as they show themselves. If the two inquiries differ at all, the difference does not touch the scientific statement of formal laws, which is what this part of the book is after. It touches only the way the results are presented to the mind. When we investigate the laws of signs _à posteriori_, from the outside, what we examine directly is language and the rules that govern its use. When we take the inner processes of thought as our direct subject, we appeal more immediately to our own consciousness. But in both cases, as we will find, the results come out formally the same. Nor could we easily believe that the countless languages and dialects of the earth would have kept so much in common, over so many ages, unless their agreement had some deep foundation in the laws of the mind itself.

2. All language is made of signs, or symbols. Words are signs. Sometimes words are said to stand for things. Sometimes they stand for the operations by which the mind combines the simple notions of things into complex conceptions. Sometimes they express relations of action, of being acted on, or of mere quality, which we see among the objects of our experience. Sometimes they express the feelings of the mind that perceives. But words, though they serve as signs in these ways and others, are not the only signs we can use. Arbitrary marks that speak only to the eye, and arbitrary sounds or gestures that speak to some other sense, are signs just as much, provided that what they stand for is defined and understood. In the mathematical sciences, letters and the symbols \(+\), \(-\), \(=\), and so on, are used as signs. There the word “sign” is usually kept for the second kind, the symbols that stand for operations or relations, rather than for the letters, which stand for the elements of number and quantity. What a sign really means does not depend on the particular shape it takes, and neither do the laws that govern its use. In this book, however, we are dealing with written signs, and the word “sign” will be used for these alone. The essential properties of signs are listed in the following definition.

_Definition_.—A sign is an arbitrary mark, having a fixed interpretation, and susceptible of combination with other signs in subjection to fixed laws dependent upon their mutual interpretation.

In plainer words: a sign is a mark chosen at will, which has a fixed meaning, and which can be combined with other signs according to fixed laws, laws that depend on what the signs mean.

3. Let us take the parts of this definition one at a time.

(1.) First, a sign is an _arbitrary_ mark. It clearly makes no difference which word or token we attach to a given idea, as long as the attachment, once made, is kept. The Romans said “civitas” for what we call a “state.” But they and we could just as well have used any other word for the same conception. In fact nothing in the nature of language would stop us from using a single letter in that sense. If we did, the laws governing the letter would be essentially the same as the laws governing “civitas” in Latin and “state” in English, at least so far as those words are governed by general principles common to all languages.

(2.) Second, each sign must have a fixed meaning throughout any one discussion or chain of reasoning. The need for this is obvious; it seems to come from the very nature of the subject. There is, however, a dispute about exactly what words or symbols stand for when they are used as names in reasoning. Some hold that they stand only for the conceptions of the mind; others, that they stand for things. The question matters little here, since its answer cannot change the laws by which signs are used. My own view is this. In reasoning, signs take the place of the mind's conceptions and operations and do their work. Those conceptions and operations represent things and the connections and relations of things, and so the signs represent things with their connections and relations too. And because signs take the place of the mind's conceptions and operations, they are subject to the laws of those conceptions and operations. This view will be explained more fully in the next chapter. Here it serves to explain the third part of the definition of a sign, namely, that its combinations are subject to fixed laws, and that these laws depend on what it means.

4. The following Proposition analyses and classifies the signs by which reasoning is carried on.

Proposition I.

Everything language does as a tool of reasoning can be done by a system of signs made of the following elements:

First, _literal symbols, such as \(x\), \(y\), and so on, standing for things as objects of our conceptions_.

Second, signs of operation, such as \(+\), \(-\), \(\times\), standing for those operations of the mind by which conceptions of things are combined or taken apart to form new conceptions out of the same elements.

Third, _the sign of identity, \(=\)_.

These symbols of logic obey definite laws in their use. Those laws partly agree with, and partly differ from, the laws of the matching symbols in algebra.

Let us take this as our test of the true elements of rational discourse: they should combine in the simplest forms and by the simplest laws, and by combining in this way they should produce every other form of language, known or conceivable. With that principle in hand, consider the following classification.

Class I.

5. Naming or describing signs: signs that give the name of a thing, or some quality or circumstance belonging to it.

To this class we can plainly assign the noun, proper or common, and the adjective. The two really differ in only one respect. The noun states outright that the individual thing or things it refers to exist; the adjective takes that existence for granted. If we attach to an adjective the subject everyone understands anyway, “being” or “thing,” it becomes in effect a noun, and for every essential purpose of reasoning the noun can replace it. Perhaps, in every shade of the mind's view, it is not quite the same to say “Water is a fluid thing” as to say “Water is fluid.” But for expressing the processes of reasoning the two are at least equivalent.

It is also clear that this class must include any sign used by convention for some circumstance or relation that would otherwise take many signs to spell out. The epithets of poetry are very often of this kind. They are usually compound adjectives, each doing the work of a description of many words. Homer's “deep-eddying ocean” packs a whole description into the single word βαθυδίνης. In the same way, any other description, whether aimed at the imagination or at the intellect, could by agreement be represented by a single sign. That sign would obey, in every essential point, the same laws as the adjective “good” or “great.” Joined to the subject “thing,” it would become in effect a noun, and a single noun could then carry the combined meaning of the thing and the quality.

6. Now, since a sign has been defined as an arbitrary mark, we are free to replace every sign of the kind just described by a letter. So let us agree to represent by a single letter, such as \(x\), the class of individuals to which a particular name or description applies. If the name is “men,” for instance, let \(x\) stand for “all men,” or the class “men.” By a class we usually mean a collection of individuals, each of which the particular name or description fits. In this book the term is stretched a little. It includes the case where just one individual answers to the name or description. It also includes the cases named by the words “nothing” and “universe,” which as classes must be understood to contain “no beings” and “all beings” respectively. Next, if an adjective such as “good” is used to describe, let a letter, say \(y\), stand for all the things the description “good” fits, that is, “all good things,” or the class “good things.” Let us further agree that the combination \(xy\) shall stand for the class of things to which the names or descriptions represented by \(x\) and \(y\) both apply at once. So if \(x\) alone stands for “white things” and \(y\) for “sheep,” let \(xy\) stand for “white sheep.” Likewise, if \(z\) stands for “horned things,” while \(x\) and \(y\) keep their meanings, let \(zxy\) stand for “horned white sheep,” that is, the collection of things to which the name “sheep” and the descriptions “white” and “horned” all apply together.

Let us now look at the laws that the symbols \(x\), \(y\), and so on, used in this sense, obey.

7. First, it is plain that in combinations like these, the order in which two symbols are written makes no difference. The expressions \(xy\) and \(yx\) both stand for the class of things to every member of which the names or descriptions \(x\) and \(y\) both apply. So we have
\[xy=yx. \qquad (1)\]

If \(x\) stands for white things and \(y\) for sheep, either side of this equation stands for the class of “white sheep.” We may form the conception in a different order, but the individual things it covers are the same. In the same way, if \(x\) stands for “estuaries” and \(y\) for “rivers,” the expressions \(xy\) and \(yx\) both stand, without distinction, for “rivers that are estuaries” or “estuaries that are rivers.” In ordinary language this combination joins two nouns, rather than a noun and an adjective as in the first example. Let a third symbol, \(z\), stand for the class of things to which the word “navigable” applies. Then any one of the expressions
\[zxy,\; zyx,\; xyz,\; \text{\&c.},\]
will stand for the class of “navigable rivers that are estuaries.”

Sometimes one descriptive term carries a hidden reference to another. Then all we need do is write that reference openly into its stated meaning, and the remarks above apply again. For instance, if \(x\) stands for “wise” and \(y\) for “counsellor,” we must say whether \(x\) means wisdom in the absolute sense or only wisdom in giving counsel. Once that is settled, the law \(xy = yx\) holds as before.

So we may use the symbols \(x\), \(y\), \(z\), and so on, in place of nouns, adjectives, and descriptive phrases, subject to two conditions. The first is a rule of interpretation: any expression in which several of these symbols are written together stands for all the objects or individuals to which their meanings all apply at once. The second is a law: the order in which the symbols follow each other makes no difference.

The rule of interpretation has now been illustrated enough. So I will not always bother to state the subject “things” when I give the meaning of a symbol that stands for an adjective. When I say, let \(x\) represent “good,” it will be understood that \(x\) means just “good” only when some other symbol supplies a subject for that quality; used alone, it means “good things.”

8. A few remarks can be added about the law just found. They will also apply, more or less, to some other laws to be derived later.

First, this law is a law of thought, not, strictly speaking, a law of things. A difference in the order of the qualities or attributes of an object, setting aside all questions of cause, is merely a difference in how we conceive it. The law (1) states as a general truth that the same thing can be conceived in different ways, and it says what that difference amounts to. It does nothing more.

Second, as a law of thought, it is actually worked out in a law of language, which is both the product and the tool of thought. Prose tends toward a uniform order. Yet even in prose, when several adjectives with a fixed meaning are applied to the same subject, the order in which they come makes no difference. And poetry owes much of its rich variety to extending this same lawful freedom to the noun as well. Milton's language is especially marked by this kind of variety. The noun often comes before the adjectives that qualify it, and it is often placed right in the middle of them. In the first few lines of his invocation to Light we meet examples like these:

	“_Offspring of heaven first-born_.”
	“The rising world of _waters dark and deep_.”
	“Bright effluence of _bright essence increate_.”

These inverted forms are not just the fruit of poetic license. They are the natural expression of a freedom that the deep laws of thought allow, but that ordinary speech, for convenience, does not use.

Third, the law expressed by (1) can be summed up by saying that the literal symbols \(x\), \(y\), \(z\), are _commutative, like the symbols of algebra_: their order can be swapped. In saying this, I do not claim that multiplication in algebra, whose fundamental law is expressed by the equation
\[xy = yx,\]
is in itself anything like the process of logical combination that \(xy\) has been made to stand for above. I claim only that if the arithmetical process and the logical process are written in the same way, their symbolic expressions will obey the same formal law. The evidence that they obey it is quite separate in the two cases.

9. The combination of two literal symbols in the form \(xy\) expresses the whole class of objects to which the names or qualities represented by \(x\) and \(y\) both apply. It follows that if the two symbols mean exactly the same thing, their combination expresses no more than either symbol alone. In that case we should have
\[xy = x.\]
But since \(y\) is supposed to mean the same as \(x\), we may put \(x\) in its place in this equation, and we get
\[xx = x.\]
that is, a class combined with itself gives that same class. Now in ordinary algebra the combination \(xx\) is written more briefly as \(x^2\). Let us adopt the same notation here. The way we write down a particular sequence of mental operations is just as arbitrary as the way we write down a single idea or operation (II. 3). In this notation, then, the equation above takes the form
\[x^2 = x, \qquad (2)\]
and it is, in fact, a second general law of the symbols by which names, qualities, or descriptions are represented.

The reader should keep in mind that, although in the earlier examples the symbols \(x\) and \(y\) were given meanings different from each other, nothing stops us from giving them exactly the same meaning. Clearly, the closer their actual meanings come to each other, the closer the class named by the combination \(xy\) comes to being the same as the class named by \(x\), and the same as the class named by \(y\). The case assumed in proving equation (2) is that of _absolute_ sameness of meaning. Language shows this law at work in practice. To say “good, good” about anything, though a clumsy and useless repetition, is the same as saying “good.” So “good, good” men are just “good” men. People do sometimes repeat words in this way to heighten a quality or strengthen a statement. But that effect is secondary and a matter of convention; it is not rooted in the inner relations of language and thought. Most of the operations we see in nature, or carry out ourselves, have a greater effect when repeated. This prepares us to expect the same thing in language, and even to repeat ourselves when we want to speak with emphasis. But neither in strict reasoning nor in exact speech is there any real justification for the practice.

10. We now turn to another class of the signs of speech, and to the laws connected with their use.

Class II.

11. Signs of the mental operations by which we gather parts into a whole, or separate a whole into its parts.

We can do more than form conceptions of objects marked out by names, qualities, or circumstances that apply to each individual in the group we are thinking about. We can also form the combined conception of a group of objects made up of smaller groups, each named or described on its own. For this we use the conjunctions “and,” “or,” and so on. “Trees and minerals” and “barren mountains, or fertile vales” are examples of this kind. Strictly speaking, when the words “and” or “or” are placed between the terms describing two or more classes of objects, they imply that those classes are entirely separate, so that no member of one is found in another. In this respect, and in every other, the words “and” and “or” are like the sign \(+\) in algebra, and their laws are the same. So the expression “men and women,” setting aside conventional meanings, is equivalent to “women and men.” Let \(x\) stand for “men” and \(y\) for “women,” and let \(+\) stand for “_and_” and “_or_.” Then we have
\[x + y = y + x, \qquad (3)\]
an equation that would hold just as well if \(x\) and \(y\) stood for _numbers_ and \(+\) were the sign of arithmetical addition.

Let the symbol \(z\) stand for the adjective “European.” Since it comes to the same thing to say “European men and women” as to say “European men and European women,” we have
\[z\left(x + y\right) = zx + zy. \qquad (4)\]
This equation, too, would hold just as well if \(x\), \(y\), and \(z\) were symbols of number and writing two letters side by side meant their algebraic product, just as, in the logical meaning given earlier, it means the class of objects to which both of the joined descriptions apply.

Those are the laws governing the sign \(+\), used here for the positive operation of gathering parts into a whole. But the very idea of an operation that makes some positive change seems to suggest the idea of an opposite, negative operation, one that undoes what the first has done. We cannot think it possible to gather parts into a whole without also thinking it possible to separate a part from a whole. In ordinary speech we express this operation by the word “except,” as in “All men _except_ Asiatics,” or “All states _except_ those which are monarchical.” Here it is understood that the things excepted are part of the things they are excepted from. Just as we wrote the operation of gathering with the sign \(+\), so we may write this negative operation with \(-\), the minus sign. So if \(x\) stands for men and \(y\) for Asiatics, that is, Asiatic men, then the conception “All men except Asiatics” will be written \(x - y\), the class of men with the Asiatic men taken out. And if \(x\) stands for “states” and \(y\) for the descriptive property “having a monarchical form,” then the conception “All states except those which are monarchical” will be written \(x - xy\): from the states, take away the states that are monarchical.

Editor's note: Boole's plus sign is not the modern "or". He uses it only to join classes that have no members in common, so that "_x_ plus _y_" means things that are either x or y but never both, and he allows "_x_ minus _y_" only when all the _y_'s are already among the _x_'s. Modern Boolean algebra, following Jevons in 1864 and later Peirce, reads plus as an inclusive "or" (either one, or both) and replaces subtraction with the complement, "not-_y_". Boole's results still come out right in his own system, but a reader who knows modern notation should not read his plus as "or".

For every _essential_ purpose of reasoning it makes no difference whether we mention the excepted cases first or last. In the same way, it makes no difference in what order we write a series of terms, some of which carry the sign \(-\). So we have, as in ordinary algebra,
\[x-y = -y + x. \qquad (5)\]
Still letting \(x\) stand for the class “men” and \(y\) for “Asiatics,” let \(z\) stand for the adjective “white.” Now to apply the adjective “white” to the group of men described by the phrase “Men except Asiatics” is the same as to say “White men, except white Asiatics.” So we have
\[z\left(x-y\right) = zx-zy. \qquad (6)\]
This too agrees with the laws of ordinary algebra.

Equations (4) and (6) can be seen as two cases of a single general law: the literal symbols \(x\), \(y\), \(z\), and so on, are distributive in their operation. The general fact this law expresses is this. Suppose some quality or circumstance is ascribed to all the members of a group, where the group was formed by gathering smaller groups together or by excluding some of them. The resulting conception is the same as if the quality or circumstance had first been ascribed to the members of each smaller group, and the gathering or excluding done afterwards. Whatever is said of the members of the whole is said of the members of all its parts, however those parts are put together.

Class III.

12. Signs that express relation, and by which we form propositions.

All verbs could properly be put in this class. But for the purposes of logic it is enough to include only the verb “to be,” in the form _is_ or _are_. Every other verb can be broken down into this element plus one of the signs of Class I. Those signs express quality or circumstance of every kind, so they can express the subject's relation of acting or being acted on, whether in the past, present, or future. Thus the proposition “Cæsar conquered the Gauls” can be broken down into “Cæsar is he who conquered the Gauls.” My reason for this analysis is as follows. Unless we understand what it means to have conquered the Gauls, that is, what the expression “One who conquered the Gauls” means, we cannot understand the sentence. So that expression is truly an element of the sentence. “Cæsar” is another element. And one more is needed: the copula _is_, to show the connection between the two. I do not claim that this is the only way to view the relation expressed by the proposition “Cæsar conquered the Gauls.” I claim only that this analysis is correct from the particular point of view we have taken, and that it is enough for the purposes of logical deduction. It may be noted that the passive and future participles of the Greek language imply the principle asserted here, namely, that the sign _is_ or _are_ can be treated as an element of every personal verb.

13. This sign, _is_ or _are_, can be represented by the symbol \(=\). Next we consider the laws, or as they would usually be called, the axioms, that this symbol brings with it.

Take the proposition “The stars are the suns and the planets,” and let stars be represented by \(x\), suns by \(y\), and planets by \(z\). Then we have
\[x=y + z. \qquad (7)\]
Now if it is true that the stars are the suns and the planets, it follows that the stars, except the planets, are suns. That gives the equation
\[x - z = y, \qquad (8)\]
which must therefore follow from (7). So a term \(z\) has been moved from one side of an equation to the other by changing its sign. This agrees with the algebraic rule of transposition.

But rather than dwell on particular cases, we can state the general axioms at once:

First, if equal things are added to equal things, the wholes are equal.

Second, if equal things are taken from equal things, the remainders are equal.

It follows that we may add or subtract equations, and use the rule of transposition just given, exactly as in ordinary algebra.

Again: suppose two classes of things, \(x\) and \(y\), are identical; that is, every member of the one is a member of the other. Then the members of the one class that have a given property \(z\) will be identical with the members of the other that have the same property \(z\). So if we have the equation
\[x = y;\]
then whatever class or property \(z\) may stand for, we also have
\[zx = zy.\]
This is the same in form as the algebraic law: if both sides of an equation are multiplied by the same quantity, the products are equal.

In the same way it can be shown that if the matching sides of two equations are multiplied together, the resulting equation is true.

14. Here, however, the likeness between this system and algebra, as algebra is usually stated, seems to stop. Suppose it is true that the members of a class \(x\) that have a certain property \(z\) are identical with the members of a class \(y\) that have the same property \(z\). It does not follow that the members of the class \(x\), all of them, are identical with the members of the class \(y\). So we cannot infer from the equation
\[zx = zy,\]
that is, “the _x_'s that are _z_ are the same as the _y_'s that are _z_,” that the equation
\[x = y\]
is also true. In other words, the algebraists' axiom that both sides of an equation may be divided by the same quantity has no formal equivalent here. I say no _formal equivalent_ because, in keeping with the general spirit of these inquiries, we do not even try to decide whether the mental operation represented by removing a logical symbol \(z\) from a combination \(zx\) is in itself like division in arithmetic. That mental operation is in fact what is usually called abstraction, and it will appear later that its laws depend on the laws already derived in this chapter. What has been shown now is that nothing among those laws matches, in _form_, a commonly accepted axiom of algebra.

But a little thought will show that even in ordinary algebra this axiom is not as general as the other axioms we have looked at. Deducing the equation \(x = y\) from the equation \(zx = zy\) is valid only when we know that \(z\) is not equal to \(0\). So if the value \(z = 0\) is allowed in the algebraic system, the axiom stops applying, and the analogy shown before remains, at least, unbroken.

15. However, there is little point, except as a matter of speculation, in tracing such likenesses with the symbols of quantity in general. We have seen (II. 9) that the symbols of logic obey the special law
\[x^2 = x.\]
Now among the symbols of number there are only two, namely \(0\) and \(1\), that obey the same formal law. We know that \(0^2 = 0\), and that \(1^2 = 1\); and the equation \(x^2 = x\), taken as an equation of algebra, has no roots other than \(0\) and \(1\). So instead of measuring how far the symbols of logic agree in form with the symbols of number in general, the more natural step is to compare them with symbols of quantity _admitting only of the values \(0\) and \(1\)_. Let us imagine, then, an algebra in which the symbols \(x\), \(y\), \(z\), etc. can take the values \(0\) and \(1\), either one, and these values only. The laws, the axioms, and the processes of such an algebra will be identical, through their whole range, with the laws, the axioms, and the processes of an algebra of logic. Only a difference of interpretation will separate them. This is the principle on which the method of this book is built.

16. It remains to show that the parts of ordinary language not yet considered in this chapter either break down into the same elements we have considered, or serve those elements by helping to define them more precisely.

We have already dealt with the noun, the adjective, and the verb, along with the particles _and_ and _except_. The pronoun can be treated as a particular form of the noun or the adjective. The adverb modifies the meaning of the verb but does not change its nature. Prepositions help to express circumstance or relation, and so they add precision and detail to the meaning of the literal symbols. The conjunctions _if_, _either_, _or_, are used mainly to express relations among propositions. It will be shown later that those same relations can be fully expressed by elementary symbols that are similar in meaning, and identical in form and law, to the symbols whose use and meaning have been explained in this chapter. As for any remaining elements of speech, examination will show that they do one of two things. Either they give a more definite meaning to the terms of discourse, and so enter into the interpretation of the literal symbols already considered; or they express some emotion or feeling that goes with the utterance of a proposition, and so do not belong to the province of the understanding, which is our only concern here. Experience in using this classification will show that it is sufficient.
