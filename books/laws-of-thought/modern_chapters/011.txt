Chapter 10: Of the Conditions of a Perfect Method

1. We have now discussed primary propositions at length, and we are about to take up secondary propositions. This point of transition between the two great divisions of the science of logic is a good place to pause. While we look back over some of the steps we have taken, we can ask what it is that makes a method perfect in a subject like the one we have been studying. I do not mean only the perfection that lies in power. I mean also the perfection that rests on our sense of what is fitting and beautiful. A careful analysis of the question would probably lead us to something like this conclusion. A perfect method should not only be efficient, doing well the work it was designed to do. It should also show a certain unity and harmony in all its parts and processes. This ideal would be most fully reached if the very forms of the method pointed back to the fundamental principles they are built on, and, if possible, to the one fundamental principle beneath them all. When we apply these thoughts to the science of reasoning, it may be wise to look beyond the analytical processes alone. We should ask what is best not only in the manner or form of deduction, but also in the system of data, or premises, from which the deduction is made.

2. As far as power alone goes, there is no doubt that the first of the methods developed in Chapter VIII. is, within its proper range, a perfect one. Bringing in arbitrary constants frees us from depending on the forms of the premises, and from any conditions among the equations that represent them. But it seems to bring in a foreign element. It is a more laborious form of solution than the second method of reduction proved in the same chapter, and a less elegant one too. There are, however, conditions under which that second method takes on a more perfect form than it usually has. Suppose we made the one fundamental condition, expressed by the equation
\[x(1-x) = 0,\]
the universal pattern for every form, so that every expression we work with obeyed the law of duality just as a single class symbol does. That would give a unity of character to both the processes and the results which could not be had in any other way. If brevity or convenience were the only things that made a method valuable, adopting such a principle would bring no advantage. To force this character onto every step of a solution would sometimes take no small labour of preliminary reduction. Still, it is interesting to know that it can be done. It is even of some importance to know the conditions under which a solution of this kind would turn up on its own. Some of these points are considered in this chapter.

Proposition I.

3. To reduce any equation among logical symbols to the form \(V=0\), in which \(V\) satisfies the law of duality,
\[V(1 - V) = 0.\]
It was shown in Chap. V. Prop. 4, that this condition holds whenever \(V\) is the sum of a series of constituents. It is also clear from Prop. 2, Chap. VI. that certain equations are all equivalent. These are the equations which, when we move everything to one side to get the form \(V=0\) and then develop the left-hand side, produce the same series of constituents with coefficients that are not zero. The particular numbers those coefficients happen to be make no difference.

So the aim of this Proposition can always be reached in three steps. Bring all the terms of an equation over to the left-hand side. Expand that side fully. Then, in the result, change every coefficient that is not zero into unity (that is, 1), leaving alone those that are 1 already.

But developing functions that contain many symbols leads to expressions inconveniently long. It is worth showing, then, how this source of complexity can be avoided in the only cases that actually come up in practice.

The great primary forms of equations were discussed in Chapter VIII. They are—
\[\begin{aligned} X &= vY, \\ X &= Y, \\ vX &= vY. \end{aligned}\]
We have seen that whenever the conditions \(X(1-X) = 0, Y(1-Y) = 0\), hold, that is, whenever _X_ and _Y_ each obey the law of duality, the first two of these equations lead us to the forms
\[\begin{aligned} X(1-Y) &= 0, \qquad (1) \\ X(1-Y) &+ Y(1-X) = 0; \qquad (2) \end{aligned}\]
the first saying that there is nothing which is _X_ and not _Y_, the second that there is nothing which is _X_ and not _Y_, and nothing which is _Y_ and not _X_. Under the same circumstances it can be shown that the last of them gives
\[v( X(1-Y) + Y(1-X) ) = 0; \qquad (3)\]
and all of these results clearly satisfy, on their left-hand sides, the condition
\[V(1-V) = 0.\]

These are the forms and conditions in which the equations of a properly expressed logical system actually present themselves. So it is always possible, by the method above, to bring them under the required law. But although each separate equation may satisfy the law this way, their equivalent sum (VIII. 4) may not. It remains to show how the condition can be imposed on the sum too.

Let us write the equation formed by adding together the separate reduced equations of the system as
\[v + v' + v'', \&c. = 0, \qquad (4)\]
an equation which by itself is equivalent to the whole system it came from. We suppose \(v, v', v''\), &c. to be class terms (IX. 1) satisfying the conditions
\[v(1-v) = 0, v'(1-v') = 0, \&c.\]
Now the full interpretation of (4) would be found by developing its left-hand side with respect to all the elementary symbols \(x\), \(y\), &c. that it contains, and setting equal to 0 every constituent whose coefficient is not zero. In other words, we would set equal to 0 every constituent found in any one of \(v\), \(v'\), \(v''\), &c. Those constituents fall into groups. First come those found in \(v\). Second, those not found in \(v\) but found in \(v'\). Third, those found in neither \(v\) nor \(v'\) but found in \(v''\). And so on. So they will be exactly the constituents found in the expression
\[v + (1 - v)v' + (1 - v)(1 - v')v''+ \&c., \qquad (5)\]
an expression in which no constituent appears twice, and which plainly satisfies the law \(V(1-V)=0\).

For example, suppose we had the expression
\[(1 - t) + v + (1 - z) + tzw,\]
in which the terms \(1-t\), \(1-z\) are bracketed to show that each is to be taken as a single class term. Following (5), we would reduce it to an expression satisfying the condition \(V(1-V)=0\) in three moves. Multiply every term after the first by \(t\). Then multiply every term after the second by \(1-v\). Last, multiply the term that remains after the third by \(z\). The result is
\[1 - t + tv + t(1 - v)(1 - z) + t(1 - v)zw. \qquad (6)\]

4. So all logical equations can be reduced to the form \(V=0\), with \(V\) satisfying the law of duality. But it would clearly be a higher degree of perfection if equations always came in this form without any preparation at all. Better still if they not only showed this form when first stated, but kept it intact after the additions we must make to reduce a system of equations to a single equivalent one. That they do not do this on their own is not really a defect of the method. It comes from the fact that our premises are not always complete, accurate, and independent. They are not complete when they involve material relations (as distinguished from formal ones) that are left unstated. They are not accurate when they imply relations that were not intended. Let us set those two points aside, since they concern us less here, and consider in what sense premises may fail to be independent.

5. A system of propositions may be called independent when no conclusion that can be drawn from one portion of the system can also be drawn from any other portion of it. Suppose the equations representing those propositions are all reduced to the form
\[V=0,\]
then this condition means that no constituent which can be made to appear in the development of one function \(V\) of the system can be made to appear in the development of any other function \(V'\) of the same system. When the condition fails, the equations of the system are not independent. This can happen in various ways. Let all the equations satisfy the law of duality on their left-hand sides. Then if a positive term \(x\) appears in the expansion of one equation, and a term \(xy\) in the expansion of another, the equations are not independent. The term \(x\) can be developed further into \(xy + x ( 1 - y)\), and so the equation
\[xy=0\]
is contained in both equations of the system, that is, both of them say that there is nothing which is both _x_ and _y_. Again, let a term \(xy\) appear in one equation and a term \(xz\) in another. Both can be developed so as to give the shared constituent \(xyz\). Other cases are easy to imagine in which premises that look quite independent at first sight are not really so. Whenever equations of the form \(V = 0\) are not truly independent in this way, then even though each one may satisfy the law of duality,
\[V(1 - V) = 0,\]
the equivalent equation we get by adding them together will not satisfy that condition, unless enough reductions have been carried out by the method of this chapter. When, on the other hand, the equations of a system both satisfy this law and are independent of one another, their sum will satisfy the same law. I have dwelt on these points longer than would otherwise be necessary. I think it important to form for ourselves, and to keep before us in all our investigations, the pattern of an ideal perfection, the goal and the guide of future efforts. In inquiries of this kind, the chief aim in improving the method should be to make the transformation of equations easier, as far as brevity allows, so that the fundamental condition spoken of above becomes universal.

In connexion with this subject the following Propositions deserve attention.

Proposition II.

If the left-hand side of any equation \(V = 0\) satisfies the condition \(V(1-V) = 0\), and if the expression of any symbol \(t\) of that equation is found as a developed function of the other symbols, then the coefficients of the expansion can take only the forms \(1\), \(0\), \(\displaystyle\frac{0}{0}\), \(\displaystyle\frac{1}{0}\).

For if we expand the equation with respect to \(t\), we get
\[Et + E'(1 - t), \qquad (1)\]
where \(E\) and \(E'\) are what \(V\) becomes when \(t\) is changed in it first to \(1\) and then to \(0\). So \(E\) and \(E'\) will themselves satisfy the conditions
\[E(1 - E) = 0,\quad E'(1 - E') = 0. \qquad (2)\]
Now (1) gives
\[t = \frac{E'}{E' - E},\]
and the right-hand side of this is to be expanded as a function of the remaining symbols. Clearly the only numerical values that \(E\) and \(E'\) can take in calculating the coefficients are \(1\) and \(0\). So only the following cases can arise:

First, \(E' = 1\), \(E = 1\), then \(\displaystyle\frac{E'}{E'-E} = \frac{1}{0}\).

Second, \(E' = 1\), \(E = 0\), then \(\displaystyle\frac{E'}{E'-E} = 1\).

Third, \(E' = 0\), \(E = 1\), then \(\displaystyle\frac{E'}{E'-E} = 0\).

Fourth, \(E' = 0\), \(E = 0\), then \(\displaystyle\frac{E'}{E'-E} = \frac{0}{0}\).

This makes the truth of the Proposition plain.

6. It is worth noting that the forms \(1\), \(0\), and \(\frac{0}{0}\) appear in the solution of equations whether or not the condition \(V(1 - V) = 0\) holds. The coefficient \(\frac{1}{0}\) is different. The terms that carry this coefficient when the condition is satisfied may take any value other than the three values \(1\), \(0\), and \(\frac{0}{0}\) when it is not. It is allowed, and it would make for uniformity, to change any coefficient of a development that does not come in one of the four forms named in this Proposition into \(\frac{1}{0}\). We treat this as the proper symbol to show that the term it is attached to must be set equal to \(0\). I shall often do this.

Proposition III.

7. The result of eliminating any symbols \(x\), \(y\), &c. from an equation \(V=0\), whose left-hand side identically satisfies the law of duality,
\[V(1-V) = 0,\]
can be obtained by developing the given equation with respect to the other symbols, and setting equal to \(0\) the sum of those constituents whose coefficients in the expansion are equal to unity.

Suppose the given equation \(V = 0\) involves only three symbols, \(x\), \(y\), and \(t\), and that \(x\) and \(y\) are to be eliminated. Let the development of the equation with respect to \(t\) be
\[At + B(1-t)=0, \qquad (1)\]
with \(A\) and \(B\) free of the symbol \(t\).

By Chap. IX. Prop. 3, the result of eliminating \(x\) and \(y\) from the given equation will have the form
\[Et+ E'(1 -t) = 0, \qquad (2)\]
in which \(E\) is the result of eliminating the symbols \(x\) and \(y\) from the equation \(A = 0\), and \(E'\) the result of eliminating them from the equation \(B = 0\).

Now \(A\) and \(B\) must satisfy the condition
\[A (1 - A) = 0, B(1 - B) = 0\]
So \(A\) (we look at this coefficient alone for now) will be either 0, or 1, or a single constituent, or the sum of some of the constituents that involve the symbols \(x\) and \(y\). If \(A = 0\), clearly \(E = 0\). If \(A\) is a single constituent, or the sum of only some of the constituents involving \(x\) and \(y\), then \(E\) will be 0 as well. The reason is that the _full_ development of \(A\) with respect to \(x\) and \(y\) will contain terms with zero coefficients, and \(E\) is the product of all the coefficients. So when \(A = 1\), \(E\) is equal to \(A\), but in every other case \(E\) is equal to 0. In the same way, when \(B = 1\), \(E'\) is equal to \(B\), but in every other case \(E'\) is zero. So the expression (2) will consist of that part of (1), if there is any, in which the coefficients \(A\), \(B\) are unity. And this reasoning is general. Suppose, for instance, that \(V\) involved the symbols \(x\), \(y\), \(z\), \(t\), and that we had to eliminate \(x\) and \(y\). Then if the development of \(V\) with respect to \(z\) and \(t\) were
\[zt + xz(1 - t) + y (1 - z) t + (1 - z) (1 - t),\]
the result we want would be
\[zt + (1 - z) (1 - t) = 0,\]
this being the part of the development whose coefficients are unity.

Hence, if from any system of equations we derive a single equivalent equation \(V = 0\), with \(V\) satisfying the condition
\[V(1 - V) = 0,\]
the ordinary processes of elimination can be dropped entirely, and the single process of development can take their place.

8. There may be no practical advantage in the method pointed out here. But it has a theoretical unity and completeness that make it worth attention, and so I shall give a later chapter (XIV.) to illustrating it. The progress of applied mathematics has offered other striking examples of whole systems of problems or equations being brought under the rule of some central law that runs through all of them.

9. From what has gone before, we can see that there is one class of propositions for which none of the special devices of preparation above are needed. It is the class marked by the following conditions:

First, That the propositions are of the ordinary kind, the kind implied by the copula _is_ or _are_, with the predicates particular.

Secondly, That the terms of the proposition are meaningful without assuming any unstated relation among the elements that go into expressing those terms.

Thirdly, That the propositions are independent.

We may, if such speculation is not altogether idle, allow ourselves a guess. These may be the conditions that language would obey, as an instrument of expression and of thought, in the hands of unerring beings who said simply what they meant, without holding anything back on the one hand and without repeating themselves on the other. Considered both in relation to the idea of a perfect language and in relation to the processes of an exact method, these conditions deserve the student's attention equally.
