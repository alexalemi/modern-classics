Chapter 3: Derivation of the Laws

1. The object of science, in the proper sense of the word, is the knowledge of laws and relations. One of the most important conditions of scientific progress is the ability to tell what is essential to that end from what is only accidentally attached to it. I say _tell apart_, because a steady devotion to science does not require us to turn our attention away completely from other speculations, often metaphysical ones, with which science is frequently bound up. Take questions like these: whether there is some underlying ground that sustains the phenomena we observe; whether causes are real; whether it is proper to speak as if one state of things leads to the next by some _operation_. Questions of this kind may have deep interest and meaning in relation to science without being, in their essence, scientific. In fact it is hardly possible to state the conclusions of natural science without borrowing the language of these ideas. Nor does this necessarily cause any practical trouble. Some people believe that there is more to the relation of cause and effect than a fixed order in which one thing follows another; some refuse to believe it. Both sides read the conclusions of physical astronomy in exactly the same way. But they agree only because they recognise a shared element of scientific truth, one that does not depend on their particular views about what causation is.

2. If this distinction matters in physical science, it deserves even more attention in the science of the powers of the intellect. For the questions this science raises are almost bound to get mixed up, at least in how they are expressed, with ways of thinking and speaking that betray a metaphysical origin. The idealist would state the laws of reasoning in one form; the sceptic, if he were true to his principles, in another. Some people regard the phenomena we are studying here as nothing more than a series of _states_ of the thinking subject, with no causal link between them. Others trace them to the _operations_ of an active intelligence. If each side were consistent, they would state those laws differently too. A different way of classifying the faculties of the mind would produce differences of the same kind. Amid so much variety, some of it only apparent and some of it real, I want to assert one principle as our only firm and stable ground. It is this: if the laws in question really are drawn from observation, then they have a real existence as laws of the human mind, whatever metaphysical theory their wording may seem to involve. They contain an element of truth that no later criticism of the nature of the mind's operations, or even of whether those operations are real, can essentially touch. Grant, if you like, that the mind is nothing but a succession of states of consciousness: a series of passing impressions, caused neither from outside nor from within, rising out of nothing and sinking back into nothing again. That is the last refinement of the sceptical intellect. Even so, the results that observation had led us to would remain true as laws of succession, or at least of a succession that has already happened. They would have to be translated into a language whose vocabulary had been stripped of every term like cause and effect, operation and subject, substance and attribute. But they would still be valid as scientific truths.

There is more. Any statement of the laws of thought that rests on actual observation must contain scientific elements that do not depend on metaphysical theories about the nature of the mind. So the practical use of those elements, in building a system or method of reasoning, must also be independent of metaphysical distinctions. For any practical application will rest on the scientific elements contained in the statement of the laws. In the same way, the practical conclusions of physical astronomy do not depend on any theory of what causes gravitation; they rest only on knowing its observed effects. And so, both in finding the laws of thought and in putting them to use once found, we are, for every truly scientific purpose, not concerned with whether any metaphysical speculation whatever is true or false.

3. Under these circumstances, the course I think best is to use the language of everyday speech as far as I can, without regard to any theory of the nature and powers of the mind that it might be thought to carry with it. For instance, it is ordinary usage to say that we talk with each other by communicating ideas, or conceptions, and that communicating them is the job of words. It is also ordinary usage to say that, when particular ideas or conceptions are presented to the mind, it has certain powers or faculties for dealing with them. By these it can fix its regard on some ideas and shut out others, or combine the given conceptions or ideas in various ways. These faculties or powers have been given different names, such as Attention, Simple Apprehension, Conception or Imagination, Abstraction, and so on. These names have not only supplied the titles of separate branches of the philosophy of the human mind; they have also passed into the common language of people. So whenever I have occasion to use these terms, I shall use them without implying that I accept the theory that the mind has such and such powers and faculties as distinct parts of its activity. Nor is it really necessary to ask whether these powers of the understanding exist separately or not. We may gather all these different titles under one general name, the _Operations_ of the human mind. We can define these operations as far as this book needs, and then try to express their ultimate laws. That is the general plan I shall follow, though now and then I will refer to the names that common agreement has given to the particular states or operations of the mind that come up along the way.

It will be most convenient to divide the more definite results of the investigation that follows into separate Propositions.

Proposition I.

4. To deduce the laws of the symbols of Logic from a consideration of those operations of the mind which are implied in the strict use of language as an instrument of reasoning.

In every discourse, whether it is the mind talking with its own thoughts or a person talking with others, there is a limit, assumed or stated, within which the subjects being talked about are confined. The most unrestricted discourse is the one in which our words are taken in the widest possible sense; for such words, the limits of discourse are as wide as those of the universe itself. But more often we confine ourselves to a smaller field. Sometimes, when we speak of men, we mean (without saying so) men only under certain circumstances and conditions: civilized men, for example, or men in the prime of life, or men under some other condition or relation. Now, whatever the extent of the field within which all the objects of our discourse are found, that field may properly be called the universe of discourse.

5. Furthermore, this universe of discourse is, in the strictest sense, the ultimate _subject_ of the discourse. Under the limits we have just described, the job of any name or descriptive term is not to call up in the mind the conception of every being or object it could apply to. It is to call up only those that exist within the assumed universe of discourse. If that universe of discourse is the actual universe of things, as it always is when our words are taken in their real and literal sense, then by men we mean _all men that exist_. But if the universe of discourse is limited by some understanding implied in advance, then we are speaking of men under the limit so introduced. In both cases the business of the word _men_ is to direct a certain operation of the mind: from the proper universe of discourse, we pick out, or fix upon, the individuals the word signifies.

6. The mental operation implied by using an adjective is of exactly the same kind. Suppose, for instance, that the universe of discourse is the actual Universe. The word _men_ tells us to select in our minds, from that Universe, all the beings the term "men" applies to. In just the same way the adjective "good," in the combination "good men," tells us to go further and select from the class of _men_ all those who have the further quality "good." And if another adjective were put in front of "good men," it would direct a further operation of the same kind, concerning whatever further quality it was chosen to express.

It is important to notice carefully what the operation described here really is, because it is conceivable that it might have been different. If the adjective were simply _attributive_, that is, if it simply attached a quality, then once a particular set of beings had been marked out by _men_, putting the adjective _good_ in front would tell us to attach the quality of goodness, in our minds, to every one of those beings. But that is not what the adjective really does. The operation we actually perform is one of _selection according to a prescribed principle or idea_. It does not matter here which faculties of the mind such an operation would be assigned to under the accepted classification of its powers. I suppose it would be thought to depend on two of them, Conception or Imagination, and Attention. To the first we might assign the forming of the general conception; to the second, the fixing of the mind's regard on those individuals, within the prescribed universe of discourse, that answer to the conception. But it seems quite possible that the power of Attention is nothing more than the power of keeping up the exercise of some other faculty of the mind. If so, we might properly assign the whole mental process just described to the single faculty of Imagination or Conception. The first step of the process would be the conception of the Universe itself, and each step after that would narrow, in a definite way, the conception so formed. Taking this view, I shall call each such step, or any definite combination of such steps, a _definite act of conception_. And I shall stretch this term to cover not only the conception of classes of objects named by particular words or by simple qualities, but also the combining of such conceptions in any way that the powers and limits of the human mind allow. In fact it covers any intellectual operation short of the one involved in building a sentence or proposition. We must now consider the general laws that such operations of the mind obey.

7. I shall now show that the laws found in the preceding chapter for the use of the literal symbols of Logic, which there were reached _à posteriori_, after the fact, from the structure of language, are really the laws of the definite mental operation just described. We begin our discourse with a certain understanding about the limits of its subject, that is, about the limits of its Universe. Every name, every descriptive term we use, directs the person we are speaking to to carry out a certain mental operation on that subject. That is how thought is communicated. But on this view each name or descriptive term is only the representative of an intellectual operation, and that operation comes first in the order of nature. So it is clear that the laws of the name or symbol must be derived ones. They must, in fact, come from the laws of the operation they represent. I shall now show that the laws of the symbol and the laws of the mental process are identical in expression.

8. Let us suppose, then, that the universe of our discourse is the actual universe, so that words are used in the full extent of their meaning. And let us consider the two mental operations implied by the words "white" and "men." The word "men" implies the operation of selecting in thought, from its subject, the universe, all men; and the resulting conception, _men_, becomes the subject of the next operation. The operation implied by the word "white" is that of selecting, from its subject, "men," all members of that class who are white. The final resulting conception is that of "white men." Now it is perfectly plain that if these operations had been performed in the reverse order, the result would have been the same. We may begin by forming the conception "_men_" and then, by a second act of the intellect, narrow it to "white men." Or we may begin by forming the conception "white objects" and then narrow it to those members of that class who are "men." As far as the result goes, it makes no difference at all. Clearly, the order of the mental processes would make no difference either if we put any other descriptive or naming terms in place of "white" and "men," provided only that their meaning was fixed and absolute. So the fact that the order of two successive acts of the faculty of Conception does not matter, where one act supplies the subject that the other works on, is a general condition of the use of that faculty. It is a law of the mind. And it is the real origin of the law of the literal symbols of Logic that is its formal expression, law (1) of Chap. II, the law that _xy_ is the same as _yx_.

9. It is just as clear that this mental operation is of such a kind that repeating it does not change its effect. Suppose that by one definite act of conception the attention has been fixed on men, and that by another exercise of the same faculty we narrow it to those of the race who are white. Then repeating the second mental act any further, narrowing the attention to white objects again, does not change in any way the conception we have reached, namely, that of white men. This too is an example of a general law of the mind, and its formal expression is the law ((2) Chap, II.) of the literal symbols, the law that _xx_ is the same as _x_.

10. Again, it is plain that from the conceptions of two distinct classes of things we can form the conception of the collection of things that the two classes make up together. And it obviously makes no difference in what order of position or of priority the two classes are presented to the mind. This is another general law of the mind, and its expression is found in (3) Chap. II, the law that _x_ + _y_ is the same as _y_ + _x_.

11. There is no need to carry this line of inquiry and comparison further. Enough illustration has been given to make the following two positions clear.

First, the operations by which the mind, using its power of imagination or conception, combines and modifies the simple ideas of things or qualities are subject to general laws, no less than those operations of the reason that work on truths and propositions.

Second, those laws are mathematical in their form, and they are actually worked out in the essential laws of human language. So the laws of the symbols of Logic can be deduced from a consideration of the operations of the mind in reasoning.

12. The rest of this chapter deals with questions about the law of thought whose expression is \(x^2 = x\) (II. 9). As has been implied (II. 15), this law is what marks off the operations of the mind in its ordinary discourse and reasoning from its operations when it is busy with the general algebra of quantity. An important part of the inquiry that follows will be to prove that the symbols \(0\) and \(1\) have a place among the symbols of Logic, and can be given a meaning there. But first it may be necessary to show how particular symbols like these can properly, and usefully, be used to represent quite separate systems of thought.

This propriety cannot rest on any shared meaning. Logic and Arithmetic (I use the second term in its widest sense, as the science of Number) are truly separate systems of thought, and properly speaking they have no subject in common. The one deals with the very conceptions of things; the other takes account only of their numerical relations. But the forms and methods of any system of reasoning depend directly on the laws the symbols obey, and only indirectly, through that link, on what the symbols mean. So it can be both proper and useful to use the same symbols in different systems of thought, provided they can be given meanings that make their formal laws identical and their use consistent. The ground for using them will then not be a shared meaning but the shared formal laws they obey in their respective systems. And that sharing of formal laws must be established on no other ground than careful observation and comparison of the results that are seen to follow, separately, from the meanings within each system under consideration.

These remarks explain the line of inquiry taken in the next Proposition. The literal symbols of Logic all obey, without exception, the law whose expression is \(x^2 = x\). Among the symbols of Number there are only two, \(0\) and \(1\), that satisfy this law. But each of these two symbols also obeys a law of its own in the system of numerical quantity. This suggests the question: what meanings must we give to the literal symbols of Logic so that these same special formal laws hold in the logical system too?

Proposition II.

13. To determine the logical value and significance of the symbols \(0\) and \(1\).

The symbol \(0\), as used in Algebra, satisfies the following formal law,
\[0 \times y = 0, \text{ or } 0y = 0, \qquad (1)\]
whatever _number_ \(y\) may stand for. For this formal law to hold in the system of Logic, we must give the symbol \(0\) a meaning such that the class represented by \(0y\) is identical with the class represented by \(0\), whatever the class \(y\) may be. A little thought will show that this condition is met if the symbol \(0\) stands for Nothing. Following an earlier definition, we may call Nothing a class. In fact, Nothing and Universe are the two limits of how far a class can extend. They are the limits of the possible meanings of general names, since no general name can refer to fewer individuals than Nothing contains, or to more than the Universe contains. Now, whatever the class \(y\) may be, the individuals it shares with the class "Nothing" are identical with those contained in the class "Nothing," for there are none. So by giving \(0\) the meaning Nothing, we satisfy law (1); and no other meaning satisfies it consistently with the perfectly general character of the class \(y\).

Second, in the system of Number the symbol \(1\) satisfies the following law, namely,
\[1 \times y = y, \text{ or } 1y = y,\]
whatever number \(y\) may stand for. Suppose this formal equation holds equally in the system of this book, in which \(1\) and \(y\) stand for classes. Then the symbol \(1\) must stand for a class such that all the individuals found in _any_ proposed class \(y\) are also all the individuals \(1y\) that the class \(y\) shares with the class represented by \(1\). A little thought will show here that the class represented by \(1\) must be "the Universe," since this is the only class in which _all_ the individuals that exist in _any_ class are found. So the meanings of the symbols \(0\) and \(1\) in the system of Logic are, respectively, _Nothing_ and _Universe_.

14. The idea of any class of objects, such as "men," brings to mind the idea of the opposite class, the beings that are not men. And the whole Universe is made up of these two classes together, since of every individual in it we may say either that it is a man or that it is not a man. So it becomes important to ask how such opposite names are to be expressed. That is the object of the following Proposition.

Proposition III.

If \(x\) represent any class of objects, then will \(1 - x\) represent the contrary or supplementary class of objects, that is, the class including all objects which are not comprehended in the class \(x\).

To make the idea clearer, let \(x\) stand for the class men, and, following the last Proposition, let us express the Universe by \(1\). Now if from the conception of the Universe, as made up of "men" and "not-men," we take away the conception of "men," what remains is the conception of the opposite class, "not-men." So the class "not-men" will be represented by \(1 - x\). And in general, whatever class of objects the symbol \(x\) stands for, the opposite class will be expressed by \(1 - x\), which reads as "the Universe, leaving out the _x_'s."

15. Strictly speaking, the following Proposition belongs to a later chapter of this book, the one devoted to _maxims_ or _necessary truths_. But the law of thought it concerns is so important that it seemed right to introduce it here.

Proposition IV.

That axiom of metaphysicians which is termed the principle of contradiction, and which affirms that it is impossible for any being to possess a quality, and at the same time not to possess it, is a consequence of the fundamental law of thought, whose expression is \(x^2 = x\).

Let us write this equation in the form
\[x - x^2 = 0,\]
from which we get
\[x\left(1-x\right) = 0; \qquad (1)\]
both of these steps being justified by the axiomatic laws of combination and transposition (II. 13). In words, the equation (1) says that the class of things that are both _x_ and not-_x_ is empty. To make the idea simpler, let us give the symbol \(x\) the particular meaning _men_. Then \(1 - x\) will represent the class of "not-men" (Prop. III.). Now the formal product of the expressions for two classes represents the class of individuals common to both (II. 6). So \(x\left(1 - x\right)\) will represent the class whose members are at once "men" and "not men," and the equation (1) thus expresses the principle _that a class whose members are at the same time men and not men does not exist_. In other words, _it is impossible for the same individual to be at the same time a man and not a man_. Now let us widen the meaning of the symbol \(x\) from "men" to any class of beings marked out by having any quality whatever. The equation (1) will then express that it is impossible for a being to have a quality and not to have that quality at the same time. But this is exactly the "principle of contradiction" that Aristotle described as the fundamental axiom of all philosophy. "It is impossible that the same quality should both belong and not belong to the same thing.…This is the most certain of all principles.…Wherefore they who demonstrate refer to this as an ultimate opinion. For it is by nature the source of all the other axioms."

Footnote: Τὸ γὰρ αὐτὸ ἅμα ὑπάρχειν τε καὶ μὴ ὑπάρχειν ἀδύνατον τῷ αὐτῷ καὶ κατὰ τὸ αὐτό… Αὕτη δὴ πασῶν ἐστὶ βεβαιοτάτη τῶν ἀρχῶν… Διὸ πάντες οἱ ἀποδεικνύντες εἰς ταύτην ἀνάγουσιν ἐσχάτην δόξαν· φύσει γὰρ ἀρχὴ καὶ τῶν ἄλλων ἀξιωμάτων αὕτη πάντων.—_Metaphysica_, III, 3.

I have brought in this interpretation not for its immediate value in the present system, but to illustrate a significant fact in the philosophy of the powers of the intellect. What has commonly been regarded as the fundamental axiom of metaphysics is only the consequence of a law of thought, and that law is mathematical in form. I also want to draw attention to the fact that the equation (1), which expresses this fundamental law of thought, is an equation of the second degree: _x_ appears in it squared. I will not speculate in this chapter about whether this has to be so by its very nature. But we may venture to say that if it were not so, the whole working of the understanding would be different from what it is. It is because the fundamental equation of thought is of the second degree that we analyse and classify by dividing things into pairs of opposites, or, as the technical term has it, by _dichotomy_ (cutting in two). Now if the equation in question had been of the third degree, and could still be interpreted as such, the mind's division of things would have had to be threefold. We would have had to proceed by a kind of _trichotomy_ (cutting in three). With the faculties we actually have, we cannot properly conceive what that would really be like; but we could still investigate its laws as an object of intellectual speculation.

Footnote: Someone might object here that if the equation \(x^2=x\) exists, then so must the equation \(x^3=x\), which is of the third degree, and ask whether that equation does not point to a process of _trichotomy_. The answer is that the equation \(x^3=x\) cannot be interpreted in the system of logic. For if we write it in either of the forms \[\begin{aligned} x\left(1-x\right)\left(1+x\right) &= 0, \qquad (2) \\ x\left(1-x\right)\left(-1-x\right) &= 0, \qquad (3) \end{aligned}\] we see that its interpretation, if it has one at all, must involve the meaning of the factor \(1+x\), or of the factor \(-1-x\). The first cannot be interpreted, because we cannot conceive of adding any class \(x\) to the universe \(1\). The second cannot be interpreted, because the symbol \(-1\) does not obey the law \(x(1-x)=0\), which all class symbols obey. So the equation \(x^3=x\) admits of no interpretation like that of the equation \(x^2=x\). Suppose, however, that the former equation were true independently of the latter. That is, suppose the act of the mind denoted by the symbol \(x\) were such that doing it a second time over, three times in all, reproduced the result of doing it once, while doing it just twice did not. Then presumably we would be able to interpret one of the forms (1), (2), which under the actual conditions of thought we cannot do. Mathematicians know of operations whose law can be fully expressed by the equation \(x^3=x\). But they are of a nature entirely foreign to the field of general reasoning. When I say it is conceivable that the law of thought might have been different from what it is, I mean only that we can frame such a hypothesis and study its consequences. Being able to do this implies no such doctrine as that the actual law of human reason is the product either of chance or of arbitrary will.

Editor's note: This is the step that made Boolean algebra the algebra of two values. Once every class symbol must obey the law that _x_ times _x_ is _x_, and the only numbers that obey it are 0 and 1, the whole system can be worked as if each symbol were either 0 or 1, nothing or everything, false or true. In 1937 Claude Shannon showed that electrical switching circuits, with each switch either open or closed, obey these same laws. That is why every digital computer calculates in Boole's algebra.
