Chapter 13: Clarke and Spinoza
(Part 2 of 2)

13. The reader will now notice that the first and the last of these four conclusions do not agree. From the hypothesis that motion exists by an endless successive communication, two consequences were drawn. First, that it has been eternally caused by an eternal intelligent being. Second, that it began in time. These are plainly at odds. And yet both are rigorous deductions from the original premises. The conflict between them is not _logical_. It is what is technically called a _material_ conflict, one that lies in the meaning of the words rather than in the form of the argument. Still, the conflict could have been stated formally in the premises. We could have added a formal proposition saying that "whatever is _externally_ caused by an eternal intelligent being, does not begin in time." Had we done so, no such conflict as now appears in our conclusions could have arisen. Formal logic can take account only of relations that are formally expressed (VI. 16). So in particular cases it may become necessary to state, in a formal way, some connection among the premises that is already contained in the very meaning of the language used, even though nobody has said it out loud.

To show what this means, let us add to the equations (2) and (4) the equation
\[px = 0,\]
which expresses the condition just mentioned. We then have
\[(1-x) \lbrace pr + (1-p)(1-r) \rbrace + r(1-p)+ px = 0. \qquad (8)\]
Eliminating \(p\) from this, we find simply
\[r = 0,\]
which expresses the proposition, _Motion does not exist by an endless successive communication_. If we now put the value of \(r\) back into (8), we have
\[(1-x)(1-p) + px = 0,\text{ or, }1-x = p;\]
and this gives the interpretation, _If motion has existed from eternity, it has been eternally caused by an eternal intelligent being_, together with the converse of that proposition.

In Prop. IX. Clarke argues that "the self-existent and original cause of all things is not a necessary agent, but a being endued with liberty and choice." The proof rests mainly on two grounds: that this cause possesses intelligence, and that there exist final causes (purposes in nature), which imply design and choice. To the objection that the supreme cause acts by necessity to produce what is best, Clarke replies that this is a necessity of fitness and wisdom, not a necessity of nature.

14. In Prop. X. he argues that "the self-existent being, the supreme cause of all things, must of necessity have infinite power." The ground of the demonstration is that, since "all the powers of all things are derived from him, nothing can make any difficulty or resistance to the execution of his will." He sets limits to this infinite power. It does not extend to the "making of a thing which implies a contradiction," or to doing that "which would imply imperfection (whether natural or moral) in the being to whom such power is ascribed." But it does extend to the creation of matter, and to the creation of an immaterial, thinking substance, gifted with a power of beginning motion and with a liberty of will or choice. On the strength of this doctrine of liberty, Clarke holds that we can give a satisfactory answer to "that ancient and great question, πόθεν τὸ κακὸν, what is the cause and original of evil?" I shall set out his argument on this point briefly. It runs as follows:

"All that we call evil is either an evil of imperfection, as the want of certain faculties or excellencies which other creatures have; or natural evil, as pain, death, and the like; or moral evil, as all kinds of vice. The first of these is not properly an evil; for every power, faculty, or perfection, which any creature enjoys, being the free gift of God,…it is plain the want of any certain faculty or perfection in any kind of creatures, which never belonged to their natures is no more an evil to them, than their never having been created or brought into being at all could properly have been called an evil. The second kind of evil, which we call natural evil, is either a necessary consequence of the former, as death to a creature on whose nature immortality was never conferred; and then it is no more properly an evil than the former. Or else it is counterpoised on the whole with as great or greater good, as the afflictions and sufferings of good men, and then also it is not properly an evil; or else, lastly, it is a punishment, and then it is a necessary consequence of the third and last kind of evil, viz., moral evil. And this arises wholly from the abuse of liberty which God gave to His creatures for other purposes, and which it was reasonable and fit to give them for the perfection and order of the whole creation. Only they, contrary to God's intention and command, have abused what was necessary to the perfection of the whole, to the corruption and depravation of themselves. And thus all sorts of evils have entered into the world without any diminution to the infinite goodness of the Creator and Governor thereof."—p. 112.

The main premises of this argument can be stated as follows.

First, all reputed evil (what is called evil) is either evil of imperfection, or natural evil, or moral evil.

Second, evil of imperfection is not absolute evil.

Third, natural evil is either a consequence of evil of imperfection, or it is compensated with greater good, or it is a consequence of moral evil.

Fourth, whatever is either a consequence of evil of imperfection, or is compensated with greater good, is not absolute evil.

Fifth, all absolute evils are included among reputed evils.

To express these premises, let us take
\[\begin{aligned} w &= \text{ reputed evil.} \\ x &= \text{ evil of imperfection.} \\ y &= \text{ natural evil.} \\ z &= \text{ moral evil.} \\ p &= \text{ consequence of evil of imperfection.} \\ q &= \text{ compensated with greater good.} \\ r &= \text{ consequence of moral evil.} \\ t &= \text{ absolute evil.} \end{aligned}\]
We treat the premises as primary propositions whose predicates are all particular, so that each subject is only some part of its predicate class. And we take the words _either, or_ as absolutely disjunctive, meaning that each alternative excludes the others. We then have the following equations:
\[\begin{gathered} w = v\left\{x(1-y)(1-z) + y(1-x)(1-z) + z(1-x)(1-y)\right\} \\ x = v(1 - t). \\ y = v \left\{p (1-q)(1-r) + q (1-p)(1-r) + r(1-p)(1-q)\right\} \\ p(1-q) + q(1-p) = v(1-t). \\ t = vw\,. \end{gathered}\]
If we eliminate the symbol \(v\) from each of these separately, we have
\[\begin{gathered} w\left\{ 1 - x(1-y)(1-z) - y(1-x)(1-z) - z(1-x)(1-y) \right\} =0, \qquad (1) \\ xt = 0, \qquad (2) \\ y\left\{ 1 - p(1-q)(1-r) - q(1-p)(1-r) - r(1-p)(1-q) \right\} =0, \qquad (3) \\ \left\{ p(1-q) + q(1-p)\right\}t = 0, \qquad (4) \\ t(1-w) = 0. \qquad (5) \end{gathered}\]

Suppose we want to find, first, what conclusion the premises allow us to draw about absolute evils, in their dependence on moral evils and on the consequences of moral evils.

For this we must find \(t\) in terms of \(z\) and \(r\).

So the symbols \(w\), \(x\), \(y\), \(p\), \(q\) must be eliminated. The work is easy, because any set of these equations can be reduced to a single equation simply by adding them.

Eliminating \(w\) from (1) and (5), we have
\[t\left\{ 1 - x(1-y)(1-z) - y(1-x)(1-z) - z(1-x)(1-y) \right\} = 0. \qquad (6)\]
Eliminating \(p\) from (3) and (4) gives
\[yqr + yqt + yt(1-r)(1-q) = 0. \qquad (7)\]
Eliminating \(q\) from this gives
\[yt(1-r) = 0. \qquad (8)\]
that is, nothing is at once natural evil, absolute evil, and not a consequence of moral evil. Eliminating \(x\) between (2) and (6) gives
\[t\left\{ yz + (1-y)(1-z) \right\} = 0. \qquad (9)\]
Eliminating \(y\) from (8) and (9) gives
\[t(1-z)(1-r) = 0.\]
This is the only relation that holds among the elements \(t\), \(z\), and \(r\). It says that there is no absolute evil which is neither moral evil nor a consequence of moral evil. From it we get
\[\begin{aligned} t &= \frac{0}{(1-z)(1-r)} \\ &= \frac{0}{0}zr + \frac{0}{0}z(1-r) + \frac{0}{0}(1-z)r + 0(1-z)(1-r) \\ &= \frac{0}{0}z + \frac{0}{0}(1-z)r; \end{aligned}\]
and its interpretation is, _Absolute evil is either moral evil, or it is, if not moral evil, a consequence of moral evil_.

Any of the results reached along the way to this solution will also yield interpretations. From (8), for example, we might deduce
\[\begin{gathered} t = \frac{0}{y(1-r)} = \frac{0}{0}yr + \frac{0}{0}(1-y)r + \frac{0}{0}(1-y)(1-r) \\ = \frac{0}{0}yr + \frac{0}{0}(1-y); \end{gathered}\]
which means, Absolute evils are either natural evils, which are the consequences of moral evils, or they are not natural evils at all.

Many other conclusions can be drawn from the given equations, in answer to any questions we care to put. I shall give a few examples of these, without showing the intermediate steps of the solution.

Quest. 1.—Can any relation be deduced from the premises connecting the following elements: absolute evils, consequences of evils of imperfection, and evils compensated with greater good?

Ans.—_No relation exists_. If we eliminate all the symbols but \(t\), \(p\), \(q\), the result is \(0 = 0\), an identity that says nothing.

Quest. 2.—Is any relation implied between absolute evils, evils of imperfection, and consequences of evils of imperfection?

Ans.—The final relation between \(x\), \(t\), and \(p\) is
\[xt + pt = 0;\]
so that
\[t = \frac{0}{p + x} = \frac{0}{0}(1-p)(1-x).\]
Therefore, _Absolute evils are neither evils of imperfection, nor consequences of evils of imperfection_.

Quest. 3.—What is the relation of natural evils to evils of imperfection and to evils compensated with greater good?

We find
\[\begin{gathered} pqy = 0, \\ \therefore y = \frac{0}{pq} = \frac{0}{0}p(1-q) + \frac{0}{0}(1-p). \end{gathered}\]
Therefore, Natural evils are either consequences of evils of imperfection which are not compensated with greater good, or they are not consequences of evils of imperfection at all.

Quest. 4.—How do those natural evils which are not moral evils stand in relation to absolute evils and to the consequences of moral evils?

If \(y(1-z) = s\), we find, after elimination,
\[\begin{gathered} ts(1-r) = 0; \\ \therefore s = \frac{0}{t(1-r)} = \frac{0}{0}tr + \frac{0}{0}(1-t). \end{gathered}\]
Therefore, Natural evils, which are not moral evils, are either absolute evils, which are the consequences of moral evils, or they are not absolute evils at all.

The following conclusions were found in the same way. In each one, the subject shows what things a description was asked for, and the predicate shows which elements the description was meant to involve:

Absolute evils, which are not consequences of moral evils, are moral and not natural evils.

Absolute evils which are not moral evils are natural evils, which are the consequences of moral evils.

Natural evils which are not consequences of moral evils are not absolute evils.

Last, let us look for a description of the evils which are not absolute, in terms of natural and moral evils.

The final equation we obtain is
\[1 - t = yz + \frac{0}{0}y(1-z) + \frac{0}{0}(1-y)z + (1-y)(1-z).\]
Read directly, this equation states a necessary truth. Read in reverse, it gives something remarkable: _Evils which are both natural and moral, and evils which are neither natural nor moral, are not absolute evils_.

This conclusion may not express a truth. But it is certainly contained in the given premises, as _formally_ stated.

15. Let us now take from the same argument a somewhat fuller set of premises. And this time let us suppose that the words _either, or_ are not absolutely disjunctive. So by the phrase "either evil of imperfection, or natural evil, or moral evil," we will mean whatever has one or more of these qualities.

Let the premises be these:

1. All evil (\(w\)) is either evil of imperfection (\(x\)), or natural evil (\(y\)), or moral evil (\(z\)).

2. Evil of imperfection (\(x\)) is not absolute evil (\(t\)).

3. Natural evil (\(y\)) is either a consequence of evil of imperfection (\(p\)), or it is compensated with greater good (\(q\)), or it is a consequence of moral evil (\(r\)).

4. Whatever is a consequence of evil of imperfection (\(p\)) is not absolute evil (\(t\)).

5. Whatever is compensated with greater good (\(q\)) is not absolute evil (\(t\)).

6. Moral evil (\(z\)) is a consequence of the abuse of liberty (\(u\)).

7. That which is a consequence of moral evil (\(r\)) is a consequence of the abuse of liberty (\(u\)).

8. Absolute evils are included in reputed evils.

We express these premises in the usual way and eliminate the indefinite symbols \(v\). This gives the following equations:
\[\begin{gathered} w(1-x)(1-y)(1-z) = 0, \qquad (1) \\ xt = 0, \qquad (2) \\ y(1-p)(1-q)(1-r) = 0, \qquad (3) \\ pt = 0, \qquad (4) \\ qt = 0, \qquad (5) \\ z(1-u) = 0, \qquad (6) \\ r(1-u) = 0, \qquad (7) \\ t(1-w) = 0. \qquad (8) \end{gathered}\]
Each of these equations satisfies the condition \(V(1-V) = 0\).

The following results are easily deduced:

Natural evil is either absolute evil, which is a consequence of moral evil, or it is not absolute evil at all.

All evils are either absolute evils, which are consequences of the abuse of liberty, or they are not absolute evils.

Natural evils are either evils of imperfection, which are not absolute evils, or they are not evils of imperfection at all.

Absolute evils are either natural evils, which are consequences of the abuse of liberty, or they are not natural evils, and at the same time not evils of imperfection.

Consequences of the abuse of liberty include all natural evils which are absolute evils and are not evils of imperfection, together with an indefinite remainder of natural evils which are not absolute, and of evils which are not natural.

16. These examples are enough to illustrate the method. The reader can easily supply others if they are wanted. We now go on to examine the most essential parts of Spinoza's demonstration. His definitions and axioms, from the opening of the first book of the Ethics, are these.

Definitions.

1. By a _cause of itself (causa sui)_, I understand that of which the essence involves existence, or that of which the nature cannot be conceived except as existing.

2. That thing is said to be finite or bounded in its own kind _(in suo genere finita)_ which may be bounded by another thing of the same kind; e. g. Body is said to be finite, because we can always conceive of another body greater than a given one. So thought is bounded by other thought. But body is not bounded by thought, nor thought by body.

3. By substance, I understand that which is in itself _(in se)_, and is conceived by itself _(per se concipitur)_, i.e., that whose conception does not require to be formed from the conception of another thing.

4. By attribute, I understand that which the intellect perceives in substance, as constituting its very essence.

5. By mode, I understand the affections of substance, or that which is in another thing, by which thing also it is conceived.

6. By God, I understand the Being absolutely infinite, that is the substance consisting of infinite attributes, each of which expresses an eternal and infinite essence.

_Explanation_.—I say absolutely infinite, not infinite in its own kind. For to whatever is only infinite in its own kind we may deny the possession of (some) infinite attributes. But when a thing is absolutely infinite, whatsoever expresses essence and involves no negation belongs to its essence.

7. That thing is termed _free_, which exists by the sole necessity of its own nature, and is determined to action by itself alone; _necessary_, or rather constrained, which is determined by another thing to existence and action, in a certain and determinate manner.

8. By eternity, I understand existence itself, in so far as it is conceived necessarily to follow from the sole definition of the eternal thing.

_Explanation_.—For such existence, as an eternal truth, is conceived as the essence of the thing, and therefore cannot be explained by mere duration or time, though the latter should be conceived as without beginning and without end.

Axioms.

1. All things which exist are either in themselves _in se_ or in another thing.

2. That which cannot be conceived by another thing ought to be conceived by itself.

3. From a given determinate cause the effect necessarily follows, and, contrariwise, if no determinate cause be granted, it is impossible that an effect should follow.

4. The knowledge of the effect depends upon, and involves, the knowledge of the cause.

5. Things which have nothing in common cannot be understood by means of each other; or the conception of the one does not involve the conception of the other.

6. A true idea ought to agree with its own object. (_Idea vera debet cum suo ideato convenire_.)

7. Whatever can be conceived as non-existing does not involve existence in its essence.

Other definitions are implied, and other axioms are quietly assumed, in some of the demonstrations. Take Prop. I., "Substance is prior in nature to its affections." Its proof consists of nothing but a reference to Defs. 3 and 5. Yet it seems to assume a further axiom: "That by which a thing is conceived is prior in nature to the thing conceived." Again, the demonstration of Prop. V. assumes that the converse of this axiom is true. Many other examples of the same kind occur. So it is impossible, by the processes of logic alone, to deduce all the conclusions of the first book of the Ethics from the axioms and definitions set at its head, which are the ones given above. In the brief analysis that follows, I shall try to set out in their proper order what seem to me to be the real premises, whether stated or implied, and to show how they lead to the conclusions Spinoza reached.

17. My view, then, is that in the course of his demonstration Spinoza divides the universe of possible existence in several parallel ways, as follows.

First, into things which are in themselves, \(x\), and things which are in some other thing, \(x'\). Since these two classes together make up the universe, we have
\[\begin{gathered} x + x'= 1; \text{ (Ax. I.)} \\ \text{or, } x = 1 - x'. \end{gathered}\]

Second, into things which are conceived by themselves, \(y\), and things which are conceived through some other thing,\(y'\); so that
\[y = 1 - y'. \text{ (Ax. II)}\]

Third, into substance, \(z\), and modes, \(z'\); so that
\[z = 1 - z'. \text{ (Def. III. V.)}\]

Fourth, into things free, \(f\), and things necessary,\(f'\); so that
\[f = 1 - f'. \text{ (Def. VII.)}\]

Fifth, into things which are causes and self-existent, \(e\), and things caused by some other thing, \(e'\); so that
\[e = 1 - e'. \text{ (Def. I. Ax. VII.)}\]
And his reasoning proceeds on the principle, sometimes stated and sometimes assumed, that these divisions are not only parallel but equivalent: that they cut the universe along the same line. Thus in Def. III., substance is made equivalent to that which is conceived by itself; so that
\[z = y .\]
Again, Ax. IV., as Spinoza actually applies it, makes a cause the same thing as that by which a thing is conceived; so that
\[y = e.\]
Again, in Def. VII., things free are identified with things self-existent; so that
\[f = e.\]
Last, in Def. V mode is made identical with that which is in another thing; so \(z' = x'\), and therefore
\[z=x.\]
All these results can be gathered into one series of equations:
\[x = y = z = f= e = 1-x' = 1-y' = 1-f'= 1-z' = 1-e'.\]
Any two members of this series joined by the sign of equality express a conclusion that legitimately follows from his system, whether or not Spinoza drew it himself. Thus the equation
\[z=1-e',\]
that is, substance is the same as what is not caused by another thing, expresses the sixth proposition of his system: One substance cannot be produced by another. In the same way the equation
\[z = e,\]
expresses his seventh proposition, "It pertains to the nature of substance to exist." There is no need to follow this train of deduction further. Spinoza uses it chiefly to deduce, according to his views, the properties of the Divine Nature, having first tried to prove that the only substance is God. In the steps of this process there seem to me to be some fallacies, which depend chiefly on the ambiguous use of words. I must draw attention to them here.

18. In Prop. V. Spinoza tries to show that "There cannot exist two or more substances of the same nature or attribute." The proof is in effect as follows. If there are more substances than one, they are told apart either by their attributes or by their modes. If by attributes, then there is only one substance with any given attribute. If by modes, then once we set these aside as not essential, no _real_ ground of distinction is left. So there exists only one substance of the same attribute. But the assumptions made here do not agree with those found in other parts of the treatise. By Def. IV., the intellect grasps substance by means of attribute. By Def. VI., a substance may have many attributes. So one substance could _conceivably_ be told apart from another by differing in some of its attributes, while the others stay the same.

In Prop. VIII. Spinoza tries to show that all substance is necessarily infinite. The proof goes like this. There exists only one substance of one attribute, Prop. V.; and it belongs to its nature to exist, Prop. VII. So it will be of its nature to exist either as finite or as infinite. But not as finite. For by Def. II. it would then have to be bounded by another substance of the same nature, which would also have to exist _necessarily_, Prop. VII. Then there would be two substances of the same attribute, which is absurd, Prop. V. Therefore substance is infinite.

In this demonstration the word "finite" is confused with the expression "finite in its own kind," Def. II. The proof thus assumes that nothing can be finite unless it is bounded by another thing of the same kind. That is not the ordinary meaning of the word. Spinoza's use of "finite" tends to make space the only form of substance, and all existing things mere affections (states) of space. This, I think, is really one of the ultimate foundations of his system.

The first scholium (explanatory remark) attached to this Proposition is remarkable. I give it in the original words: "Quum finitum esse revera sit ex parte negatio, et infinitum absoluta affirmatio existentiae alicujus naturae, sequitur ergo ex sola Prop. VII. omnem substantiam debere esse infinitam." That is: since being finite is in truth partly a negation, and being infinite the absolute affirmation of the existence of some nature, it follows from Prop. VII. alone that every substance must be infinite. Now this is really an assertion of the principle that Clarke affirmed and Butler disputed (XIII. 11): that necessary existence implies existence in every part of space. This principle will probably be found at the base of every attempt to prove, _à priori_, the existence of an Infinite Being.

From the general properties of substance stated above, together with the definition of God as the substance consisting of infinite attributes, Spinoza's particular doctrines about the Divine Nature follow necessarily. Substance is self-existent, free, and causal in its very nature; it is the thing in which other things are, and by which they are conceived. So the same properties are asserted of the Deity. He is self-existent, Prop. XI.; indivisible, Prop. XIII.; the only substance, Prop. XIV.; the Being in which all things are, and by which all things are conceived, Prop. XV.; free, Prop. XVII.; the immanent cause of all things (the cause that acts from within them), Prop. XVIII. The proof that God is the only substance is drawn from Def. VI., which is read as declaring that "God is the Being absolutely infinite, of whom no attribute which expresses the essence of substance can be denied." Every conceivable attribute is thus assigned to Him by definition. And Prop. V. has settled that there cannot exist two substances of the same attribute. It follows that God is the only substance.

The "Ethics" of Spinoza, like much of his other writing, is set out in geometrical form, as a chain of definitions, axioms, and propositions. Even so, it does not give the symbolic method of this book much useful practice. Of course any train of reasoning can be handled by that method once its ultimate premises have been correctly identified. But here such treatment hardly differs from the processes of the original demonstrations, except in using letters for words. Reasoning that consists so largely of a play on terms defined as equivalent is not often met with. I have given a few pages here to setting it out more because of the interest of its subject than because of the merits of its demonstrations, however highly some people value them.

19. I do not think anyone can come away from reading the arguments of Clarke and Spinoza without a deep conviction that every attempt is futile to establish, entirely _à priori_, the existence of an Infinite Being, His attributes, and His relation to the universe. The basic principle of all such speculations is that whatever we can clearly conceive must exist. Even if that principle is granted to be true, it fails to do what it is meant to do. For how shall the finite comprehend the infinite? Yet before any solid ground can be laid for knowing, _à priori_, things infinite and eternal, we must grant that such a conception is possible, and in some sense more than simply removing the limits from the existence we experience through the senses. Spinoza affirms the reality of such knowledge plainly and explicitly: "Mens humana adaequatum habet cognitionem aeternae et infinitae essentiae Dei" (Prop. XLVII., Part 2nd), that is, the human mind has an adequate knowledge of the eternal and infinite essence of God. Compare this with Prop. XXXIV., Part 2nd: "Omnis idea quae in nobis est absoluta sive adaequata et perfecta, vera est;" (every idea which in us is absolute, or adequate and perfect, is true); and with Axiom VI., Part 1st, "Idea vera debet cum suo ideato convenire" (a true idea must agree with its object). What is more, he makes this kind of knowledge the essential part of all other knowledge: "De natura rationis est res sub quadam aeternitatis specie percipere" (Prop. XLIV., Cor. II., Part 2nd), that is, it is of the nature of reason to perceive things under a certain form of eternity. Suppose it were said that the human mind tends to rise in contemplation from the particular toward the universal, from the finite toward the infinite, from the passing toward the eternal; and that this tendency suggests to us, with high probability, that more exists than the senses perceive or the understanding grasps. That statement might be accepted as true, at least for a great many minds. But there is a class of speculations whose character must be explained partly by other causes. One is impatience with knowledge that is only probable or limited, which is so often all we can really reach. Another is a desire for absolute certainty, where we have been granted only hints enough to mark out the path of duty before us, but not enough to satisfy the demands of the speculative intellect. A third, perhaps, is dissatisfaction with the present state of things. When these motives gain too much weight, the more sober method of analogy and probable induction falls into neglect. Yet that method is, beyond all question, the one best suited to our present condition. To infer an intelligent cause from the abundant evidence of design all around us; to rise to the idea of a moral Governor of the world from studying the make-up and the moral provisions of our own nature: these are only the feeble steps of an understanding limited in its faculties and in its materials of knowledge. But they are worth more than the ambitious attempt to reach a certainty that cannot be had on the ground of natural religion. And as these were the most ancient foundations, so they are still the most solid ones, Revelation being set apart, for the belief that the course of this world is not abandoned to chance and inexorable fate.
