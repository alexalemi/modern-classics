Chapter 22: On the Nature of Science, and the Constitution of the Intellect
(Part 1 of 2)

1. By the constitution of a system I mean the sum of the causes and tendencies that produce its observed character when they work without interference, under the conditions the system seems to be adapted to. We judge that adaptation by studying the circumstances in which the system acts most freely, produces its most harmonious results, or in some other way fulfils the apparent design of its construction. In some cases we know clearly the causes on which a system's working depends, and its conditions and its end as well. That is the most perfect kind of knowledge we can have of any subject. In other cases we know the causes at work only partly or imperfectly. Even so, we can work out to some extent the laws by which they act, and beyond that we can discover general tendencies and infer a further purpose. This, I think, is how people have rightly concluded that there is a moral faculty in our nature. They have not concluded it because we understand the particular instruments it works by, as we connect the eye with the faculty of sight. Nor have they concluded it because people agree on universal rules of conduct. They have concluded it for this reason. The feeling of moral approval or disapproval shows itself, in one form or another, in everyone. And wherever human progress can be seen, wherever a society is neither standing still nor hurrying to decay, that feeling tends to attach itself to certain kinds of actions, by common consent, and in a way that points to both permanence and law. Always and everywhere, the appearance of Order gives a presumption, not measurable but real (XX. 22), that some end or purpose is being fulfilled, and that there is a ground of orderly causation behind it.

2. The particular question of the constitution of the intellect has drawn the efforts of speculative minds in every age; that hardly needs saying. It appeals to the desire for knowledge, which the greatest masters of ancient thought believed to be inborn in our species. And it adds to the ordinary strength of that motive a human and personal interest. A real devotion to truth is seldom one-sided in its aims. It urges us to range over the fair fields of observing the outside world, and it forbids us to neglect the study of our own faculties. Even in ages most given over to material interests, part of the current of thought has been turned inward. The desire to understand that by which we understand everything else has been baffled only to be renewed.

This _persistence_ would probably not have lasted among sincere seekers after truth if most of them had been convinced that such speculations are hopelessly barren. We may suppose they felt something like this. Some error and uncertainty always comes with partial information, and will always cling to the results of these inquiries; but a residue of real knowledge may still remain. The contradictions we meet are more often in the words than in the things. Above all, even _probable_ conclusions take on an interest and a value here from their subject, which makes them worth a place beside the more definite and more splendid results of physical science. These considerations seem to me perfectly legitimate. Many of the problems about the nature and constitution of the mind must be presumed insoluble. But there are others on which we can reach a limited but not doubtful knowledge, and others again on which we can reach the conclusions of a highly probable analogy. Day and night do not meet at a sharp edge. They are separated by a twilight zone, through which the light of the one fades gradually into the darkness of the other. In the same way, every region of positive knowledge is surrounded by a debatable, speculative territory, over which it spreads some of its influence and its light. So there may be questions about the constitution of the intellect that cannot be settled absolutely in the present state of knowledge, and yet receive enough reflected light to make their probable answer not hard to find. And there may be questions about the nature of science, and even about particular truths and doctrines of science, on which anyone who accepts the general principles of this work cannot help forming definite opinions, perhaps different from those usually held today. In what follows I will sum up some of the more definite conclusions established in the earlier parts of this treatise. Then I will point out one or two trains of thought, connected with the general aims just mentioned, which those conclusions seem to me fitted to suggest.

3. Among the conclusions about the intellectual constitution that may count as positive knowledge, we may list the scientific laws of thought and reasoning on which the general methods of this treatise are built, together with the principles (Chap. V.) that have governed how they are applied. Here are some of them. The division of the domain of thought into two spheres, distinct but existing side by side (IV. XI.). The subjection of the operations of the intellect within those spheres to one common system of laws (XI.). The general mathematical character of those laws, and their actual expression (II. III.). How far those laws are akin to the laws of thought in the domain of number, and the point where they part from them. The commanding place, among all the subjects of thought that logic deals with, of the two limiting conceptions of the universe and of eternity. The relation of those conceptions to the fundamental conception of unity in the science of number. These results, and many like them, are not to be ranked as merely probable or analogical conclusions. They have a right to be counted as truths of science. Whether we call them metaphysical or not makes no difference. The evidence they rest on is of a different kind from that of physical science, but it is not inferior in value to any evidence that can be brought for the general truths of physical science.

Again, it is agreed that all the more exact forms of knowledge advance in a certain order. Every branch of physical science begins with observation. It moves on, by comparing facts, to a likely guess at the law that connects them. It tests that guess by new experiments, designed so that, if the guess is sound, they will raise its probability without limit. And finally, once the law of the phenomenon is settled with enough confidence, the search for causes crowns the inquiry, carried on by the right mixture of hypothesis and deduction. The particular faculties and laws whose nature this work has examined play their part in this advancing order of knowledge. So it is clear that if we want to study impartially either the nature of science, or the constitution of the intellect in its relation to science, no part of these two series of truths should be treated in isolation. Above all, truths that in any way _supplement_ each other should be considered in their bearing on each other and in their connection.

4. Take, then, the need for an experimental basis for all positive knowledge, and set it beside the existence and the peculiar character of the system of mental laws, principles and operations this book has studied. Together they throw light on some important questions that still largely divide the world of speculative thought. From the particular facts experience gives us, how do we reach the general propositions of science? What is the nature of those propositions? Are they simply collections of experience, or does the mind supply some connecting principle of its own? In a word, what is the nature of scientific truth, and what grounds the confidence with which it asks to be accepted?

It must be clear that no single, general answer can be given to questions like these. In some cases they do not even need discussing. We all know cases in which a general proposition merely states, _per enumerationem simplicem_ (by simply counting the cases), a fact established by actual observation of every case the proposition covers. On that ground the astronomer asserts that all the known planets move from west to east around the sun. But there are also cases in which a general proposition is assumed because it has been observed true in particular instances, and that truth is then extended to instances not observed. No principle of purely deductive reasoning can justify that step. When Kepler concluded, from a great many observations of the planet Mars, that it moves in an ellipse, his conclusion was larger than his premises, larger indeed than any premises mere observation could supply. What further element, then, is needed to give such generalizations even a provisional validity? It is the ability, built into our nature, to recognize Order, together with the presumption, however we come by it, that the phenomena of Nature are connected by a principle of Order. Without these, the general truths of physical science could never have been found. Grant that the method this sets up can lead us only to probable or approximate results. All that follows is that most of the generalizations of physical science have only a probable or approximate truth. Our hold on knowledge is secure for this reason: wherever such conclusions truly represent the way Nature is made, our confidence in them is confirmed without limit, and soon cannot be told apart from certainty. The principle I have just named as the basis of inductive reasoning also lets us settle the much-disputed question of whether reasoning needs general propositions. The logician says that no conclusion can be deduced from particular premises. Modern writers of high reputation have argued that all reasoning goes from particular truths to particular truths. Their example is this: when we conclude from the fact that certain members of a class have a property that some other member has it too, we need not first establish the intermediate _general_ conclusion that _all_ the members of the class have it. Whether or not that is so, the principle of order or analogy on which the reasoning proceeds must be either stated or grasped as a general truth, or the final conclusion has no validity. In this form, at least, the need for general propositions as the basis of inference is confirmed. And I believe that need is implied by the very existence, and still more by the particular _nature_, of the faculties whose laws this work has investigated. For if we analyze the process of reasoning carefully, we find that it sets aside everything peculiar to the individual the conclusion is about, and attends only to the properties that define that individual as a member of the class.

5. Besides the general propositions we get by induction from the compared facts of experience, there are others that belong to the domain of what is called _necessary_ truth. The general propositions of arithmetic are of this kind, and so are the propositions stating the laws of thought on which the general methods of this treatise are founded. These propositions can not only be verified strictly in particular instances; they are made plain in all their generality by studying particular instances. Then there are general propositions that express necessary truths but cannot be exactly verified, because our senses are imperfect. Some, if not all, of the propositions of geometry are like this; but geometry is not the only place where such propositions are found. The question of their nature and origin is a very old one. It is more closely bound up with the inquiry into the constitution of the intellect than any other I have mentioned, so it is fair to consider it here. Among the opinions most widely held on the subject are these. Some have held that propositions of this class exist in the mind independently of experience, and that the conceptions they are about are the imprints of eternal archetypes, the original patterns of things. Plato filled his ideal world with such archetypes, though he thought of them as having a reality of which all the objects of sense are only a faint shadow or dim hint. Others, in various ways, have argued the opposite. They have said that the things such propositions are about are copies of individual objects of experience; or that they are mere names; or that they are themselves individual objects of experience, so that the propositions about them are only partly true, because those objects are imperfect. Last, some have said that they are products of the intellect, formed by abstraction from our sense perceptions of individual things, but formed so as to become what individual things never can be, subjects of science: that is, subjects about which exact and general propositions can be asserted. And there are perhaps still other views, in some of which the element of the senses dominates, and in others the element of the intellect, or the ideal.

Now suppose we take the last of the views just mentioned (I do not propose to consider either the purely ideal view or the purely nominalist one, which holds that general terms are mere names). And suppose we ask what, in the sense described, are the proper objects of science: the objects about which its propositions are true with no mixture of error. I believe only one answer can be given. Neither the individual objects of experience, nor, in all probability, the mental images they call up, have any strict claim to that title. It seems certain that nowhere in nature or in art do we meet anything that agrees exactly with the geometrical definition of a straight line, a triangle or a circle, though the difference may be too small for the senses to detect. And it is at least doubtful whether we can form a perfect mental image, or conception, that agrees with the definition any more exactly. But it is not doubtful that such conceptions, however imperfect, point to something beyond themselves; and as we approach that something step by step, all imperfection tends to disappear. The perfect triangle, square or circle does not exist in nature. It escapes all our powers of _picturing_ it in the mind, and it is presented to our thought only as the limit of a process of abstraction that never ends. Yet, by a wonderful faculty of the understanding, it can be made the subject of propositions that are _absolutely_ true. So the domain of reason turns out to be larger than the domain of imagination. If anyone thinks that we can picture to ourselves, with strict accuracy, the scientific elements of form, direction, size and the rest, then for that person these things, as we actually conceive them, will be the proper objects of science. But I think the fairer opinion is that an incurable imperfection clings to every attempt we make to grasp these elements precisely. In that case all we can say is this. External objects in reality, or the conceptions of our imagination through abstraction, approach certain _limiting_ states, which perhaps are never actually reached. The closer they come to those states, the closer the general propositions of science about those things or conceptions come to absolute truth, and the actual departure from it tends to disappear. To some extent the same remarks apply to the physical sciences too. What have been called the “fundamental ideas” of those sciences, such as force, polarity, crystallization and so on, are not, as I see it, products of the intellect independent of experience; nor are they mere copies of external things. On the one hand, they need experience as their necessary starting point. On the other, they need the power of abstraction to form them, exercised in obedience to some general faculty or disposition of our nature, which always prompts us to search for order and fits us to recognize it. So we study the effects of gravitation on the motions of the heavenly bodies approximately, by reference to the _limiting_ supposition that the planets are perfect spheres or spheroids. We work out approximately the path of a ray of light through the atmosphere by a process that sets aside every disturbing effect of temperature. And this is the order of proceeding in all the higher paths of human knowledge. What is remarkable about these processes of the intellect is the disposition, and the matching ability, to rise from the imperfect pictures given by the senses, and from the variety of individual experience, to the perception of general, and perhaps unchanging, truths. Wherever this disposition and this ability come together, each series of connected facts in nature can hint at an order more exact than the one it directly shows. For it can serve as the ground and the occasion for using those powers whose task is to grasp general truths: truths that are indeed exemplified in a world of changing phenomena, but never exemplified perfectly.

6. The ultimate laws of thought are mathematical in form. Put that truth beside the fact that we can make mistakes, and it gives ground for some remarkable conclusions. If we looked at the scientific truth alone, we might be led to infer an almost exact parallel between the operations of the intellect and the movements of external nature. Imagine someone well versed in physical science, but not used to reflecting on the nature of his own faculties, who is told that the laws of those faculties have been proved to be mathematical. Once his first disbelief had passed, he would probably come away with the impression that the order of thought must _therefore_ be as necessary as the order of the material universe. We know that in natural science the absolute connection between the starting point and the end point of a problem, shown in mathematical form, is a fitting symbol of the physical necessity that binds effect to cause. The necessary sequence of states and conditions in the inorganic world, and the necessary connection of premises and conclusion in the exact proofs we apply to it, seem to run side by side. It might be asked which of the two has the first claim to the word “necessary”: the observed constancy of Nature, or the unbreakable connection of propositions in all valid reasoning about her works. Historically we would perhaps give the first claim to the former; philosophically, to the latter. But the fact of the connection cannot be disputed, and the analogy it points to is obvious.

If the laws of valid reasoning, then, were obeyed without exception, there would be a very close parallel between the operations of the intellect and those of external Nature. Both would be subject to laws mathematical in form and expression, and subject with absolute obedience; and that would stamp one common character on both. The reign of necessity over the intellectual world and the physical world would be equally complete and universal.

But it is not so. The observation of external Nature bears ever stronger witness that uniform working and unvarying obedience to appointed laws hold throughout her whole domain. The slightest attention to the processes of the intellectual world shows us a different state of things. The mathematical laws of reasoning are, strictly speaking, the laws of _right_ reasoning only, and they are actually broken again and again. Error, which has no place in the material system, takes up a large place here. We must accept this as one of those ultimate facts whose origin it is not the business of science to determine. We must admit that there exist laws which even the rigour of their mathematical form does not protect from being broken. We must grant them an authority whose essence is not power: a supremacy that the analogy of the unbreakable order of the natural world does nothing to help us understand.

Because the distinction just pointed out is _real_, it does not depend on any particular views we may hold about other parts of the mind's constitution. If we regard the intellect as free, and this seems to be the view most in keeping with the general spirit of these speculations, then its freedom must be set against the rule of necessity, not against the existence of a certain rightful supremacy of truth. The laws of correct inference can be broken, but they _exist_ no less truly for that. And they keep the same character and authority if we adopt the hypothesis of necessity in its most extreme form. Let it be granted that the laws of valid reasoning, as this work determines them (or, more generally, as they would finally appear at the end of an exhaustive analysis), form only a _part_ of the system of laws that governs the actual processes of reasoning, right or wrong. Let it be granted that if we knew that system completely, we would see that the whole working of the intellect is _necessary_, just as the movements of the inorganic world are necessary. And let it finally be granted, as a consequence of this hypothesis, that incorrect reasoning, or error, wherever it appears, comes from other laws interfering with the laws that produce _right_ reasoning. Even so, it would remain true that among the laws of the intellect there are some set apart from the rest by a special character, namely this. Every movement of the intellectual system carried out under their direction alone is _right_; and every interference with them by other laws is not just interference, but _violation_. We cannot help feeling that this would give the laws in question a character of distinction and of rank above the rest. They would seem all the more clearly to point to a final purpose that is not always fulfilled, and to hold an authority that is built in and rightful, but does not always command obedience.

A little thought will show that there is nothing like this in the government of the world by natural law. The realm of inorganic Nature allows neither preferences nor distinctions. We cannot pick out some of her laws from the rest and declare that they alone deserve obedience, or that they alone are charged with carrying out her highest purpose. On the contrary, all her laws seem to stand on the same level; and the more we know of them, the more their united action seems necessary to the harmony of the system, and, so far as we can understand it, to its general _design_. Consider the most striking departures from apparent order in the inorganic world: the disturbances of the planets in their orbits, the interruption of crystallization when a foreign force intrudes, and others of the same kind. It hardly needs saying how often these either merge into the idea of some higher scheme of order, or lose their abnormal look to a more attentive and better-informed eye. Only one explanation of these facts can be given, namely that the distinction between _true_ and _false_, between _correct_ and _incorrect_, exists in the processes of the intellect, but not in the realm of physical necessity. As we go up from the lower stages of living things to the higher level of conscious intelligence, this contrast gradually dawns on us. Wherever life appears, the rule of rigid law gives way to some degree before that mysterious principle of activity. So, although the bodies of the animal kinds follow certain general types, those types are always realized imperfectly, perhaps in relation to the highest standards of beauty and proportion. Art in our day swings between two choices. One is the exact imitation of individual forms. The other is the effort, by abstracting from all such forms, to reach the idea of an ideal grace and expression that perhaps never appears perfectly in any earthly shape. Again, there are the purposeful adaptations by which species, without giving up their type, become fitted to new conditions or new homes. These come about only slowly; but they seem to come about not by the fated power of outside circumstances, but by an energy called forth from within. So life in all its forms can be contrasted with the passive fixity of inorganic nature. But the perfection of the types in which life takes bodily form is to some degree an ideal thing, and we cannot define exactly the highest excellence of form and adaptation they _suggest_. So the contrast here is less sharp than the contrast between the processes of the intellect and those of the purely material world. For the definite, technical character of the mathematical laws that govern both of these throws into stronger light the basic difference between the kinds of authority those laws exercise in governing them.

7. There is one more case, connected with the general aims of this chapter, in which comparing truths or facts drawn from different sources suggests an instructive train of thought. It consists in comparing the laws of thought, in their scientific expression, with the actual forms that physical speculation in early ages, and metaphysical speculation in every age, have tended to take. I want to draw attention here to two illustrations in particular.

First, it has been shown (III. 13) that there is a scientific connection between the conception of unity in number and the conception of the universe in logic. They hold the same relative place in their two systems, and they obey the same formal laws. Now think of the Greek mind in its early stage of activity, a stage no less marked, and perhaps no less _necessary_, in the progress of the human intellect than the age of Bacon or of Newton. The great problems of Nature were then beginning to open up, while the means of observation were still lacking and its necessity was not understood. To that mind, the terms “Universe” and “The One” seem to have meant almost the same thing. The first aim of philosophy was to say what that unity was, of which all existence was thought to be a manifestation. Thales looked for this fundamental unity in water. Anaximenes and Diogenes took it to be air. Hippasus of Metapontum and Heraclitus of Ephesus declared that it was fire. Parmenides, less definite or less confident in his views, simply declared that all existing things were One. Melissus said that the Universe was infinite, incapable of change or motion, One, like itself, and that motion did not exist but only seemed to. Anaxagoras of Clazomenae, following perhaps in the steps of his fellow citizen Hermotimus, looked for the cause of the world and of its order in Intelligence; and to the reflective mind of Aristotle his spirit seemed sober next to the rashness of earlier speculation. The pantheistic leaning (the belief that God and the whole of things are one) that ran through many of these speculations shows plainly in the words of Xenophanes, the founder of the Eleatic school, who, “surveying the expanse of heaven, declared that the One was God.” There are perhaps few, if any, of the ways in which unity can be conceived that we do not find applied in these ancient doctrines: unity in the abstract, as numerical or as rational; unity in the concrete, as a passive substance, or as a central and living principle. The writings of Aristotle, which I have mainly drawn on, are full of allusions of this kind; yet of most of the people who once gave themselves to such speculations, the very names have probably been lost. It is a strange truth, but a telling one. Nature, in everything but the face of the heavens, must have looked like little more than a scene of unexplained disorder; popular belief was scattered among its many gods. And yet the idea of a first unity, if only in a crude, material form, struck the deepest root of all, and lived on in many a thoughtful heart through the chill of a lifelong disappointment and an endless search! Second, the law of thought expressed by an equation of the second degree, which this treatise has called the law of duality, is just as closely allied to the leaning of ancient thought toward the kinds of philosophical speculation known as dualism (the view that everything comes from two opposed principles). The theory of Empedocles, which explained the apparent contradictions of nature by tracing them to two opposing principles, “strife” and “friendship”; and the theory of Leucippus, which broke all existence down into two elements, a _plenum_ (the full) and a vacuum: these are of this kind. The famous comparison of the universe to a lyre or a bow, whose “recurrent harmony” comes from opposite states of tension, betrays the same origin. In the system of Pythagoras, which seems to have combined dualism with other elements drawn from the study of numbers and their relations, ten fundamental pairs of opposites are recognized: finite and infinite, even and odd, one and many, right and left, male and female, rest and motion, straight and curved, light and darkness, good and evil, the square and the oblong. Alcmaeon accepted the same fundamental dualism, but without the definite numerical limit it has in the Pythagorean system. The grand development of this idea, however, is found in the ancient Manichaean doctrine, which not only formed the basis of the religious system of Persia, but spread widely through other regions of the East and became memorable in the history of the Christian Church. Aristotle describes the origin of dualism as a speculative opinion, before it was connected with the personification of the Evil Principle, and as the natural successor of the doctrines that had assumed the first unity of Nature: “Since there manifestly existed in Nature things opposite to the good, and not only order and beauty, but also disorder and deformity; and since the evil things did manifestly preponderate in number over the good, and the deformed over the beautiful, some one else at length introduced strife and friendship as the respective causes of these diverse phænomena.” And in Greece, indeed, the dualist theory seems to have gained ground mainly as a philosophical opinion, or as an addition to philosophical speculation. The moral application of the doctrine that best suited the Greek mind survives in Plato's great opposition of “being and non-being”: being joined with whatever is good and true, with the eternal ideas and the world of archetypes; non-being with evil, with error, with the passing phenomena of the present scene. Here the two forms of speculation we have considered were blended together. Nor did these tendencies of thought show themselves only in the youth and maturity of Greek philosophy. Ages of imitation took up the same spirit and made it their own. This influence was felt above all wherever the genius of Plato held sway. The unity of all real being, and its identity, in essence, with truth and goodness; the illusion, the deep unreality, of everything that merely appears: these were the views, these the habits of thought, that his influence chiefly encouraged. From this came the strong leaning toward mysticism that took hold of the Greek schools of philosophy once Greece's days of renown, in thought and in public life alike, were over, and that reached its peak among the Platonists of Alexandria. The treatises falsely ascribed to Dionysius the Areopagite carried the same influence, much altered by contact with Aristotle's doctrines, to the scholastic debaters of the Middle Ages. It can give no fair ground for dispute to say that the tone of thought this encouraged was as little in keeping with true devotion as with a sober philosophy. The kindly influence of human affections, and our homely dealings with the common things of life, make up a large part of the true discipline of our nature, true because it is the one intended for us. They would be poorly replaced by the contemplation even of the highest object of thought, when an excess of abstraction turns that object into something about which not one meaningful proposition can be either affirmed or denied. I will only touch on the related speculations about the Divine Nature that credited it with the perfect union of opposite qualities, and on the remarkable treatises of Anselm, meant to found a theory of the universe on the parallels between thought and being. There the first unity is pictured as dwelling in the one eternal Truth. The way Nature conforms to her laws, and the way moral beings obey the commands of what is right, are that same Truth seen in action; and the world itself is only an expression of the self-reflecting thought of its Author. The revival of the older forms of speculation in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries was more marked still. The friends and companions of Lorenzo the Magnificent, the thinkers known in England as the Cambridge Platonists, and many meditative minds scattered across Europe gave themselves afresh either to solving the ancient problem, De Uno, Vero, Bono (“Of the One, the True, the Good”), or to proving that all such inquiries are futile and vain. It would be easy to trace the logical elements that underlie all these speculations, and from which they seem to borrow at least their form, in the outlines of more modern systems. This is especially true of the joining of the doctrine of absolute unity with the distinction between the _ego_ and the _non-ego_ (the self and the not-self) as the pattern of Nature, which is the basis of the philosophy of Hegel. Speculative minds have tried to climb to some high peak of truth from which they could survey the whole framework and connection of things _in the order of deductive thought_. Their attempts have differed less in the forms of theory they produced than in the meanings given to those forms. And here lies the real question about how philosophical systems shape a person's character and life. It matters little whether people agree in tracing all the forms and conditions of being back to a first unity. It matters a great deal what they take that unity to be, and what relations, besides that of mere cause, it bears to themselves. Here too we may feel how powerless mere logic is. The deepest knowledge of the laws of the understanding cannot settle the problems that lie nearer our hearts, as the passing years strip from our life the illusions of its golden dawn.
