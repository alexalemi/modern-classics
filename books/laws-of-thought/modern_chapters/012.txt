Chapter 11: Of Secondary Propositions, and of the Principles of Their Symbolical Expression

1. We established in Chap. IV. that every logical proposition belongs to one of two great classes, Primary Propositions and Secondary Propositions. The preceding chapters have dealt with the first class. We now turn to Secondary Propositions, that is, propositions about other propositions, considered as true or false. The inquiry we are beginning will follow the same general order and course as the one we have already carried out. The two inquiries differ in the subjects of thought they deal with. They do not differ in the formal and scientific laws they reveal, or in the methods and processes built on those laws. We might, to some degree, have expected this. It fits everything we know of the uniformity of Nature, and everything we believe of the unchanging constancy of the Author of Nature, to suppose that the mind should have a harmony and uniformity no less real than the one the physical sciences reveal to us. This is a mind, after all, that has been given high powers, not only for dealing with the world around it, but for knowing itself and reflecting on the laws of its own make-up. Expectations like this must never become the main rule of our inquiries. Nor should they ever draw us away from the patient research by which we find out how things actually are within the particular field we are investigating. But once the grounds of a resemblance have been properly and independently established, it is not at odds even with purely scientific aims to reflect on that resemblance, to trace how far it reaches, and to receive whatever hints it seems to give us of truths not yet discovered. This does not set aside the need for a final appeal to fact. Nor does it stretch analogy beyond its proper job, which is to suggest relations that independent inquiry must then either confirm or reject.

2. _Secondary Propositions are those which concern or relate to Propositions considered as true or false_. We express the relations of _things_ by primary propositions. But we can also make propositions themselves the subject of thought, and express our judgments about them. The expression of any such judgment is a secondary proposition. There is no proposition at all about which enough knowledge would not let us make one of two assertions: either that the proposition is true, or that it is false. Each of these assertions is a secondary proposition. "It is true that the sun shines" and "It is not true that the planets shine by their own light" are examples. In the first, the proposition "The sun shines" is asserted to be true. In the second, the proposition "The planets shine by their own light" is asserted to be false. Secondary propositions also include every judgment that expresses a relation or dependence among propositions. Conditional propositions belong here, such as "If the sun shine the day will be fair." So do most disjunctive propositions, such as "Either the sun will shine, or the enterprise will be postponed." The first example expresses how the truth of the proposition "The day will be fair" depends on the truth of the proposition "The sun will shine." The second expresses a relation between the two propositions "The sun will shine" and "The enterprise will be postponed": the truth of one shuts out the truth of the other. To the same class of secondary propositions we must also assign every proposition that asserts that several propositions are true together or false together, such as "It is not true both that 'the sun will shine' and that 'the journey will be postponed.'" These different elements can even be mixed in a single secondary proposition. It may contain both the disjunctive element, expressed by _either_, _or_, and the conditional element, expressed by _if_; and the propositions it connects may themselves be compound. Take this: _If_ "the sun shine," _and_ "leisure permit," then _either_ "the enterprise shall be commenced," _or_ "some preliminary step shall be taken." Here several propositions are joined together, not at random or without meaning, but so as to express a _definite_ connection among them, a connection that concerns whether each is true or false. So by our definition this combination is a Secondary Proposition.

The theory of Secondary Propositions deserves careful study, both for its many applications and for the close and harmonious analogy, already mentioned, that it bears to the theory of Primary Propositions. I want to say a little more about each of these points.

3. First, the reasonings of everyday life take the form of secondary propositions at least as often as the form of primary ones. The moralist and the metaphysician, too, perhaps talk less often about things and their qualities than about principles and hypotheses, about truths and how truths are connected and related to one another. Our experience is narrow, and the conclusions it suggests on the great unsolved questions of morals and society show their human origin in more ways than one. Universal principles certainly exist. But the partial formulas that make up our knowledge of how those principles apply are subject to conditions, exceptions, and failures. So in the fields of inquiry that ought, by their subject matter, to be the most interesting of all, much of what we actually know is hypothetical. We shall see later that writers on speculative philosophy have tended strongly to adopt these same forms of thought. A general method for handling hypothetical propositions, and the other kinds of secondary propositions, will therefore open a more interesting field of applications than any we have met so far.

4. Second, the theory of Secondary Propositions is interesting for the close and remarkable analogy it bears to the theory of Primary Propositions. We shall see that the formal laws governing the operations of the mind are expressed in exactly the same way in both cases. So the mathematical processes built on those laws are the same as well, and the methods worked out in the earlier part of this book will still serve in the new applications we are about to take up. But while the laws and processes of the method stay the same, the rule of interpretation must be adapted to new conditions. In place of classes of things we shall have propositions. In place of the relations of classes and individuals we shall consider the connections of propositions, or of events. Still, however much the two systems differ in what they mean and how they are read, a harmonious relation will be seen to run through both. This analogy helps us overcome every difficulty that remains. It is also interesting to study for its own sake, and it is conclusive proof of the unity of character that marks the make-up of the human faculties.

Proposition I.

5. To investigate the nature of the connexion of Secondary Propositions with the idea of Time.

To begin this inquiry, we must state clearly what the analogy is that links Secondary Propositions to Primary ones.

Primary Propositions express relations among things. They treat those things as parts of a universe, and the matter of our discourse is confined within that universe's limits, whether or not those limits are the same as the limits of the actual universe. The relations expressed are essentially _substantive_, that is, relations among things themselves: some, all, or none of the members of one class are also members of another class. Every subject that primary propositions refer to, and every relation among those subjects that they express, is of this kind.

With secondary propositions we find ourselves dealing with a different kind both of subject and of relation. The subjects we deal with are themselves propositions. So we may ask: can we regard these subjects, too, as _things_, and assign them, by analogy with the earlier case, to a universe of their own? Again, the relations among these propositions are relations of being true or false at the same time, not of being the same thing. When we express the connection between two different propositions, we do not say that one _is_ the other. Instead, depending on the meaning we want, we use forms of speech like these: "_Either_ the proposition \(X\) is true, _or_ the proposition \(Y\) is true"; "If the proposition \(X\) is true, the proposition \(Y\) is true"; "The propositions \(X\) and \(Y\) are jointly true"; and so on.

In considering relations like these, we do not need to explore the whole range of their possible meaning. That could draw us into metaphysical questions about causation, which lie beyond the proper limits of science. It is enough to find some meaning they certainly have, and which is adequate for logical deduction. Take as an example the conditional proposition "If the proposition \(X\) is true, the proposition \(Y\) is true." One meaning it certainly has is that the _time_ in which the proposition \(X\) is true is _time_ in which the proposition \(Y\) is true. This is only a relation of existing together, and it may or may not exhaust the meaning of the proposition. But it really is part of what the proposition states, and it is enough for every purpose of logical inference.

Everyday language supports this view, that secondary propositions are essentially connected with the notion of time. We limit a primary proposition with the word "some," but a secondary proposition with the word "sometimes." To say "Sometimes injustice triumphs" amounts to saying that there are times when the proposition "Injustice now triumphs" is true. There are, it is true, propositions whose truth is not limited to particular periods or occasions in this way. These are true through all time, and have been given the name "eternal truths." Every reader of Plato and Aristotle will know the distinction. Aristotle in particular uses it to contrast the abstract truths of science, such as the propositions of geometry, which are always true, with the chance relations of things as they appear, which are sometimes true and sometimes false. But the forms of language in which both kinds of proposition are expressed show that both depend on the idea of time: in the one case limited to some finite stretch, in the other stretched out to eternity.

6. Someone may object that in ordinary reasoning we are often quite unaware of this notion of time, even though it is built into the very language we use. The remark is fair, but it only shows that we usually reason with the help of words and the forms of a well-built language, without attending to the deeper grounds on which those forms were established. The course of this investigation will illustrate the same principle. I shall use the notion of time to work out the laws for expressing secondary propositions, and the laws by which the symbols that express them combine. But once those laws and forms are settled, the notion of time (essential, as I believe it is, for that purpose) can in practice be dropped. We can then pass from the forms of common language to the closely matching forms of the symbolic instrument of thought developed here, use its processes, and interpret its results, without being aware of the idea of time at all.

Proposition II.

7. To establish a system of notation for the expression of Secondary Propositions, and to show that the symbols which it involves are subject to the same laws of combination as the corresponding symbols employed in the expression of Primary Propositions.

Let the capital letters \(X\), \(Y\), \(Z\), stand for the elementary propositions about whose truth or falsehood we want to assert something, or among which we want to express some relation in the form of a secondary proposition. And let the matching small letters \(x\), \(y\), \(z\), stand for operations of the mind, in the following sense. Let \(x\) represent an act of the mind by which we fix our attention on that portion of time during which the proposition \(X\) is true; and let this be what we mean when we say that \(x\) _denotes_ the time during which the proposition \(X\) is true. Let us also use the connecting signs +, -, =, and so on, in the following sense. Let \(x+y\) stand for the total of the portions of time during which the propositions \(X\) and \(Y\) are respectively true, those times being entirely separate from each other. In the same way, let \(x-y\) stand for the time that is left when we take away, from the portion of time during which \(X\) is true, the portion during which \(Y\) is true, that portion being (by supposition) included in the first. And let \(x=y\) mean that the time during which the proposition \(X\) is true is the same as the time during which the proposition \(Y\) is true. We shall call \(x\) the _representative symbol_ of the proposition \(X\), and so on for the others.

From these definitions it follows that we shall always have
\[x + y = y + x,\]
since each side will stand for the same total of time.

Next, let \(xy\) represent performing one after the other the two operations represented by \(y\) and \(x\). That is, it stands for the whole mental operation made up of these steps. First, the mind selects the portion of time during which the proposition \(Y\) is true. Second, out of that portion of time, it selects whatever part of it is time during which the proposition \(X\) is true. The result of these two steps is to fix the mind's attention on the whole of the portion of time during which the propositions \(X\) and \(Y\) are both true.

From this definition it follows that we shall always have
\[xy = yx. \qquad (1)\]
For suppose we first mentally select the portion of time during which the proposition \(Y\) is true, and then, out of that, the part during which \(X\) is true. Or suppose we first select the portion of time during which the proposition \(X\) is true, and then, out of that, the part during which the proposition \(Y\) is true. Either way we reach the same final result: the portion of time during which the propositions \(X\) and \(Y\) are both true.

By carrying this reasoning further we can show that the laws by which the symbols \(x\), \(y\), \(z\), and so on, combine, under the interpretation given them here, are expressed in exactly the same way as the laws by which the same symbols combine under the interpretation given them in the first part of this book. The reason for this identity is plain. In both cases we are studying the operations of the same faculty, or the same combination of faculties. The essential character of those operations does not change, whether we suppose them to be at work on the universe of things, which contains all existence, or on the whole of time, in which all events take place and to some part of which, at least, every assertion, truth, and proposition refers.

So, besides the laws already stated, we shall have, by (4), Chap. II., the law whose expression is
\[x(y + z)=xy + xz; \qquad (2)\]
that is, the time when _X_ is true and either _Y_ or _Z_ is true is the time when _X_ and _Y_ are true together with the time when _X_ and _Z_ are true. And above all we shall have the fundamental law of duality, (2) Chap. II., whose expression is
\[x^2 = x, \quad \text{or,} \quad x (1 - x) = 0; \qquad (3)\]
that is, there is no time when _X_ is both true and not true. This law separates the system of thought in logic from the system of thought in the science of quantity, and it gives the processes of logic a completeness and generality they could not otherwise have.

8. Again, this law (3), like the other laws, is satisfied by the symbols \(0\) and \(1\). So we are led, as before, to ask whether those symbols can be given a meaning in the present system of thought. The same reasoning we followed before shows that they can, and justifies the following two statements.

First, in the expression of secondary propositions, \(0\) represents _nothing_ with respect to time; that is, no time at all.

Second, in the same system, \(1\) represents the universe, or the whole of time, that the discourse is taken to be about in any way.

In primary propositions the universe of discourse is sometimes limited to a small part of the actual universe of things, and sometimes is the whole of that universe. In the same way, in secondary propositions the universe of discourse may be limited to a single day, or to the passing moment, or it may take in the whole of time. It may, in the most literal sense, be "eternal." Indeed, unless the nature of the discourse states or implies some limit, the proper meaning of the symbol \(1\) in secondary propositions is "eternity," just as its proper meaning in the primary system is the whole universe of what actually exists.

9. Instead of using the symbols \(x\), \(y\), \(z\), to represent the truth of propositions, we could just as properly use them to represent the occurrence of events. In fact, the occurrence of an event both implies and is implied by the truth of a proposition, namely the proposition which asserts that the event occurs. Either meaning of the symbol \(x\) carries the other with it. It will be very convenient to be able to use our symbols in whichever of these two equivalent meanings the circumstances of a problem make most suitable, and I shall take that liberty whenever it is needed. In problems of pure logic I shall treat the symbols \(x\), \(y\), and so on, as representing elementary propositions, among which the premises express some relation. In the mathematical theory of probability, which, as I said before (I. 12), rests on a foundation of logic, and which this work goes on to treat in a later portion (in the chapters on probability, not included in this edition), I shall use the same symbols for the simple events whose implied or required frequency of occurrence is among the things that theory reckons with.

Editor's note: Boole reads the symbol of a secondary proposition as the portion of time during which that proposition is true. Modern logic drops the idea of time altogether. Instead it gives each proposition one of just two truth values, true or false, written 1 or 0, which is exactly where Boole's own algebra of 0 and 1 lands. This chapter is the ancestor of what is now called propositional logic.

Proposition III.

10. To deduce general Rules for the expression of Secondary Propositions.

In the questions that arise from this Proposition, full proof is less necessary, because they match exactly the questions we have already settled for primary propositions. We shall first consider how to express terms, and then how to express the propositions that connect them.

Since 1 stands for the whole of time, and \(x\) for the portion of it during which the proposition \(X\) is true, \(1 - x\) will stand for the portion of time during which the proposition \(X\) is false.

Again, since \(xy\) stands for the portion of time during which the propositions \(X\) and \(Y\) are both true, combining this with the last remark leads us to the following interpretations.

The expression \(x(1-y)\) will represent the time during which the proposition \(X\) is true and the proposition \(Y\) false. The expression \((1-x)(1-y)\) will represent the time during which the propositions \(X\) and \(Y\) are both false at once.

The expression \(x(1-y)+y(1-x)\) will express the time during which either \(X\) is true or \(Y\) true, but not both; for that time is the sum of the times when each is true on its own, without the other. The expression \(xy+(1-x)(1-y)\) will express the time during which \(X\) and \(Y\) are either both true or both false.

If another symbol \(z\) comes in, the same principles still apply. So \(xyz\) stands for the time when the propositions \(X\), \(Y\), and \(Z\) are all true at once; \((1-x)(1-y)(1-z)\) for the time when they are all false at once; and the sum of these two expressions would stand for the time when they are either all true together or all false together.

The general principles of interpretation at work in these examples need no further illustration or fuller statement.

11. We can now set out and study the laws for expressing propositions, case by case. First, though, I want to draw attention to one principle of fundamental importance. The principles of expression laid down so far are perfectly general. They let us limit our assertions that propositions are true or false to any particular parts of the whole of time that makes up the universe of our discourse, whether that whole is an unlimited eternity, or a period with a fixed beginning and end, or the passing moment. Yet in the actual practice of human reasoning such limits are not usually used. When we assert that a proposition is true, we generally mean that it is true throughout the whole of the time our discourse refers to. And when several assertions that propositions are simply true or false are made together as the premises of a logical proof, they all refer to the same universe of time, not to particular, limited parts of it. In the realm of necessary truth that the exact sciences study, every assertion of a truth may be the assertion of an "eternal truth." In reasoning about passing events (some state of affairs in society, for instance), each assertion may be tied directly to the present time, "Now." But in both cases, unless something is stated to the contrary, each separate proposition refers to the same stretch of time. The cases we then have to consider are the following.

First, _to express the proposition, "The proposition \(X\) is true."_

Here we must express that, within the limits of time to which the matter of our discourse is confined, the proposition \(X\) is true. The time during which the proposition \(X\) is true is denoted by \(x\), and the stretch of time our discourse refers to is represented by 1. So we have
\[x=1 \qquad (4)\]
as the expression required: the time when _X_ is true is the whole of time.

Second, _to express the proposition, "The proposition \(X\) is false."_

Here we must express that, within the limits of time our discourse refers to, the proposition \(X\) is false; in other words, that within those limits there is no portion of time during which it is true. The portion of time during which it is true is \(x\). So the equation required will be
\[x = 0. \qquad (5)\]
We could also get this result by setting the expression for the time during which the proposition \(X\) is false, namely \(1-x\), equal to the whole of time, 1. This gives
\[\begin{aligned} 1-x &= 1, \\ \text{whence } x &= 0. \end{aligned}\]

Third, to express the disjunctive proposition, "Either the proposition \(X\) is true or the proposition \(Y\) is true," where it is implied that the two propositions exclude each other, that is, that only one of them is true.

The time during which either the proposition \(X\) is true or the proposition \(Y\) is true, but not both, is represented by the expression \(x(1-y) + y(1-x)\). So we have
\[x(1-y) + y(1-x) = 1, \qquad (6)\]
as the equation required.

Suppose instead that in this proposition the words _either_, _or_, are not meant to be strictly exclusive, so that the propositions \(X\) and \(Y\) might both be true at once. Then we must add the term \(xy\) to the left-hand side of the equation above. We shall then have
\[\begin{gathered} xy + x(1-y) + (1-x)y = 1, \\ \text{or }x + (1 - x)y = 1. \qquad (7) \end{gathered}\]
that is, at every time either _X_ is true, or, if it is not, _Y_ is.

Fourth, _to express the conditional proposition, "If the proposition \(Y\) is true, the proposition \(X\) is true."_

Since whenever the proposition \(Y\) is true the proposition \(X\) is true, it is necessary and sufficient here to express that the time during which the proposition \(Y\) is true is time during which the proposition \(X\) is true. In other words, it is some indefinite portion of the whole time during which the proposition \(X\) is true. Now the time during which the proposition \(Y\) is true is \(y\), and the whole time during which the proposition \(X\) is true is \(x\). Let \(v\) be a symbol of time indefinite. Then \(vx\) will represent an indefinite portion of the whole time \(x\). So we shall have
\[y = vx\]
as the expression of the given proposition: the time when _Y_ is true is some of the time when _X_ is true.

12. When \(v\) is taken in this way as a symbol of time indefinite, \(vx\) may be understood to represent the whole of the time \(x\), or an indefinite part of it, or no part of it at all; for any of these meanings can be produced by fixing the arbitrary symbol \(v\) in a particular way. If \(v\) is fixed to represent a time that includes the whole time \(x\), then \(vx\) will represent the whole time \(x\). If \(v\) is fixed to represent a time some part of which lies inside the time \(x\), but which does not fill up that time, then \(vx\) will represent a part of the time \(x\). Lastly, if \(v\) is fixed to represent a time no part of which is shared with any part of the time \(x\), then \(vx\) will take the value 0, and will amount to "no time," or "never."

Now notice that the proposition "If \(Y\) is true, \(X\) is true" does not assert that either of the propositions \(X\) and \(Y\) is true. It fits equally well with several suppositions. It fits the supposition that the truth of the proposition \(Y\) is a condition the truth of the proposition \(X\) cannot do without; in that case we shall have \(v=1\). It fits the supposition that \(Y\) expresses a condition which, when it is met, assures us that \(X\) is true, but that \(X\) can be true without that condition being met; in that case \(v\) stands for a time some part of which lies within the whole time \(x\). And lastly it fits the supposition that the proposition \(Y\) is never true at all; in that case \(v\) represents some time no part of which is shared with any part of the time \(x\). All these cases are covered by the general supposition that \(v\) is a symbol of time indefinite.

Fifth, _to express a proposition that is both conditional and disjunctive_.

The general form of a conditional proposition is "If \(Y\) is true, \(X\) is true," and by the last section its expression is \(y = vx\). Following the usage we set up for primary propositions, we may properly call \(Y\) and \(X\) the terms of the conditional proposition they belong to. We may also adopt the language of ordinary logic, which calls the term \(Y\), the one the word _if_ is attached to, the "antecedent" of the proposition, and the term \(X\) the "consequent."

Now suppose that the terms, instead of being simple propositions as in the case above, are each, or either of them, disjunctive propositions, joining different terms with the words _either_, _or_. Here are some examples, in which \(X\), \(Y\), \(Z\), and so on, stand for simple propositions.

First, if either \(X\) is true or \(Y\) is true, then \(Z\) is true.

Second, if \(X\) is true, then either \(Y\) is true or \(Z\) true.

Third, if either \(X\) is true or \(Y\) is true, then either \(Z\) and \(W\) are both true, or they are both false.

Clearly, in these cases the relation of the antecedent to the consequent is not affected by the fact that one or both of those terms is disjunctive. So all we need to do is find, by the principles already established, the proper expressions for the antecedent and the consequent, attach the indefinite symbol \(v\) to the consequent, and set the two equal. For the propositions above, this gives the following equations, in order:
\[\begin{aligned} &\text{1st. }x(1 - y) + (1 - x)y = vz. \\ &\text{2nd. }x = v \{y(1-z)+z(1-y)\}. \\ &\text{3rd. }x (1 - y) + y (1 - x) = v \{ zw + (1 - z) (1 - w) \} \end{aligned}\]
The rule shown here applies in general.

One can imagine cases in which the disjunctive and conditional elements enter a compound proposition in some other way than this. But I do not know of any that the natural needs of human reasoning ever actually present to us, so I shall not discuss them. Leaving them out will cause no serious difficulty. The principles underlying the applications above are perfectly general, and a little thought will adapt them to any case one can imagine.

13. The laws of expression just stated already contain the laws of interpretation. The equation
\[x= 1\]
must be understood to say that the proposition \(X\) is true; the equation
\[x = 0,\]
that the proposition \(X\) is false. The equation
\[xy= 1\]
will say that the propositions \(X\) and \(Y\) are both true together; and the equation
\[xy= 0\]
that they are not both true together.

In the same way the equations
\[\begin{aligned} x(1-y) + y(1-x) &= 1, \\ x (1 - y) + y (1 - x) &= 0, \end{aligned}\]
will assert, respectively, the truth and the falsehood of the disjunctive proposition "Either \(X\) is true or \(Y\) is true." The equations
\[\begin{aligned} y &= vx \\ y &= v(1-x) \end{aligned}\]
will express, respectively, the propositions "If the proposition \(Y\) is true, the proposition \(X\) is true" and "If the proposition \(Y\) is true, the proposition \(X\) is false."

In the chapters that follow we shall often meet a case in which some terms on one side of an equation have the indefinite symbol \(v\) attached and others do not. The following example will illustrate it. Suppose we have
\[y = xz + vx (1 - z).\]
This says that the time during which the proposition \(Y\) is true is made up of all the time during which \(X\) and \(Z\) are true together, plus an indefinite portion of the time during which \(X\) is true and \(Z\) false. From this we can see, first, that if \(Y\) is true, then either \(X\) and \(Z\) are true together, or \(X\) is true and \(Z\) false; and second, that if \(X\) and \(Z\) are true together, \(Y\) is true. The second of these may be called the reverse interpretation. It takes the antecedent from the right-hand side of the equation, and the consequent from the left-hand side. What makes this reverse reading possible is that the right-hand side has a term whose coefficient is unity (that is, 1). The general principle involved may be stated as follows.

14. Principle.—Any constituent term or terms in a particular member of an equation which have for their coefficient unity, may be taken as the antecedent of a proposition, of which all the terms in the other member form the consequent.

In plainer words: any compartment on one side of an equation that has 1 in front of it can serve as the "if" part of a proposition whose "then" part is everything on the other side. So the equation
\[y = xz + vx (1 - z) + (1 - x) (1 - z)\]
would have the following interpretations.

Direct Interpretation.—If the proposition \(Y\) is true, then either \(X\) and \(Z\) are true, or \(X\) is true and \(Z\) false, or \(X\) and \(Z\) are both false.

Reverse Interpretation.—_If either \(X\) and \(Z\) are true, or \(X\) and \(Z\) are false, \(Y\) is true_.

These partial interpretations, taken together, express the whole meaning of the given equation.

15. Let me repeat a remark made earlier. The idea of time seems to be an essential element in the theory of how secondary propositions are interpreted. Yet in practice it can be set aside as soon as the laws of expression and of interpretation have been firmly established. Indeed, the forms those laws produce seem to match the forms of a perfect language. Imagine any known language freed from its idioms and stripped of everything superfluous. Then imagine expressing some proposition in that language in the simplest and most literal way, the way most faithful to the principles of pure and universal thought on which all languages are founded, which all of them display, but from which all of them have strayed more or less. Passing from such a language to the notation of algebra would mean nothing more than swapping one set of signs for another, with no essential change of form or character. For the elements among which a relation is expressed, whether things or propositions, we would write letters; for the disjunctive conjunction "or" we would write +; for the connecting copula, the sign of relation, we would write =. I need not follow this analogy further. How real and complete it is will become clearer as we study the forms of expression that turn up in later applications of this theory, and compare them more directly with that imperfect yet noble instrument of thought, the English language.

16. Before leaving the subject of this chapter, I want to add a few remarks on the general analogy between the theory of Primary Propositions and that of Secondary Propositions.

We could no doubt have founded the theory of Primary Propositions on the simple notion of space, in the same way that the theory of secondary propositions has been founded on the notion of time. If we had, the analogy we are considering might have agreed a little more closely with the view of those who regard space and time as merely "forms of the human understanding," conditions of knowledge that the very make-up of the mind imposes on everything it takes in. But this view cannot be proved. And it ties us to accepting "place," τὸ ποῦ (the Greek "where"), as an essential category of existence. Whether it is one or not lies, I suspect, beyond the reach of our faculties. But it can be shown, and I believe it has been shown, that the formal processes of reasoning in primary propositions do not require, as an essential condition, that the things we reason about appear in space. Those processes would apply just as strictly to forms of existence, if there are any, that lie beyond the realm of things extended in space. Something perhaps similar happens in many known examples in geometry and dynamics: we can set out the formal analysis of problems that rest on some intellectual conception of space different from the one our senses present, or the one our imagination can picture. So I believe that the idea of space is not essential to developing a theory of primary propositions. But I am inclined to think, though I wish to speak cautiously on a question so very difficult, that the idea of time is essential to establishing a theory of secondary propositions. There seem to be grounds for thinking that space might have appeared to the human mind differently from the way it does, without any change in the faculties involved in _reasoning_. There do not seem to be the same grounds, at least, for supposing that time could have appeared to us otherwise than it does. But let me set these speculations aside, since they may not be entirely free from presumption. Let it simply be affirmed that the real reason the symbol \(1\) represents, in primary propositions, the universe of things and not the space they occupy is this: the sign of identity \(=\), joining the two sides of the equations, implies that the things the two sides represent are identical, not merely that they are found in the same portion of space. Let it be affirmed, in the same way, that the reason the symbol \(1\) in secondary propositions represents, not the universe of events, but the eternity in whose successive moments and periods they unfold, is this: the same sign of identity, joining the logical sides of the equations, implies, not that the events the two sides represent are identical, but that the times at which they occur are the same. These reasons seem to me to settle the immediate question of interpretation. In an earlier treatise on this subject (Mathematical Analysis of Logic, p. 49), I followed Wallis's theory of the Reduction of Hypothetical Propositions, and was led to interpret the symbol \(1\) in secondary propositions as the universe of "cases" or "conjunctures of circumstances." But that view requires us to define what a "case," or a "conjuncture of circumstances," means; and whatever that term contains beyond the notion of time is certainly foreign to the aims of formal logic, and restricts its processes.

Footnote: Our perception presents space to us as having three dimensions: length, breadth, and depth. But in a large class of problems, those about the properties of curved surfaces, the rotation of solid bodies about axes, the vibrations of elastic media, and so on, this limit to three looks arbitrary in the mathematical working. If one paid attention only to the processes of solution, one could find no reason why space should not exist in four dimensions, or in any greater number. The intellectual procedure in the imaginary world this suggests can be grasped by the clear light of analogy.
