Chapter 22: On the Nature of Science, and the Constitution of the Intellect
(Part 2 of 2)

8. Human opinion is extremely arbitrary, so no one will expect, and I do not claim, that the forms described above are the only ones in which speculative minds have shaped their guesses at the problem of existence. Under particular influences other forms of doctrine have arisen, though these, not infrequently, are the same forms in disguise. But the theories we have looked at are very widespread, and they bear a plain likeness to the laws of thought as they have been expressed in this book. Together, these two facts may fairly be taken to point to a connection between the two systems. Every other act and procedure of the mind is open to its own particular fallacies. In the same way, the working of the law of thought that this book has called the law of duality may have its own particular tendency to error. That tendency would be to inflate a mere lack of agreement into a real opposition. The mind necessarily sees the world as made of parts that complete one another; this error would turn that view into the conception of a world divided at its root between opposing powers. Such a tendency, together with some large but hasty generalizations from the phenomena, may have been the origin of dualism, quite apart from the question whether dualism, in any form, is a true theory or not. Here, however, it matters more to look closely at how these ancient forms of speculation, revived in our own day, bear on the progress of real knowledge. On this point, following what was said in the previous article, I want to add these remarks:

First, all sound philosophy rules against such speculations, if they are offered as a way of finding out how things are actually made. It may be that the progress of natural knowledge is moving toward the recognition of some central Unity in Nature. There can be little doubt of one kind of unity, the kind that consists in the parts of a system being related to one another. And able men have speculated, not without grounds, that the physical forces are bound together more closely than the mere idea of a system would lead us to guess. It may further be that, within that supposed unity, there are some general principles of division and reunion; that these, under the Supreme Will, are the sources of much of the _related_ variety of Nature. The cases of sex and of polarity have been offered in support of such a view. And I will venture to add, as a supposition, that it is not very unlikely that in some such way the make-up of things outside us corresponds to the make-up of the mind within. But if such a correspondence is ever proved to exist, it will come as the last generalization of human knowledge, not as the first principle of scientific inquiry. The natural order of discovery runs from the particular to the universal. And we can say with confidence that we have not yet gone far enough along that road to tell what the ultimate forms are, into which all the special differences of Nature will merge, and from which they will receive their explanation.

Second, suppose this correspondence between the forms of thought and the actual make-up of Nature were proved. Whatever connection or relation it might be thought to set up between the two systems, it would not touch the question of their independence from each other in the least. It would in no way follow that one system is merely the _product_ of the other. Too great a taste for metaphysical speculation seems, in some cases, to have bred a leaning toward exactly this kind of illusion. Take the many attempts that have been made to explain the existence of evil. One of them tries to give evil a merely _relative_ character, to found it on a kind of logical opposition to good, which is treated as equally relative. It is enough to say that the assumption is entirely groundless. What evil may be in the eyes of infinite wisdom and purity we can, at best, only dimly guess. But to us, in all its forms, whether pain or lack, or moral wrongdoing, or the woe that comes as its punishment, it can wear only one face: that of a sad and stern reality. Against it the whole preventive force of our nature may rightly be brought to bear, and on grounds that go somewhat beyond even the highest kind of prudence. What has been said on this particular question applies equally to many other disputed points of philosophy, such as whether space and time are real outside us. We have no right to reduce these to mere forms of the understanding, though they certainly mark out the present range of our knowledge. And, to speak more generally, there is no warrant for the extremely _subjective_ leaning of much modern speculation. Whenever several hypotheses are, as far as the intellect can see, equally consistent with an observed fact, the instinctive testimony of consciousness about their relative worth must be allowed to carry _authority_.

Third, the study of the laws of thought cannot tell us how things are actually made. Nor can it explain the facts bound up with that make-up which have puzzled the wise and saddened the thoughtful in every age. Still less, then, can it lift us above the present conditions of our being, or lend its support to the doctrine that we can have an _intuitive_ knowledge of the infinite and the unconditioned, whether that knowledge is sought in the realm of Nature or above it. We can never be said to _comprehend_ something that is presented to thought as the limit of an endless process of abstraction. A progression _ad infinitum_ (going on without end) is impossible for finite powers. But though we cannot comprehend the infinite, there may be even scientific grounds for believing that human nature is made in some relation to it. We cannot perfectly express the laws of thought, or set out in the most general sense the methods built on them, without at least implying the elements that ordinary language names by the words “Universe” and “Eternity.” In the pure abstractions of geometry, and in the domain of logic too, we see that the empire of Truth is in a certain sense larger than the empire of Imagination. There are many special fields of knowledge that can only be fully surveyed from a point outside them. In the same way, the theory of the processes of the intellect, even though it applies only to finite objects, seems to require us to recognise a sphere of thought from which all limits are taken away. So, on the one hand, we cannot find in the laws of thought and their analogies enough proof for the conclusions of a too daring mysticism. On the other hand, we would be wrong to treat them as suggesting nothing at all. They are parts of our intellectual nature, and it seems not unlikely that they make their presence felt in other ways than by simply laying down the conditions of formal inference. Whatever grounds we have for linking them with the particular bent of physical speculation among the Ionian and Italic philosophers, the same grounds exist for linking them with a cast of mind that is both more common and more legitimate. At least we should not put down to chance influences that meditative spirit which delights most in communing with the outward grandeur of Nature at the very moment it is most aware of truths that last forever. It is the spirit that reads in the night sky a bright display of order; or that feels, in some wild scene among the hills, hints of something more than that abstract eternity which had rolled away before ever their dark foundations were laid.

9. I will not follow further a line of thought that may seem to some too speculative. Instead, let us briefly review the positive results we have reached. We have seen that there are faculties in our nature that let us climb from the particular facts of experience to the general propositions on which Science is based. There are also faculties whose task is to deduce, from general propositions accepted as true, the particular conclusions they contain. We have seen that these faculties, in their working, are subject to laws that can be expressed with scientific precision. But those laws carry an authority of their own: compared with the authority of the laws of nature, it is distinct, _sui generis_ (one of a kind), and not borrowed from anything else. Further, there has appeared to be a clear fitness between the procedure of the intellect that has thus come to light and the conditions of the system of things around us. By those conditions I mean such facts as these: that there are species linked by general likenesses, and facts grouped under general laws; and that permanence is joined with order, which both gives stability to the knowledge we have gained and lays a foundation for the hope of endless progress. Human nature, quite apart from the tendencies it is seen to show or that it displays, turns out to be _constituted_ in a certain relation to Truth. And this relation, taken as a subject of speculative knowledge, can be studied in its details just as the several fields of physical science can, taken in the same light; and it is just as worthy of being studied. I would especially draw attention to one view of how the intellect is constituted. On this view it is subject to laws that are fixed in their character but do not work by force of necessity. It is freed from the rule of fate without being abandoned to the lawlessness of chance. We cannot accept this view without accepting, at least as _probable_, what it seems to hint, by analogy, about another and higher side of our nature. That side is its subjection, in the sphere of duty as in the sphere of knowledge, to fixed laws whose authority does not consist in power; it is the way our nature is made with reference to an ideal standard and a final purpose. It has been thought, it is true, that scientific work breeds a habit of mind that either overlooks the specific differences between the moral world and the material one, or treats the moral world as no proper subject for exact knowledge. No doubt every exclusive pursuit tends to produce one-sided views. And a mind that has long and deeply absorbed itself in scenes where the rule of physical necessity is unquestioned and supreme may find it hard to admit that another order of things is possible. But it is the _exclusiveness_ of this devotion to one sphere of knowledge that lets the prejudice take hold of the mind, if it takes hold at all. Apply scientific methods to the phenomena of the intellect, in an impartial spirit of inquiry, and without overlooking the elements of error and disturbance in our nature, which must be accepted as _facts_ though they cannot be counted as _laws_. Such a study seems to supply the materials of a truer analogy.

10. If anyone asks what _practical_ end inquiries like these point to, the answer is that there are various matters in which people's actions are decided mainly by their speculative views of human nature. Education, taken in its widest sense, is one of them. Every inquiry into its nature and its methods must rest, finally, on some prior theory of what man is: what his various faculties were designed for, and what motives have the power to move them to sustained action and to draw out their most perfect and most lasting results. It may be doubted whether these questions have ever been considered both fully and impartially in the light suggested here. Seen from this wider view of our nature, the highest training of taste through the study of the pure models of antiquity, and the widest acquaintance with the facts and theories of modern physical science, can only appear as parts of a complete intellectual training. Looking at the means to be used from the same point of view, we might be led to ask a question. Our age appeals almost entirely to the spirit of rivalry or of greed. Does that not tend to weaken the influence of more lasting motives, which seem to have been planted in our nature for this very end? On these and many other questions (the proper limits of authority; how to reconcile freedom of thought with the discipline of feelings, habits and manners; and the whole _moral_ side of the subject) what unsettled opinion, what variety of practice, we meet with! And yet, in the sober judgment of reason, nothing within the reach of human effort carries more weight and importance than this, taken, as I have said, in its widest meaning. Now, whatever makes our view of human nature more exact and definite, in any of its real aspects, tends in the same degree to bring these questions within narrower bounds and to narrow the range of their possible answers. In this way even speculative inquiries may prove rich in the most important principles of action.

11. Perhaps the most plainly legitimate bearing of such speculations is on the question of the place of mathematics in the system of human knowledge, and of the nature and use of mathematical studies as a training of the mind. No one who has followed recent debates can think this an unimportant question. Those who have held that mathematics has a fundamental place in both respects have drawn one of their strongest arguments from the way things actually are made. The material world is subject in all its parts to the relations of number. All actions, whether mechanical, chemical, electrical or thermal, seem not only to be measurable in themselves but to be linked to one another by numerical relations of a perfectly definite kind, even to the point of turning one into another. But that opinion seems to me to rest on a deeper basis than this. The laws of thought, in all its processes of conception and of reasoning, in all the operations of which language is the expression or the tool, are of the same kind as the laws of the recognised processes of mathematics. I do not claim that we need to know those laws in order to think coherently, or, in the ordinary sense of the words, to reason well. People draw inferences without any awareness of the elements on which the whole procedure depends. Still less do I wish to raise the reasoning faculty above the faculties of observation, of reflection and of judgment. But human thought, traced to its ultimate elements, shows itself in mathematical forms. On that very ground we may presume that the mathematical sciences hold, by the way our nature is made, a fundamental place in human knowledge, and that no system of mental training can be complete or fundamental which leaves them out altogether.

But the very same considerations show, just as forcefully, the error of those who treat the study of mathematics and its applications as a sufficient basis either for knowledge or for training. If the material world is made mathematically, it is not made only so. The mind, in its power of formal reasoning, obeys mathematical laws, whether it knows it or not. But through its other powers, of feeling and of action, through its sense of beauty and of moral fitness, through its deep springs of emotion and affection, it claims a relation to a different order of things. There is, besides, a breadth of intellectual vision, a power of sympathy with truth in all its forms and appearances, which is not measured by the strength and subtlety of the faculty of argument. Even the revelation of the material universe, in its boundless size, its all-pervading order and the constancy of its laws, is not necessarily grasped most fully by the man who has traced with the minutest accuracy the steps of the great demonstration. And if we take into our view the interests and duties of life, how little do any processes of mere reasoning help us to understand the weightier questions they raise! Training the mathematical or deductive faculty is truly a part of intellectual training; and it is just as truly only a part. The prejudice that would either banish any one field of knowledge or faculty of mind, or make it supreme, shows more than an error of judgment. It shows a lack of that intellectual modesty which cannot be separated from a pure devotion to truth. It takes upon itself the right to criticise a constitution of things that no human decree set up and none can repeal. It sets aside the ancient and just conception of truth as one though manifold. Much of this error, as it actually exists among us, seems to come from the specialised and isolated character of scientific teaching, a character which the error, in its turn, tends to encourage. The study of philosophy, despite a few notable exceptions, has failed to keep pace with the advance of the several fields of knowledge, though it is the task of philosophy to settle how those fields are related to one another. Yet we cannot help seeing this particular evil as part of a larger system. It is connected with the too common view of knowledge as a merely worldly thing, and with the undue dominance, already mentioned, of motives founded on regard for knowledge's worldly advantages, motives that are legitimate within their proper limits. In the extreme case it is easy to see what must happen. If such motives keep working, unchecked by any higher principles of action and uncorrected by the personal influence of greater minds, they must tend to lower the standard of thought about the objects of knowledge, and to make empty and useless whatever elements of a noble faith may still survive. And to the degree that these conditions are realised, the same effects must always follow. Perhaps this is why we sometimes find truer conceptions of the unity of the different parts of Truth, of their living connection, and of their subordination to a moral purpose, among those who acknowledge nothing higher than the changing face of collective humanity, than among those who profess an intellectual allegiance to the Father of Lights. But these questions cannot be pursued further here. To some they will seem foreign to the stated design of this work. But they have arisen naturally, either out of the speculations that design involved, or in the course of the reading and reflection that seemed necessary to carry it out.
