Chapter 5: Principles of Symbolic Reasoning

1. The chapters so far have done three things. They have investigated the fundamental laws of the operations of the mind in reasoning. They have shown how those laws become the laws of the symbols of logic. And they have set out the principles of expression by which one kind of proposition, the kind called primary, can be written in the language of symbols. All of this was, in the strictest sense, preparation. But it is preparation we cannot do without, because it leads to one of the chief aims of this book: to build a system, or method, of logic on an exact summary of the fundamental laws of thought. Before we start, I think it necessary to point out certain things about the nature of this aim and about the means of reaching it.

2. My first point is that a method in logic can be general only so far as its elementary processes and laws are general. Take one example. In the earlier sections we investigated, among other things, the laws of the logical process of _addition_, the process symbolized by the sign \(+\). We found those laws by studying instances. In every one of those instances it was a necessary condition that the classes or things added together in thought should be mutually exclusive. Indeed the expression \(x + y\) seems to make no sense unless we assume that the things represented by \(x\) and the things represented by \(y\) are entirely separate, with no individuals in common. Conditions much like this one were also present in the acts of conception from which we learned the laws of the other symbolic operations. So a question arises. Must we restrict our use of these symbolic laws and processes by the same conditions of interpretability under which we first learned them? If we must, then plainly no general method in logic is possible. If we need not, then how are we to think about processes that seem to have no meaning in the very field of thought they are meant to serve? These questions do not belong to logic alone. They apply equally to every developed form of human reasoning that works through a symbolic language.

3. My second point is that this apparent failure of process and interpretation to match up never shows itself in the _ordinary_ uses of human reason. There, no operation is performed whose meaning and use are not seen. Most minds are not satisfied when merely formal reasoning links their premises to their conclusions. Every step of the chain, every intermediate result established along the way, must make sense too. And no doubt this is both how ordinary reasoning actually works and an important safeguard in the reasoning and talk of everyday life.

Perhaps many people would want to extend the same principle to the general use of symbolic language as a tool of reasoning. They might argue like this. The laws or axioms that govern our use of symbols were established by studying only the cases in which interpretation is possible. So we have no right to apply them to other cases, where interpretation is impossible or doubtful, even if (as should be admitted) we apply them only in the intermediate steps of a proof. If this objection were conclusive, we would have to admit that a symbolic method in logic offers little advantage. Perhaps the advantage would shrink to the mechanical gain of using short, handy symbols in place of clumsier ones. But the objection itself is mistaken. Whatever we might expect _à priori_, it is an unquestionable fact that a conclusion reached by any symbolic process of reasoning is valid whether or not we can interpret the formal results that turned up at the different stages along the way. There are, in fact, certain general principles about the use of symbolic methods. I shall first state them as they apply to logic in particular, and then offer some remarks on their nature and on why they deserve to be accepted.

4. The conditions of valid reasoning with the help of symbols are these.

First, a fixed interpretation must be given to the symbols used to express the data, and the laws by which those symbols combine must be correctly worked out from that interpretation.

Second, the formal processes of solution or proof must be carried out from start to finish in obedience to all the laws found in this way, without asking whether the particular results obtained along the way can be interpreted.

Third, the final result must be interpretable in form, and it must actually be interpreted according to the same system of interpretation that was used to express the data. On these principles the following observations may be made.

5. The need for a fixed interpretation of the symbols has already been discussed enough (II. 3). The need for the final result to be in a form to which that interpretation can be applied rests on an obvious principle. Symbols are a means to an end, and the end is knowledge of some fact or truth we can understand. For that end to be reached, the final result, which expresses the symbolic conclusion, must be in an interpretable form. The greatest difficulty, however, is likely to be felt over the second of the general principles or conditions above (V. 4), and on this point a few more words are needed.

I would say, then, that this principle may be seen as resting on a general law of the mind. We do not know that law _à priori_, that is, before experience. We learn it the way we learn the other laws of the mind: by seeing the general principle clearly displayed in a particular instance. Take a single example of reasoning in which symbols are used according to laws founded on their interpretation, but without keeping that interpretation constantly in view, so that the chain of proof leads us through intermediate steps that cannot be interpreted to a final result that can. Such an example seems not only to establish that this particular application is valid, but also to reveal the general law at work in it. No pile of further instances can properly add weight to that evidence. More instances may give us a clearer idea of the common element of truth on which the principle rests, and so prepare us to accept it. Where someone does not feel the direct force of the evidence, they may serve as a check, _à posteriori_, that the principle works in practice. But none of this touches the point I am making: the general principle must be seen in the particular instance, seen to be general in its reach as well as true in the special example. The use of the uninterpretable symbol \(\sqrt{-1}\) in the intermediate steps of trigonometry illustrates what I mean. I believe there is no way of explaining that use which does not quietly assume the very principle in question. That principle is not, I think, justified by formal reasoning from any other grounds. But it seems to deserve a place among those self-evident truths which in some sense make general knowledge possible at all, and which may rightly be regarded as expressions of the mind's own laws and make-up.

6. Here is how this principle will be applied in the present work. We have seen that any system of propositions can be expressed by equations in symbols \(x\), \(y\), \(z\). Wherever interpretation is possible, these symbols obey laws identical in form with the laws of a system of number symbols that can take only the values \(0\) and \(1\) (II. 15). But the formal processes of reasoning depend only on the laws of the symbols, not on what the symbols are taken to mean. So we are allowed to treat the symbols \(x\), \(y\), \(z\) as if they were number symbols of exactly that kind. In practice, we may set aside the logical meaning of the symbols in a given equation; turn them into number symbols that can take only the values \(0\) and \(1\); carry out on them, as numbers, every process the solution requires; and at the end give them back their logical meaning. This is the procedure actually used in this book, though I shall not stop each time to restate what kind of change has been made. When the symbols \(x\), \(y\), \(z\) are treated as numbers of this kind, the processes we apply to them are not bound by the conditions of thought that would bind them if they were applied to purely logical symbols. That gives us a freedom of operation without which the search for a general method in logic would be hopeless.

Now this system of processes would lead us to nothing intelligible unless the final equations it produced were in a form that could be interpreted once the symbols had their logical meaning back. There is, however, a general method of bringing equations into such a form, and the rest of this chapter is devoted to it. I shall say little here about how the method makes interpretation possible, since that question is kept for the next chapter. Instead I shall confine myself mostly to the process itself, which may be called a process of "development." Before describing it, a few observations will help.

7. Suppose we are looking at some class of things with one question in mind: which of its members have a certain property \(x\), and which lack it? Every individual in the class either has the property or does not. So we can divide the class into two portions: those individuals that have the property, and those that do not. We can make this division in thought before we know anything else about how the class is made up. Whatever else we later learn can only tell us, more or less precisely, what further conditions hold for the portion that has the property and for the portion that lacks it. Suppose, then, we learn this much: the members that have the property \(x\) also have a certain property \(u\), and these two conditions together are enough to define them. Then we can represent that portion of the original class by the expression \(ux\) (II. 6), that is, the things that are both _u_ and _x_. Suppose we also learn that the members of the original class that do not have the property \(x\) satisfy a condition \(v\), and are defined by it. Then clearly those members will be represented by the expression \(v \left( 1 -x \right)\), the things that are _v_ and not _x_. So the class as a whole will be represented by
\[ux + v \left( 1 - x \right);\]
in words, the _x_'s that are _u_, together with the not-_x_'s that are _v_. This may be regarded as a general developed form for expressing any class of objects considered with respect to whether they have or lack a given property \(x\).

The general form we have just reached on purely logical grounds can also be derived by a separate route, from the formal laws that the symbols \(x\), \(y\), \(z\) obey equally under their logical and under their numerical interpretation, as mentioned above (V. 6).

8. _Definition_.—Any algebraic expression containing a symbol \(x\) is called a function of \(x\), and may be written in the short general form \(f\left(x\right)\). Any expression containing two symbols, \(x\) and \(y\), is likewise called a function of \(x\) and \(y\), and may be written in the general form \(f\left(x, y\right)\); and so on for any number of symbols.

So the form \(f\left(x\right)\) could stand for any of the following functions, for instance \(x\), \(1-x\), \(\frac{1+x}{1-x}\), and so on; and \(f\left(x,y\right)\) could stand equally for any of the forms \(x + y\), \(x - 2y\), \(\frac{x + y}{x - 2y}\), and so on.

By the same notation, if in any function \(f\left(x\right)\) we replace \(x\) by \(1\), the result is written \(f\left(1\right)\); if in the same function we replace \(x\) by \(0\), the result is written \(f\left(0\right)\). For example, if \(f\left(x\right)\) stands for the function \(\frac{a + x}{a - 2x}\), then \(f\left(1\right)\) will stand for \(\frac{a + 1}{a - 2}\), and \(f\left(0\right)\) will stand for \(\frac{a}{a}\).

9. _Definition_.—Take any function \(f\left(x\right)\) in which \(x\) is a logical symbol, or a number symbol that can take only the values \(0\) and \(1\). The function is said to be developed when it has been brought into the form \(ax + b\left(1-x\right)\), with \(a\) and \(b\) chosen so that the result is equivalent to the function we started from. In words, a developed function says how much of it goes with the _x_'s (the coefficient _a_) and how much with the not-_x_'s (the coefficient _b_).

This definition assumes that any function \(f\left(x\right)\) can be written in this form. The following Proposition shows that the assumption is justified.

Proposition I.

10. To develop any function \(f\left(x\right)\) in which \(x\) is a logical symbol.

By the principle laid down in this chapter, we may lawfully treat \(x\) as a number symbol that can take only the values \(0\) and \(1\).

Let us assume, then, that
\[f\left(x\right) = ax + b \left(1 - x\right),\]
and put \(x = 1\). The second term vanishes, because one minus one is zero, and we have
\[f\left(1\right) = a.\]
Next, in the same equation, put \(x = 0\). Now the first term vanishes, and we have
\[f\left(0\right) = b.\]
So the values of _a_ and _b_ are found. Substituting them in the first equation, we get
\[f\left(x\right) = f\left(1\right) x + f\left(0\right)\left(1-x\right); \qquad (1)\]
which is the development we were looking for. Read aloud, it says: the function equals what it becomes when _x_ is 1, multiplied by _x_, plus what it becomes when _x_ is 0, multiplied by one minus _x_. The right-hand side of this equation correctly represents the function \(f\left(x\right)\), whatever form that function may have. The reason is simple. Regarded as a number symbol, \(x\) can take only the values \(0\) and \(1\), and for each of these values the development
\[f\left(1\right)x + f\left(0\right) \left(1-x\right),\]
takes the same value as the function \(f\left(x\right)\). When _x_ is 1, it reduces to the first coefficient; when _x_ is 0, to the second; and those are exactly the values of the function itself.

Footnote: Some readers may be interested to notice that the development of \(f\left(x\right)\) found in this chapter holds, in the logical system, exactly the place that the expansion of \(f\left(x\right)\) in ascending powers of \(x\) holds in ordinary algebra. In fact it can be obtained by taking the well-known theorem of Taylor, namely \[f\left(x\right) = f\left(0\right) + f^{\prime}\left(0\right)x + f^{\prime\prime}\left(0\right)\frac{x^2}{1\cdot{}2} + f^{\prime\prime\prime}\left(0\right)\frac{x^3}{1\cdot2\cdot3}, \text{ \&c.} \qquad (1)\] and bringing in the condition \(x (1 - x) = 0\). From that condition we find \(x^2 = x\), \(x^3 = x\), and so on, and so \[f\left(x\right) = f\left(0\right) + \left\{f' \left(0\right) + \frac{f'' \left(0\right)}{1 \cdot 2} + \frac{f''' \left(0\right)}{1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3} + \text{ \&c.}\right\} x. \qquad (2)\] But putting \(x=1\) in (1), we get \[f(1) = f(0) + f'(0) + \frac{f''(0)}{1 \cdot 2} + \frac{f'''(0)}{1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3} + \text{ \&c.}\] so that \[f'(0) + \frac{f''(0)}{1 \cdot 2} + \text{ \&c.} = f(1) - f(0),\] and (2) becomes, on substitution, \[\begin{gathered} f(x) = f(0) + \{f(1)-f(0)\} x, \\ = f(1)x + f(0)(1-x), \end{gathered}\] which is the form in question. This proof assumes that \(f\left(x\right)\) can be expanded in a series of ascending powers of \(x\), so it is less general than the one in the text.

As an illustration, suppose we are asked to develop the function \(\frac{1+x}{1+2x}\). When \(x=1\), we find \(f\left(1\right) = \frac{2}{3}\), and when \(x = 0\), we find \(f\left(0\right) = \frac{1}{1}\), or \(1\). So the required expression is
\[\frac{1+x}{1+2x} = \frac{2}{3}x + 1 - x ~;\]
and this equation holds for each of the values the symbol \(x\) can take. (Try it: with _x_ equal to 1 both sides come to two-thirds, and with _x_ equal to 0 both sides come to 1.)

Proposition II.

To expand or develop a function of any number of logical symbols.

Let us begin with the case of two symbols, \(x\) and \(y\), and let us write the function to be developed as \(f\left(x, y\right)\).

First, we treat \(f\left(x, y\right)\) as a function of \(x\) alone and expand it by the general theorem (1). This gives
\[f\left(x,y\right) = f\left(1,y\right)x + f\left(0,y\right)\left(1-x\right); \qquad (2)\]
where \(f\left(1, y\right)\) stands for what the given function becomes when, in place of \(x\), we write \(1\) in it; and \(f\left(0,y\right)\) stands for what the same function becomes when, in place of \(x\), we write \(0\) in it.

Now we take the coefficient \(f\left(1, y\right)\), treat it as a function of \(y\), and expand it in the same way. We get
\[f\left(1, y\right) = f\left(1, 1\right)y + f\left(1, 0\right)\left(1 - y\right), \qquad (3)\]
where \(f\left(1, 1\right)\) stands for what \(f\left(1, y\right)\) becomes when \(y\) is set equal to \(1\), and \(f\left(1, 0\right)\) for what \(f\left(1, y\right)\) becomes when \(y\) is set equal to \(0\).

In the same way, the coefficient \(f\left(0, y\right)\) expands to
\[f\left(0, y\right) = f\left(0, 1\right)y + f\left(0, 0\right)\left(1 - y\right). \qquad (4)\]
Now put into (2), in place of \(f\left(1, y\right)\) and \(f\left(0, y\right)\), their values from (3) and (4), and we have
\[\begin{gathered} f\left(x, y\right) = f\left(1, 1\right)xy + f\left(1, 0\right)x\left(1 - y \right) + f\left(0, 1\right)\left(1 - x \right)y \qquad (5) \\ + f\left(0, 0\right)\left(1 - x \right)\left(1 - y \right), \qquad (6) \end{gathered}\]
which is the expansion required. Here \(f\left(1, 1\right)\) stands for what \(f\left(x, y\right)\) becomes when we set \(x=1\), \(y=1\) in it; \(f\left(1, 0\right)\) for what \(f\left(x, y\right)\) becomes when we set \(x=1\), \(y=0\); and so on for the rest. In words, the four terms sort everything into four compartments: things that are _x_ and _y_; things that are _x_ but not _y_; things that are _y_ but not _x_; and things that are neither. Each compartment is multiplied by the value the function takes when _x_ and _y_ are set to 1 or 0 to match it.

So, for example, if \(f\left(x, y\right)\) stands for the function \(\frac{1-x}{1-y}\), we find
\[f\left(1, 1\right) = \frac{0}{0},\qquad f\left(1, 0\right) = \frac{0}{1},\qquad f\left(0, 1\right) = \frac{1}{0},\qquad f\left(0, 0\right) = 1\]
and so the expansion of the given function is
\[\frac{0}{0}xy + 0x\left(1-y\right) + \frac{1}{0}\left(1-x\right)y + \left(1-x\right)\left(1-y\right).\]
We shall see in the next chapter that the forms \(\frac{0}{0}\) and \(\frac{1}{0}\) have a very important logical interpretation in expressions like this one. The first of them, zero over zero, is known to mathematicians as the symbol of an indeterminate quantity.

Next, suppose the function to be expanded contains three symbols, so that we can write it in the general form \(f\left(x, y, z\right)\). Proceeding as before, we get
\[\begin{aligned} f\left(x,y,z\right) &= f\left(1,1,1\right)xyz + f\left(1,1,0\right)xy\left(1-z\right) + f\left(1, 0, 1\right)x\left(1-y\right)z \\ &+ f\left(1,0,0\right)x\left(1-y\right)\left(1-z\right) + f\left(0,1,1\right)\left(1-x\right)yz \\ &+ f\left(0,1,0\right)\left(1-x\right)y\left(1-z\right) + f\left(0,0,1\right)\left(1-x\right)\left(1-y\right)z \\ &+ f\left(0,0,0\right)\left(1-x\right)\left(1-y\right)\left(1-z\right) \end{aligned}\]
where \(f\left(1, 1, 1\right)\) stands for what the function \(f\left(x, y, z\right)\) becomes when we set \(x = 1\), \(y = 1\), \(z = 1\) in it, and so on for the rest. With three symbols there are eight compartments, one for each way of having or lacking each of the three properties.

11. It is now easy to see the general law that governs the expansion of any given function, and to turn the method of expanding into a rule. But before stating that rule, it will help to make the following observations.

Each expansion we have obtained is made up of certain terms that contain the symbols \(x\), \(y\), and so on, multiplied by coefficients that do not contain those symbols. So the expansion of \(f\left(x\right)\) consists of the two terms \(x\) and \(1 - x\), multiplied by the coefficients \(f\left(1\right)\) and \(f\left(0\right)\) respectively. And the expansion of \(f\left(x, y\right)\) consists of the four terms \(xy\), \(x\left(1 - y\right)\), \(\left(1 - x\right)y\), and \(\left(1 - x\right)\left(1 - y\right)\), multiplied by the coefficients \(f\left(1, 1\right)\), \(f\left(1, 0\right)\), \(f\left(0, 1\right)\), \(f\left(0, 0\right)\) respectively. The terms \(x\), \(1 - x\) in the first case, and the terms \(xy\), \(x\left(1 - y\right)\), and so on in the second, we shall call the _constituents_ of the expansion. Each constituent names one of the compartments described above: _xy_ the things that are both _x_ and _y_, _x_ times one minus _y_ the things that are _x_ but not _y_, and so on down to the things that are neither. Clearly their form does not depend on the form of the function being expanded. In the constituent \(xy\), the symbols \(x\) and \(y\) are called its _factors_.

The general rule of development will therefore have two parts. The first tells us how to form the _constituents_ of the expansion; the second, how to find their coefficients. It runs as follows.

First. _To expand any function of the symbols \(x\), \(y\), \(z\)_.—Form a series of constituents in this way. Let the first constituent be the product of all the symbols. In this product change any one symbol, say \(z\), into \(1 - z\), to get the second constituent. Then in both of these change another symbol, say \(y\), into \(1 - y\), to get two more constituents. Then in the four constituents now obtained change another symbol, say \(x\), into \(1 - x\), to get four new ones; and so on, until there are no more symbols left to change. Each change doubles the list, so two symbols give four constituents, three give eight, and so on.

Second. _To find the coefficient of any constituent_.—If that constituent has \(x\) as a factor, change \(x\) into \(1\) in the original function; but if it has \(1 - x\) as a factor, change \(x\) into \(0\) in the original function. Do the same for the symbols \(y\), \(z\), and so on. The value of the function once all these changes are made, worked out in full, is the coefficient we want.

The sum of the constituents, each multiplied by its own coefficient, is the expansion required.

Editor's note: What Boole calls the development of a function into its constituents is what is now called its disjunctive normal form, and his constituents are what computer scientists call minterms. Listing all the constituents (two of them, then four, then eight, and so on) and keeping only the ones a premise allows is the very method Jevons teaches by hand in Lesson 23 of his Elementary Lessons in Logic, which is in this collection. It is also the ancestor of the truth table.

12. It is worth noticing that a function can be developed with respect to symbols it does not actually contain. Suppose, for example, that we follow the rule to develop the function \(1-x\) with respect to the symbols \(x\) and \(y\). We have,

	When | \(x = 1\) | and | \(y = 1\) | the given function | \(= 0\).
	 | \(x = 1\) | " | \(y = 0\) | " " | \(= 0\).
	 | \(x = 0\) | " | \(y = 1\) | " " | \(= 1\).
	 | \(x = 0\) | " | \(y = 0\) | " " | \(= 1\).

So the development is
\[1-x = 0xy + 0x\left(1-y\right)+\left(1-x\right)y+\left(1-x\right)\left(1-y\right);\]
and this is a true development. Adding the terms \(\left(1-x\right)y\) and \(\left(1-x\right)\left(1-y\right)\) gives back the function \(1-x\). In words: the not-_x_'s are just the not-_x_'s that are _y_ together with the not-_x_'s that are not _y_.

The symbol \(1\), developed by the rule with respect to the symbol \(x\), gives
\[x + 1 - x.\]
Developed with respect to \(x\) and \(y\), it gives
\[xy + x\left(1-y\right) + \left(1-x\right)y + \left(1-x\right) \left(1-y\right).\]
In the same way, developed with respect to any set of symbols, it produces a series made up of all the possible constituents of those symbols. Since 1 stands for the whole universe of discourse, this says that the compartments together fill the universe.

13. A few more remarks on the nature of these general expansions are in order. Let us take as our example the general theorem (5), the pattern of development for functions of two logical symbols.

First, that theorem is entirely true and meaningful when \(x\) and \(y\) are number symbols of the kind considered in this chapter, whatever algebraic form the function \(f\left(x, y\right)\) is given. So it may be used, with full meaning, at any stage of the work between the moment we change the symbols from their logical to their numerical interpretation, as described above, and the moment we finally give them back their logical interpretation.

Second, the theorem is entirely true and meaningful when \(x\) and \(y\) are logical symbols, provided the function \(f\left(x, y\right)\) has a form that represents a _class or collection of things_. In that case the right-hand side can always be interpreted logically. For instance, if \(f\left(x, y\right)\) stands for the function \(1 - x + xy\), applying the theorem gives
\[\begin{aligned} 1-x+xy &= xy+0x\left(1-y\right)+\left(1-x\right)y+\left(1-x\right)\left(1-y\right), \\ &= xy+\left(1-x\right)y+\left(1-x\right)\left(1-y\right), \end{aligned}\]
and this result is both meaningful and true. Read in words, the function names the not-_x_'s together with the _x_'s that are _y_; the expansion names the same things compartment by compartment: the things that are _x_ and _y_, the _y_'s that are not _x_, and the things that are neither. Only the compartment of _x_'s that are not _y_ is left out.

So we may regard the theorem as true and meaningful for number symbols of the kind described _always_, and for logical symbols _always when interpretable_. Whenever it is used in this book, then, it must be understood that if the expansion obtained cannot be interpreted, the symbols \(x\), \(y\) are being taken as number symbols of that particular kind.

But although the expansion cannot always be interpreted directly, it always leads us straight to results that can. For example, the expression \(x-y\) gives, on development, the form
\[x\left(1-y\right)-y\left(1-x\right),\]
which in general cannot be interpreted. We cannot, in thought, take away from the class of things that are \(x\)'s and not \(y\)'s the class of things that are \(y\)'s and not \(x\)'s, because the second class is not contained in the first. But suppose the form \(x - y\) appeared as the left-hand side of an equation whose right-hand side was \(0\). Then on development we should have
\[x\left(1-y\right)-y\left(1-x\right)=0.\]
Now it will be shown in the next chapter that this equation, with \(x\) and \(y\) regarded as number symbols of the kind described, splits at once into the two equations
\[x\left(1-y\right)=0, \quad y\left(1-x\right)=0,\]
and these equations can be read directly in logic once the symbols \(x\) and \(y\) are given logical meanings: there is nothing that is _x_ and not _y_, and nothing that is _y_ and not _x_. In other words, the _x_'s and the _y_'s are the same things. It should also be remarked that although _functions_ do not necessarily become interpretable when developed, _equations_ can always be brought by this process into interpretable forms.

14. The following Proposition establishes some important properties of constituents. In stating it, the symbol \(t\) is used to stand for any constituent of an expansion, whichever it may be. So if the expansion is of a function of two symbols \(x\) and \(y\), \(t\) stands for any of the four forms \(xy\), \(x\left(1 - y\right)\), \(\left(1 - x\right)y\), and \(\left(1 - x\right)\left(1 - y\right)\). Where we need to represent the constituents of an expansion by single symbols and still tell them apart, we shall mark the difference with subscripts. So \(t_1\) might be used for \(xy\), \(t_2\) for \(x \left(1 - y\right)\), and so on.

Proposition III.

Any single constituent \(t\) of an expansion satisfies the law of duality, whose expression is
\[t\left(1-t\right) = 0.\]
The product of any two different constituents of an expansion is equal to \(0\), and the sum of all the constituents is equal to \(1\).

In words: nothing both belongs to a compartment and lies outside it; no two compartments share any member; and all the compartments together make up the universe of discourse.

First, take the particular constituent \(xy\). We have
\[xy \times xy = x^2y^2.\]
But \(x^2 = x\), \(y^2 = y\), by the fundamental law of class symbols; so
\[xy \times xy = xy.\]
Or, representing \(xy\) by \(t\),
\[t \times t = t,\]
or
\[t\left(1 -t\right) = 0.\]
In the same way the constituent \(x \left(1 - y\right)\) satisfies the same law. For we have
\[\begin{gathered} x^2 = x, \left(1 - y\right)^2 = 1 - y, \\ \therefore \left\{x\left(1-y\right)\right\}^2 = x\left(1-y\right), \text{ or } t\left(1-t\right) = 0. \end{gathered}\]
Now every factor of every constituent has either the form \(x\) or the form \(1 - x\). So the square of each factor equals that factor, and therefore the square of the product of the factors, that is, of the constituent, equals the constituent. So, with \(t\) standing for any constituent, we have
\[t^2 = t, \text{ or } t\left(1 -t\right) = 0.\]

Second, the product of any two constituents is \(0\). This follows plainly from the general law of the symbols expressed by the equation \(x \left(1 - x\right) = 0\), which says that nothing is both _x_ and not _x_. For whichever two constituents of the same expansion we take, there will be at least one factor \(x\) in the one matched by a factor \(1 - x\) in the other.

Third, the sum of all the constituents of an expansion is unity, that is, 1. This is plain when we add the two constituents \(x\) and \(1 - x\), or the four constituents \(xy\), \(x(1-y)\), \((1-x)y\), \((1-x)(1-y)\). But it is also proved, and more generally, by expanding \(1\) in terms of any set of symbols (V. 12). The constituents in that case are formed as usual, and all the coefficients are unity.

15. With the Proposition above we may connect the following one.

Proposition IV.

If \(V\) stands for the sum of any series of constituents, each with coefficient \(1\), then the condition
\[V\left(1-V\right) = 0\]
is satisfied. In words, any collection of whole compartments, taken together, obeys the law of duality just as a single class symbol does.

Let \(t_1\), \(t_2\) …\(t_n\) be the constituents in question. Then
\[V=t_1 + t_2 \dots + t_n.\]
Squaring both sides, and noting that \(t_1^2 = t_1\), \(t_1 t_2 = 0\), and so on, we have
\[V^2 = t_1 + t_2 \dots + t_n;\]
so that
\[V = V^2.\]
Therefore
\[V\left(1-V\right) = 0.\]
