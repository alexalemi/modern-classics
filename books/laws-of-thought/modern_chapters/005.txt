Chapter 4: Division of Propositions

1. We have now investigated the laws of the mental operations involved in conception, or imagination, and explained the matching laws of the symbols that represent them. The next step is to put these results to practical use. There are three uses: first, expressing the complex terms of propositions; second, expressing propositions themselves; and last, building a general method of deductive analysis. This chapter deals mainly with the first of these. As an introduction to it, we need to establish the following Proposition:

Proposition I.

All logical propositions may be considered as belonging to one or the other of two great classes, which may be called "Primary" or "Concrete Propositions," and "Secondary" or "Abstract Propositions."

Every assertion we make belongs to one of two kinds. Either it states a relation among _things_, or it states (or amounts to stating) a relation among _propositions_. An assertion about the properties of things, or the phenomena they show, or the circumstances they are in, is strictly the assertion of a relation among things. For the purposes of logic, to say that "snow is white" is the same as to say that "snow is a white thing." An assertion about facts or events, and how they connect with and depend on one another, usually amounts, for the same purposes, to saying that certain propositions about those events are related in their truth or falsehood. The first class of propositions, about _things_, I call "Primary." The second class, about _propositions_, I call "Secondary." In practice this distinction nearly, but not quite, lines up with the usual logical division of propositions into categorical and hypothetical.

For instance, the propositions "The sun shines" and "The earth is warmed" are primary. The proposition "If the sun shines the earth is warmed" is secondary. To say "The sun shines" is to say "The sun is that which shines." It states a relation between two classes of things, namely "the sun" and "things which shine." The secondary proposition, on the other hand, states a relation of dependence between the two primary propositions "The sun shines" and "The earth is warmed." I do not claim that the relation between these propositions is a relation of cause and effect, as the relation between the facts they express is. I claim only that the relation among the propositions implies the relation among the facts, and is implied by it, so closely that for the purposes of logic it can stand in for it.

2. Suppose that instead of the proposition "The sun shines" we say "It is true that the sun shines." Then we are no longer speaking directly about things. We are speaking about a proposition concerning things, namely the proposition "The sun shines." So the proposition in which we say this is a secondary one. In this way every primary proposition can give rise to a secondary proposition: the one that asserts its truth, or declares its falsehood.

The little words _if_, _either_, and _or_ will usually signal that a proposition is secondary, but they do not have to. The proposition "Animals are either rational or irrational" is primary. It cannot be broken into "Either animals are rational or animals are irrational," and so it does not state a relation of dependence between the two propositions joined in that second, disjunctive sentence. The words _either_ and _or_ are in fact no _test_ of what kind a proposition is, even though they turn up more often in secondary propositions. Even the word _if_ can appear in a primary proposition. "Men are, if wise, then temperate," is an example. It cannot be broken into "If all men are wise, then all men are temperate."

3. It is not my purpose to discuss the strengths or weaknesses of the usual division of propositions. I will only say this about the classification used here. Its principle is clear and definite in use; it marks a real and fundamental difference among propositions; and it is essential to developing a general method of reasoning. Nor is it any objection that a primary proposition can be put into a form in which it becomes secondary. In that case we take no direct account of the things connected in the primary proposition. We consider only the proposition itself, as _true_ or as _false_.

4. In this book the same symbols will be used to express both primary and secondary propositions, and, as we shall see, they obey the same laws. The difference between the two cases is not a difference of form but of interpretation. In both, the relation the proposition sets out to express will be written with the sign \(=\). In primary propositions, the two sides joined by this sign will usually stand for the "terms" of the proposition, or, to give them their more specific names, its subject and predicate.

Proposition II.

5. To work out a general method, based on listing the possible cases, for expressing any class or collection of things that can be a "term" of a Primary Proposition.

First, suppose the class or collection is defined only by names or qualities that all its members share. Then its expression is a single term, in which the symbols for those names or qualities are joined with no sign between them, as in algebraic multiplication. So if \(x\) stands for opaque substances, \(y\) for polished substances, and \(z\) for stones, we have
\(xyz =\) opaque polished stones;
\(xy (1 - z) =\) opaque polished substances which are not stones;
\(x (1 - y)(1 - z) =\) opaque substances which are not polished, and are not stones;
and so on for any other combination. Notice that each of these expressions obeys the same law of duality as the single symbols it contains. So
\[\begin{aligned} & xyz \times xyz = xyz; \\ & xy (1 - z) \times xy (1 - z) = xy (1 - z); \end{aligned}\]
and so on: taking the opaque polished stones together with the opaque polished stones gives nothing new. Any term like these we shall call a "class term," because it expresses a class of things through the properties or names its members have in common.

Second, suppose we are speaking of a collection whose different portions are defined by different properties, names, or attributes. Then the expressions for those portions must be formed separately and joined by the sign \(+\). But if the collection was formed by removing a defined portion from some wider collection, the sign \(-\) must be placed before the symbolic expression of the portion removed. A few more remarks on these symbols are in order.

6. Broadly speaking, the symbol \(+\) is the equivalent of the words "and" and "or," and the symbol \(-\) the equivalent of the word "except." Of "and" and "or," the first is usually used when the collection described is the subject of a proposition, the second when it is the predicate. "The scholar _and_ the man of the world desire happiness" illustrates the first case. "Things possessing utility are _either_ productive of pleasure _or_ preventive of pain" illustrates the second. Now whenever an expression with these words appears in a primary proposition, it matters a great deal whether the groups they separate in thought are meant to be completely distinct and mutually exclusive, or not. Does "Scholars and men of the world" include or exclude those who are both? Does "Either productive of pleasure or preventive of pain" include or exclude things that are both? My view is that, strictly speaking, "and" and "or" do have this power of separating, or excluding. On that view the statement "All \(x\)’s are either \(y\)’s or \(z\)’s," taken rigorously, means "All \(x\)’s are either \(y\)’s, but not \(z\)’s," or "\(z\)’s but not \(y\)’s." But it must also be admitted that the "jus et norma loquendi" (the law and rule of speech, that is, ordinary usage) seems rather to favour the opposite reading. "Either \(y\)’s or \(z\)’s" would generally be understood to include things that are \(y\)’s and \(z\)’s at once, along with things that are one but not the other. Remember, though, that the symbol \(+\) does have this separating power. So we must break any disjunctive expression we meet into parts that really are separate in thought, and then join their expressions with the symbol \(+\).

So, depending on the meaning intended, the expression "Things which are either \(x\)’s or \(y\)’s" will have two different symbolic equivalents. If we mean "Things which are \(x\)’s but not \(y\)’s, or \(y\)’s but not \(x\)’s," the expression will be
\[x (1 - y) + y ( 1 - x);\]
the symbol \(x\) standing for \(x\)’s, and \(y\) for \(y\)’s. If, however, we mean "Things which are either \(x\)’s, or, if not \(x\)’s, then \(y\)’s," the expression will be
\[x + y(1 - x).\]
This expression allows for things that are both \(x\)’s and \(y\)’s at once. It could be written out more fully as
\[xy + x (1 - y) + y ( 1 - x);\]
but adding the first two terms of this, the _x_’s that are _y_’s and the _x_’s that are not, simply gives back all the _x_’s, and so the previous expression.

Notice that the expressions just given obey the fundamental law of duality (III. 16). So we have
\[\begin{aligned} \{x (1 - y) + y (1 - x)\}^2 &= x (1 -y) + y (1 - x), \\ \{x + y(1-x)\}^2 &= x+y(1-x). \end{aligned}\]
We shall see later that this is only one case of a general law of expressions that stand for "classes or collections of things."

7. The results of these inquiries can be summed up in the following rule of expression.

Rule.—Express simple names or qualities by the symbols \(x\), \(y\), \(z\), etc., and their contraries by \(1 - x\), \(1 - y\), \(1 - z\), etc. Express classes of things defined by common names or qualities by joining the corresponding symbols as in multiplication. Express collections of things made up of portions that differ from one another by joining the expressions for those portions with the sign \(+\). In particular, express "Either \(x\)’s or \(y\)’s" by \(x (1 - y) + y (1 - x)\) when the classes denoted by \(x\) and \(y\) are exclusive, and by \(x + y (1 - x)\) when they are not exclusive. In the same way, express "Either \(x\)’s, or \(y\)’s, or \(z\)’s" by \(x(1-y)(1-z) + y(1-x)(1-z) + z(1-x)(1-y)\) when the classes denoted by \(x\), \(y\), and \(z\) are meant to be mutually exclusive, and by \(x + y (1 - x) + z (1 - x) (1-y)\) when they are not meant to be exclusive; and so on.

8. On this rule of expression rests the reverse rule, the rule of interpretation. The following examples should illustrate both well enough. For brevity, leaving out the universal subject "things" or "beings," let us take
\[x = \text{hard}, y = \text{elastic}, z = \text{metals};\]
and we get the following results:

"Non-elastic metals" will be expressed by \(z (1 - y)\), the metals that are not elastic;

"Elastic substances with non-elastic metals," by \(y + z (1 - y)\);

"Hard substances, except metals," by \(x-z\);

"Metallic substances, except those which are neither hard nor elastic," by \(z-z(1 - x)(1 - y)\), or by \(z \{1 - (1 - x) (1 - y)\}\) (see (6), Chap. II.).

In the last example, what we really had to express was "Metals, except not hard, not elastic, metals." Words like "and" placed between _adjectives_ usually add nothing, and so they must not be expressed in symbols.

So "Metals hard and elastic" is the same as "Hard elastic metals," and is expressed by \(xyz\).

Take next the expression "Hard substances, except those which are metallic and non-elastic, and those which are elastic and non-metallic." Here the word _those_ means hard substances. So the expression really means _hard substances, except hard substances that are metallic and non-elastic, and hard substances that are non-metallic and elastic_; the word _except_ covers both of the classes that follow it. The complete expression is
\[\begin{aligned} x - \{xz (1 - y) &+ xy (1 - z)\}; \\ \text{or, } x &- xz (1 - y) - xy (1 - z). \end{aligned}\]
that is, the hard things, with the hard non-elastic metals and the hard elastic non-metals taken away.

9. The Proposition just given, with its illustrations, is a necessary first step toward the next one, which will complete the plan of this chapter.

Proposition III.

To work out, by examining their possible kinds, a general method for expressing Primary or Concrete Propositions.

A primary proposition, in the most general sense, consists of two terms and asserts that a relation holds between them. These terms need not be names of one word. They may stand for any collection of objects, of the sort we have just been considering. How to express the terms is therefore already covered by the general rules above. What remains is to find out how to express the relations between them. This will plainly depend on the kind of relation, and above all on whether, in that relation, the terms are understood to be universal or particular. That is, it depends on whether we speak of the whole of the collection a term refers to, or speak indefinitely of the whole or of a part of it, which is what the word "some" usually means.

Suppose we want to express a relation of identity between the two classes "Fixed Stars" and "Suns," that is, to express that "All fixed stars are suns" and "All suns are fixed stars." Here, if \(x\) stands for fixed stars and \(y\) for suns, we have
\[x = y\]
as the equation required. It reads: the fixed stars and the suns are one and the same class.

In the proposition "All fixed stars are suns," the term "all fixed stars" would be called the _subject_, and "suns" the _predicate_. Let us stretch the meaning of _subject_ and _predicate_ as follows. By subject we will mean the first term of any affirmative proposition, the term that comes before the copula _is_ or _are_. By predicate we will mean the second term, the one that comes after the copula. And let us allow that either of them may be universal or particular, so that either the whole class is meant, or only a part of it. Then we have the following Rule for cases like the last example:—

10. Rule.—When both Subject and Predicate of a Proposition are universal, form the separate expressions for them, and connect them by the sign =.

This case usually comes up in the definitions of a science, or of subjects handled in the manner of pure science. Mr. Senior’s definition of wealth is a good example:

"Wealth consists of things transferable, limited in supply, and either productive of pleasure or preventive of pain."

Before putting this definition into symbols, notice that the word _and_ here adds nothing. Wealth is defined by having three properties or qualities, not by being made up of three classes or collections of objects. So, leaving out the _and_, let us set
\[\begin{aligned} w &= \text{wealth.} \\ t &= \text{things transferable.} \\ s &= \text{limited in supply.} \\ p &= \text{productive of pleasure.} \\ r &= \text{preventive of pain.} \end{aligned}\]

Now it is clear from the subject that the phrase "Either productive of pleasure or preventive of pain," in this definition, is meant to say "Either productive of pleasure; or, if not productive of pleasure, preventive of pain." So the class of things this phrase would define on its own would consist of all things productive of pleasure, together with all things that are not productive of pleasure but are preventive of pain. Its symbolic expression would be
\[p+(1-p)r.\]
that is, the pleasure-giving things, plus the things that give no pleasure but prevent pain.

Now we put this expression in brackets, to show that both its terms are meant, and attach to it the symbols \(s\) and \(t\), which limit it to things "transferable" and "limited in supply." That gives the following symbolic equivalent of the original definition:
\[w = st\{p + r(1-p)\}. \qquad (1)\]
In words: wealth is exactly the class of things that are transferable, limited in supply, and either pleasure-giving or, failing that, pain-preventing.

Suppose instead that "Either productive of pleasure or preventive of pain" were meant to pick out only things productive of pleasure without being preventive of pain, \(p (1 - r)\), or preventive of pain without being productive of pleasure, \(r (1 - p)\), leaving out the things that are both. Then the definition in symbols would be
\[w = st\{p(1-r) + r(1-p)\}. \qquad (2)\]

All this agrees with what was said earlier in more general terms. The reader may wonder what would happen if we translated the phrase "Things productive of pleasure or preventive of pain" word for word, as \(p + r\), making the equation of the definition
\[w = st(p + r). \qquad (3)\]

The answer is that this would be equivalent to (2), with one extra implication: that the classes of things denoted by \(stp\) and \(str\) are quite distinct. In other words, it would imply that among things transferable and limited in supply there exist none in the universe that are at once productive of pleasure and preventive of pain. How to find the full meaning of any equation will be explained later. What has been said may show that before we try to translate our data into the strict language of symbols, it is above all necessary to find out what our words are _intended_ to mean. But no one who values correct thinking, and who regards the right use of language as both its tool and its safeguard, will count this necessity as a burden.

11. Let us next consider the case in which the predicate of the proposition is particular, for example "All men are mortal."

Here our meaning is clearly "All men are some mortal beings," and we must find the expression for the predicate, "some mortal beings." So let \(v\) stand for a class indefinite in every respect but one: some of its members are mortal beings. And let \(x\) stand for "mortal beings." Then \(vx\) will stand for "some mortal beings." So if \(y\) stands for men, the equation we want is
\[y = vx.\]
It reads: the men are some of the mortal beings, that is, the class of men is some part, we do not say which, of the class of mortals.

Editor's note: The indefinite class v is Boole's way of saying "some." Later logic stopped using it. Today "Some X are Y" is read as saying that the class of things that are both X and Y is not empty, and the idea of "some" became what logicians call the existential quantifier, "there exists at least one."

From these considerations we get the following Rule for expressing an affirmative universal proposition whose predicate is particular:

Rule.—Express as before the subject and the predicate, attach to the latter the indefinite symbol \(v\), and equate the expressions.

Clearly \(v\) is a symbol of the same kind as \(x\), \(y\), and the rest, and it obeys the general law
\[v^2 = v, \quad \text{or} \quad v (1 - v) = 0.\]
that is, the indefinite class, like every class, taken together with itself is just itself.

So to express the proposition "The planets are either primary or secondary," we would follow the rule like this.
Let x represent planets (the subject);
\[\begin{aligned} y &= \text{primary bodies;} \\ z &= \text{secondary bodies;} \end{aligned}\]
then, assuming that the word "or" separates the class of "primary" bodies completely from the class of "secondary" bodies, as far as they come into the proposition, we find as the equation of the proposition
\[x = v \left\{ y \left( 1 - z \right) + z \left( 1 - y \right) \right\}. \qquad (4)\]
that is, the planets are some of the things that are either primary and not secondary, or secondary and not primary.

It is worth noticing that in this case the _literal_ translation of the premises into the form
\[x = v (y + z) \qquad (5)\]
would be exactly equivalent, because \(v\) is an indefinite class symbol. Still, form (4) is the better one, because the expression
\[y \left( 1 - z \right) + z \left( 1 - y \right)\]
consists of terms that stand for classes quite distinct from each other, and it obeys the fundamental law of duality.

Take the proposition "The heavenly bodies are either suns, or planets, or comets," and represent these classes of things by \(w\), \(x\), \(y\), \(z\), respectively. If we suppose that none of the heavenly bodies belongs to two of these divisions at once, its expression will be
\[w = v \left\{ x \left( 1 - y \right) \left( 1 - z \right) + y \left( 1 - x \right) \left( 1 - z \right) + z \left( 1 - x \right) \left( 1 - y \right) \right\}\]

But suppose it were meant that the heavenly bodies were either suns, or, if not suns, planets, or, if neither suns nor planets, fixed stars. That meaning does not rule out some of them belonging at once to two, or to all three, of the divisions of suns, planets, and fixed stars. Then the expression required would be
\[w = v \left\{ x + y \left( 1 - x \right) + z \left( 1 - x \right) \left(1 -y \right) \right\}. \qquad (6)\]

The examples above are descriptions, not definitions. In fact the predicates of propositions are usually particular. When they are not, either the predicate is a singular term (the name of one individual), or instead of the copula "is" or "are" we use some other form of connection that tells us the predicate is to be taken universally.

12. Consider next the case of universal negative propositions, for example "No men are perfect beings."

Plainly we are not speaking here of a class called "no men" and asserting that all its members are "perfect beings." What we are really doing is making an assertion about "_all men_": that they are "_not perfect beings_." So the true meaning of the proposition is this:

"All men (subject) are (copula) not perfect (predicate);" so if \(y\) stands for "men," and \(x\) for "perfect beings," we have
\[y = v \left( 1 - x \right),\]
that is, the men are some of the beings that are not perfect; and the same goes for any other case. So we have the following Rule:

Rule.—To express any proposition of the form "No \(x\)’s are \(y\)’s," convert it into the form "All \(x\)’s are not \(y\)’s," and then proceed as in the previous case.

13. Consider, last, the case in which the subject of the proposition is particular, for example "Some men are not wise." Here, as we have noted, the negative _not_ may properly be attached, at least for the purposes of logic, to the predicate _wise_. We do not mean to say that it is untrue that "Some men are wise." We mean to say of "some men" that they lack wisdom. So the proper form of the proposition is "Some men are not-wise." Now put \(y\) for "men," and \(x\) for "wise," that is, "wise beings." Bring in \(v\) as the symbol of a class indefinite in every respect but this one: it contains some individuals of the class it is placed before. Then we have
\[v y = v \left( 1 - x \right).\]
It reads: some men are the same as some not-wise beings, that is, some part of the class of men lies inside the class of the not-wise.

14. We can gather everything we have settled into the following general Rule:

General Rule for the Symbolic Expression of Primary Propositions.

First, if the proposition is affirmative, form the expression of the subject and that of the predicate. If either of them is particular, attach to it the indefinite symbol \(v\), and then equate the resulting expressions.

Second, if the proposition is negative, first express its true meaning by attaching the negative word to the predicate, then proceed as above.

One or two more examples should be enough to illustrate it.

Ex.—"No men are placed in exalted stations, and free from envious regards."

Let \(y\) stand for "men," \(x\) for "placed in exalted stations," and \(z\) for "free from envious regards."

Now the class described as "placed in exalted station" and "free from envious regards" is expressed by \(xz\). So the opposite class, those to whom this description does not apply, will be represented by \(1 - xz\), and all men are referred to this class. So we have
\[y = v \left( 1 - xz \right).\]
that is, the men are some of the beings who are not both highly placed and free from envy. If the proposition had been put in the equivalent form "Men in exalted stations are not free from envious regards," its expression would have been
\[yx = v \left( 1 - z \right).\]
We shall see later that this expression really is equivalent to the previous one, given the assumption involved, namely that \(v\) is an indefinite class symbol.

Ex.—"No men are heroes but those who unite self-denial to courage."

Let \(x\) = "men," \(y\) = "heroes," \(z\) = "those who practise self-denial," and \(w\) = "those who possess courage."

What is really asserted is that "men who do not possess courage and practise self-denial are not heroes."

So we have
\[x \left( 1 - zw \right) = v \left( 1 - y \right)\]
as the equation required: the men who do not have both self-denial and courage are some of the beings who are not heroes.

15. To close this chapter, it may be interesting to set side by side the great leading types of propositions as expressed in symbols. If we agree to let \(X\) and \(Y\) stand for the symbolic expressions of the "terms," the things related, these types will be
\[\begin{aligned} X &= vY, \\ X &= Y, \\ vX &= vY. \end{aligned}\]
In the first, only the predicate is particular; in the second, both terms are universal; in the third, both are particular. Some lesser forms are really included under these. For instance, if \(Y = 0\), the second form becomes
\[X=0;\]
which says that there is no such thing as an _X_; and if \(Y = 1\) it becomes
\[X = 1;\]
which says that _X_ is the whole universe of discourse. Both these forms can be interpreted. Notice also that the expressions \(X\) and \(Y\), if they rest on a careful enough analysis of what the "terms" of the proposition mean, will obey the fundamental law of duality, which requires that
\[\begin{aligned} X^2 = X &\text{or} X \left( 1 - X \right) = 0, \\ Y^2 = Y &\text{or} Y \left( 1 - Y \right) = 0. \end{aligned}\]
