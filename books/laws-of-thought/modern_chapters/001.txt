Chapter 1: Nature and Design of This Work
(Part 1 of 2)

1. This book has four aims. The first is to investigate the fundamental laws of those operations of the mind by which we reason. The second is to express those laws in the symbolic language of a calculus (a system of symbols with fixed rules for working with them, as algebra is), and on that foundation to build the science of logic and its method. The third is to make that method the basis of a general method for applying the mathematical theory of probability. The last is to gather, from the pieces of truth these inquiries bring to light, some likely hints about the nature and make-up of the human mind.

2. That this plan is not entirely new hardly needs saying. Its two main practical branches, logic and probability, have drawn a great deal of attention from philosophers. In its ancient and medieval form, logic is tied almost entirely to one great name, Aristotle. It reached the ancient Greeks in the treatises of his Organon, which are partly technical and partly metaphysical, and it has come down to our own day with hardly any essential change. Original inquiry has flowed instead toward questions of general philosophy. These questions began in the quarrels of the logicians, but they outgrew their origin and gave each later age of speculation its own particular bent. Think of the eras of Porphyry and Proclus, of Anselm and Abelard, of Ramus, and of Descartes, and of the final protests of Bacon and Locke. Each shows the study of logic reaching out to shape the course of human thought, sometimes by raising topics rich in debate, sometimes by provoking protest against logic's own inflated claims.

The history of the theory of probability looks quite different. It has grown steadily, the way a science grows. At its birth it had the young genius of Pascal; in its maturity, the most difficult of all the mathematical researches of Laplace; and other names hardly less distinguished could be added. Logic has been remarkable for the related questions of metaphysics it gave rise to; probability has been remarkable for the push it gave to the higher branches of mathematics. Both subjects, moreover, have rightly been seen as serving a theoretical end as well as a practical one. Logic is not only for drawing correct conclusions from given premises. And probability does more than teach us how to put life insurance on a secure footing, or how to boil down whatever is valuable in countless observations in astronomy, in physics, or in that field of social inquiry which is fast becoming very important. Both studies matter in another way too: they shed light on the powers of the mind. They show how language and number serve as tools for reasoning. They reveal something of how the different powers of the intellect we all share are connected. They set out the essential standards of truth and correctness in the two realms of knowledge, the demonstrative (what can be proved) and the probable. And those standards are not imposed from outside; they are rooted deep in the way the human faculties are made. These theoretical goals are no less interesting, no less dignified, and, I would add, no less important than the practical aims that history has tied them to. Our higher powers of thought give us all our knowledge of the world and of ourselves that goes beyond what the senses simply perceive. To uncover their hidden laws and relations is a goal that no rational mind needs to be persuaded of.

3. Some parts of this plan have occurred to others before me. But its overall conception, its method, and to a large extent its results are, I believe, original. So in this chapter I will offer some preliminary statements and explanations, to make the real aim of the book clear and its subject easier to follow.

The first aim, then, is to investigate the fundamental laws of those operations of the mind by which reasoning is carried out. There is no need to argue here that the operations of the mind really do, in some real sense, obey laws, and therefore that a science of the mind is _possible_. If anyone doubts this, the way to meet the doubt is not to try to settle it _à priori_, by argument in advance of the evidence. The way is to point the doubter to the evidence of actual laws, which means pointing him to an actual science. So the answer to that doubt belongs not in the introduction to this book but in the book itself. Let us grant, then, that a science of the powers of the intellect is possible, and consider for a moment how we are to obtain it.

4. Like every other science, the science of the mind's operations must rest first of all on observation. Here, what we observe are the very operations and processes whose laws we want to find. Every science needs this foundation in experience. But there are some special differences in how experience yields general truths when the subject is the mind and when the subject is external nature. We need to look at these differences.

The general laws of nature are not, for the most part, things we directly perceive. Either they are inductive inferences from a large body of facts, stating the truth those facts have in common; or, at least to begin with, they are physical hypotheses about causes, which explain the phenomena with unfailing precision and let us predict new combinations of them. In every case, and in the strictest sense of the word, they are _probable_ conclusions. They come ever closer to certainty as experience confirms them more and more. But they never entirely lose the character of probability, in the strict and proper sense of that term. Knowledge of the laws of the mind is different. It does not need a large collection of observations as its basis. We see the general truth in the particular instance, and repeating the instance does not confirm it any further. An obvious example will show this. There is a rule of reasoning called the _dictum_ of Aristotle, _de omni et nullo_ ("about all and about none": roughly, whatever is affirmed or denied of a whole class is affirmed or denied of everything in it). We may ask whether this rule expresses a primary law of human reasoning. But there is no question that it expresses a general truth of logic. And reflecting on a single instance of it is enough to show that truth in all its generality. This is evidence that the rule rests on some general law or laws of the mind. It also illustrates the point that we do not reach such general truths by induction from many instances: they are contained in seeing one instance clearly. Alongside this comes another truth that is just as important. Our knowledge of the laws on which the science of the intellect rests, however much or little of it we have, is not probable knowledge. For in the particular example we see not only the general truth but also that it is a certain truth, a truth in which our confidence will not keep growing as we see it confirmed again and again in practice.

5. But if the general truths of logic win our agreement the moment the mind sees them, what makes it hard to build a science of logic? Not, we may answer, gathering the material, but sorting it out: telling what kind of truth each one is, and settling its place and its relation to the rest. Every science consists of general truths, but only some of those truths are primary and fundamental; the rest are secondary and derived. Kepler's laws of elliptical motion are general truths of astronomy, but they are not its fundamental truths. The same holds in the purely mathematical sciences. An almost endless variety of known theorems, and an infinite number of others still unknown, all rest together on the foundation of a few simple axioms; and yet all of them are _general_ truths. I would add that to a mind refined enough, these truths would shine out by their own light. It would not need the connecting links of thought, the steps of tiring and often painful deduction, by which we actually come to know them. Let us call fundamental those laws and principles from which all the other general truths of a science can be deduced, and into which they can all be broken down again. Are we wrong, then, to count this as the true science of logic: one that lays down certain elementary laws, confirmed by the mind's own testimony; that lets us deduce from them, by uniform processes, the whole chain of their secondary consequences; and that supplies, for practical use, methods of perfect generality? Consider whether any science, taken either as a system of truth or as the basis of a practical art, can properly have any other test of whether its laws are complete and fundamental. The test is how complete its system of derived truths is, and how general the methods it supports. Other questions may certainly come up. Convenience, custom and personal preference may press their claims, and they deserve attention. But on the question of what makes a science in its pure, abstract integrity, I believe nothing besides the test above really counts.

6. The second aim of this book is to express the fundamental laws of reasoning in the symbolic language of a calculus. Here it is enough to say that the laws themselves suggest this way of expressing them, and make it uniquely fit for our purposes. There is a close analogy between the operations of the mind in reasoning generally and its operations in the particular science of algebra. More than that: to a large extent the two kinds of operation follow exactly the same laws. Of course, the laws must be worked out separately in each case. Any agreement in form between them can only be established _à posteriori_, after the fact, by actually comparing them. To borrow the notation of the science of number and simply assume that the rules for using it will stay the same in a new use would be mere hypothesis. There are, it is true, certain general principles, grounded in the very nature of language, that govern how symbols are used; and symbols are just the elements of a scientific language. Up to a point these elements are arbitrary. What they mean is purely a matter of convention: we may use them in any sense we like. But that freedom has two essential limits. First, once we have fixed a symbol's meaning, we must never depart from it within the same piece of reasoning. Second, the laws by which we reason with the symbols must be based only on that fixed meaning. Under these principles, if the laws of the symbols of logic turn out to agree with those of algebra, the result can only be an agreement of processes. The two fields of meaning stay separate and independent, each with its own laws and conditions.

Now, the investigations in the following pages present logic, on its practical side, as a system of processes carried out with symbols that have a definite meaning and that obey laws based on that meaning alone. But at the same time they show those laws to be identical in form with the laws of the general symbols of algebra, with one single addition: the symbols of logic obey a further special law (Chap. II.), which the symbols of quantity, as such, do not obey. I will not dwell here on what this law is or on the evidence for it; those questions are fully discussed later. But this law is the essential ground of difference between the kinds of inference logic deals with and those that arise in the particular science of number, and so it deserves more than a passing mention. It may be said to lie at the very foundation of general reasoning. It governs those acts of conception or imagination that come before the processes of logical deduction, and it gives those processes much of their actual form and expression. So we may say that this law is the seed, the first principle, and that every approach to a general method in logic is a more or less complete growth from it.

7. We have already laid down the principle (5) that, in the science of logic, whether an assumed system of laws is sufficient and truly fundamental must be seen partly in how perfect the methods are that it leads to. It remains, then, to ask what a general method in logic requires, and how far the system of this book meets those requirements.

Logic deals with two kinds of relation: relations among things, and relations among facts. But facts are expressed by propositions, so the second kind can, at least for the purposes of logic, be turned into relations among propositions. Suppose we say that the fact or event \(A\) always follows the fact or event \(B\). To this extent, at least, that is the same as saying that the truth of the proposition asserting that the event \(B\) happens always implies the truth of the proposition asserting that the event \(A\) happens. So instead of saying that logic deals with relations among things and relations among facts, we may say that it deals with relations among things and relations among propositions. An example of the first kind is the proposition “All men are mortal”; of the second, the proposition “If the sun is totally eclipsed, the stars will become visible.” The first expresses a relation between “men” and “mortal beings”; the second, a relation between the simple propositions “The sun is totally eclipsed” and “The stars will become visible.” I count among such relations those that affirm or deny that things exist, and those that affirm or deny that propositions are true. Now let us call the things or propositions among which a relation is expressed the elements of the propositions that express it. With this definition we can say that the _premises_ of any logical argument express _given_ relations among certain elements, and that the conclusion must express an _implied_ relation among those elements, or among some of them: that is, a relation that the premises imply, or contain by way of inference.

8. With this settled, a general method in logic seems to need the following.

First, since the conclusion must express a relation among all or some of the elements in the premises, we need a way of eliminating the elements we do not want to appear in the conclusion, and of finding the full relation the premises imply among the elements we want to keep. In the language of ordinary logic, the elements that do not appear in the conclusion are called middle terms. The kind of elimination shown in logic textbooks takes two propositions that share an element, the middle term, and deduces a conclusion connecting the two terms that remain. But the problem of elimination as this book sees it is much wider. It aims not just to eliminate one middle term from two propositions, but to eliminate middle terms from propositions in general, however many terms and propositions there are and however they are connected. Neither logic nor algebra, as they now stand, offers any exact parallel to this. In algebra, as is well known, elimination (combining equations so that a symbol drops out) is limited in this way: from two equations we can eliminate one symbol of quantity; from three equations, two symbols; and in general, from \(n\) equations, \(n-1\) symbols. This condition, necessary in algebra, seems to hold in the existing logic too, but it has no essential place in logic as a science. There, no relationship at all can be proved between the number of terms to be eliminated and the number of propositions they are to be eliminated from. From the equation representing a single proposition we can eliminate any number of the symbols that stand for terms or elements in logic; and from any number of equations representing propositions, we can eliminate one such symbol, or any other number of them, in the same way. One general process does this in every case. This is one of the many remarkable consequences of that distinctive law of the symbols of logic which I have already pointed out.

Second, a general method in logic should be able to express the final relation among the elements of the conclusion as any admissible _kind_ of proposition, or with the terms in any chosen _order_. Among the kinds we may count those that logicians call categorical, hypothetical, disjunctive, and so on (plain statements, “if” statements, “either-or” statements). Under the choice of order we may put everything that depends on which elements appear in the subject or in the predicate, or in the antecedent or in the consequent (the “if” part or the “then” part), of the proposition that forms the “conclusion.” But setting aside the technical language of the old schools, let us ask what genuinely different kinds of problem may come up. We have seen that the elements of the final, inferred relation may be either _things_ or _propositions_. Take the first case. We might be asked to deduce from the premises a definition or description of some one thing, or class of things, among the elements of the conclusion, in terms of the other things it involves. Or we might form the idea of some thing or class of things that involves more than one of the elements of the conclusion, and ask for its expression in terms of the other elements. Now take the second case, where the elements kept in the conclusion are propositions. Then we might want to settle questions like these. Given the premises, is any of these propositions, taken by itself, true or false? Are particular combinations of them true or false? If we assume a particular proposition to be true, does anything follow about the other propositions, and if so, what? If we assume some particular condition about certain of the propositions, does anything follow about the others, and what? And so on. These, I say, are general questions, and a general method in logic ought to be able to answer them. Perhaps we can gather them all into one statement of the final problem of practical logic. Given a set of premises expressing relations among certain elements, whether things or propositions: required, explicitly, the whole relation that follows among _any_ of those elements, under any proposed conditions, and in any proposed form. That this problem can be solved in all its aspects will appear later. But I have not gone into the nature and functions of a general method in logic just to point that out. The reader needs to understand the specific goals of the investigation we are now beginning, and the principles that are to guide us toward them.

9. It might be said at this point that Aristotle's logic, in its rules of syllogism and conversion, already sets out the elementary processes of which all reasoning consists, and that beyond these there is neither room nor need for a general method. (A syllogism draws a conclusion from two premises that share a middle term; conversion swaps the subject and predicate of a proposition.) I have no wish to point out the defects of the common logic, and I will refer to it only as far as I must to show the real nature of this book in its true light. With that purpose alone, I would make two remarks. First, syllogism, conversion and the rest are not the ultimate processes of logic. This book will show that they rest on, and can be broken down into, deeper and simpler processes, and that these are the real elements of method in logic. Nor is it in fact true that all inference can be reduced to the particular forms of syllogism and conversion (see Chap. xv.). Second, even if all inference could be reduced to these two processes (and some have held that it can be reduced to syllogism alone), a general method would be just as necessary. We would still have to decide in what order the processes should follow one another, as well as what each one is, in order to reach the relation we want. By the relation we want, I mean the full relation that, given the premises, connects any elements we choose from them, and that expresses this relation in any form and order we choose. If we may judge by the mathematical sciences, which are the most perfect examples of method we know, this _directive_ function, deciding what to do and in what order, is method's chief task and its mark of distinction. The fundamental processes of arithmetic, for example, are in themselves only the elements of a possible science. Saying what they are is the first business of its method; arranging their order is its later and higher task. In the more complex examples of logical deduction, and especially in those that underlie the solution of hard questions in the theory of probability, we cannot do without the help of a directive method, and only a calculus can supply one.

10. Why the ultimate laws of logic are mathematical in form; why they are identical, except at a single point, with the general laws of number; and why they differ at that one point: on these questions it would come close to presumption to try to give a firm answer. They probably lie beyond the reach of our limited faculties. The mind may perhaps be allowed to know the laws it is itself subject to without being given any understanding of their ground and origin, or even, except to a very limited degree, of how well they suit their purpose compared with other systems of law we can imagine. Science does not need that knowledge anyway. Science properly concerns itself with what is; it does not look for reasons why one thing was preferred or appointed over another. These considerations are a sufficient answer to every protest against presenting logic in the form of a calculus. We do not adopt this form because we choose to give logic this way of showing itself. We adopt it because the ultimate laws of thought make it possible, dictate its character, and seem to rule out presenting the science perfectly in any other form. We should remember that the business of science is not to create laws but to discover them. We do not make the constitution of our own minds, however much it may be in our power to shape their character. And just as the laws of the human intellect do not depend on our will, so the forms of the science built on them are, in everything essential, independent of personal choice.

11. Besides setting out the principles of this method in general, the book will apply it to the analysis of a wide variety of propositions, and of chains of propositions that form the premises of demonstrative arguments. These examples are taken from various writers; they differ greatly in complexity, and they cover a wide range of subjects. Some may feel that I have allowed myself too much freedom in choosing them, but I do not think I need to apologize for that. Everyone admits that logic, as a science, can be applied very widely; and it is just as certain that its ultimate forms and processes are mathematical. So any objection _à priori_ to using such forms and processes on a problem of morals or of general philosophy must rest on a misunderstanding or a false analogy. It is not of the essence of mathematics to deal with the ideas of number and quantity. Whether it would be good, as a general habit of mind, to apply symbolic processes to moral argument is another question. Perhaps, as I have said elsewhere, the perfection of the method of logic may be valuable chiefly as evidence that its principles are true in theory. The last thing that anyone who knows the value of mental toil and struggle would want is to replace ordinary reasoning, or to bind it to the rigor of technical forms. That toil and struggle give the mind an athlete's strength; they teach it to wrestle with difficulties and to rely on itself in an emergency. Still, there may be cases, even among matters that everyone agrees belong to ordinary reasoning, where the value of a scientific procedure comes to be felt and recognized. Some examples of this kind will be found in this book.
