Chapter 1: Nature and Design of This Work
(Part 2 of 2)

12. The general doctrine and method of logic explained above also form the basis of a theory of probability, and of a method to go with it. So building that theory and method on these principles will be a separate aim of this book (in the chapters on probability, not included in this edition). It may help to say something here about the nature of this application, and especially about the kind of solutions it leads to. For that purpose I will need to say a little more about the forms in which the results of the logical analysis are presented.

Why must a method in logic come first, as the basis of a theory of probability? The reason can be put in a few words. Suppose we want to know how the expected frequency of some event depends on the known frequencies of other events. Before we can find that out, we must know how the events themselves depend on one another. To put it technically, we must be able to express the event whose probability we are looking for as a function of the events whose probabilities are given. And working out that expression always belongs to logic. Probability, however, in its mathematical sense, can be measured in numbers. So the subject of probability belongs equally to the science of number and to logic. This book differs from all earlier ones in recognizing that both these elements stand side by side. That difference decides, in a great many cases, whether a problem can be solved at all, and it also brings new and important elements into the solutions. So I think it necessary to set out here, at some length, the particular consequences of the theory developed in the following pages.

13. The measure of the probability of an event is usually defined as a fraction. Its numerator is the number of cases favourable to the event, and its denominator is the whole number of cases, favourable and unfavourable, all of them supposed equally likely to happen. This book adopts that definition. At the same time, it shows that there is another way of looking at the subject (I will come to it shortly) that could just as well be taken as fundamental, and that would lead to exactly the same methods and conclusions. I may add this. As far as the accepted conclusions of the theory of probability go, and as far as they follow from its basic definitions, they do not differ from the results of the method of this book (which I take to be equally sound as inference).

Questions in the theory of probability come in many shapes, and algebraic and other conditions can change them in many ways. Even so, there seems to be one general type to which every such question can be brought, or at least as much of each question as truly belongs to the theory of probability. In terms of the _data_ (what is given) and the _quæsitum_ (what is sought), that type can be described as follows. First, the data are the probabilities of one or more given events. Each is either the probability that its event simply happens, or the probability that it happens under certain stated conditions. Second, the _quæsitum_, the thing sought, is the probability that some other event happens, either simply or under conditions. This event is expressed differently from those in the data, but it involves, more or less, the same elements. As for the data, they are either _causally given_, as when we work out the probability of a particular throw of a die from what we know of how the die is made, or they come from watching repeated cases of events succeeding or failing. In the second case, the probability of an event can be defined as the limit that the ratio of favourable cases to all observed cases approaches as the observations go on without end (assuming that nature is uniform). Last, one important distinction remains about the nature of the events, or how they relate. Events are either _simple_ or _compound_. A compound event is one whose expression in language, or whose conception in thought, depends on the expression or conception of other events, which may be called _simple_ in relation to it. To say “it rains” or “it thunders” is to state that a simple event happens. But to say “it rains and thunders,” or “it either rains or thunders,” is to state that a compound event happens, because the expression of that event depends on the elementary expressions “it rains” and “it thunders.” So the test of a simple event is not that its nature is somehow simple. It rests entirely on the way the event is expressed in language or conceived in thought.

14. Now one general problem that the existing theory of probability can solve is this: given the probabilities of any simple events, find the probability of a given compound event, that is, of an event put together in a given way out of those simple events. The problem can also be solved when the compound event whose probability is sought is subject to given conditions, that is, to conditions that also depend in a given way on the given simple events. Besides this general problem, there are some particular problems whose principle of solution is known. Various questions about _causes_ and _effects_ can be solved by known methods on the particular assumption that the causes exclude one another, but apparently not otherwise. Beyond this, it is not clear that anyone has made progress toward what may be called the general problem of the science, namely: given the probabilities of any events, simple or compound, conditional or unconditional, find the probability of any other event, just as freely chosen in its expression and conception. In stating this problem I do not even require that the events whose probabilities are given and the event whose probability is sought should share some elements. It is the job of a method to decide whether the data of a problem are enough for the purpose, and, when they are not, to show where they fall short.

The method of this book can solve this problem in its most unrestricted form. To be more precise, its theoretical solution is given completely, and its practical solution comes down to purely mathematical processes, such as solving and analysing equations. The order and character of the general solution can be described as follows.

15. First, it is always possible, by the preliminary method of the calculus of logic, to express the event whose probability is sought as a logical function of the events whose probabilities are given. The result looks like this. Suppose that \(X\) stands for the event whose probability is sought, and \(A\), \(B\), \(C\), &c. for the events whose probabilities are given, simple or compound. Then the _whole_ relation of the event \(X\) to the events \(A\), \(B\), \(C\), &c. comes out in the form of what mathematicians call a _development_. In the most general case it has four distinct classes of terms. The first class expresses those combinations of the events \(A\), \(B\), \(C\) that both necessarily go with the event \(X\) and necessarily signal that it has happened. The second class expresses the combinations that necessarily go with the event \(X\) but do not necessarily imply it. The third class expresses the combinations that cannot happen together with the event \(X\), though they are not otherwise impossible. The fourth class expresses the combinations that cannot happen under any circumstances. I will not dwell on this account of what the logical analysis of the problem yields, except to remark that its elements are exactly the ones by which our expectation of the event \(X\), as it depends on what we know of the events \(A\), \(B\), \(C\), is affected, and the only ones by which it can be. General reasoning would confirm this conclusion. But general reasoning would not usually manage to untangle the complicated web of events and circumstances out of which the solution just described has to be drawn. Reaching that result is the first step toward the complete solution of the question I set. Note that up to this point the process is logical, that is, it is carried out with symbols that have a logical meaning, and it ends in an equation that can be interpreted as a _proposition_. Let us call this result the _final logical equation_.

The second step of the process deserves close attention. From the final logical equation that the first step has led us to, we read off, by inspection, a series of algebraic equations that contain the complete solution of the problem implicitly. Of how this passage is made, it is enough to say this. There is a definite relation between two sets of laws: the laws by which the probabilities of events are expressed as algebraic functions of the probabilities of the other events they depend on, and the laws by which the logical connection of the events is itself expressed. Like the other agreements of formal law mentioned already, this relation is not founded on a hypothesis. We learn it by observation (I.4) and reflection. If, however, we assumed it _à priori_, in advance of the evidence, as the very basis of the definition of probability, strict deduction would lead from it to the accepted numerical definition as a necessary consequence. As I have already remarked (I.12), the theory of probability stands in an equally close relation to logic and to arithmetic. As far as results go, it makes no difference whether we regard it as springing from arithmetic, or as founded on the relations that connect the two together.

16. The general solutions this method yields have some features, of interest perhaps to the mathematician, that are worth pointing out.

First, since the method does not depend on how many data there are or what kind, it still works when the data are not enough to fix the value sought. When that happens, the final expression of the solution will contain terms with arbitrary constant coefficients. Matching these, there will be terms in the final logical equation (I. 15). Interpreting those terms tells us what new data we need to fix the values of the constants, and so to make the numerical solution complete. If such data cannot be had, we can still give the constants their limiting values \(0\) and \(1\), and so find the limits within which the probability sought must lie, whatever further experience may show. When the event whose probability is sought is _quite_ independent of those whose probabilities are given, the limits found this way will be \(0\) and \(1\), just as they plainly ought to be, and interpreting the constants will only lead us back to a restatement of the original problem.

Second, the expression of the final solution will always involve one particular quantity, found by solving an algebraic equation. When that equation is of high degree, there may seem to be a difficulty about which root to choose. In some cases, it is true, both the given elements and the element sought are so obviously restricted by necessary conditions that no choice is left. But in complicated cases it would be hopeless to find such conditions by unaided reasoning. A separate method is needed for this purpose, one that might fairly be called the Calculus of Statistical Conditions. I will not go further into it here than to say two things. Like the first method, it is based on the use of the “final logical equation.” And it definitely fixes, first, the conditions that the numbers in the data must satisfy for the problem to be real, that is, drawn from a _possible experience_; and second, the numerical limits within which the probability sought would have had to fall if, instead of being found by theory, it had been found directly by observation of the same system of phenomena the data came from. Clearly these limits will be actual limits of the probability sought. Now, if the data are taken to satisfy the conditions just assigned to them, then in every case I have examined there turns out to be one root, and only one, of the final algebraic equation that meets the required limits. Every source of ambiguity is thus removed. It even seems that new truths about the theory of algebraic equations come to light along the way. It is remarkable that this special quantity depends only on the _data_ of the problem, and not at all on its _quæsitum_. So solving one particular problem unties the knot for a whole system of problems, namely all the problems that share the same data, whatever their _quæsita_ may be. This matters whenever we need to draw a series of connected conclusions from one particular set of data. It also gives the solutions of particular problems the family likeness, drawn from their dependence on one central and fundamental unity, that often marks the use of general methods.

17. These considerations, and others like them, justify calling the method of this book a general method for solving questions in the theory of probability. But it does not follow that we can always do without hypotheses. As I have noted, a solution may consist entirely of terms with arbitrary constant coefficients; it may, in fact, be wholly indefinite. If we used only the data of experience, the method of this book would give results of that kind for some of the most important questions it covers. To get a _definite_ solution in such cases, we must fall back on hypotheses that have more or less probability of their own but cannot be exactly verified. Generally such hypotheses differ from the direct results of experience in being logical rather than numerical in character: they lay down the conditions under which phenomena occur, rather than how often they occur. That difference, however, does not matter. Whatever their nature, once assumed, the hypotheses must be counted among the actual data, though they will obviously give the solution itself something of a hypothetical character. Understood this way, with these possible sources for the data actually used, the method is perfectly general. But it is no more answerable for the correctness of the hypotheses brought in than for the correctness of the numerical data taken from experience.

To illustrate, the theory of reducing astronomical observations rests partly on hypotheses. It makes certain assumptions about the nature of error, such as that an error is equally likely to fall on the side of excess or of defect, and so on. Without them it would be impossible to draw any _definite_ conclusions from a set of observations that disagree. But once such assumptions are granted, the rest of the investigation falls strictly within the theory of probability. The same holds for the important problem of working out, from the records of the majorities of a deliberative assembly, the average probability that any one of its members judges correctly. Applied to the bare numerical data, the method of this book gives a solution of the wholly indefinite kind described above. And, to show still more strikingly how insufficient those data are on their own, the interpretation of the arbitrary constants (I. 16) in the solution merely gives back a restatement of the original problem. But if we admit the hypothesis that each mind forms its opinion independently, either absolutely, as in the speculations of Laplace and Poisson, or within limits set by the actual data, as will be seen in this treatise, Chap. XXI. (in the chapters on probability, not included in this edition), the problem takes on a far more definite character. Clearly, then, the further value of the theory of probability must depend a great deal on forming such intermediate hypotheses correctly, wherever the purely experimental data are not enough for a _definite_ solution and the further experience that the interpretation of the final logical equation points to cannot be had. On the other hand, too ready a habit of forming hypotheses on subjects that by their very nature lie beyond human knowledge is bound to damage the credit of the theory of probability, and to throw doubt, in the public mind, on even its most legitimate conclusions.

18. It would perhaps be premature to speculate here on whether the methods of abstract science are ever likely to serve the study of social problems as well as they have served the various branches of physical inquiry. Trying to settle that question on purely _à priori_ grounds, by argument in advance of the evidence, would very likely mislead us. For example, the fact of human free will would seem at first sight to rule out the idea that the movements of society should ever show the kind of orderly development we expect under the rule of physical necessity. Yet the researches of the statistician already reveal facts that contradict that expectation. The records of crime and poverty, for instance, show a degree of regularity unknown in regions untouched by the disturbing influence of human wants and passions. On the other hand, bad seasons, volcanic eruptions, the spread of blight among plants or of epidemic disease among animals, things apparently or mainly the product of natural causes, refuse to be brought under regular laws that we can grasp. “Fickle as the wind” is a proverb. Reflecting on these points teaches us to correct our first judgments somewhat. We learn not to expect, under the rule of necessity, an order that human observation can perceive, unless the interplay of the causes producing it is simple enough. Nor should we think, on the other side, that free agency in the individual cannot coexist with regularity in the movements of the system he is one unit of. Human freedom stands out as an apparent fact of our consciousness. It is also, I believe, a highly probable conclusion by analogy (Chap. XXII., not included in this edition) from the nature of that part of the mind whose scientific constitution we are able to investigate. But whether we accept it as a fact resting on consciousness or as a conclusion approved by reason, it must be understood in a way that does not conflict with an established result of observation. That result is this: phenomena that involve large masses of people do actually show a very remarkable degree of regularity. This lets us collect, in each age, the materials on which any estimate of its state and progress, as far as these show in outward results, must depend. So there is no sound _à priori_ objection to the possibility of the kind of data needed as the experimental foundation of a science of social statistics. Further, whatever else this book may accomplish, I expect it to leave no doubt that there exists a system of abstract principles, and of methods founded on them, by which any collection of social data can be made to yield, explicitly, whatever information it implicitly contains. Where the data are extremely complex, getting this information out may be very hard. The difficulty would not come from any flaw in the theory, but from the sheer labour of the calculations it calls for. It is quite possible that in many cases the difficulty would be one that only combined effort could overcome. But this much, I believe, can be affirmed with perfect safety: we have in theory in every case, and in practice as far as the necessary labour of calculation can be supplied, the means of drawing out from statistical records the seeds of general truths that lie buried in the mass of figures.

19. Beyond these general positions, though, I do not venture to speak with confidence. Applying scientific methods to statistical records might produce results beyond those that can be found without such help. Whether those extra results would repay the labour enough to make it worth carrying out such investigations on a proper scale is a question that perhaps only experience could settle. It is to be hoped, and it might be expected without much presumption, that here as elsewhere the abstract doctrines of science will serve more than intellectual pleasure. Consider the apparent order in which the sciences have developed and, one after another, lent their help to the service of mankind. In that light it does not seem very unlikely that a day may come when similar help will come from branches of knowledge still more closely bound up with human welfare. For the present, though, let the speculations of this book rest simply on their claim to be true.

20. Last, I intend to try to draw from the scientific results of the earlier inquiries some general hints about the nature and constitution of the human mind (in the final chapter, not included in this edition). There is no need here to go into the grounds on which such an inference is possible. One or two general remarks may show the path I will try to follow. It must be admitted that our views of the science of logic are bound to influence greatly, perhaps mainly to decide, our opinions about the nature of the intellectual faculties. Take, for example, this question. Does reasoning consist merely in applying certain first or necessary truths that were stamped on the mind from the start? Or is the mind itself a seat of law, whose working is as plain and as conclusive in the particular case as in the general formula? Or, as some writers of note seem to hold, is all reasoning about particulars? This question, I say, does not merely affect the science of logic. It also bears on forming sound views of how the intellectual faculties are constituted. Again, suppose we conclude that the mind is by its original constitution a seat of law. Then the question of what kind of subjection to law this is also has deep speculative interest. Is it, for instance, an obedience founded on necessity, like the obedience that keeps the heavens turning and preserves the order of nature? Or is it a subjection of some quite different kind? Further, if the mind really is shown to be subject to law, and if its laws are correctly stated, then the question of how those laws are likely, or bound, to influence the course of human thought in different ages is one of great importance. It well deserves patient investigation, as a matter both of philosophy and of history. These and other questions I propose to discuss, however imperfectly, in the concluding part of this book. They belong, perhaps, to the domain of probable or conjectural knowledge rather than to that of positive knowledge. But it may happen that where there is not enough warrant for the certainties of science, there are grounds of analogy good enough to suggest highly probable opinions. It has seemed better to me to keep this discussion entirely for after the main business of this book, which is the investigation of scientific truths and laws. Experience teaches us clearly enough that the right order of progress in every search for truth is from the known to the unknown. There are parts, even of the philosophy and constitution of the human mind, that have been put fully within reach of our investigation. To make a proper knowledge of those parts of our nature the basis of every attempt to see into the shadows and uncertainties of the conjectural realm that lies beyond and above them: that is the course best suited to the limits of our present condition.
