Thucydides was an Athenian who fought in the war he wrote about. In 424 BC, ten years in, he was a general with a squadron in the northern Aegean and arrived too late to stop the Spartan commander Brasidas taking the city of Amphipolis. Athens exiled him for it. He spent the next twenty years out of the city, which meant he could talk to men on the Spartan side as well as his own, and he says himself that the leisure of exile was useful to him. He had begun collecting material at the outbreak in 431 BC, on the judgment that this war would be bigger than any before it.

His method is stated at the start and it is the thing to know before beginning. He gives no divine causes. Events are explained by what men wanted, feared and calculated, and where the evidence is thin he says so. The speeches — and there are many, because this is a war argued about in assemblies before it is fought — are his own composition: he keeps as close as he can to the general sense of what was actually said, and writes what the occasion seemed to call for. Herodotus, a generation earlier, collected what he was told and said when he did not believe it. Those two books are the two ways of doing this, and both are in this collection. Thucydides wrote his to be useful rather than entertaining, and called it a possession for all time rather than a piece written to win applause for the moment.

The two most famous passages sit side by side. At the end of the first year Pericles delivers the funeral oration over the Athenian dead, the fullest statement anyone made of what Athens thought it was. In the next chapter the plague arrives in the crowded city, Thucydides catches it and recovers, and he describes the symptoms and the behavior of the dying with the flatness of a man taking notes. Pericles was dead of it within two years. Later come the Melian dialogue, where Athenian envoys tell a small neutral island that the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must, and the expedition to Sicily, which is the longest and worst thing in the book.

The text stops in the middle of a sentence in the twenty-first year, 411 BC. Thucydides seems to have died before finishing it; the war ran seven more years and Athens lost. This edition is the complete text, all eight books, modernized from Richard Crawley's English translation of 1874.
