Essay Three: What Is the Meaning of Ascetic Ideals?
(Part 5 of 6)

XXI

Where *this* whole kind of priestly medicine is concerned, the "guilty" kind, every word of criticism is one word too many. That such an orgy of feeling, of the kind the ascetic priest likes to prescribe for his patients in this case (under the holiest of names, it goes without saying, and just as thoroughly soaked in the holiness of his purpose), has ever really *done good* to any sick person — who would have the stomach to defend a claim like that? At the very least we ought to agree on what the word "do good" means. If what is meant is that such a system of treatment has *improved* man, I do not object: only I add what "improved" means when I say it — exactly as much as "tamed," "weakened," "discouraged," "refined," "made delicate," "unmanned" (in other words, almost as much as *damaged*…). But when the people in question are mainly the sick, the out-of-sorts, the depressed, then such a system, even granting that it made the sick person "better," under all circumstances makes him *sicker*; just ask the doctors of the insane what a methodical course of penance-torments, fits of contrition and convulsions of redemption always brings with it. Ask history too: everywhere the ascetic priest has pushed through this treatment of the sick, sickness has every time grown deeper and wider with uncanny speed. What was always the "success"? A shattered nervous system, on top of whatever was already sick; and that on the largest scale as on the smallest, in individuals as in masses. In the wake of the training in penance and redemption we find enormous epidemics of epilepsy, the greatest history knows of, like those of the St. Vitus and St. John dancers of the Middle Ages; as another form of its aftermath we find terrible paralyses and lasting depressions, by which the temperament of a people or a city (Geneva, Basel) is sometimes turned once and for all into its opposite; — here belongs the witch-hysteria too, something related to sleepwalking (eight great epidemic outbreaks of it between 1564 and 1605 alone) —; in its wake we likewise find those mass deliriums hungry for death, whose dreadful cry, "evviva la morte," long live death, was heard across the whole of Europe, interrupted now by lustful, now by destruction-crazed quirks of temper: just as the same swing of feeling, with the same breaks and reversals, is still observed everywhere today, in every case where the ascetic doctrine of sin once again scores a great success (the religious neurosis *appears* as a form of "evil being": of that there is no doubt. What it is? That is the question.)

Seen on the grand scale, the ascetic ideal and its sublime moral cult, this most ingenious, most unscrupulous and most dangerous systematizing of every means of emotional excess under the protection of holy intentions, has written itself into the whole history of man in a terrible and unforgettable way; and unfortunately *not only* into his history… I could hardly name anything else that has done such destructive harm to the *health* and racial vigor of Europeans in particular as this ideal has; we may, without any exaggeration, call it *the real disaster* in the history of the health of European man. At most, one influence might be set beside it: the specifically Germanic one, I mean the alcohol poisoning of Europe, which so far has kept strict pace with the political and racial dominance of the Germanic peoples (— wherever they injected their blood, they injected their vice too). — Third in the series would be syphilis — next in line, but at a great distance.

XXII

The ascetic priest has ruined the health of the soul wherever he has so much as come to power; consequently he has also ruined *taste* in the arts and in letters — and he is ruining it still. "Consequently"? — I hope you will simply grant me that "consequently"; at any rate I do not intend to prove it first. A single pointer: it concerns the foundation book of Christian literature, its real model, its "book in itself." Even in the midst of the Greek and Roman splendor, which was a splendor of books as well, face to face with an ancient world of writings that had not yet withered and been smashed to pieces, at a time when one could still read certain books for which we would now trade half of whole literatures, the simplicity and vanity of Christian agitators — they are called the Church Fathers — already dared to decree: "*we* too have our classical literature, *we do not need that of the Greeks*" — and with that they pointed proudly to books of legends, letters of apostles and little apologetic tracts, roughly the way the English "Salvation Army" fights its fight against Shakespeare and other "heathens" today with a literature of the same kind.

I do not love the "New Testament," you will have guessed it already; it almost unsettles me to stand so alone with my taste as regards this most valued, most overvalued piece of writing (the taste of two thousand years is *against* me): but what can I do! "Here I stand, I can do no other" — I have the courage of my bad taste. The *Old* Testament — yes, that is something else altogether: all honor to the Old Testament! In it I find great human beings, a heroic landscape, and something of the very rarest on earth, the incomparable naivety of the *strong heart*; more than that, I find a people. In the New, by contrast, nothing but the small business of little sects, nothing but rococo of the soul, nothing but curlicues, crooked corners and oddities, nothing but the stale air of the prayer meeting, not to forget an occasional whiff of pastoral sweetness that belongs to the period (*and* to the Roman province) and is not so much Jewish as Hellenistic. Humility and self-importance side by side; a chattiness of feeling that almost numbs you; passionateness, no passion; painful posturing; good breeding was plainly missing here altogether. How can anyone make such a fuss over his little failings as these pious little men do! Nobody gives a hoot about it; let alone God. In the end they even want "the crown of eternal life," all these little people of the provinces: but what for? in return for what? Immodesty can go no further. An "immortal" Peter: who could stand *him*! They have an ambition that makes you laugh: *that* crowd chews over its most private concerns, its stupidities, its sadnesses and its street-corner worries, as if the in-itself of things were obliged to care about them; *that* crowd never tires of wrapping God himself up in the smallest misery they happen to be stuck in. And this constant being on first-name terms with God, in the worst possible taste! This Jewish, and not merely Jewish, pawing at God with snout and paw!…

There are small despised "heathen peoples" in eastern Asia from whom these first Christians could have learned something essential, some *tact* in reverence; those peoples, as Christian missionaries testify, do not permit themselves to take the name of their god into their mouths at all. That strikes me as delicate enough; what is certain is that it is too delicate not only for "first" Christians: to feel the contrast, just remember Luther, that "most eloquent" and most immodest peasant Germany has ever had, and the Lutheran tone of voice, which he liked best of all precisely in his conversations with God. Luther's resistance to the mediating saints of the Church (and in particular to "the devil's sow, the pope") was, there is no doubt about it, at bottom the resistance of a lout who was annoyed by the *good etiquette* of the Church, that etiquette of reverence belonging to priestly taste, which admits only the more consecrated and the more silent into the holy of holies and shuts it against the louts. These were to have no say here, precisely here, once and for all — but Luther the peasant flatly wanted it otherwise, that was not *German* enough for him: above all he wanted to speak directly, to speak himself, to speak "without ceremony" with his God… Well, he did it. — The ascetic ideal, as you can guess, was never and nowhere a school of good taste, still less of good manners — at best it was a school of priestly manners —: the reason is that it has something in its very body that is the deadly enemy of all good manners — a lack of measure, a hatred of measure, it is itself a "non plus ultra," an extreme nothing goes beyond.

XXIII

The ascetic ideal has ruined not only health and taste; it has ruined a third thing, a fourth, a fifth, a sixth — I will take care not to say *what* else (when would I ever finish!). It is not what this ideal has *done* that I mean to bring to light here; rather, solely and entirely what it *means*, what it lets us guess at, what lies hidden behind it, beneath it, inside it, what it is the provisional, indistinct expression of, loaded down with question marks and misunderstandings. And it was only with *this* purpose in view that I had the right not to spare my readers a glance at the monstrousness of its effects, including its disastrous effects: to prepare them, that is, for the last and most terrible aspect the question of the meaning of that ideal has for me. What does the *power* of that ideal itself mean, the *monstrousness* of its power? Why has it been given room to this extent? why has it not been better resisted? The ascetic ideal expresses a will: *where* is the opposing will in which an *opposing ideal* would express itself? The ascetic ideal has a *goal* — and it is general enough that all the other interests of human existence, measured against it, look petty and narrow; it interprets ages, peoples, human beings without mercy in the light of this one goal, it allows no other interpretation, no other goal, it rejects, denies, affirms, confirms only in the sense of *its* interpretation (— and was there ever a system of interpretation thought through more completely to the end?); it submits to no power, it believes instead in its precedence over every power, in its unconditional *distance of rank* with regard to every power — it believes that no power on earth exists that does not first have to receive from it a meaning, a right to exist, a value, as a tool for *its* work, as a way and means to *its* goal, to one goal… Where is the *counterpart* to this closed system of will, goal and interpretation? Why is the counterpart *missing*?… Where is the *other* "one goal"?…

But I am told that it is *not* missing, that it has not only fought a long and successful fight against that ideal but has actually, in all the main things, already become master over it: our whole modern *science* is the witness for this, I am told — this modern science which, as a genuine philosophy of reality, plainly believes in itself alone, plainly possesses the courage to be itself and the will to be itself, and has gotten along well enough so far without God, without a beyond and without the virtues of denial. But with noise like that and agitators' chatter you get nowhere with me: these trumpeters of reality are bad musicians, their voices, audibly enough, do *not* come from the depths, it is *not* the abyss of the scientific conscience that speaks out of them — for today the scientific conscience is an abyss —, in such trumpeters' mouths the word "science" is simply an indecency, an abuse, a piece of shamelessness. The exact opposite of what is claimed here is the truth: science today has absolutely *no* faith in itself, let alone an ideal *above* itself — and wherever it is still passion, love, fire, *suffering*, there it is not the opposite of that ascetic ideal but rather *its most recent and most noble form* itself. Does that sound strange to you?…

There are plenty of honest and modest working folk among the scholars of today too, who like their little corner and who, precisely because they like it in there, now and then get a little immodestly loud with the demand that people *ought* to be content today in general, and especially in science — there is so much useful work to be done there. I do not disagree; least of all would I want to spoil these honest workers' pleasure in their craft: for I take pleasure in their work. But the fact that rigorous work is now being done in science and that there are contented workers proves absolutely *nothing* about whether science as a whole has a goal today, a will, an ideal, a passion of great faith. The opposite, as I said, is the case: where science is not the most recent form in which the ascetic ideal appears — and those are cases too rare, too noble, too select for the general verdict to be bent by them — science today is a *hiding place* for every kind of ill humor, unbelief, gnawing worm, self-contempt, bad conscience — it is the *restlessness* of having no ideal itself, the suffering from the *lack* of a great love, the dissatisfaction with an *unwilling* contentment. Oh, what does science not hide today! how much, at the very least, it is *meant* to hide! The competence of our best scholars, their mindless industry, their heads smoking day and night, their very mastery of the craft — how often the real meaning of all that lies in no longer letting themselves see something or other! Science as a means of numbing oneself: *do you know that?*… You wound them — everyone who deals with scholars has the experience — sometimes to the bone with a harmless word, you turn your scholarly friends bitter against you at the very moment you mean to honor them, you drive them beside themselves, merely because you were too coarse to guess whom you were really dealing with: with *sufferers* who refuse to admit to themselves what they are, with people numbed and senseless who fear only one thing: *coming to consciousness*…

XXIV

— And now, by contrast, look at those rarer cases I spoke of, the last idealists there are today among philosophers and scholars: do we perhaps have in them the *opponents* of the ascetic ideal we were looking for, its *counter-idealists*? Indeed, they *believe* themselves to be that, these "unbelievers" (for that is what they all are); being opponents of this ideal seems to be precisely their last scrap of faith, so serious are they on this point, so passionate do their words and gestures become right here: — but does that already mean that what they believe has to be *true*?… We "seekers of knowledge" have by now grown suspicious of believers of every kind; our suspicion has gradually trained us to conclude the reverse of what people used to conclude: namely, wherever the strength of a belief pushes itself far into the foreground, to infer a certain weakness in its provability, even the *improbability* of what is believed. We too do not deny that faith "makes blessed": *that is exactly why* we deny that faith *proves* anything — a strong faith that makes blessed is a reason to suspect the thing it believes in; it does not establish "truth," it establishes a certain probability — of *deception*. How do things stand in this case, then? — These deniers and outsiders of today, these people who are unconditional in one thing, in their claim to intellectual cleanliness, these hard, strict, abstinent, heroic spirits who make up the honor of our age, all these pale atheists, antichrists, immoralists, nihilists, these skeptics, these "ephectics" who hold back from every judgment, these *hectics* of the spirit, wasting as if with fever (and hectics they are, one and all, in some sense or other), these last idealists of knowledge, in whom alone the intellectual conscience dwells and has taken flesh today — they believe themselves, in fact, as detached as possible from the ascetic ideal, these "free, *very* free spirits": and yet, let me reveal to them what they cannot see for themselves — for they stand too close to themselves — this ideal is precisely *their* ideal too, they themselves represent it today, and perhaps no one else does, they themselves are its most spiritualized offspring, its most advanced troop of warriors and scouts, its most insidious, most tender, most elusive form of seduction: — if I am a guesser of riddles in anything, let it be with *this* sentence!… These are far from being *free* spirits: *for they still believe in truth*…

When the Christian crusaders in the East ran into that invincible order of Assassins, that order of free spirits par excellence, whose lowest ranks lived in an obedience that no order of monks has ever matched, they also picked up, by one route or another, a hint about that symbol and password which was reserved for the highest ranks alone, as their secret: "Nothing is true, everything is permitted"… Well now, *that* was *freedom* of the spirit, *with that* the faith in truth itself had been *given notice*… Has a European, a Christian free spirit ever yet strayed into this sentence and its labyrinthine *consequences*? does he know the Minotaur of this cave *from experience*?… I doubt it; more than that, I know otherwise: — nothing is more foreign to these people who are unconditional in one thing, these *so-called* "free spirits," than freedom and release from chains in that sense; in no respect are they more firmly bound, and in their faith in truth in particular they are firm and unconditional as no one else is. I know all this perhaps from too close by: that venerable philosophers' abstinence to which such a faith commits a person, that stoicism of the intellect which in the end forbids itself the No just as strictly as the Yes, that *wanting* to stand still before the factual, before the *brute* fact, that fatalism of the "little facts" (this little fact-alism, as I call it) in which French science now seeks a kind of moral precedence over German, that renouncing of interpretation altogether (of forcing, adjusting, shortening, leaving out, padding, inventing, falsifying and whatever else belongs to the *essence* of all interpreting) — all that, reckoned on the grand scale, expresses asceticism of virtue just as well as any denial of the senses does (at bottom it is only one mode of that denial). But what *compels* them to it, that unconditional will to truth, is the *faith in the ascetic ideal itself*, even if as its unconscious command — make no mistake about it — it is the faith in a *metaphysical* value, a value *of truth in itself*, such as is guaranteed and certified in that ideal alone (it stands and falls with that ideal).

Strictly judged, there is no such thing as a science "without presuppositions"; the idea of one is unthinkable, a fallacy: a philosophy, a "faith," must always be there first, so that from it science can gain a direction, a meaning, a limit, a method, a *right* to exist. (Whoever understands it the other way around, whoever sets out, for instance, to put philosophy "on a strictly scientific foundation," must first turn not only philosophy but truth itself *on its head*: the worst breach of decency there can be toward two such venerable ladies!) Yes, there is no doubt about it — and here I let my "gay science" have its say, compare its fifth book, p. 263 — "the truthful man, in that daring and final sense which faith in science presupposes, *thereby affirms another world* than that of life, nature and history; and insofar as he affirms this 'other world,' what? must he not by that very act deny its counterpart, this world, *our* world?… It is still a *metaphysical faith* on which our faith in science rests — we seekers of knowledge of today too, we godless ones and anti-metaphysicians, we too still take *our* fire from the blaze that a faith thousands of years old has lit, that Christian faith which was Plato's faith too, that God is truth, that truth is *divine*… But what if precisely this is becoming less and less believable, if nothing proves to be divine any longer except error, blindness, the lie — if God himself proves to be our *longest lie*?" — —

At this point it is necessary to stop and think for a long time. Science itself now *needs* a justification (which is not yet to say that there is one for it). Look, on this question, at the oldest and the newest philosophies: none of them shows any awareness of how far the will to truth itself first needs a justification; here is a gap in every philosophy — where does it come from? Because the ascetic ideal has so far been *master* over all philosophy, because truth was posited as being, as God, as the highest court of appeal itself, because truth was not *allowed* to be a problem at all. Is this "allowed" understood? — From the moment faith in the God of the ascetic ideal is denied, *there is also a new problem*: that of the *value* of truth. — The will to truth needs a critique — let us define our own task with these words — for once, as an experiment, the value of truth must be *called into question*… (Anyone who finds this too briefly said is advised to read over that section of "The Gay Science" which bears the title "How far we too are still pious," pp. 260ff., best of all the whole fifth book of that work, and likewise the preface to "Daybreak.")
