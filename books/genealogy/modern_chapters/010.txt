Essay Three: What Is the Meaning of Ascetic Ideals?
(Part 3 of 6)

XII

Suppose that a will to contradiction and anti-nature like this, a will made flesh, is brought to *philosophize*: on what will it vent its innermost willfulness? On whatever is felt most certainly of all to be true, to be real: it will look for *error* precisely where the real instinct of life most unconditionally locates truth. It will, for instance, as the ascetics of the Vedanta philosophy did, demote the body to an illusion, and pain along with it, and multiplicity, and the whole conceptual opposition of "subject" and "object" — errors, nothing but errors! To refuse to believe in your own self, to deny yourself your own "reality" — what a triumph! — and no longer merely over the senses, over what is in plain sight, but a far higher kind of triumph, an act of violence and cruelty against *reason*: a lust that reaches its peak when the ascetic self-contempt, the self-mockery of reason, decrees: "there *is* a realm of truth and of being, but reason, of all things, is *shut out* of it!"… (Let me say in passing: even in Kant's concept of the "intelligible character of things" something of this lecherous ascetic double-dealing survives, this love of turning reason against reason: for "intelligible character," in Kant, means a kind of property of things about which the intellect grasps just this much: that for the intellect it is — *completely and utterly ungraspable*.) — Finally, as seekers of knowledge, of all people, let us not be ungrateful for such resolute reversals of the familiar perspectives and valuations, with which the spirit has raged against itself for all too long, to all appearances wantonly and uselessly: to see differently for once in this way, to *want* to see differently, is no small discipline and preparation of the intellect for its eventual "objectivity" — not "objectivity" understood as "disinterested contemplation" (which is a non-concept and an absurdity), but as the ability to *have your For and Against under your control* and to switch them in and out: so that you know how to use precisely the *difference* of perspectives and of the interpretations our feelings make, in the service of knowledge. For from now on, my dear philosophers, let us guard better against the dangerous old conceptual fairy tale that set up a "pure, will-less, painless, timeless subject of knowledge"; let us be on guard against the tentacles of such contradictory concepts as "pure reason," "absolute spirituality," "knowledge in itself": — these always demand that we conceive of an eye that cannot be conceived at all, an eye that is supposed to have no direction whatsoever, in which the active and interpreting forces are supposed to be tied off, to be missing, though it is only through them that seeing becomes a seeing of something; so what is demanded here is always an absurdity and a non-concept of an eye. There is *only* a perspectival seeing, *only* a perspectival "knowing"; and the *more* feelings we allow to have their say about a thing, the *more* eyes, different eyes, we know how to bring to bear on the same thing, the more complete our "concept" of that thing, our "objectivity," will be. But to eliminate the will altogether, to unhook the feelings one and all, supposing we could do it: what? would that not mean *castrating* the intellect?…

XIII

But let us come back. A self-contradiction like the one the ascetic seems to present, "life *against* life," is — this much is obvious at first sight — simply nonsense, once you reckon it physiologically and no longer psychologically. It can only be *apparent*; it must be a kind of provisional expression, an interpretation, a formula, an arrangement, a psychological misunderstanding of something whose real nature could not be understood for a long time, could not for a long time be named *as it is in itself* — a mere word, wedged into an old *gap* in human knowledge. And let me set the facts against it, briefly: *the ascetic ideal springs from the protective and healing instinct of a degenerating life*, a life that tries by every means to hold itself together and fights for its existence; it points to a partial physiological blockage and exhaustion, against which the deepest instincts of life, the ones still intact, fight on without pause, with ever new means and inventions. The ascetic ideal is one such means: so the truth is exactly the reverse of what the worshippers of this ideal believe — in it and through it, life wrestles with death and *against* death; the ascetic ideal is a trick for the *preservation* of life. That it could rule over man and grow so powerful, to the degree history shows, and especially wherever the civilizing and taming of man was carried through — that expresses a great fact, the *sickliness* of the type of man we have had so far, or at least of the tamed man: the physiological wrestling of man with death (more precisely: with weariness of life, with exhaustion, with the wish for the "end"). The ascetic priest is the wish to be otherwise, to be elsewhere, made flesh — indeed the highest degree of that wish, its real fervor and passion: but it is precisely the *power* of his wishing that is the chain binding him here; precisely through it he becomes a tool that must work to create more favorable conditions for being here and being human — precisely with this *power* he keeps them clinging to existence, the whole herd of those who turned out badly, the out-of-sorts, the shortchanged, the failures, the sufferers from themselves of every kind, by instinctively going ahead of them as their shepherd. You understand me already: this ascetic priest, this apparent enemy of life, this *denier* — he, of all people, belongs among the very great *conserving* and Yes-*creating* powers of life…

What is that sickliness rooted in? For man is sicker, less certain, more changeable, less settled than any other animal, no doubt of it — he is *the* sick animal: where does that come from? Certainly he has also dared more, innovated more, defied more, challenged fate more than all the other animals put together: he, the great experimenter on himself, the unsatisfied, the unsated, who wrestles with animal, nature and gods for the final mastery — he, the still unconquered, the eternally future one, who can no longer find any rest from his own pressing strength, so that his future digs into the flesh of every present, relentless as a spur: — how could such a brave and rich animal not also be the most endangered, the longest and most deeply sick of all sick animals?… Man is sick of it, often enough; there are whole epidemics of this being sick of it (— around 1348, for instance, the time of the Dance of Death): but even this disgust, this weariness, this vexation with himself — all of it bursts out of him so powerfully that it immediately becomes a new chain. The No he says to life brings to light, as if by magic, an abundance of more tender Yeses; indeed, when he *wounds* himself, this master of destruction, of self-destruction — afterward it is the wound itself that forces him *to live*…

XIV

The more normal sickliness is in man — and we cannot deny this normality — the higher we should honor the rare cases of strength in soul and body, the *lucky strokes* of man, and the more strictly we should guard those who turned out well from the worst air of all, the air of the sickroom. Do we do that?… The sick are the greatest danger to the healthy; disaster for the strong comes *not* from the strongest but from the weakest. Does anyone know that?… On the whole, it is not at all the fear of man that we should wish to see lessened: for that fear forces the strong to be strong, and sometimes terrible — it keeps the well-turned-out type of man *upright*. What is to be feared, what works its doom like no other doom, would be not the great fear but the great *disgust* with man; likewise the great *pity* for man. Suppose these two were to mate one day: something of the uncanniest kind would at once and inevitably come into the world, the "last will" of man, his will to nothingness, nihilism. And in fact: much has been prepared for it. Anyone who has not only a nose to smell with but eyes and ears too will sense, almost everywhere he so much as sets foot today, something like the air of a madhouse, of a hospital — I am speaking, as is only fair, of the cultural regions of man, of every kind of "Europe" there now is on earth. The *sickly* are man's great danger: *not* the evil, *not* the "beasts of prey." Those who are failures from the start, the beaten-down, the broken — they are the ones, it is the *weakest*, who most undermine life among men, who most dangerously poison and call into question our trust in life, in man, in ourselves. Where can we escape it, that veiled look that leaves you carrying a deep sadness away with you, that backward-turned look of the one misborn from the beginning, which betrays how such a man talks to himself — that look which is a sigh. "If only I were someone else! that is what this look sighs: but there is no hope. I am what I am: how could I get free of myself? And yet — *I am sick of myself!*"… On such a soil of self-contempt, a real swamp soil, every weed grows, every poisonous plant, and all of it so small, so hidden, so dishonest, so sickly-sweet. Here the worms of revenge and rancor teem; here the air stinks of secrets and of things that cannot be admitted; here the web of the most malicious conspiracy is constantly being spun — the conspiracy of the suffering against those who turned out well and victorious; here the very sight of the victorious is *hated*. And what dishonesty, not to admit this hatred as hatred! What an outlay of big words and poses, what an art of "upright" slander! These failures: what noble eloquence flows from their lips! How much sugary, slimy, humble devotion swims in their eyes! What do they actually want? At least to *act out* justice, love, wisdom, superiority — that is the ambition of these "lowest ones," these sick ones! And how clever such an ambition makes them! Admire above all the counterfeiter's skill with which the stamp of virtue, even the ring, the golden ring of virtue, is imitated here. They have now taken out a lease on virtue entirely for themselves, these weak and hopelessly sickly people, no doubt about it: "we alone are the good, the just," so they say, "we alone are the men of good will." They walk around among us as living reproaches, as warnings to us — as if health, turning out well, strength, pride, the feeling of power were in themselves vicious things, for which one would someday have to atone, bitterly atone: oh how ready they are, at bottom, to *make* others atone, how they thirst to be *hangmen*!

Among them there are plenty of the vengeful disguised as judges, who constantly carry the word "justice" in their mouths like poisonous spit, lips always pursed, always ready to spit at everything that does not look discontented and goes its way in good spirits. Nor is that most disgusting species of the vain missing among them: the lying freaks who are out to act out "beautiful souls" and to bring, say, their botched sensuality to market, wrapped in verses and other diapers, as "purity of heart": the species of moral onanists and "self-gratifiers." The will of the sick to act out *some* form of superiority, their instinct for the crooked paths that lead to a tyranny over the healthy — where would it not be found, this will to power of the very weakest! The sick woman in particular: no one outdoes her in refinements for ruling, oppressing, tyrannizing. Nor does the sick woman spare anything living, anything dead; she digs up the most deeply buried things again (the Bogos of East Africa say: "woman is a hyena"). Look into the background of every family, every corporate body, every community: everywhere the struggle of the sick against the healthy — a quiet struggle, mostly, with little poison powders, with pinpricks, with the sly play of a martyr's face, but sometimes also with that sick-person's Pharisaism of the *loud* gesture, which likes best to play "noble indignation." It would like to make itself heard even in the consecrated halls of science, this hoarse indignant barking of sickly dogs, the snapping dishonesty and rage of such "noble" Pharisees (— I remind readers who have ears, once again, of that Berlin apostle of revenge, Eugen Dühring, who makes the most indecent and repulsive use of the big moral drum in Germany today: Dühring, the first moral bigmouth there now is, even among his own kind, the antisemites). These are all men of ressentiment, these physiologically botched, these worm-eaten people, a whole quivering ground of underground revenge, inexhaustible, insatiable in outbursts against the happy and just as much in masquerades of revenge, in pretexts for revenge: when would they actually reach their last, finest, most sublime triumph of revenge? Without a doubt, when they succeeded in shoving their own misery, all misery whatsoever, *onto the conscience* of the happy: so that one day the happy began to be ashamed of their happiness and perhaps said to one another: "it is a disgrace to be happy! *there is too much misery!*"… But there could be no greater and more fatal misunderstanding than if the happy, those who turned out well, the powerful in body and soul, began in this way to doubt their *right to happiness*. Away with this "world turned upside down"! Away with this shameful softening of feeling! That the sick should *not* make the healthy sick — and that is what such a softening would amount to — that should surely be the highest point of view on earth: — but for that it is above all necessary that the healthy stay *separated* from the sick, guarded even from the sight of the sick, so that they do not mistake themselves for the sick. Or would it perhaps be their task to be nurses or doctors?… But they could not misjudge and deny *their* task worse than that — the higher *should* not degrade itself into a tool of the lower; the pathos of distance *should* keep the tasks apart too, for all eternity! Their right to exist, the privilege of the bell with a full ring over the jangling, cracked one, is a thousand times greater, after all: they alone are the *guarantors* of the future, they alone are *under obligation* to the future of man. What *they* can do, what *they* should do, no sick person could ever be allowed to do or asked to do: but *so that* they can do what only *they* should do, how could they still be free to play the doctor, the comforter, the "Savior" of the sick (the German word is Heiland, which means the healer as well as the one who saves)?… And therefore good air! good air! And away, in any case, from the neighborhood of all the madhouses and hospitals of culture! And therefore good company, *our* company! Or solitude, if it must be! But away, in any case, from the foul fumes of inner rot and of the secret worm-eating of the sick!… So that we, my friends, may defend ourselves at least a while longer against the two worst plagues, which may have been saved up precisely for us — against the *great disgust with man*! against the *great pity for man*!…

XV

If you have grasped in all its depth — and I demand that here, of all places, you *reach deep*, grasp deeply — why it absolutely can *not* be the task of the healthy to nurse the sick, to make the sick healthy, then you have also grasped one more necessity — the necessity of doctors and nurses *who are themselves sick*: and now we have the meaning of the ascetic priest and hold it with both hands. The ascetic priest has to count for us as the predestined Savior, shepherd and advocate of the sick herd: only then do we understand his enormous historical mission. *Rule over the suffering* is his kingdom; his instinct points him to it; in it he has his very own art, his mastery, his kind of happiness. He must be sick himself, he must be related from the ground up to the sick and the shortchanged, in order to understand them — in order to get along with them; but he must also be strong, more master of himself even than of others, undamaged above all in his will to power, so that he has the trust and the fear of the sick, so that he can be for them a hold, a resistance, a support, a constraint, a taskmaster, a tyrant, a god. He has to defend them, his herd — against whom? Against the healthy, no doubt, and also against envy of the healthy; he must be the natural opponent *and despiser* of all rough, stormy, unbridled, hard, violent, beast-of-prey health and power. The priest is the first form of the *more delicate* animal, the one that despises more readily than it hates. He will not be spared having to wage war on the beasts of prey, a war of cunning (of "spirit") more than of force, as goes without saying — for that he will sometimes find it necessary to develop almost a new type of beast of prey in himself, or at least *to signify* one — a new animal terribleness in which the polar bear, the supple, cold, waiting tiger-cat and not least the fox seem bound into a unity as attractive as it is frightening. Suppose need forces him to it: then he steps, bear-solemn, venerable, clever, cold, deceptively superior, as herald and mouthpiece of more mysterious powers, right into the midst of the other kind of beast of prey itself, resolved to sow suffering, discord, self-contradiction on this ground wherever he can and, only too sure of his art, to become master over *the suffering* at any time. He brings ointments and balm with him, no doubt; but first he needs to wound in order to be a doctor; and then, as he stills the pain the wound makes, *he poisons the wound at the same time* — for that above all is what he is good at, this magician and tamer of beasts of prey, in whose circle everything healthy necessarily becomes sick and everything sick necessarily becomes tame. He defends his sick herd well enough, in fact, this strange shepherd — he defends it against itself too, against the badness, spite, malice smoldering in the herd itself and whatever else all addicts and invalids have in common among themselves; he fights cleverly, hard and secretly against the anarchy and the ever-beginning self-dissolution within the herd, in which that most dangerous blasting powder and explosive, *ressentiment*, constantly piles up and piles up. To discharge this explosive so that it blows up neither the herd nor the shepherd — that is his real feat, his highest usefulness too; if we wanted to sum up the value of the priestly existence in the shortest formula, we would have to say straight out: the priest is the one who *changes the direction* of ressentiment.

For every sufferer instinctively looks for a cause of his suffering; more precisely, for a doer; more precisely still, for a *guilty* doer who is capable of suffering — in short, for something living on which he can discharge his emotions, in fact or in effigy, on any pretext at all: for the discharge of emotion is the sufferer's greatest attempt at relief, that is to say at *numbing*, the narcotic he involuntarily craves against torment of any kind. Here alone, I suspect, is where we find the real physiological cause of ressentiment, of revenge and of their relatives: in a craving, that is, for the *numbing of pain through emotion*: — people usually look for it, quite wrongly as it seems to me, in the defensive counter-blow, a mere protective measure of reaction, a "reflex movement" in the event of some sudden injury or danger, of the kind a frog without a head still carries out to get rid of a corrosive acid. But the difference is fundamental: in the one case you want to prevent further damage; in the other, you want to *numb* a tormenting, secret pain that is becoming unbearable, by means of a more violent emotion of some kind, and to get it out of consciousness at least for the moment — for that you need an emotion, the wildest emotion possible, and to stir it up, the first pretext that comes along. "Someone must be to blame for my feeling bad" — this way of reasoning is characteristic of all sickly people, and all the more so the more the true cause of their feeling bad, the physiological one, stays hidden from them (— it may lie, say, in a disease of the sympathetic nerve, or in an excessive secretion of bile, or in a shortage of potassium sulphate and phosphate in the blood, or in pressure in the abdomen that dams up the circulation of the blood, or in degeneration of the ovaries and the like). All sufferers are frighteningly ready and inventive with pretexts for painful emotions; they enjoy their very suspicion, their brooding over wrongs and apparent slights; they root through the entrails of their past and present for dark, questionable stories where they are free to wallow in a tormenting suspicion and get drunk on their own poison of malice — they tear open the oldest wounds, they bleed from scars healed long ago, they make wrongdoers out of friend, wife, child and whatever else is nearest to them. "I am suffering: someone must be to blame for this" — so thinks every sickly sheep. But its shepherd, the ascetic priest, says to it: "Quite right, my sheep! someone must be to blame for it: but you yourself are this someone, you alone are to blame for it — *you yourself are to blame for yourself, and no one else!*"… That is bold enough, false enough: but one thing at least is achieved by it; by it, as I said, the direction of ressentiment is — *changed*.

XVI

We can now guess what, as I picture it, life's instinct as a healer has at least *attempted* through the ascetic priest, and what purpose the temporary tyranny of such paradoxical and paralogical concepts as "guilt," "sin," "sinfulness," "corruption," "damnation" had to serve: to make the sick *harmless* up to a certain point, to destroy the incurable by their own hand, to give the more mildly ill a strict direction toward themselves, a backward direction for their ressentiment ("one thing is needful" —), and in this way to *exploit* the bad instincts of all sufferers for the purpose of self-discipline, self-surveillance, self-overcoming. It goes without saying that with a "medication" of this kind, a mere medication of the emotions, there can be absolutely no question of a real *cure* of the sick in the physiological sense; we could not even claim that the instinct of life had in any way had a cure in view, or in mind. A kind of crowding together and organization of the sick on the one hand (— the word "church" is the most popular name for it), a kind of provisional safeguarding of those who turned out healthier, the more abundantly endowed, on the other, and so the tearing open of a *gulf* between healthy and sick — for a long time that was everything! And it was a great deal! it was *a very great deal*!…

(In this essay, as you can see, I am starting from an assumption that, for readers of the kind I need, I do not first have to justify: that "sinfulness" in man is not a fact but only the interpretation of a fact, namely of a physiological low mood — the latter seen from a moral-religious perspective that no longer has any binding force for us. — That someone *feels* "guilty," "sinful," is absolutely no proof yet that he is right to feel so; any more than someone is healthy merely because he feels healthy. Just remember the famous witch trials: in those days the most sharp-sighted and humane judges had no doubt that guilt was present here; the "witches" *themselves had no doubt about it* — and yet the guilt was missing. — To put that assumption in a broader form: "pain of the soul" itself does not count for me as a fact at all, but only as an interpretation (a causal interpretation) of facts that so far cannot be stated exactly: and so as something that still hangs completely in the air and has no scientific standing — a fat word, really, put in the place of a question mark that is downright spindle-thin. When someone cannot cope with a "pain of the soul," the fault, to put it crudely, is *not* in his "soul"; more probably in his belly (crudely put, as I said: which by no means expresses a wish to be heard crudely, understood crudely, as well…). A strong, well-turned-out man digests his experiences (deeds and misdeeds included) the way he digests his meals, even when he has tough morsels to swallow. If he "cannot cope" with an experience, this kind of indigestion is just as physiological as the other kind — and often, in fact, only one of the consequences of the other. — With a view like this, between ourselves, you can still be the strictest opponent of all materialism…)
