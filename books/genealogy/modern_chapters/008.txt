Essay Three: What Is the Meaning of Ascetic Ideals?
(Part 1 of 6)

Carefree, mocking, violent — that is how wisdom wants *us*: she is a woman, she only ever loves a warrior.

Thus spoke Zarathustra.

I

What is the meaning of ascetic ideals? — For artists, nothing, or too many things; for philosophers and scholars, something like a nose and an instinct for the conditions most favorable to a high spirituality; for women, at best, one *more* charm of seduction, a little languid softness on beautiful flesh, the angelic look of a pretty, fat animal; for the physiologically botched and the out-of-sorts (for the *majority* of mortals), an attempt to feel "too good" for this world, a holy form of debauchery, their chief weapon in the fight against slow pain and boredom; for priests, the real priestly faith, their best tool of power, and also the "very highest" permission to have power; for saints, finally, an excuse for hibernation, their lust for glory in its last and newest form, their rest in nothingness ("God"), their form of madness. But the fact *that* the ascetic ideal has meant so much to man at all — that expresses the basic fact of the human will, its horror vacui, its horror of a vacuum: *he needs a goal* — and he would rather will *nothingness* than *not* will. — Do you understand me?… Have I been understood?… *"Not in the least, sir!"* — Then let us start again from the beginning.

II

What is the meaning of ascetic ideals? — Or, to take one single case, about which I have been asked for advice often enough: what does it mean, for example, when an artist like Richard Wagner, in his old age, pays homage to chastity? In a certain sense, of course, he always did; but only at the very end in an ascetic sense. What does this change of "sense" mean, this radical reversal of sense? — for that is what it was: with it Wagner leapt straight over into his own opposite. What does it mean when an artist leaps over into his own opposite?… Here, assuming we are willing to pause a little over this question, we are at once reminded of the best, strongest, most cheerful, *bravest* time there perhaps ever was in Wagner's life: that was the time when he was inwardly and deeply occupied with the idea of Luther's wedding. Who knows what accidents actually decided that today we have the Meistersinger instead of that wedding music? And how much of the one may still echo in the other? But there is no doubt that this "Luther's Wedding" too would have been in praise of chastity. Admittedly also in praise of sensuality: — and that is just how it would seem right to me, that is just how it would also have been "Wagnerian." For between chastity and sensuality there is no necessary opposition; every good marriage, every real love affair of the heart, is beyond this opposition. Wagner would have done well, it seems to me, to bring this *pleasant* fact home to his Germans once again with the help of a graceful and brave Luther comedy, for among the Germans there are and always have been many slanderers of sensuality; and perhaps Luther's merit is nowhere greater than right here: he had the courage of his *sensuality* (— in those days they called it, delicately enough, "evangelical freedom"…). But even where that opposition between chastity and sensuality really does exist, it luckily need not be a tragic opposition, not by a long way. That should hold at least for all those mortals who have turned out better and are in better spirits, who are far from simply counting their shaky balance between "animal and angel" as one of the arguments against existence — the finest and brightest, like Goethe, like Hafiz, have even seen in it one *more* charm of life. Such "contradictions" are exactly what seduce us into existence… On the other hand, it goes without saying that once the swine who have turned out badly are brought to worship chastity — and there are such swine! — they will see and worship in it only their own opposite, the opposite of the botched swine — oh, with what tragic grunting and zeal! one can imagine it — that embarrassing and superfluous opposition which Richard Wagner, undeniably, still wanted to set to music and put on the stage at the end of his life. *What for, though?* as we may fairly ask. For what did the swine matter to him, what do they matter to us? —

III

Along the way, of course, that other question cannot be avoided: what did he actually have to do with that manly (ah, so unmanly) "simpleton from the country," that poor devil and child of nature, Parsifal, whom he finally makes a Catholic by such slippery means — what? was this Parsifal even meant *seriously*? One might be tempted to guess the opposite, even to wish it — that Wagner's Parsifal was meant cheerfully, as a closing piece and satyr play, so to speak, with which Wagner the tragedian wanted to take his leave of us, of himself, and above all *of tragedy*, in a way fitting and worthy of him in particular: namely with an excess of the highest and most willful parody of the tragic itself, of the whole dreadful earthly seriousness and earthly misery of former days, of the *crudest form* of the ascetic ideal's unnaturalness, overcome at last. That, as I said, would have been exactly worthy of a great tragedian: for a tragedian, like every artist, reaches the last summit of his greatness only when he knows how to see himself and his art *beneath* him — when he knows how to *laugh* at himself. Is Wagner's "Parsifal" his secret laugh of superiority at himself, the triumph of the final, highest artistic freedom, artistic otherworldliness, that he had won at last? We would like to wish it, as I said: for what would a Parsifal *meant seriously* be? Do we really have to see in it (as someone put it, against me) "the monstrous offspring of a hatred of knowledge, spirit and sensuality gone mad"? A curse on the senses and the spirit in one hatred and one breath? A falling-away and a turning back to ideals that are Christian, sickly and afraid of the light? And in the end even a denial of himself, a crossing-out of himself, on the part of an artist who until then had aimed with all the power of his will at the opposite, namely at the *highest spiritualization and sensualization* of his art? And not only of his art: of his life too. Remember how enthusiastically Wagner in his day walked in the footsteps of the philosopher Feuerbach: Feuerbach's phrase about "healthy sensuality" — in the thirties and forties that rang in Wagner's ears, as in those of many Germans (— they called themselves the "*young* Germans"), like the word of redemption. Did he in the end *learn otherwise* on this point? It seems, at the very least, that in the end he had the will to *teach otherwise* on it… And not only with the Parsifal trombones blaring down from the stage: — in the murky writings of his last years, as unfree as they are helpless, there are a hundred places where a secret wish and will betrays itself, a faint-hearted, uncertain, unconfessed will to preach, quite literally, turning back, conversion, denial, Christianity, the Middle Ages, and to tell his disciples "it is nothing! Seek salvation elsewhere!" Even the "blood of the Redeemer" is invoked at one point…

IV

If I am to say what I think in such a case, which has much that is embarrassing about it — and it is a *typical* case —: we certainly do best to separate an artist from his work far enough that we do not take him as seriously as his work. He is, after all, only the precondition of his work, the womb, the soil, sometimes the dung and manure on which, out of which, it grows — and so, in most cases, something we have to forget if we want to enjoy the work itself. Insight into the *origin* of a work is the business of the physiologists and vivisectors of the spirit: never, ever of the aesthetic people, the artists! The poet and shaper of Parsifal could no more be spared a deep, thorough, even terrible immersion in the contrasts of the medieval soul, a climb down into them, a hostile withdrawal from all height, rigor and discipline of the spirit, a kind of intellectual *perversity* (if you will forgive me the word), than a pregnant woman can be spared the disgusting and strange things of pregnancy: things which, as I said, we have to *forget* in order to enjoy the child. We should guard against the confusion that an artist himself falls into only too easily, out of what the English call psychological contiguity, things lying side by side in the mind: as if he himself *were* what he is able to portray, think up, express. In fact the situation is this: *if* he were just that, he would simply never portray it, think it up, express it; a Homer would not have written an Achilles, a Goethe no Faust, if Homer had been an Achilles and if Goethe had been a Faust. A complete and whole artist is separated for all eternity from the "real," from what is actual; on the other hand we can understand how he might at times grow tired of this eternal "unreality" and falseness of his innermost existence, tired to the point of despair — and that he then very likely tries, for once, to reach over into what is most forbidden to him of all, into the actual, to actually *be*. With what success? You can guess… It is *the typical half-wish* of the artist: the same half-wish to which the aging Wagner also fell victim, and which he had to pay for so dearly, so disastrously (— through it he lost the valuable part of his friends). But in the end, quite apart from this half-wish, who would not wish altogether, for Wagner's own sake, that he had taken leave of us and his art *differently*, not with a Parsifal, but more victoriously, more sure of himself, more Wagnerian — less misleading, less ambiguous about what he wanted as a whole, less Schopenhauerian, less nihilistic?…

V

— So what is the meaning of ascetic ideals? In the case of an artist, we are beginning to see it: *nothing at all*!… Or so many things that it is as good as nothing at all!… Let us first eliminate the artists: they are nowhere near independent enough in the world and *against* the world for their valuations, and the changes in them, to deserve any interest *in themselves*! At all times they have been the valets of some morality or philosophy or religion; quite apart from the fact that, unfortunately, they have often enough been the all-too-pliant courtiers of their followers and patrons, and keen-nosed flatterers of old powers or of powers just coming up. At the very least they always need a bulwark, a backing, an authority already established: artists never stand on their own, standing alone goes against their deepest instincts. So, for example, Richard Wagner took the philosopher Schopenhauer, "when the time had come," as his front man, as his bulwark: — who would think it even conceivable that he could have had the *courage* for an ascetic ideal without the backing that Schopenhauer's philosophy offered him, without the authority of Schopenhauer, which in the seventies was *gaining the upper hand* in Europe? (and that is without even reckoning whether, in the *new* Germany, an artist would have been possible at all without the milk of a pious, Reich-pious way of thinking).

— And with that we have arrived at the more serious question: what does it mean when a real *philosopher* pays homage to the ascetic ideal, a spirit who really stands on his own, like Schopenhauer, a man and a knight with a gaze of bronze, who has the courage to be himself, who knows how to stand alone and does not wait first for front men and nods from above? — Let us consider right away Schopenhauer's remarkable position on *art*, which some kinds of people even find fascinating: for it was plainly this that *first* brought Richard Wagner over to Schopenhauer (talked into it, as everyone knows, by a poet, by Herwegh), and so completely that a full-blown theoretical contradiction tore open between his earlier and his later aesthetic creed — the former expressed, for example, in "Opera and Drama," the latter in the writings he published from 1870 on. In particular, and this is perhaps the strangest thing, from then on Wagner ruthlessly changed his judgment on the value and place of *music* itself: what did he care that until then he had made music into a means, a medium, a "woman," which absolutely needed a purpose, a man, in order to thrive — namely drama! He grasped all at once that *more* could be made of Schopenhauer's theory and innovation to the greater glory of music — namely with the *sovereignty* of music as Schopenhauer understood it: music set apart from all the other arts, the independent art in itself, *not*, like them, offering images of the world of appearances, but speaking the language of *the* will itself, straight out of the "abyss," as its most personal, most original, least derivative revelation. With this extraordinary rise in the value of music, as it seemed to grow out of Schopenhauer's philosophy, *the musician* himself also went up in price all at once, as never before: he now became an oracle, a priest, indeed more than a priest, a kind of mouthpiece of the "in itself" of things, a telephone from the beyond — from then on he talked not only music, this ventriloquist of God — he talked metaphysics: what wonder that one day he finally talked *ascetic ideals*?…

VI

Schopenhauer made use of Kant's version of the aesthetic problem — though he certainly did not look at it with Kant's eyes. Kant meant to do art an honor when, among the predicates of the beautiful, he favored and put in the foreground those that make up the honor of knowledge: impersonality and universal validity. This is not the place to argue whether that was not, in the main, a mistake; all I want to underline is that Kant, like all philosophers, instead of approaching the aesthetic problem from the experiences of the artist (the creator), thought about art and the beautiful only from the "spectator's" side, and in doing so, without noticing it, got the "spectator" himself into the concept "beautiful." If only the philosophers of the beautiful had at least known this "spectator" well enough! — known, that is, as a great *personal* fact and experience, as a wealth of their own strong experiences, desires, surprises, raptures in the field of the beautiful! But the opposite, I fear, was always the case: and so from the very start we get from them definitions in which, as in that famous definition Kant gives of the beautiful, the lack of any finer self-experience sits in the shape of a fat worm of basic error. "Beautiful," Kant said, "is what pleases *without interest*." Without interest! Compare with this definition that other one, made by a real "spectator" and artist — Stendhal, who once calls the beautiful a promise of happiness. Here, at any rate, the very thing Kant singles out as the one mark of the aesthetic state is *rejected* and struck out: disinterestedness. Who is right, Kant or Stendhal? — If, of course, our aestheticians never tire of throwing into the scales, in Kant's favor, that under the spell of beauty one can look *even* at unclothed female statues "without interest," then we may be allowed to laugh a little at their expense: — the experiences of *artists* on this ticklish point are "more interesting," and Pygmalion, at any rate, was *not* necessarily an "unaesthetic man." Let us think all the better of the innocence of our aestheticians, which is mirrored in such arguments; let us count it to Kant's credit, for example, what he has to teach, with the naivety of a country parson, about the peculiarities of the sense of touch!

— And here we come back to Schopenhauer, who stood far closer to the arts than Kant did and yet never got out from under the spell of Kant's definition: how did that happen? The circumstance is strange enough: he interpreted the words "without interest" for himself in the most personal way, out of an experience that must have been one of the most regular in his life. On few things does Schopenhauer speak as confidently as on the effect of aesthetic contemplation: he says of it that it counteracts precisely *sexual* "interestedness," rather like lupulin and camphor; he never tired of glorifying *this* getting free of the "will" as the great advantage and use of the aesthetic state. Indeed one might be tempted to ask whether his basic conception of "will and representation," the thought that there can be a redemption from the "will" only through "representation," did not take its origin from a generalization of that sexual experience. (In all questions about Schopenhauer's philosophy, by the way, we should never lose sight of the fact that it is the conception of a young man of twenty-six; so that it shares not only in what is specific to Schopenhauer but in what is specific to that season of life.) Let us hear, for example, one of the most explicit passages among the countless ones he wrote in honor of the aesthetic state ("The World as Will and Representation," I, 231); let us hear the tone in it, the suffering, the happiness, the gratitude with which such words were spoken. "This is the painless state that Epicurus praised as the highest good and as the state of the gods; for that moment we are rid of the vile pressure of the will, we keep the Sabbath from the prison labor of willing, the wheel of Ixion stands still"… What vehemence of words! What images of torment and of long weariness! What an almost pathological setting of "that moment" against the rest of time, the usual "wheel of Ixion," the "prison labor of willing," the "vile pressure of the will"! — But suppose Schopenhauer were right a hundred times over for his own person, what would that do for insight into the nature of the beautiful? Schopenhauer described one effect of the beautiful, the will-calming one — is it even the regular one? Stendhal, as I said, a nature no less sensual than Schopenhauer but one that turned out happier, stresses another effect of the beautiful: "the beautiful *promises* happiness"; to him it is precisely the *arousing of the will* (of "interest") by the beautiful that seems to be the fact of the matter. And could we not, in the end, object to Schopenhauer himself that he is quite wrong to think himself a Kantian here, that he did not understand Kant's definition of the beautiful in a Kantian way at all — that the beautiful pleased him too out of an "interest," even out of the very strongest, most personal interest of all: that of the tortured man who gets free of his torture?… And to come back to our first question, "what does it *mean* when a philosopher pays homage to the ascetic ideal?", here we at least get a first hint: he wants *to get free of a torture*. —

VII

Let us not pull long faces right away at the word "torture": precisely in this case there is enough to set against it, enough to subtract — there is even something to laugh at. Let us not underestimate, above all, the fact that Schopenhauer, who really did treat sexuality as a personal enemy (its tool included, woman, this "instrument of the devil"), *needed* enemies in order to stay in good spirits; that he loved grim, bilious, black-green words; that he raged for the sake of raging, out of passion; that he would have fallen ill, would have become a *pessimist* (— for he was not one, however much he wished to be) without his enemies, without Hegel, woman, sensuality and the whole will to exist, to stay on. Otherwise Schopenhauer would *not* have stayed on, you can bet on that, he would have run away: but his enemies held him fast, his enemies seduced him back into existence again and again, his anger was, just as with the ancient Cynics, his balm, his recreation, his wages, his remedy against disgust, his *happiness*.

So much for what is most personal in the case of Schopenhauer; on the other hand there is also something typical about him — and only here do we come back to our problem. It is undeniable that as long as there are philosophers on earth, and everywhere there have been philosophers (from India to England, to take the opposite poles of the gift for philosophy), there exists a genuine philosophers' irritability and grudge against sensuality — Schopenhauer is only its most eloquent and, if one has the ear for it, also its most thrilling and delightful outburst —; there exists likewise a genuine philosophers' bias and warmth toward the whole ascetic ideal; we should not fool ourselves about that, or talk ourselves out of it. Both belong, as I said, to the type; if a philosopher lacks both, then he is — you may be sure of it — never anything but a "so-called" one. What does that *mean*? For this state of affairs has first to be interpreted: *in itself* it just stands there, dumb for all eternity, like every "thing in itself." Every animal, and so also the philosophical beast, strives instinctively for an optimum of favorable conditions under which it can let its strength out completely and reach its maximum in the feeling of power; every animal just as instinctively, and with a fineness of scent that is "higher than all reason," abhors every kind of troublemaker and obstacle that lies or could lie across this path to the optimum (— it is *not* its path to "happiness" that I am talking about, but its path to power, to action, to the most powerful doing, and in most cases, in fact, its path to unhappiness). In this way the philosopher abhors *marriage*, together with whatever might talk him into it — marriage as an obstacle and a disaster on his path to the optimum. Which great philosopher has been married so far? Heraclitus, Plato, Descartes, Spinoza, Leibniz, Kant, Schopenhauer — they were not; more than that, one cannot even *imagine* them married. A married philosopher belongs *in comedy*, that is my proposition: and that exception, Socrates — the malicious Socrates got married, it seems, ironically, precisely in order to demonstrate *this* proposition. Every philosopher would speak as Buddha once spoke when the birth of a son was announced to him: "Râhula is born to me, a fetter is forged for me" (Râhula here means "a little demon"); every "free spirit" would have to come to a thoughtful hour, assuming he has had a thoughtless one before it, like the hour that once came to this same Buddha — "cramped and crowded, he thought to himself, is life in the house, a place of impurity; freedom is in leaving the house": "and because he thought this, he left the house." The ascetic ideal points out so many bridges to *independence* that a philosopher cannot hear the story of all those resolute men who one day said No to all unfreedom and went off into some *desert* without rejoicing inwardly and clapping his hands: even granting that they were merely strong donkeys and the very opposite of a strong spirit. So what does the ascetic ideal mean in the case of a philosopher? My answer is — you will have guessed it long ago: at the sight of it the philosopher smiles, as at an optimum of the conditions for the highest and boldest spirituality — he does *not* deny "existence" with it, rather he affirms in it *his* existence and *only* his existence, and perhaps to the point where the wicked wish is not far from his mind: let the world perish, let there be philosophy, let there be the philosopher, let there be *me*!…
