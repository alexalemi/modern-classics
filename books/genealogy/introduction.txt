Friedrich Nietzsche (1844–1900) wrote On the Genealogy of Morals in about three weeks in July 1887, at Sils-Maria in the Swiss Alps, and had it printed in Leipzig that November at his own expense. He was forty-two, retired from his professorship at Basel for eight years, half-blind and often ill, and almost unread. The year before, he had published Beyond Good and Evil, and almost nobody had understood it. The Genealogy was meant to say the same things more plainly. Its subtitle is "A Polemic": it is written to start a fight.

The book is a preface and three essays. The first asks where our words "good" and "evil" came from, and answers that there were once two moralities: the nobles' "good and bad," which meant strong and weak, and a morality invented by the people they ruled, who turned the nobles' good into "evil" and called their own weakness virtue. Nietzsche calls this the slave revolt in morality. The second essay traces guilt and conscience back to debts and punishment, and argues that the bad conscience is cruelty turned inward. The third asks why priests, philosophers, artists and saints have worshipped self-denial, and answers that suffering people would rather suffer for a reason than for none.

A few of his terms need a word first. Ressentiment is French, and he uses the French: not ordinary resentment but a lasting, powerless grudge that has to take its revenge in imagination. The "blond beast" of the first essay is the lion, the beast of prey, which he says lies inside every noble race, Roman, Arab, Japanese and Germanic alike. The Nazis later quoted the phrase as racial praise of Germans. Nietzsche himself mocks German nationalists throughout, and calls antisemites liars and speculators. His sister Elisabeth, who married one and controlled his papers after his collapse in 1889, did much to spread the misreading.

He also makes claims a modern reader should meet as his, not ours. In the second essay he asserts that Africans feel pain less than Europeans do, a common belief of his century with no basis; it is translated as he wrote it. His arguments about Jews, priests, women and the weak are given at full strength, because the book cannot be judged otherwise.

This edition is translated from Nietzsche's German, with Horace B. Samuel's 1913 English version consulted for sense only. His dashes, italics, rhetorical questions and the imagined reader he keeps interrupting himself to answer are all kept. Each essay keeps his own Roman section numbers.
