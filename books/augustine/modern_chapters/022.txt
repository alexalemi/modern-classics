Book Ten: Memory

(Part 2 of 5)

Great is this power of memory, exceedingly great, my God: a vast and boundless room. Who has ever reached the bottom of it? And yet this is a power of mine, and belongs to my nature; and I do not myself take in everything that I am. So the mind is too narrow to hold itself. And where can what it does not hold of itself be? Outside it, and not inside? Then how does it not take itself in? An extraordinary astonishment comes over me at this; I am seized with amazement.

And men go off to marvel at the heights of mountains, and the huge waves of the sea, and the broad courses of rivers, and the sweep of the ocean, and the circling of the stars — and they pass themselves by. They do not marvel that when I spoke of all these things I was not seeing them with my eyes, and yet could not have spoken of them unless I were at that moment actually seeing, inwardly in my memory, the mountains and waves and rivers and stars I had seen, and the ocean that I take on trust — and seeing them with the same vast distances between them as when I saw them outside. And yet I did not draw them into myself by seeing them, when I looked at them with my eyes; they themselves are not in me, only their images. And I know by which bodily sense each of them was stamped on me.

But it is not only these things that the immeasurable capacity of my memory holds. Here too is everything I learned of the liberal arts and have not yet forgotten, moved as it were to some inner place which is not a place at all. And it is not the images of them that are there but the things themselves. For whatever I know of literature, or of the art of argument, or of how many kinds of question there are, exists in my memory in such a way that I have not taken in the image and left out the thing; it is not as if it had sounded and passed away like a voice fixed on the ear by an impression, which could be recalled as if it were still sounding when it is not; or as a smell, which while it passes and evaporates into the air affects the sense of smell and so conveys into the memory an image of itself, which we renew by remembering; or as food, which certainly has no taste any more once it is in the stomach and yet still in a manner tastes in the memory; or as anything the body perceives by touch, which the memory still conceives after it has been taken away from us. For those things are not transferred into the memory; only their images are caught up, with an admirable speed, and stored in remarkable cabinets, so to speak, and from there wonderfully brought out again by the act of remembering.

But now, when I hear that there are three kinds of question — whether a thing is, what it is, and what sort it is — I do indeed hold the images of the sounds that make up those words, and know that those sounds passed with a noise through the air and are now gone. But the things themselves that those sounds signify I never reached with any bodily sense, and never made out anywhere except in my mind; and it is not their images I have laid up in my memory but the things themselves. How they got into me, let them say if they can; for I have gone over all the entrances of my flesh and cannot find which one they came in by. The eyes say, "If those images were coloured, we reported them." The ears say, "If they made a sound, we announced them." The nostrils say, "If they smelled, they came past us." Taste says, "Unless they have a flavour, do not ask me." Touch says, "If it has no size, I did not handle it; and if I did not handle it, I gave no notice of it." So where and how did these things get into my memory? I do not know how. When I learned them, I was not taking somebody else's word for them but recognising them in my own mind; and approving them as true, I entrusted them to my memory, laying them up so as to bring them out whenever I wanted. So they were in my heart before I learned them, and yet they were not in my memory. Where then? And why, when they were spoken, did I recognise them and say, "That is right, that is true", unless they were already in the memory, but thrown back and buried in deeper recesses, so that if somebody else's prompting had not drawn them out, I might have been unable to conceive them at all?

So we find that to learn things of this kind — things whose images we do not take in through the senses, but which we perceive inwardly by themselves, without images, as they are — is nothing but to gather up by thinking, and by attending to make sure, the things the memory already held scattered and unarranged, so that they are laid ready to hand in that same memory where before they lay unknown, scattered and neglected, and so come readily to a mind grown familiar with them. And how many things of this sort my memory carries that have already been found out and, as I said, laid ready to hand — things we are said to have learned and come to know! If I stop calling them to mind even for a short time, they sink again and slip back, as it were, into deeper recesses, so that they have to be thought out from there again as if they were new, since they have nowhere else to be; they must be gathered together again in order to be known, that is, collected out of their scattering. This is where the Latin word for thinking comes from: cogo means to collect, and cogito, to think, means to collect again. And the mind has taken this word for its own, so that what is properly called thinking is not what is collected in any old way but what is re-collected, that is, brought together in the mind.

The memory also contains countless principles and laws of numbers and dimensions, none of them stamped there by any bodily sense, since they have no colour or sound or taste or smell or feel. I have heard the sound of the words that stand for them when they are discussed; but the sounds are one thing and the things another. The sounds are different in Greek and Latin; the things are neither Greek nor Latin nor any other language. I have seen architects' lines, the very finest, like a spider's thread; but those are still different — they are not the images of the lines my bodily eye showed me. Anyone knows them who recognises them inside himself without any conception whatever of a body. I have also perceived the numbers of the things we count with all the senses of my body; but the numbers we count with are different, and are not the images of those, and therefore they truly are. Let the man who cannot see them laugh at me for saying this, and I shall pity him for laughing.

All these things I remember, and I remember how I learned them. I have also heard, and remember, many things falsely objected against them; and though the objections are false, it is not false that I remember them. And I remember distinguishing between those truths and these falsehoods brought against them. And I can see that distinguishing them now is one thing, and remembering that I often distinguished them when I often thought about them is another. So I remember having often understood these things; and what I now make out and understand, I lay up in my memory, so that later I may remember that I understood it now. So I also remember having remembered; just as later, if I call to mind that I have been able to remember these things now, it will be by the power of memory that I call it to mind.

The same memory also contains the feelings of my mind — not in the way my mind itself contains them when it feels them, but in a quite different way, according to a power of its own. For without rejoicing I remember having rejoiced, and without sorrow I recall past sorrow. I look back without fear on having once been afraid, and call to mind a past desire without desire. Sometimes the opposite happens, and I remember a past sorrow with joy, and a past joy with sorrow. There is nothing remarkable in this as far as the body goes, since the mind is one thing and the body another; so if I remember a past bodily pain with joy, that is not so surprising. But this memory itself is mind. When we give something into somebody's charge to be kept in memory, we say, "Make sure you keep it in mind"; and when we forget, we say, "It did not come to my mind," or, "It slipped my mind," calling the memory itself the mind. That being so, how is it that when I remember a past sorrow with joy, the mind has joy and the memory has sorrow — the mind being joyful over the joyfulness in it, and the memory not being sad over the sadness in it? Does memory perhaps not belong to the mind? Who would say that? So the memory is, so to speak, the stomach of the mind, and joy and sadness are like sweet and bitter food, which, when committed to the memory, are passed into that stomach, where they can be stored but cannot be tasted. It is ridiculous to imagine these are really alike — and yet they are not entirely unlike.

But now, it is out of my memory that I bring it when I say there are four disturbances of the mind: desire, joy, fear, sorrow. And whatever I can argue about them, dividing each into its subordinate kinds and defining it, I find in my memory what to say and bring it out from there — and yet I am not disturbed by any of these disturbances when I remember them by calling them to mind. And before I recalled and brought them back, they were there, which is why they could be brought out by recollection. Perhaps, then, they come out of the memory as food is brought up out of the stomach by chewing the cud. Then why does the man who is arguing and recollecting like this not taste in the mouth of his thinking the sweetness of joy or the bitterness of sorrow? Is the comparison unlike in this, because it is not alike in every respect? For who would willingly talk about these things if, every time we named grief or fear, we were forced to be sad or afraid? And yet we could not talk about them if we did not find in our memory not only the sounds of the names, according to the images stamped there by the senses of the body, but ideas of the things themselves — which we never received by any bodily entrance, but which the mind, perceiving them by the experience of its own passions, committed to the memory, or which the memory retained of itself without being given them.

But whether by images or not, who can readily say? I name a stone, I name the sun, the things themselves not being present to my senses but their images to my memory. I name a bodily pain, and it is not present to me when nothing aches; yet unless its image were present in my memory I should not know what to say about it, or be able to tell pain from pleasure in discussion. I name bodily health; and being sound in body, the thing itself is present with me — and yet unless its image were also present in my memory, I could not possibly recall what the sound of the name means. And a sick man, hearing health named, would not recognise what was said, unless the same image were retained by the power of memory, though the thing itself were absent from his body. I name the numbers we count with; and it is not their images but the numbers themselves that are present in my memory. I name the image of the sun, and that image is present in my memory — for I do not recall the image of its image; the image itself is present to me as I call it to mind. I name memory, and I recognise what I name. And where do I recognise it, but in memory itself? Is memory also present to itself by an image, and not by itself?

And what about when I name forgetfulness, and recognise what I name? Where would I recognise it from, if I did not remember it? I am not talking about the sound of the word but about the thing it means; and if I had forgotten that, I could not recognise what the sound means. So when I remember memory, memory itself is present to itself through itself; but when I remember forgetfulness, both memory and forgetfulness are present — memory, by which I remember, and forgetfulness, which I remember. But what is forgetfulness except the absence of memory? How then is it present for me to remember it, when its being present makes remembering impossible? But if what we remember we hold in memory, and if without remembering forgetfulness we could never recognise the thing meant when we heard the word, then forgetfulness is retained by the memory. So it is present, so that we do not forget it — and being present, we forget. Are we to understand from this that forgetfulness, when we remember it, is present to the memory not by itself but by its image? Because if it were present by itself, it would not make us remember but forget. Who will search this out? Who will grasp how it works?

Lord, I certainly labour at it, and labour in myself; I have become a heavy soil needing too much sweat of the brow. We are not now investigating the regions of the sky, or measuring the distances of the stars, or asking how the earth is balanced. It is I myself who remember; I, the mind. There is nothing so remarkable if what I am not is far from me. But what is nearer to me than myself? And look: the power of my own memory is not understood by me, and I cannot so much as name myself without it. For what am I to say, when it is plain to me that I remember forgetfulness? Am I to say that what I remember is not in my memory? Or am I to say that forgetfulness is in my memory precisely so that I should not forget? Both are utterly absurd. What third way is there? How can I say the image of forgetfulness is retained by my memory and not forgetfulness itself, when I remember it? And how could I say even that, seeing that when the image of anything is stamped on the memory the thing itself must be present first for the image to be stamped from? For that is how I remember Carthage, and every place I have been, and the faces of men I have seen, and the things reported by the other senses, and the health or sickness of my body. When those things were present, my memory took images from them, which, being present with me, I could look at and bring back in my mind when I remembered them in their absence. If, then, this forgetfulness is held in the memory through its image and not through itself, then plainly it must once have been present for the image to be taken. But when it was present, how did it write its image in the memory, seeing that forgetfulness by being present rubs out even what it finds already noted down? And yet, in whatever way — and that way is past conceiving and past explaining — I am certain that I do remember forgetfulness itself, the very thing by which what we remember is rubbed out.

Great is the power of memory, a fearful thing, my God, a deep and boundless complexity; and this thing is the mind, and this is myself. What am I then, my God? What kind of nature am I? A life various and manifold and enormously immense. Look, in the plains and caves and caverns of my memory, countless and countlessly full of countless kinds of things — either by images, as with all bodies, or by being actually there, as with the arts, or by certain notions or impressions, as with the feelings of the mind, which the memory holds even when the mind is not feeling them, since whatever is in the memory is also in the mind — over all these I run and fly; I dive in on this side and on that as far as I can, and there is no end to it. So great is the power of memory, so great the power of life, even in a man who is mortal.

What shall I do, then, my true life, my God? I shall go beyond this power of mine that is called memory; I shall go beyond it, so that I may come near to You, sweet light. What do You say to me? See, I am climbing up through my mind towards You who remain above me. Yes, I shall now go beyond this power of mine that is called memory, wanting to reach You where You can be reached, and to hold fast to You where one can hold fast to You. For even animals and birds have memory; otherwise they could not find their way back to their dens and nests, or do many other things they are used to; indeed they could not get used to anything except by memory. So I shall go beyond memory too, so as to reach him who has separated me from the four-footed beasts and made me wiser than the birds of the air. I shall go beyond memory too — and where shall I find You, truly good and certain sweetness? And where shall I find You? If I find You outside my memory, then I do not hold You in my memory. And how shall I find You, if I do not remember You?

For the woman who had lost her coin and looked for it with a light would never have found it unless she remembered it. When it was found, how would she have known it was the right one unless she remembered it? I remember looking for and finding many things; and I know this from it, that when I was looking for one of them and was asked, "Is this it? Is that it?" I kept saying no until the thing I was looking for was held out to me. If I had not remembered it, whatever it was, then even if it were held out to me I still would not find it, because I could not recognise it. And that is always the way when we look for and find something lost. Even so, when something disappears from sight by accident but not from the memory — any visible object — its image is still kept inside, and it is looked for until it is restored to sight; and when it is found, it is recognised by the image that is within. And we do not say we have found what was lost unless we recognise it; and we cannot recognise it unless we remember it. So it was lost to the eyes but kept in the memory.
