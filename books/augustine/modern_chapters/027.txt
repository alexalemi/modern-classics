Book Eleven: Time and Eternity

(Part 2 of 3)

Nor do You come before time by time; otherwise You would not come before all times. You come before all past things by the loftiness of an ever-present eternity, and You go beyond all future things because they are future, and when they come they will be past; but You are the same, and Your years do not run out. Your years neither come nor go, while ours both come and go, so that they may all come. Your years stand together, because they do stand; departing years are not pushed out by arriving ones, since they do not pass away — whereas ours will all exist only when they no longer exist. Your years are a single day; and Your day is not a daily one but a Today, seeing that Your Today does not give way to a tomorrow, since neither does it take the place of a yesterday. Your Today is eternity; and therefore You fathered the co-eternal one, to whom You said, Today I have begotten You. You have made all things; and before all times You are, and there was no time in which there was no time.

So there was no time at which You had not made anything, since You made time itself. And no times are co-eternal with You, because You remain; and if they remained, they would not be times.

For what is time? Who can readily and briefly explain it? Who can even take it in in thought, so as to say a word about it? And yet what do we mention more familiarly and knowingly in conversation than time? And we understand it when we speak of it, and understand it when we hear somebody else speak of it. What is time, then? If no one asks me, I know; if I want to explain it to someone who asks, I do not know. And yet I say boldly that I know this much: that if nothing passed away there would be no past time, and if nothing were coming there would be no future time, and if nothing existed there would be no present time. So how do those two times exist, the past and the future, seeing that the past now is not and the future is not yet? And as for the present, if it were always present and never passed into the past, it would not be time at all but eternity. So if present time only comes into existence, if it is to be time, by passing into the past, how can we say that it exists either — when the cause of its existing is that it shall not exist? So that we cannot truly say that time exists, except because it is tending not to exist.

And yet we say "a long time" and "a short time" — and still, only of past or future time. A long time past we call, for instance, a hundred years ago; and a long time to come, a hundred years hence. And a short time past we call, say, ten days ago; and a short time to come, ten days hence. But in what sense is something long or short that does not exist? For the past is not now, and the future is not yet. So let us not say "it is long", but of the past, "it was long", and of the future, "it will be long."

Lord, my light, will Your truth not laugh at man here too? For that past time which was long: was it long when it was already past, or when it was still present? It could be long when there was something there that could be long; but once past it no longer existed, and so what did not exist at all could not be long either. So let us not say, "past time was long", since we shall not find anything that was long, seeing that once it was past it no longer existed. Let us say instead, "that present time was long" — because when it was present it was long. It had not yet passed away into non-existence, and so there was something there that could be long; but once it was past, what ceased to exist ceased to be long as well.

Let us see, then, soul of man, whether present time can be long; for it is given to you to feel and to measure lengths of time. What will you answer me? Are a hundred years, when present, a long time? Look first at whether a hundred years can be present. For if the first of these years is now running, it is present, but the other ninety-nine are to come and so are not yet; and if the second year is running, one is now past, another present, and the rest to come. And so if we take any year in the middle of the hundred to be present, all the ones before it are past and all the ones after it are to come; so a hundred years cannot be present. But see at least whether the one now running is present. For if the current month is its first, the rest are to come; if the second, the first is already past and the rest are not yet. So the year now running is not present either; and if it is not present as a whole, then the year is not present. For twelve months make a year, of which whichever one is running is present, while the rest are past or to come. Though the current month is not present either, but only one day of it — the rest being to come if it is the first, past if the last, and if any in the middle, then part past and part to come.

See how the present time, which alone we found could be called long, is cut down to the length of barely one day. But let us examine that too, since not even one day is present as a whole. It is made up of twenty-four hours of night and day, of which the first has the rest to come, and the last has them past, and any in the middle has those before it past and those behind it to come. And that one hour passes away in flying particles. Whatever of it has flown away is past; whatever remains is to come. If an instant of time can be conceived that cannot be divided into the smallest particles of moments, that alone is what may be called present. And it flies with such speed from future into past that it cannot be extended by the slightest pause; for if it could, it would be divided into past and future. The present has no extension.

So where is the time we can call long? Is it the future? Of the future we do not say, "it is long", because it does not yet exist so as to be long; we say, "it will be long". So when will it be? If even then, while it is still to come, it will not be long — since what can be long does not yet exist — and if it will therefore be long only when, out of a future that does not yet exist, it begins to exist and has become present, so that there is something there that can be long: then present time cries out, in the words above, that it cannot be long.

And yet, Lord, we do perceive intervals of time and compare them, and say that some are shorter and others longer. We even measure how much longer or shorter this time is than that, and answer, "This is double, or triple"; or, "That is single, or just the same as this." But we measure times as they pass, by perceiving them. As for the past, which no longer exists, or the future, which does not exist yet, who can measure them? Unless a man dares to say that what does not exist can be measured. So when time is passing it can be perceived and measured; but once past it cannot, because it does not exist.

I am asking, Father, not asserting: my God, direct me and guide me. Who will tell me that there are not three times, past, present and future, as we learned as boys and taught boys ourselves — but only the present, because the other two do not exist? Or do they exist too, and when it becomes present out of the future, does it come out of some hidden place, and when it retreats from present into past, does it go into one? For where did the men who foretold things to come see them, if they did not yet exist? What does not exist cannot be seen. And men who relate things past could not relate them if they did not make them out in their minds; and if those things did not exist, they could not be made out at all. So things past and things to come do exist.

Let me look further, Lord. My hope, do not let my purpose be thrown into confusion. If times past and to come exist, I want to know where they are. And even if I cannot manage that, I do know that wherever they are, they are not there as future or as past, but as present. For if they are future there too, they are not there yet; and if they are past there too, they are no longer there. So wherever whatever exists is, it is only as present. Although when past events are related, it is not the things themselves that are drawn out of the memory, which are past, but words conceived from the images of the things, which as they passed left their traces in the mind through the senses. My childhood, which no longer exists, is in past time, which no longer exists; but now, when I recall its image and describe it, I am looking at it in the present, because it is still in my memory. Whether there is a similar cause for foretelling things to come — so that images of things which do not yet exist may be perceived beforehand, already existing — I confess, my God, I do not know. But this I do know: that we generally think ahead about our future actions, and that this forethinking is present, while the action we are thinking ahead about does not yet exist, because it is to come. And when we have set about it and begun to do what we were thinking of, then the action will exist, because then it is no longer future but present.

Whichever way, then, this secret pre-perceiving of things to come works, only what exists can be seen. And what now exists is not future but present. So when things to come are said to be seen, it is not the things themselves that are seen, which do not yet exist — that is, which are to be — but perhaps their causes or their signs, which already exist. So they are not future but present to those who now see the thing from which the future is foreconceived in the mind and foretold. And these foreconceptions exist now; and those who foretell those things are looking at conceptions present in front of them. Let the great variety of things supply me an example. I look at the dawn; I predict that the sun is about to rise. What I am looking at is present; what I am signalling ahead is to come — not the sun, which already exists, but the sunrise, which does not exist yet. And yet if I did not picture the sunrise itself in my mind, as I am doing now while I speak of it, I could not foretell it. But the dawn light I make out in the sky is not the sunrise, though it comes before it; and neither is that picture in my mind. Those two are seen as present now, so that the other, which is to be, may be foretold. So future things do not yet exist; and if they do not yet exist, they do not exist; and if they do not exist, they cannot be seen. But they can be foretold from present things, which already exist and are seen.

You then, ruler of Your creation: by what means do You teach souls things to come? For You taught Your prophets. By what means do You, to whom nothing is to come, teach things to come — or rather, teach present things about the future? For what does not exist cannot be taught. This road is too far beyond my sight; it is too mighty for me, I cannot reach it. But from You I can, when You grant it, sweet light of my hidden eyes.

What is clear and plain now is that neither things to come nor things past exist. Nor is it properly said that there are three times, past, present and to come; but perhaps it might properly be said that there are three times: a present of things past, a present of things present, and a present of things future. For these three do exist in some fashion in the soul, and I do not see them anywhere else: the present of things past is memory; the present of things present is sight; the present of things future is expectation. If we may put it that way, then I see three times, and I admit there are three. Let it also be said that there are three times, past, present and to come, in our incorrect way of speaking. I do not object or argue or find fault, so long as what is said is understood — that neither what is to be exists now, nor what is past. There are very few things we say properly; most things we say improperly, and still what is meant is understood.

I said just now that we measure times as they pass, so as to be able to say that this time is twice as long as that, or that this is just the same as that, and so with any other measurable parts of time. So, as I said, we measure times as they pass. And if anyone asked me how I know, I might answer: I know that we do measure, and that we cannot measure things that do not exist, and that things past and to come do not exist. But how do we measure present time, seeing it has no extension? It is measured while it is passing, and when it has passed it is not measured, since there will be nothing there to measure. But where does it pass from, and by what road, and where to, while it is being measured? Where from, but from the future? By what road, but through the present? Where to, but into the past? So from what does not yet exist, through what has no extension, into what no longer exists. And yet what are we measuring, if not time in some extension? For we do not say single and double and triple and equal, or anything else of the sort that we say about time, except of extensions of time. So in what extension do we measure time as it passes? In the future, which it passes from? But we do not measure what does not yet exist. In the present, which it passes through? But an extension of no size we do not measure. In the past, which it passes into? But we do not measure what no longer exists either.

My soul is on fire to know this most intricate riddle. Do not shut it away, Lord my God, good Father; through Christ I beg You, do not shut these familiar yet hidden things away from my longing, so that it is stopped from getting into them; let them dawn through Your enlightening mercy, Lord. Whom shall I ask about these things? And to whom shall I confess my ignorance more usefully than to You, to whom these studies of mine, so violently kindled towards Your Scriptures, are no trouble? Give me what I love; for I do love, and even that You gave me. Give, Father, who truly know how to give good gifts to Your children. Give, because I have taken it on myself to know, and there is trouble in front of me until You open it up. By Christ I beg You, in his name, holy of holies, let nobody disturb me. For I believed, and therefore I speak. This is my hope; this is what I live for, that I may contemplate the delights of the Lord. You have made my days grow old, and they pass away, and how, I do not know. And we talk about time and time, times and times: "How long since he said this?" "How long since he did that?" "How long since I saw that?" "This syllable takes twice the time of that short one." These are the words we speak and hear, and are understood and understand. They are utterly obvious and ordinary, and the very same things are also far too deeply hidden, and their discovery would be something new.

I once heard a learned man say that time is constituted by the motions of the sun, the moon and the stars, and I did not agree. Why should the motions of all bodies not be times just as well? Or if the lights of heaven stopped and a potter's wheel went round, would there be no time for us to measure those turnings by, and to say either that it moved with equal intervals, or that if it turned sometimes slower and sometimes faster, some turns were longer and others shorter? Or, while we said this, would we not be speaking in time ourselves? Or would there be in our words some short syllables and some long, except because these sounded in a shorter time and those in a longer? God, grant men to see in a small thing the principles common to things great and small. The stars and lights of heaven are also for signs and for seasons and for years and for days; they are. And yet I would not say that the going round of that wooden wheel was a day, nor would he say that it was therefore not time.

I want to know the force and nature of time, by which we measure the motions of bodies and say, for example, that this motion is twice as long as that. For I ask this. "Day" means not only the sun's stay above the earth, according to which day is one thing and night another, but also its whole circuit from east round to east again, according to which we say "so many days have passed", the night being included when we say "so many days", and the nights not counted separately. Seeing, then, that a day is completed by the motion of the sun and by his circuit from east round to east, I ask: does the motion alone make the day, or the length of time in which the motion is completed, or both? If the first, then we should have a day even if the sun finished his course in as small a space of time as an hour. If the second, then it would not make a day if between one sunrise and the next there were only as short a stay as an hour; the sun would have to go round twenty-four times to complete one day. If both, then neither could be called a day: not if the sun ran his whole round in the space of an hour, and not if, while the sun stood still, as much time went by as the sun usually takes to run his whole course from morning to morning.

So I shall not now ask what it is that is called a day, but what time is — the time by which, measuring the circuit of the sun, we should say that it was finished in half the time it usually takes, if it were finished in as small a space as twelve hours; and comparing the two, should call this a single time and that a double one, even supposing the sun to run his round from east to east sometimes in the single and sometimes in the double time. So let nobody tell me that the motions of the heavenly bodies constitute times; because when at one man's prayer the sun stood still until he could finish his victorious battle, the sun stood still and time went on. For that battle was fought and finished in its own allotted space of time. So I perceive time to be a kind of extension. But do I perceive it, or only seem to perceive it? You, light and truth, will show me.
