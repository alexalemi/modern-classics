Book Five: Faustus, Rome, and the Road to Milan

(Part 2 of 2)

But why I left there and went here, You knew, God, and You did not show it either to me or to my mother, who grieved bitterly over my journey and followed me as far as the sea. She held on to me by force, so as either to keep me back or come with me — and I deceived her, pretending I had a friend I could not leave until he had a fair wind to sail. I lied to my mother, and to such a mother, and got away. And You have mercifully forgiven me even for that, preserving me from the waters of the sea, full of loathsome filth as I was, and keeping me for the water of Your grace, by which, when I was washed clean, the streams from my mother's eyes would be dried up — the streams with which she daily watered the ground under her face for me. And since she refused to go home without me, I only just persuaded her to spend that night in a place near our ship, where there was a chapel in memory of the blessed Cyprian. That night I slipped away, and she stayed behind weeping and praying. And what, Lord, was she asking of You with all those tears, except that You would not let me sail? But You, in the depth of Your counsels, hearing the main point of her desire, took no notice of what she was asking at that moment, so as to make me what she was always asking for. The wind blew and filled our sails and drew the shore out of our sight; and in the morning she was there, frantic with grief, filling Your ears with complaints and groans, which You then disregarded — while through my own desires You were hurrying me on to the end of all desire, and the earthly part of her love for me was being chastened by the appointed whip of sorrow. For she loved having me with her, as mothers do, but far more than most; and she did not know what great joy You were about to make for her out of my absence. She did not know, and so she wept and wailed; and in that agony there appeared in her the inheritance of Eve, looking in sorrow for what she had brought forth in sorrow. And yet, after accusing me of treachery and hard-heartedness, she turned again to pleading with You for me, went back to her usual place, and I went to Rome.

And there I was met by the whip of bodily illness, and I was going down to hell carrying all the sins I had committed against You and against myself and against others — many and serious, over and above the bond of original sin by which we all die in Adam. For You had forgiven me none of these things in Christ, and he had not abolished by his cross the enmity I had incurred with You by my sins. How could he, by the crucifixion of a phantom, which is what I believed him to be? The death of my soul was as real as the death of his flesh seemed to me false; and as real as the death of his body was, so unreal was the life of my soul, which did not believe it. And now, as the fever rose, I was going, and going for ever. If I had left this world then, where would I have gone but into the fire and torments my deeds deserved in the truth of Your ordering? And she did not know this, and prayed for me while she was away. But You, present everywhere, heard her where she was, and where I was You had compassion on me: so that I recovered my bodily health, still deranged as I was in my sacrilegious heart. For in all that danger I did not want Your baptism. I had been better as a boy, when I begged for it from my mother's devotion, as I have already recounted and confessed. But I had grown up to my own shame, and I madly laughed at the prescriptions of Your medicine, when You would not let me, such as I was, die a double death. If my mother's heart had been pierced by that wound, it could never have healed. For I cannot describe the love she had for me, and with how much more violent anguish she was now in labour with me in the spirit than she had been in childbirth in the flesh.

I do not see how she could have been healed, if such a death of mine had struck through the depths of her love. And where would those strong, unceasing prayers of hers have been, addressed to You alone without a break? But would You, God of mercies, despise the contrite and humbled heart of that chaste and sober widow — so frequent in almsgiving, so full of duty and service to Your saints, letting no day pass without an offering at Your altar, coming to Your church twice a day, morning and evening, without any interruption, and not for idle gossip and old wives' tales, but so that she might hear You in Your discourses and You might hear her in her prayers? Could You despise the tears of such a woman, and refuse Your help — tears with which she begged You not for gold or silver or any changeable and passing good, but for the salvation of her son's soul, You by whose gift she was what she was? Never, Lord. You were at hand, hearing and acting, in the order You had already determined it should be done. Far be it from You to have deceived her in the visions and answers You gave her, some of which I have mentioned and some of which I have not, which she stored up in her faithful heart and, always praying, pressed on You as Your own handwriting. For since Your mercy endures for ever, You are willing to become a debtor by Your promises to those whose debts You forgive entirely.

So You brought me through that illness and healed the son of Your handmaid, in body for the time being, so that he might live for You to give him a better and more lasting health. And even then, in Rome, I attached myself to those deceiving and deceived "holy ones" — not only to their hearers, of whom the man in whose house I fell ill and recovered was one, but also to those they call "the Elect". For I still thought that it is not we who sin, but some other nature, I do not know what, that sins in us. It pleased my pride to be free of blame; and when I had done something wrong, not to confess I had done anything, so that You might heal my soul because it had sinned against You. I preferred to excuse it and accuse something else, I did not know what, which was with me but was not me. But in truth it was entirely me, and my irreligion had divided me against myself; and that sin was the more incurable in that I did not judge myself a sinner. It was a loathsome wickedness that I would rather have You, almighty God, defeated in me to my destruction, than myself defeated by You to my salvation. You had not yet set a watch before my mouth and a door of safekeeping round my lips, so that my heart should not turn aside to wicked talk, making excuses for sins along with men who do evil; and so I was still attached to their Elect.

But now, despairing of getting anywhere in that false doctrine, I held even the things I had decided to be content with, if I could find nothing better, more loosely and carelessly. For a thought half rose in me that those philosophers they call the Academics were wiser than the rest, because they held that men ought to doubt everything and laid it down that no truth can be grasped by a human being — for at that stage I did not even understand what they meant, and I too was firmly convinced that they thought what they are commonly reported to have thought. And I quite freely and openly discouraged my host from the over-confidence I could see he had in the fables the books of Manichaeus are full of. And yet I lived on more intimate terms with them than with people outside the heresy. I no longer maintained it with my old eagerness; but my closeness to that sect — Rome secretly harbouring a great many of them — made me slower to look for another way, especially since I despaired of finding, in Your church, Lord of heaven and earth, creator of everything visible and invisible, the truth they had turned me away from. It seemed to me utterly unfitting to believe that You had the shape of human flesh and were bounded by the bodily outlines of our limbs. And because, when I tried to think about my God, I could think of nothing but a mass of bodies — since whatever was not that did not seem to me to be anything at all — this was the greatest and almost the only cause of my unavoidable error.

For it made me believe that evil too was some substance of the same kind, with its own foul and hideous bulk: either thick, which they called earth, or thin and subtle like the body of the air, which they imagine to be some malignant mind creeping through that earth. And because a sort of reverence obliged me to believe that the good God never created any evil nature, I imagined two masses, opposed to each other, both without limit, but the evil one narrower and the good one broader. And from this poisonous beginning the rest of the sacrilegious notions followed. For when my mind tried to get back to the Catholic faith, I was driven off, because what I thought was the Catholic faith was not. And it seemed to me more reverent to believe You, my God, to whom Your mercies confess out of my mouth, to be unbounded at least on the other sides, though on the one side where the mass of evil was set against You I was forced to admit You bounded — rather than to imagine You bounded on all sides by the shape of a human body. And it seemed better to believe that You had created no evil, which in my ignorance seemed to me not merely something but a bodily substance, since I could not conceive of mind except as a subtle body spread out in definite spaces, than to believe that the nature of evil, as I conceived it, could come from You. And I believed even our saviour himself, Your Only Begotten, had been stretched out, so to speak, for our salvation from the mass of Your most shining substance — so as to believe nothing about him except what I could imagine in my emptiness. His nature being like that, I thought it could not be born of the Virgin Mary without being mixed with the flesh; and how what I had pictured to myself could be mixed with flesh and not defiled, I could not see. So I was afraid to believe him born in the flesh, in case I should be forced to believe him defiled by the flesh. Your spiritual ones will now smile at me mildly and lovingly, if they read these confessions of mine. But that is what I was.

Furthermore, the criticisms the Manichees made of Your Scriptures I thought could not be answered. And yet at times I really did want to talk these various points over with somebody very well versed in those books, and test what he thought about them. For the words of a man called Helpidius, speaking and arguing face to face against the Manichees, had begun to stir me even at Carthage; because he had produced things out of the Scriptures that were not easily resisted, and the Manichees' answer struck me as weak. That answer they did not like to give in public, but only to us in private: that the Scriptures of the New Testament had been corrupted by somebody or other, who wanted to graft the law of the Jews onto the Christian faith — and yet they never produced any uncorrupted copies themselves. But I, who could conceive only of bodily things, was held down mainly by those "masses", violently oppressed and practically suffocated by them; and panting under them for the breath of Your truth, I could not breathe it pure and untainted.

So I began working hard at the thing I had come to Rome for, teaching rhetoric; and first at gathering a few pupils at my house, through whom and to whom I was beginning to be known. And then I found other offences going on in Rome that I had not been exposed to in Africa. It was true that the "wreckings" by dissolute young men were not practised here, as I had been told. But all of a sudden, people said, a group of students will conspire together to avoid paying their teacher's fee and move to another one — breakers of faith, who hold justice cheap for love of money. My heart hated these people too, though not with a perfect hatred: for I probably hated them more because of what I stood to lose by them than because of the sheer illegality of what they did. Certainly such people are base, and they whore away from You, loving these fleeting mockeries of temporary things, and filthy money, which dirties the hand that grabs it; hugging the fleeting world and despising You, who stay, and call back, and forgive the adulterous human soul when she comes back to You. And now I hate such crooked people, though I love them if they can be put right, so that they might prefer the learning they are acquiring to money, and prefer You to the learning, God, the truth and fullness of guaranteed good, and the purest peace. But in those days I disliked their being bad more for my own sake than I wanted them good for Yours.

So when the people of Milan sent to the prefect of the city at Rome, asking him to supply them with a lecturer in rhetoric for their city and send him at public expense, I applied — through the very people drunk on Manichaean vanities whom I was to be freed from by going, though neither they nor I knew it — for Symmachus, then prefect of the city, to test me by setting me a subject and then send me. So I came to Milan, and to Ambrose the bishop, known throughout the world as one of the best of men, Your devoted servant, whose eloquent preaching then dispensed to Your people in abundance the flour of Your wheat, the gladness of Your oil, and the sober intoxication of Your wine. I was led to him by You without knowing it, so that through him I might knowingly be led to You. That man of God received me like a father and showed me an episcopal kindness at my arrival. From then on I began to love him — at first not as a teacher of truth, which I had utterly given up hope of finding in Your church, but as a man who was kind to me. And I listened carefully to him preaching to the people, not with the intention I should have had, but testing his eloquence, as it were, to see whether it lived up to its reputation or ran fuller or thinner than reported. I hung on his words attentively, and about the substance of them I was a careless and scornful bystander. I was delighted by the sweetness of his delivery, which was more learned than Faustus's but less winning and harmonious in manner. As to the substance there was no comparison, for the one was wandering about among Manichaean delusions and the other was teaching salvation soundly. But salvation is far from sinners, and that is what I was as I stood in front of him — and yet I was drawing nearer, little by little and without noticing.

For although I took no trouble to learn what he was saying but only to hear how he said it — that empty concern being all I had left, having despaired of any road to You open to a human being — nevertheless, along with the words I was choosing to attend to, the substance I meant to reject came into my mind as well, since I could not separate them. And while I opened my heart to take in how eloquently he spoke, there also came in how truly he spoke — but gradually. First these matters began to look defensible to me; and the Catholic faith, for which I had thought nothing could be said against the Manichees' objections, I now thought could be maintained without embarrassment, especially after I had heard one or two passages of the Old Testament explained, and often explained figuratively — passages which, when I took them literally, had killed me spiritually. So when a great many places in those books had been explained, I began to blame my own despair for believing that no answer at all could be given to those who hated and mocked the Law and the Prophets. And yet I did not therefore see that the Catholic way was the one to hold, just because it too could produce learned defenders who could answer objections at length and with some show of reason; nor that what I held was to be condemned just because both sides could be defended. For the Catholic case seemed to me not so much defeated as still not yet victorious.

At this point I bent my mind hard to seeing whether I could by any certain proof convict the Manichees of falsehood. If I could once have conceived of a spiritual substance, all their strongholds would have been beaten down and thrown out of my mind altogether; but I could not. Even so, on the structure of this world and the whole of nature that the senses of the flesh can reach, the more I considered and compared, the more I judged the views of most of the philosophers to be far more probable. So, in the manner of the Academics as they are supposed to be, doubting everything and wavering between everything, I settled at least this far: that the Manichees were to be abandoned. I judged that even while I was in doubt I could not stay in a sect I already ranked below some of the philosophers — to which philosophers, however, since they were without the saving name of Christ, I absolutely refused to trust the cure of my sick soul. So I decided to remain a catechumen in the Catholic Church, which my parents had commended me to, until something certain should dawn on me to steer my course by.
