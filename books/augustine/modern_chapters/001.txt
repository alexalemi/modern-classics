Book One: Infancy, Boyhood, and the Beginnings of Sin

(Part 2 of 2)

As a boy I had already heard about an eternal life promised to us through the humility of the Lord our God stooping down to our pride; and from my mother's womb — she hoped greatly in You — I was marked with the sign of his cross and seasoned with his salt. You saw, Lord, how once when I was still a boy I was seized by a sudden stomach complaint and was close to death. You saw, my God, because You were already my keeper, how eagerly and with what faith I begged for the baptism of Your Christ, my God and Lord, from the loving care of my mother and of Your Church, the mother of us all. And my mother in the flesh was greatly troubled, since with a heart pure in Your faith she was labouring even more lovingly to give birth to my salvation; she would have hurried to see me consecrated and cleansed by the healing sacraments, confessing You, Lord Jesus, for the forgiveness of sins — except that I suddenly recovered. So my cleansing was put off, on the grounds that if I lived I was bound to be polluted again, and that the stains of sin after that washing would carry greater and more dangerous guilt. I already believed by then; so did my mother and the whole household, except my father. But he did not defeat my mother's devotion in me, and win the point that because he did not yet believe, I should not believe either. She took great care that You, my God, should be my father rather than he was; and in this You helped her to prevail over her husband, whom she went on obeying, better woman though she was — obeying You in that too, since You have commanded it.

I beg You, my God, I would very much like to know, if You are willing to tell me: why was my baptism put off then? Was it for my good that the rein was let out, so to speak, and I was free to sin? Or was it not let out? If it was not, why do we still hear on every side, "Leave him alone, let him do what he likes, he is not baptised yet"? Whereas about bodily health nobody says, "Let him be wounded worse, he is not healed yet." How much better it would have been to be healed at once, and then, by my own efforts and my friends', to keep my soul's recovered health safe in Your keeping, since You gave it. Better indeed. But what great waves of temptation seemed to be hanging over me after boyhood! My mother could see them coming, and preferred to expose the raw clay to them, from which I might be moulded afterwards, rather than the finished cast.

In boyhood itself, though — so much less feared for me than adolescence was — I did not love study, and I hated being made to do it. But I was made to do it, and it was good for me, though I did not do well; because I would not have learned unless I had been forced. And nobody does well against his will, even if what he does is good in itself. Nor did the people forcing me do well either; the good came to me from You, my God. They cared nothing for what I would do with what they forced me to learn, except to satisfy the insatiable appetites of a rich poverty and a shameful glory. But You, who have numbered the very hairs of our head, used the error of everybody who pushed me to learn for my good — and used my own error, in refusing to learn, as my punishment, which was a fair penalty for so small a boy and so great a sinner. So by people who did wrong You did right by me; and by my own sin You justly punished me. For You have ordered it, and so it is, that every disordered love is its own punishment.

But why did I so hate Greek, which I studied as a boy? I still do not entirely know. I loved Latin — not what my first teachers taught, but what the so-called grammarians taught. Those first lessons, reading and writing and arithmetic, I found as much of a burden and a penalty as any Greek. And where did that come from, again, except the sin and vanity of this life, because I was flesh, and a breath that passes away and does not come back? Those first lessons were certainly the better ones, because they were the more certain; by them I gained, and still have, the power to read whatever I find written and to write whatever I want. Whereas in the others I was made to learn the wanderings of a man called Aeneas, while forgetting my own wanderings, and to weep for a dead Dido because she killed herself for love — while with dry eyes I put up with my own wretched self, dying among these things, far from You, God my life.

For what is more wretched than a wretch who has no pity on himself, and weeps at Dido's death for love of Aeneas but does not weep at his own death for want of love of You, God? Light of my heart, bread of the inmost part of my soul, power that gives vigour to my mind and quickens my thought: I did not love You. I was unfaithful to You, and all around me, in the same unfaithfulness, the voices echoed, "Well done! Well done!" For friendship with this world is unfaithfulness to You — and the "Well done! Well done!" goes on until a man is ashamed not to be like that. And for none of this did I weep, I who wept for Dido killed, "seeking with the sword the last extremity of her wound", while I myself was seeking a worse extremity: the lowest of Your creatures, having abandoned You, earth going back into earth. And if I was forbidden to read all this, I was miserable that I could not read what made me miserable. Madness like that is reckoned a higher and richer education than the one that taught me to read and write.

But now, my God, cry aloud in my soul, and let Your truth tell me: Not so, not so. That first study was far better. For I would far rather forget the wanderings of Aeneas and all the rest than forget how to read and write. There is a curtain hanging over the door of the grammar school, true enough. But it is less a symbol of anything mysterious than a cover for error. Let the people I no longer fear not shout me down, while I confess to You, my God, whatever my soul wants to confess, and consent to the condemnation of my bad ways in order to love Your good ones. Let neither the buyers nor the sellers of grammar shout me down. If I ask them whether it is true that Aeneas once came to Carthage, as the poet says, the less educated will answer that they do not know and the better educated that he never did. But if I ask what letters the name "Aeneas" is spelled with, everyone who has learned it will answer correctly, according to the signs people have agreed on among themselves. And if I ask which could be forgotten with the least damage to the business of living, reading and writing or these poetic fictions, who cannot see what everybody must answer, unless he has entirely forgotten himself? So I sinned as a boy in preferring the empty studies to the useful ones — or rather in loving the one and hating the other. "One and one is two, two and two is four" was a hateful singsong to me; while "the wooden horse packed with armed men", and "the burning of Troy", and "the sad ghost of Creusa" were the favourite show of my vanity.

Then why did I hate the Greek classics, which tell the same sort of tales? Homer too is a skilful weaver of such fictions, and is most sweetly empty, and yet he was bitter to my boyish taste. And I suppose Virgil would be the same to Greek children, made to learn him as I was made to learn Homer. The truth is that difficulty — the difficulty of a foreign language — poured gall over all the sweetness of Greek storytelling. I did not understand a word of it, and to make me understand I was driven hard with cruel threats and punishments. There was a time when as an infant I knew no Latin either; but I learned that without fear or suffering, simply by paying attention, among the caresses of my nurses and the jokes of the people around me, smiling and playfully egging me on. I learned it with no punishment pressing me forward, because my own heart pressed me to give birth to what was in it — which I could only do by learning words, not from the people who taught but from the people who talked to me, and in whose ears I in turn gave birth to whatever I was thinking. So there is no doubt that free curiosity has more power to make us learn these things than terrified compulsion. Only, compulsion restrains the wanderings of that freedom, by Your laws, my God — Your laws, running from the schoolmaster's cane to the martyr's trials, which are able to mix a wholesome bitterness for us, calling us back to Yourself from the deadly sweetness that lures us away.

Hear my prayer, Lord. Do not let my soul give way under Your discipline, and do not let me give way in confessing to You all the mercies by which You have drawn me out of my worst ways, so that You might become a delight to me above all the attractions I once chased; so that I may love You completely, and take hold of Your hand with all my heart, and You may rescue me from every temptation right to the end. For look, Lord, my king and my God: let whatever useful thing I learned as a child be for Your service — that I speak, write, read, count, let it be for Your service. For You gave me Your discipline while I was learning trivialities, and You have forgiven my sin of enjoying them. In them, it is true, I learned many a useful word — but those can be learned just as well in things that are not trivial, and that is the safe road for a young person's feet.

But woe to you, torrent of human custom! Who can stand against you? How long before you run dry? How long will you go on rolling the sons of Eve into that huge, hideous ocean, which even the ones who climb the cross barely get across? Did I not read in you about Jove the thunderer and adulterer? He could hardly be both — but the invented thunder was there to give cover and encouragement to the real adultery. And now which of our gowned professors will listen soberly to a man from their own school who cries out, "These were Homer's fictions, transferring human things to the gods; if only he had brought divine things down to us"? Though it would have been truer to say: "These are indeed his fictions — but by giving a divine nature to wicked men he made crimes stop being crimes, so that anybody committing them could seem to be imitating not degraded men but the gods in heaven."

And still, you hellish torrent, the sons of men are thrown into you with rich rewards for mastering such learning. A great ceremony is made of it when it is done in the forum, in full view of laws granting a salary on top of the pupils' fees — and you lash your rocks and roar, "This is where words are learned! This is where you get the eloquence you must have to win your case or defend your position!" As if we should never have known words like "golden shower", "lap", "seduce", "the temples of heaven", and the rest of that passage, unless Terence had put a dissolute young man on the stage holding Jupiter up as his model for seduction:

	Looking at a picture where the story was painted
	of Jove coming down in a shower of gold
	into Danaë's lap, to seduce a woman.

And then see how he works himself up to lust as if on heavenly authority:

	And what god? Great Jove,
	who shakes the highest temples of heaven with his thunder,

	and I, a poor mortal man, not do the same?
	I did it, and I did it with all my heart.

Not one word is learned any more easily for all this filth; but by means of the words the filth is committed with less shame. I do not blame the words themselves, which are like choice and precious vessels — I blame the wine of error that drunken teachers pour out to us in them, and if we will not drink it too we are beaten, with no sober judge to appeal to. Yet, my God, in whose presence I can now remember this without being hurt by it, I learned all this willingly, unhappily, with great delight, and for it I was pronounced a boy of promise.

Bear with me, my God, while I say something about my cleverness, which was Your gift, and about the follies I wasted it on. A task was set me, troublesome enough to my soul, with praise or disgrace at stake and a beating to fear: to speak the words of Juno as she raged and grieved that she could not

	turn this Trojan prince away from Italy.

I had heard that Juno never said any such words; but we were made to go wandering after these poetic fictions, and to say in prose roughly what the poet had said in verse. And the boy who was applauded most was the one in whom rage and grief stood out most, dressed in the most fitting language and keeping up the dignity of the character. What is that to me, my true life, my God — that my recitation was applauded above so many others of my own age and class? Is it not all smoke and wind? Was there nothing else to exercise my wits and my tongue on? Your praises, Lord, Your praises might have propped up the still tender shoot of my heart with the support of Your Scriptures, and it would not have trailed away among these empty trifles, a dirtied prey for the birds of the air. For there is more than one way of sacrificing to the rebel angels.

But is it any wonder that I was carried off into trivialities and went away from Your presence, my God, when the models set in front of me were men who, if they made a grammatical slip in describing some action of their own that was not in itself wicked, were criticised and ashamed — but who, when they described their own disordered lives in rich, well-ordered, ornamented language, were praised and gloried in it? You see all this, Lord, and keep quiet; slow to anger, and full of mercy and truth. Will You keep quiet for ever? Even now You are drawing out of this horrible pit the soul that seeks You, that thirsts for Your delights, whose heart says to You, I have sought Your face; Your face, Lord, will I seek. For to have one's love darkened is to be far away from You. It is not with our feet, or by changing place, that people leave You or come back to You. Did that younger son of Yours look for horses or carriages or ships, or fly away on visible wings, or travel by moving his legs, in order to waste in riotous living, in a far country, everything You gave him when he left? You were a loving father when You gave it, and more loving still when he came back with nothing. So it is in lustful, that is to say darkened, love that the real distance from Your face consists.

Look, Lord God — look patiently, as You always do — at how carefully the sons of men keep the agreed rules about letters and syllables handed down to them by those who spoke before, while neglecting the eternal covenant of everlasting salvation handed down from You. So much so that a man teaching or learning the inherited laws of pronunciation will offend people more by dropping his aitch and saying "a uman being", against the laws of grammar, than if he, a human being, hates a human being, against Your law. As if any enemy could do more harm than the hatred with which he is inflamed against him; or could wound the man he persecutes more deeply than he wounds his own soul by his hostility. Certainly no knowledge of letters can be so deeply rooted in us as the record of conscience — that he is doing to another what he would hate to have done to himself. How deep Your ways are, God, the only great one, sitting silent on high and by a tireless law dealing out a punishing blindness to lawless desires. Chasing a reputation for eloquence, a man standing before a human judge, surrounded by a human crowd, denouncing his enemy with the fiercest hatred, will take the greatest care not to murder the word "human being" by a slip of the tongue — and take no care at all not to murder a real human being in the fury of his heart.

This was the world at whose gate I lay, unhappy, in my boyhood; this was the arena where I was more afraid of committing a grammatical error than, having committed one, of envying those who had not. These things I say and confess to You, my God; and for these things I was praised by the people whom I then thought it the whole of virtue to please. For I could not see the pit of foulness I had been thrown into, away from Your eyes. In their sight, what was fouler than I already was, displeasing even people like myself, deceiving my tutor and my teachers and my parents with countless lies, out of love of play and eagerness to watch pointless shows and restlessness to copy them? I stole, too, from my parents' cellar and table, driven by greed, or so that I would have something to give the boys who sold me their games — games they enjoyed just as much as I did. And in these games I often cheated my way to victory, conquered myself all the while by a vain desire to be first. And what could I bear less, and denounce more fiercely when I caught somebody at it, than the very thing I was doing to others? And when I was caught and denounced myself, I would rather pick a fight than give in. Is this the innocence of boyhood? It is not, Lord, it is not; I beg Your mercy, my God. These very same sins, as the years go on, simply move from tutors and teachers to magistrates and kings, from nuts and balls and pet sparrows to gold and estates and slaves — exactly as harsher punishments take the place of the cane. It was the small stature of childhood that You, our king, praised as an emblem of humility, when You said, Of such is the kingdom of heaven.

Yet, Lord, to You, creator and governor of the universe, most excellent and most good, thanks would still be due, our God, even if You had meant me to have boyhood and nothing more. For even then I existed, I was alive, I felt; I had a built-in care for my own well-being, a trace of that mysterious oneness I came from. I guarded the wholeness of my senses by an inward sense; and in these tiny pursuits, and in my thoughts about tiny things, I learned to take delight in truth, I hated being deceived, I had a strong memory, I was given the gift of speech, I was comforted by friendship, I avoided pain and lowness and ignorance. In so small a creature, what was not remarkable, what was not admirable? But all of it is my God's gift; I did not give it to myself. And these things are good, and all of them together are me. So he who made me is good, and he is my good; and I will rejoice before him for every good thing I had as a boy. It was my sin that I looked for pleasures and heights and truths not in him but in his creatures — in myself and others — and so fell headlong into sorrows and confusions and errors. Thanks be to You, my joy and my glory and my confidence, my God; thanks be to You for Your gifts. Only keep them for me. For then You will keep me, and the things You have given me will be enlarged and made perfect, and I myself will be with You, since even my existing is Your gift.
