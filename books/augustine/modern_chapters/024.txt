Book Ten: Memory

(Part 4 of 5)

There is another evil of the day, and I wish the day's own evil were enough for it. By eating and drinking we repair the daily wearing away of our body, until You destroy both the stomach and the food, when You shall kill my emptiness with a wonderful fullness and clothe this corruptible thing with an eternal incorruption. But for now the necessity is sweet to me, and I fight against that sweetness so as not to be taken prisoner; and I carry on a daily war by fasting, often bringing my body into subjection — and my pains are removed by pleasure. For hunger and thirst are a kind of pain: they burn and kill like a fever, unless the medicine of food comes to our aid. And since that is ready to hand through the comfort of Your gifts, with which land and water and air serve our weakness, our calamity gets called gratification.

You have taught me this: that I should set about taking food as medicine. But while I am passing from the discomfort of emptiness to the contentment of being filled, the trap of craving is set for me in that very passage. For the passing is itself a pleasure, and there is no other way to get where we must go. And since health is the reason for eating and drinking, a dangerous pleasure attaches itself as an attendant, and mostly tries to get in front, so that it is for her sake that I do what I say I am doing, or want to do, for the sake of health. And the two do not have the same measure: what is enough for health is too little for pleasure. And it is often uncertain whether it is the body's necessary care still asking for sustenance, or a voluptuous deceitfulness of greed offering its services. The unhappy soul is glad of that uncertainty, and prepares an excuse in it to shield itself, glad that it is not clear what would be enough for a moderate health, so that under the cloak of health it can disguise the business of gratification. These temptations I try to resist daily, and I call on Your right hand and refer my perplexities to You, since I still have no settled policy about them.

I hear the voice of my God commanding: Do not let your hearts be weighed down with overeating and drunkenness. Drunkenness is far from me; may You have mercy, so that it does not come near. But overeating sometimes creeps up on Your servant; may You have mercy, so that it may be far from me. For nobody can be self-controlled unless You give it. Many things You give us when we pray for them; and whatever good we received before we prayed, we received from You — and indeed we received it beforehand so that we might afterwards know this. I was never a drunkard, but I have known drunkards made sober by You. So it was from You that those who were never such should not become such, as it was from You that those who had been such should not always be so; and it was from You that both should know who it was from. I heard another word of Yours: Do not go after your appetites, and turn away from your own pleasure. And by Your kindness I have heard a saying I have greatly loved: neither if we eat shall we have more, nor if we do not eat shall we have less — which is to say that the one will not make me rich, nor the other wretched. And I heard another: I have learned, in whatever state I am, to be content with it; I know how to have plenty and how to go without. I can do all things through Christ who strengthens me. There is a soldier of the heavenly camp, and not the dust that we are. But remember, Lord, that we are dust, and that You made man out of dust; and he was lost and is found. And he could not do this of himself, because the man I loved so much, saying this by the in-breathing of Your inspiration, was made of the same dust. I can do all things, he says, through him who strengthens me. Strengthen me, so that I can. Give what You command, and command what You will. He admits that he has received; and when he boasts, he boasts in the Lord. And I have heard another begging that he might receive: Take from me, he says, the desires of the belly. From which it appears, my holy God, that You give when what You command to be done is done.

You have taught me, good Father, that to the pure all things are pure, but that it is bad for a man who eats and gives offence by eating; and that every creature of Yours is good and nothing is to be refused that is received with thanksgiving; and that food does not commend us to God; and that no man should judge us over food or drink; and that the man who eats should not despise the man who does not, and the man who does not eat should not judge the man who does. These things I have learned; thanks be to You, praise be to You, my God, my master, knocking at my ears and lighting up my heart. Deliver me from every temptation. It is not uncleanness in food I am afraid of but the uncleanness of craving. I know that Noah was allowed to eat every kind of meat that was fit for food; that Elijah was fed on meat; that John, endowed with an admirable abstinence, was not polluted by feeding on living creatures, namely locusts. And I know that Esau was tricked by craving lentils, and that David blamed himself for wanting a drink of water, and that our king was tempted not over meat but over bread. And so the people in the desert also deserved rebuke, not for wanting meat but because in their desire for food they grumbled against the Lord.

Set among these temptations, then, I struggle daily against craving in eating and drinking. For it is not the sort of thing I can settle to cut off once and for all and never touch again, as I could with concubinage. The bridle of the throat has to be held tempered between slackness and stiffness. And who is there, Lord, who is not carried somewhat beyond the limits of necessity? Whoever he is, he is a great man; let him make Your name great. But I am not such a man, for I am a sinful man. And yet I too magnify Your name; and he who has overcome the world makes intercession to You for my sins, counting me among the weak members of his body, because Your eyes have seen what is unfinished in him, and in Your book all shall be written.

The attractions of smell do not concern me much. When they are missing I do not miss them; when they are there I do not refuse them; and I am always ready to be without them. So it seems to me — and perhaps I am deceived. For that too is a mournful darkness, by which my own capacities are hidden from me, so that when my mind asks itself about its own powers it does not readily dare to believe itself, because even what is in it is mostly hidden until experience brings it out. And nobody ought to feel safe in a life the whole of which is called a trial: a man who has been capable of turning from worse to better may just as well be turned from better to worse. Our only hope, our only confidence, our only sure promise is Your mercy.

The delights of the ear had bound and mastered me more firmly, and You loosened me and set me free. Now I do rest a little in the melodies that Your words breathe a soul into, when they are sung with a sweet and well-tuned voice — though not so as to be stuck there, since I can get free whenever I want. But along with the words, which are their life and by which they gain admission to me, the melodies look for a place of some honour in my affections, and I can hardly find them a suitable one. Sometimes I seem to myself to give them more honour than is fitting, when I feel that our minds are raised to a flame of devotion more holily and more fervently by the holy words themselves when they are sung than when they are not; and that the various feelings of our spirit have, by a sweet variety, their own proper measures in the voice and the singing, which stir them up by some hidden correspondence. But this contentment of the flesh, to which the soul must not be handed over to be enfeebled, often deceives me — the senses not waiting on reason patiently enough to follow her, but, having been let in merely for her sake, trying to run ahead and lead her. So in these things I sin without noticing, and notice afterwards.

At other times, over-anxious to avoid this very deception, I go wrong in being too strict — sometimes so far as to wish the whole melody of the sweet music used for David's psalter banished from my ears and from the church's too. And the safer way seems to me the one I have often been told about Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria, who made the reader recite the psalm with so slight an inflection of the voice that it was nearer speaking than singing. And yet again, when I remember the tears I shed at the psalm-singing of Your church in the first days of my recovered faith, and how at this time I am moved not by the singing but by the things sung, when they are sung with a clear voice and the most suitable setting, then I acknowledge the great usefulness of this practice. So I waver between the danger of pleasure and the proved wholesomeness, and I am inclined — though not pronouncing an irrevocable opinion — to approve the custom of singing in church, so that by the delight of the ears weaker minds may rise to a feeling of devotion. Yet when it happens that I am more moved by the voice than by the words being sung, I confess that I have sinned and deserve punishment, and then I would rather not hear the music. Look at the state I am in: weep with me, and weep for me, all of you who regulate your own feelings inwardly so that good action follows. Those of you who do not act, these things do not touch you. But You, Lord my God, listen; look, and see, and have mercy and heal me, You in whose presence I have become a problem to myself; and that is my weakness.

There remains the pleasure of these eyes of my flesh, to make my confessions about in the hearing of the ears of Your temple, those brotherly and devout ears; and so to conclude the temptations of the appetite of the flesh, which still attack me while I groan and long to be clothed with my house from heaven. The eyes love beautiful and varied shapes, and bright and soft colours. Let these not occupy my soul; let God occupy it instead, who made these things — very good indeed, but he is my good, not they. And they affect me while I am awake all day long, and I get no rest from them, as I do from music, and sometimes in silence from all voices. For this queen of colours, the light, bathing everything we look at, wherever I am during the day, gliding past me in all its various forms, soothes me even when I am busy with other things and not noticing it. And it winds itself in so strongly that if it is suddenly taken away it is looked for with longing, and if it stays away long it saddens the mind.

Light, which Tobit saw when, his own eyes closed, he taught his son the way of life, and went before him with the feet of charity, never straying! Light, which Isaac saw when his fleshly eyes were heavy and closed with age, and it was granted him to bless his sons without knowing which was which, and by blessing them to know them. Light, which Jacob saw when he too, blind with great age, with an illumined heart shed light in the persons of his sons on the different tribes of a future people, foreshadowed in them; and laid his hands, crossed in a mystery, on his grandsons by Joseph, not as their father corrected him by his outward eye, but as he himself inwardly discerned. This is the light; it is one, and all are one who see and love it. But that bodily light I was speaking of seasons the life of this world for its blind lovers with an enticing and dangerous sweetness. But those who know how to praise You for it, "Lord, creator of everything", take it up into Your hymns and are not taken up by it into their sleep. That is what I want to be. These seductions of the eyes I resist, so that my feet, which I walk Your road on, may not be trapped. And I lift my invisible eyes to You, so that You may pull my feet out of the snare. You pull them out again and again, since they get caught. You never stop pulling them out, while I keep tangling myself in the traps laid on every side; because You who keep Israel will neither slumber nor sleep.

What countless trifles men have added, made by all sorts of arts and manufactures, in our clothes and shoes and utensils and works of every kind, and in pictures and images too, far beyond any necessary and moderate use and any devout meaning — added just to tempt their own eyes! Outwardly following what they make themselves, and inwardly abandoning him by whom they were made themselves, and destroying what they were made to be. But I, my God and my glory, sing a hymn to You for this too, and dedicate praise to him who dedicates me; because those beautiful patterns which pass through men's souls into their skilful hands come from that beauty which is above our souls, which my soul sighs after day and night. But the makers and followers of the outward beauties take from there their rule for judging beauty, and not their rule for using it. And he is there, though they do not notice him, so that they might not wander off but keep their strength for You, and not scatter it abroad on pleasurable exhaustion. And I, though I say this and see it, get my own steps tangled in these outward beauties; and You pull me out, Lord, You pull me out, because Your loving-kindness is before my eyes. For I am wretchedly caught, and You mercifully pull me out — sometimes without my noticing, when I had only lightly touched them; at other times painfully, because I had stuck fast.

To this is added another form of temptation, dangerous in more ways. Besides the craving of the flesh, which consists in the delight of all the senses and pleasures, and in which its slaves waste away and perish far from You, the soul has, through the same bodily senses, a certain empty and prying desire, dressed up under the title of knowledge and learning: not of taking delight in the flesh, but of making experiments through the flesh. Since its seat is the appetite for knowledge, and sight is the sense chiefly used to gain knowledge, it is called in the language of Scripture the lust of the eyes. For seeing belongs properly to the eyes; and yet we use the word of the other senses too when we set them to look for knowledge. We do not say, listen how it flashes, or smell how it glows, or taste how it shines, or feel how it gleams; all of these are said to be seen. And yet we say not only, see how it shines, which the eyes alone can perceive, but also, see how it sounds, see how it smells, see how it tastes, see how hard it is. And so the general experience of the senses is called, as I say, the lust of the eyes, because the function of seeing, in which the eyes have the first claim, is taken over by the other senses on the analogy of sight when they go looking for any knowledge.

But from this it can be seen more plainly where the object of the senses is pleasure and where it is curiosity. Pleasure looks for objects that are beautiful, tuneful, fragrant, tasty, soft; curiosity looks for the opposite as well, for the sake of the trial — not for the sake of suffering the annoyance, but out of the appetite for trying and knowing. For what pleasure is there in seeing something in a mangled corpse that makes you shudder? And yet if one is lying nearby, they crowd round to be made sad and to turn pale. They are afraid to see it even in their sleep — as if anyone had forced them to see it while they were awake, or any report of its beauty had drawn them there! It is the same with the other senses, which it would take a long time to go through. From this disease of curiosity come all the strange sights put on in the theatre. From it men go on to search out the hidden powers of nature, which is beside our purpose, which it does no good to know, and which men want for nothing but the knowing. From it too, if magical arts are enquired into with the same aim of perverted knowledge. From it too, in religion itself, God is put to the test, when signs and wonders are demanded of him not for any good purpose but simply to try him out.

In this vast wilderness full of traps and dangers, I have cut off and thrown out of my heart a great many of them, as You have given me to do, God of my salvation. And yet when do I dare to say — since so many things of this kind buzz round our daily life on every side — when do I dare to say that none of this catches my attention or arouses an idle interest in me? It is true that the theatres do not carry me away now, and I do not care to know the courses of the stars, and my soul never consulted the ghosts of the dead; I detest all sacrilegious mysteries. And yet, Lord my God, to whom I owe humble and single-hearted service, by what tricks and suggestions does the enemy work on me to want some sign! But I beg You, by our king and by our pure and holy country Jerusalem, that as any consenting to it is far from me, so may it be further and further off still. But when I pray to You for somebody's salvation, my aim and intention are quite different. You give me, and will go on giving me, the will to follow You willingly, doing what You want.

Even so, in how many petty and contemptible things is our curiosity tempted daily, and how often do we give way? Who can count them? How often do we start out as though we were merely putting up with people telling us pointless stories, so as not to offend the weak, and then gradually take an interest in them? I no longer go to the circus to see a dog chasing a hare; but out in the country, if I pass one, that chase may distract me even from some weighty thought and pull me after it — not that I turn my horse aside, but I still turn my mind that way. And unless You, having made me see my own weakness, quickly warned me either to rise towards You by some thought arising from the sight itself, or else to despise it altogether and pass on, I stand there dully fixed on it. And what about when I am sitting at home and a lizard catching flies, or a spider tangling them as they blunder into her nets, takes my attention? Is it a different thing because they are only small creatures? I go on from them to praise You, the wonderful creator and orderer of everything — but that is not what draws my attention first. It is one thing to get up quickly and another not to fall. And my life is full of such things; and my one hope is Your wonderful great mercy. For when our heart becomes the container of such things and is overloaded with crowds of this abundant emptiness, then our prayers are often interrupted and distracted by it too; and while in Your presence we direct the voice of our heart to Your ears, this business of the greatest importance is broken off by the rushing in of I do not know what idle thoughts. Are we to count this too among the things of slight importance? Or can anything bring us back to hope except Your complete mercy, since You have begun to change us?
