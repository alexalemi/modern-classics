Book Six: Ambrose, Alypius, and the Struggle to Believe

(Part 3 of 3)

Nebridius was there too. He had left his home near Carthage, and Carthage itself where he had lived a good deal, and left his excellent family estate and house and a mother who was not going to follow him, and had come to Milan for no other reason than to live with me in a burning search for truth and wisdom. Like me he sighed, like me he wavered: a passionate searcher after the true life and a most acute examiner of the hardest questions. So there were the mouths of three beggars, sighing out their needs to one another and waiting on You to give them their food at the proper time. And in all the bitterness that by Your mercy followed our worldly business, when we looked towards the end — why we should be suffering all this — darkness met us, and we turned away groaning and saying, How long is this going to go on? We said it often, and saying it we did not give the things up, because as yet nothing certain had dawned that we might take hold of once we had let them go.

And as I went over things again and again, what most astonished me was the length of time since that nineteenth year of mine, when I first began to burn with the desire for wisdom, and settled that when I found her I would abandon all the empty hopes and lying frenzies of pointless desires. And here I was, in my thirtieth year, stuck in the same mud, greedy to enjoy present things that slipped away and wasted my soul, while I said to myself: "Tomorrow I shall find it. It will show itself plainly and I shall take hold of it. Look, Faustus the Manichee will come and clear everything up. You great men, you Academics — is it true then that no certainty can be reached to order one's life by? No; let us search all the harder and not despair. Look, the things in the church's books are not absurd to us now, which once seemed absurd, and can be taken another way, in a good sense. I shall take my stand where my parents put me as a child, until the plain truth is found. But where is it to be looked for, and when? Ambrose has no free time; we have no free time to read. Where shall we even find the books? Where or when are we to get them? Who can we borrow them from? Let fixed times be set aside and hours arranged for the health of our soul. Great hope has dawned: the Catholic faith does not teach what we thought, and what we pointlessly accused it of; its instructed members hold it profane to believe God is bounded by the shape of a human body. And do we hesitate to knock, so that the rest may be opened to us? Our pupils take up the mornings; what do we do with the rest of the day? Why not this? But then when do we pay our respects to our powerful friends, whose favour we need? When do we compose what we can sell to students? When do we rest, and let our minds off this intensity of care?

"Let everything go to ruin. Let us dismiss these empty vanities and give ourselves to the one search for truth. Life is empty, death is uncertain; if it steals up on us suddenly, in what condition shall we leave here? Where shall we learn what we have neglected to learn here? Shall we not rather suffer the punishment for this neglect? What if death itself cuts off and ends all care and feeling? Then that must be settled. But God forbid it should be so! It is not empty and pointless that the outstanding dignity and authority of the Christian faith has spread over the whole world. God would never have done such great things for us if the life of the soul came to an end with the death of the body. So why put off abandoning worldly hopes and giving ourselves entirely to the search for God and the blessed life? But wait — those things are pleasant too; they have a real and not a small sweetness. We must not give them up lightly, for it would be a disgrace to go back to them again. Look, it is no great matter now to obtain some post — and then what more should we want? We have plenty of powerful friends; if nothing else turns up and we are in a hurry, at least a governorship could be given us. And a wife with some money of her own, so that she does not add to our expenses. And that would be the limit of our wanting. Many great men, very much worth imitating, have devoted themselves to the study of wisdom while married."

While I went over all this, and these winds shifted and drove my heart this way and that, time went on; and I put off turning to the Lord, and from day to day I put off living in You, and did not put off dying daily in myself. Loving a happy life, I was afraid of it in its own home, and looked for it by running away from it. I thought I would be too miserable unless I was folded in a woman's arms; and it did not occur to me that the medicine of Your mercy could cure that weakness, since I had not tried it. As for self-control, I supposed it was in our own power, though I could not find that power in myself, being foolish enough not to know what is written: nobody can be self-controlled unless You give it — and that You would give it, if I knocked at Your ears with inward groaning and threw my care on You with a settled faith.

Alypius did keep me from marrying, arguing that if I married we could by no means live together in the love of wisdom with undistracted leisure, as we had long wanted to. In this matter he was at that time remarkably pure — the more remarkably since at the very start of his adolescence he had gone that way and had not got stuck in it, but had felt remorse and revulsion at it, and had lived with complete self-control ever since. But I opposed him with the examples of men who had cherished wisdom as married men, and served God acceptably, and kept their friends and loved them faithfully. I fell a long way short of their greatness of spirit; tied down by the disease of the flesh and its deadly sweetness, I dragged my chain along, dreading to be unfastened, and pushing his good advice away as though it were the hand of someone trying to unchain me, and as though my wound had been rubbed by it. What is more, the serpent spoke to Alypius himself through me, weaving pleasant traps with my tongue and laying them across his path, where his virtuous and free feet might be caught.

For he was astonished that I, whom he thought well of, should be stuck so fast in the birdlime of that pleasure as to insist, whenever we discussed it, that I could never live a single life. And when I saw him astonished, I said in my own defence that there was a great difference between his momentary and barely remembered experience of that life, which he could therefore easily despise, and my continued familiarity with it — and that if the honourable name of marriage were added to mine, he ought not to be surprised that I could not despise it. And then he too began to want to be married: not overcome by any desire for the pleasure, but out of curiosity. He said he would like to know what this thing was, without which my life, which he found so agreeable, would to me not be life at all but a punishment. For his mind, free of that chain, was amazed at my slavery, and through the amazement was going on to a wish to try it, and from there to the trial itself, and from there perhaps to sink into the very bondage he was amazed at — since he was willing to make a covenant with death, and the man who loves danger will fall into it. For whatever honour there may be in the work of ordering a marriage and a family well, it moved neither of us much. What tormented me for the most part was the habit of satisfying an appetite that could not be satisfied, which held me prisoner; what was leading him captive was an admiring curiosity. That was where we were, until You, most high, not abandoning our dust, and pitying us in our misery, came to our help by wonderful and secret ways.

Continual efforts were made to get me married. I proposed, I was accepted, chiefly through my mother's efforts, on the grounds that once I was married the health-giving baptism might wash me clean — and she was glad to see me being made fitter for it daily, and noticed that her prayers and Your promises were being fulfilled in my faith. At that time, both at my request and out of her own longing, she begged You daily with strong cries of the heart to show her something about my future marriage in a vision. You never would. She did see certain empty and fantastic things, of the kind the energy of a human mind busy with a subject throws together; and she told me about them, not with the confidence she usually had when You showed her something, but dismissively. She said she could tell the difference between Your revelations and her own soul's dreams by a certain feeling she could not put into words. Still, the matter was pushed on, and a girl was asked for in marriage, two years under the proper age; and since she was pleasing, we waited for her.

And a number of us friends, discussing the matter together and loathing the turbulent turmoil of human life, had debated and almost decided on living apart from business and the bustle of people. We were to manage it like this: we would bring in whatever each of us could get, and make one household of everything, so that through the honesty of our friendship nothing would belong to anyone in particular, but the whole thing, drawn from all of us, should belong as a whole to each, and everything to everybody. We thought there might be about ten people in this society, some of them very rich, especially Romanianus, a man from my own town and a very close friend of mine since childhood, whom the difficult tangles of his affairs had brought up to court. He was the keenest on this project, and his voice carried great weight in it, because his ample estate far exceeded anyone else's. We had also settled that two of us should serve as annual officers and provide everything necessary, leaving the rest undisturbed. But when we began to consider whether the wives that some of us already had, and others hoped to have, would allow this, the whole plan we had been shaping so well fell to pieces in our hands, and was smashed and thrown aside. From there we went back to sighs and groans, and our feet went back to following the broad and beaten roads of the world; for there were many thoughts in our heart, but Your counsel stands for ever. Out of that counsel You laughed at ours and prepared Your own, meaning to give us food at the proper time and to fill our souls with blessing.

Meanwhile my sins were multiplying; and when the woman I lived with was torn from my side as an obstacle to my marriage, my heart, which had held fast to her, was torn and wounded and bleeding. And she went back to Africa, vowing to You never to know another man, leaving with me the son I had by her. But I in my unhappiness could not imitate even a woman. I could not bear the delay, since it would be two years before I could have the girl I had asked for; and being not so much a lover of marriage as a slave of lust, I got myself another woman, though not a wife, so that by the servitude of a lasting habit the disease of my soul might be kept going at full strength, or even increased, into the domain of marriage. And that wound of mine was not healed which had been made by cutting the first woman away; after the inflammation and the sharpest pain it went dead, and my pain grew less sharp and more hopeless.

Praise to You, glory to You, fountain of mercies. I was growing more wretched and You were coming nearer. Your right hand was always ready to pull me out of the mud and wash me thoroughly, and I did not know it. Nothing called me back from a still deeper pit of bodily pleasures except the fear of death and of Your judgement to come, which through all my changes never left my chest. And when I argued with my friends Alypius and Nebridius about the nature of good and evil, I held that in my view Epicurus would have carried off the prize, if I had not believed that after death there remained a life for the soul and places of repayment according to what men deserve — which Epicurus refused to believe. And I asked: if we were immortal and going to live in perpetual bodily pleasure with no fear of losing it, why should we not be happy, and what else should we be looking for? I did not know that great misery was involved in this very thing: that being so sunk and blinded, I could not make out that light of excellence and beauty which is to be embraced for its own sake, which the eye of flesh cannot see and which is seen by the inner man. Nor did I, unhappy as I was, consider where it came from that even about these things, foul as they were, I talked with pleasure to my friends; and that I could not be happy without friends, even on the notions of happiness I had then, however great the supply of bodily pleasures. And these friends I loved for their own sake alone, and I felt that they loved me back for mine alone.

What twisted roads! Pity the reckless soul that hoped, by leaving You, to get something better. It turned this way and that, on its back and its sides and its front, and everything hurt; and You alone are rest. And look, You are close at hand, and free us from our miserable wanderings, and set us on Your road, and comfort us and say: "Run; I will carry you; I will bring you through; and there too I will carry you."
