Book Nine: Baptism, Ostia, and the Death of Monica

(Part 3 of 3)

We were saying this: suppose that for someone the tumult of the flesh fell silent; silent the images of earth and water and air; silent the poles of the sky; silent the soul to herself, going beyond herself by not thinking about herself; silent all dreams and imagined revelations, every tongue and every sign and everything that exists only by passing away — since all of them say, if anyone can hear, We did not make ourselves; he made us who remains for ever. Suppose that having said this they too fell silent, having only turned our ears towards the one who made them; and he alone spoke, not through them but by himself, so that we might hear his word, not through any tongue of flesh, nor an angel's voice, nor a clap of thunder, nor in the dark riddle of a likeness, but might hear the one we love in these things, might hear his very self without them — as the two of us were now straining, and in a flash of thought touched that eternal wisdom which remains above everything. Suppose this could be prolonged, and all other visions of a wholly different kind withdrawn, and this one alone should seize and absorb and wrap up the man who saw it in those inward joys, so that life could be for ever like that one moment of understanding we had just been sighing after. Would this not be, Enter into your master's joy? And when will that be? When we shall all rise again, though we shall not all be changed.

That is what I was saying — and even if not in exactly this way and in these same words, still, Lord, You know that on the day when we were talking about these things, and this world with all its delights became worthless to us as we spoke, my mother said: "Son, for my part I take no further pleasure in anything in this life. What I am still doing here, and why I am here, I do not know, now that my hopes in this world have been fulfilled. There was one thing I wanted to stay alive a while for, and that was to see you a Catholic Christian before I died. My God has done this for me more than abundantly, since I now see you despising earthly happiness as well, and made his servant. What am I doing here?"

What answer I gave her to this I do not remember. For scarcely five days later, or not much more, she went down with a fever; and one day in that illness she fainted and was withdrawn for a while from the things around her. We ran to her; but she was soon brought back to her senses, and looking at me and my brother standing by her, she asked us, "Where was I?" And then, looking hard at us, dazed with grief: "Here," she said, "you will bury your mother." I kept quiet and held back my tears. But my brother said something, wishing for her, as the happier lot, that she might die not in a foreign place but in her own country. At that she looked anxious and checked him with her eyes, for still having a taste for such things; and then she looked at me and said, "See what he says." And soon after, to both of us: "Lay this body anywhere; do not let any concern about that trouble you. This one thing I ask: that you remember me at the altar of the Lord, wherever you may be." And having got this thought out in whatever words she could, she fell silent, being wearied by her worsening illness.

But I, considering Your gifts, unseen God, which You pour into the hearts of Your faithful, out of which wonderful fruits grow, rejoiced and gave thanks to You, remembering what I had known before: how careful and anxious she had always been about her burial place, which she had provided and prepared for herself beside her husband's body. Because they had lived in great harmony together, she had also wanted — so little can the human mind take in things divine — to have this addition to that happiness, and to have it remembered among men, that after her pilgrimage across the seas the earthly part of this united pair had been allowed to be united under the same earth. But when this empty wish had begun to fade from her heart through the fullness of Your goodness I did not know; and I was delighted and full of wonder that she had said what she said — though indeed in that conversation of ours at the window, when she said, "What am I still doing here?", there had been no sign of any wish to die in her own country. I heard afterwards, too, that while we were at Ostia she talked one day, with a mother's confidence, when I was not there, with some of my friends about contempt for this life and the blessing of death. And when they were amazed at the courage You had given a woman, and asked whether she was not afraid to leave her body so far from her own city, she answered, "Nothing is far from God; and there is no danger that at the end of the world he will not know where to raise me up from." So on the ninth day of her illness, in the fifty-sixth year of her life and the thirty-third of mine, that religious and holy soul was freed from the body.

I closed her eyes; and an enormous grief flowed into my heart and was overflowing into tears, while at the same time my eyes, under the violent command of my mind, drank their own spring completely dry. And it went badly with me in that struggle. But when she breathed her last, the boy Adeodatus burst out into a loud lament; and being checked by all of us, he fell silent. In the same way a childish feeling in me, which was finding its outlet in weeping through my heart's youthful voice, was checked and silenced. For we did not think it right to mark that funeral with tearful lamentation and groaning, since that is mostly how people express grief for the dead as though they were unhappy or altogether finished — whereas she was neither unhappy in her death nor altogether finished. Of that we were assured on good grounds: the evidence of her good life and her unpretended faith.

What was it, then, that hurt me so badly inside, if not a fresh wound made by the sudden tearing away of that sweetest and dearest habit of living together? I did take joy in her testimony, when, in that last illness of hers, mixing her endearments with what I did for her, she called me dutiful, and mentioned with great affection that she had never heard a harsh or reproachful sound come out of my mouth against her. And yet, my God, who made us, what comparison is there between the honour I paid her and her slavery for me? So, deprived of so great a comfort in her, my soul was wounded, and that life was torn apart, which had been made one out of hers and mine together.

Once the boy had been quieted from his weeping, Euodius took up the psalter and began to sing, and our whole household answered him, the psalm: I will sing of mercy and judgement to You, Lord. And hearing what we were doing, many brothers and religious women gathered; and while those whose office it was made the arrangements for the burial in the usual way, I, in another part of the house where it was fitting, talked about something suited to the occasion with those who thought it right not to leave me. And with this balm of truth I eased the torment that You knew about and they did not, as they listened intently and supposed I had no feeling of grief at all. But in Your hearing, where none of them could hear, I blamed the weakness of my feelings and held back the flood of my grief; and it gave way to me a little, and then came back like a tide — though not so as to burst out in tears or change my expression. And I knew what I was holding down in my heart. And being very displeased that these human things had such power over me, things which must necessarily happen in the due order and appointment of our natural condition, I grieved over my grief with a fresh grief, and so was worn down by a double sorrow.

And so the body was carried to the burial; we went and came back without tears. Not even in the prayers we poured out to You, when the sacrifice of our ransom was offered for her, with the body already by the graveside before being laid in it, as the custom is there — not even during those prayers did I weep. Yet all that day I was heavily sad in secret, and with a troubled mind I asked You as best I could to heal my grief. And You did not, impressing on my memory, I believe, by this one instance, how strong the bond of every habit is, even on a soul that no longer feeds on a deceiving word. It also seemed a good idea to go and take a bath, having heard that the bath is called balneum from the Greek balaneion because it drives sadness out of the mind. And this too I confess to Your mercy, Father of the fatherless: that I bathed, and was the same afterwards as before. The bitterness of my grief could not be sweated out of my heart. Then I slept, and woke again, and found my grief had softened considerably. And as I was alone in my bed, I remembered those true verses of Your Ambrose. For You are the

	Maker of all things, Lord
	  and ruler of the height,
	who, dressing the day in light, have poured
	  soft sleep across the night,
	so that our tired limbs
	  may find their strength renewed,
	and hearts that sink and falter rise,
	  and sorrows be subdued.

And then little by little I got back my old way of thinking about Your handmaid: her holy way of life towards You, her holy tenderness and attentiveness towards us, which I had suddenly been deprived of. And I wanted to weep in Your sight, for her and for myself, on her behalf and on my own. And I let the tears I had been holding back overflow as much as they wanted, spreading my heart out on them; and it found rest in them, because it was in Your hearing and not in the hearing of some man who would have put a scornful interpretation on my weeping. And now, Lord, I confess it to You in writing. Let anyone read it who will and interpret it how he will; and if he finds it a sin that I wept for my mother for a small part of an hour — the mother who was dead for the time being to my eyes, who for so many years had wept for me so that I might live in Yours — let him not laugh at me, but rather, if he is a man of large charity, let him weep himself for my sins to You, the Father of all the brothers of Your Christ.

But now, with my heart cured of that wound, in which it might have seemed blameworthy for an earthly feeling, I pour out to You, our God, on behalf of that handmaid of Yours, a quite different kind of tears, flowing from a spirit shaken by thoughts of the dangers facing every soul that dies in Adam. And although she had been made alive in Christ even before her release from the flesh, and had lived to the praise of Your name in her faith and her way of life, still I do not dare to say that from the time You gave her new birth in baptism no word ever came out of her mouth against Your commandment. Your Son, the truth, has said: whoever says to his brother, You fool, shall be in danger of hell fire. And it will go badly even with the most praiseworthy human life, if You set mercy aside and examine it. But because You are not severe in enquiring after sins, we confidently hope to find some place with You. And whoever counts up his real merits to You, what is he counting up but Your own gifts? If only men would know themselves to be men, and whoever boasts would boast in the Lord.

So I, my praise and my life, God of my heart, setting aside for a moment her good deeds, for which I thank You with joy, now beg You for my mother's sins. Hear me, I entreat You, by the medicine of our wounds, who hung on the tree and now sits at Your right hand and pleads with You for us. I know that she acted mercifully, and forgave her debtors their debts from her heart. Forgive her debts too, whatever she may have run up in all those years since the water of salvation. Forgive her, Lord, forgive, I beg You; do not enter into judgement with her. Let Your mercy be lifted above Your justice, since Your words are true and You have promised mercy to the merciful — and You made them merciful in the first place, who will have mercy on whom You will have mercy, and will have compassion on whom You have had compassion.

And I believe You have already done what I ask; but accept, Lord, the freewill offerings of my mouth. For when the day of her dissolution was close, she gave no thought to having her body sumptuously wrapped or embalmed with spices, and did not want a choice monument or to be buried in her own country. These things she did not lay on us. She only wanted her name remembered at Your altar, which she had served without missing a single day, and from which she knew the holy sacrifice was distributed by which the record of debt against us is wiped out; through which the enemy was defeated, who, adding up our offences and looking for something to charge us with, found nothing in the one in whom we conquer. Who shall give him back the innocent blood? Who shall repay him the price he bought us with, and so take us away from him? To the sacrament of that ransom Your handmaid bound her soul by the bond of faith. Let nobody separate her from Your protection; let neither the lion nor the dragon get between by force or by trickery. For she will not answer that she owes nothing, in case she is convicted and seized by the crafty accuser; she will answer that her sins have been forgiven her by the one to whom nobody can repay the price that he, who owed nothing, paid for us.

So may she rest in peace with the husband before and after whom she never had another; whom she obeyed, patiently bringing forth fruit for You, so that she might win him too for You. And inspire, Lord my God, inspire Your servants my brothers, Your sons my masters, whom I serve with my voice and heart and pen: inspire all of them who read these confessions to remember at Your altar Monica Your handmaid, along with Patricius, once her husband, through whose bodies You brought me into this life, though how I do not know. Let them with devout affection remember my parents in this passing light; and my brothers under You our Father in our Catholic mother; and my fellow citizens in that eternal Jerusalem which Your pilgrim people sigh after from their setting out until their return. So that my mother's last request of me may be fulfilled for her more abundantly through the prayers of many, brought by these confessions of mine, than it could be through my prayers alone.
