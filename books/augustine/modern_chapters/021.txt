Book Ten: Memory

(Part 1 of 5)

Let me know You, Lord, who know me; let me know You as I am known. Power of my soul, come into it and make it fit for Yourself, so that You may have and hold it without spot or wrinkle. This is my hope, and that is why I speak; and in this hope I rejoice, whenever my rejoicing is healthy. Other things in this life deserve less grief the more they are grieved over, and deserve more grief the less people grieve over them. For You love the truth, and the man who does the truth comes to the light. This is what I want to do in my heart in front of You, in confession — and in my writing, in front of many witnesses.

And from You, Lord, to whose eyes the depths of a man's conscience are naked, what could be hidden in me even if I refused to confess it? I would be hiding You from me, not me from You. But now, since my groaning is evidence that I am displeased with myself, You shine out and are pleasing and loved and longed for; so that I may be ashamed of myself and give myself up and choose You, and please neither You nor myself except in You. To You, then, Lord, I am open, whatever I am; and with what benefit I confess to You I have already said. And I do not do it with words and sounds of the flesh, but with the words of my soul and the cry of the thought that Your ear knows. For when I am bad, confessing to You is nothing but being displeased with myself; and when I am good, it is nothing but not taking the credit for it — because You, Lord, bless the godly man, but first You make him righteous while he is ungodly. So my confession, my God, in Your sight, is made silently and not silently: silently as far as sound goes, and crying out as far as feeling goes. For I say nothing right to men that You have not heard from me first; and You hear nothing of the kind from me that You have not first said to me.

So what have I to do with men, that they should hear my confessions — as if they could heal all my weaknesses? A race curious to know other people's lives and lazy about mending their own. Why do they want to hear from me what I am, when they will not hear from You what they are themselves? And how do they know, when they hear about me from me, whether I am telling the truth, seeing that no man knows what is in a man except the spirit of the man which is in him? But if they hear about themselves from You, they cannot say, "The Lord is lying." For what is hearing about themselves from You but knowing themselves? And who knows himself and says, "That is false," unless he is lying himself? But because love believes all things — at least among those it knits to itself and makes one — I too, Lord, will confess to You in such a way that men may hear, to whom I cannot prove that I am confessing truly. And yet they believe me, those whose ears love opens to me.

But You, my inmost physician, make plain to me what good it does. The confessions of my past sins, which You have forgiven and covered, so that You might bless me in Yourself, changing my soul by faith and by Your sacrament — those confessions, when they are read and heard, stir up the heart, so that it does not sleep in despair and say, "I cannot," but wakes up in the love of Your mercy and the sweetness of Your grace, by which anyone who is weak is strong, once through it he has become aware of his own weakness. And good people are glad to hear of the past evils of those who are now free of them: not because they are evils, but because they have been and are not. But with what benefit, Lord my God, to whom my conscience confesses daily, trusting more in the hope of Your mercy than in her own innocence — with what benefit, I ask, do I in this book confess to men as well, in Your presence, what I now am and not what I have been? That other benefit I have seen and spoken of. But what I now am, at the very moment of making these confessions, a good many people want to know, who have known me and who have not, who have heard from me or about me; and their ear is not at my heart, where I am, whatever I am. So they want to hear me confess what I am inside, where neither their eye nor their ear nor their understanding can reach; they want it, and are ready to believe — but will they know? For love, which makes them good, tells them that I do not lie in my confessions; and it is love in them that believes me.

But what benefit would they get from hearing it? Do they want to be glad along with me, when they hear how near I come to You by Your gift, and to pray for me when they hear how far I am held back by my own weight? To such people I shall reveal myself. For it is no small benefit, Lord my God, that thanks should be given to You by many on our behalf, and that You should be asked by many for us. Let a brotherly mind love in me what You teach us is to be loved, and lament in me what You teach us is to be lamented. Let it be a brotherly mind and not a stranger's — not the mind of those foreign children whose mouth talks emptiness and whose right hand is a right hand of wickedness, but the brotherly mind that rejoices for me when it approves of me and is sorry for me when it disapproves, because whether it approves or disapproves it loves me. To such people I shall reveal myself: they will breathe more easily at my good deeds and sigh over my bad ones. My good deeds are Your arrangements and Your gifts; my bad ones are my offences and Your judgements. Let them breathe more easily at the one and sigh at the other; and let hymns and weeping go up into Your sight out of the hearts of my brothers, who are Your censers. And You, Lord, take pleasure in the incense of Your holy temple; have mercy on me according to Your great mercy, for Your own name's sake; and in no way abandoning what You have begun, complete what is unfinished in me.

This is the benefit of confessing what I am, not what I have been: to confess it not only in front of You, in a secret exultation with trembling and a secret sorrow with hope, but also in the ears of the believing sons of men, who share my joy and are partners in my mortality, my fellow citizens and fellow pilgrims, some ahead of me and some following, companions on my road. These are Your servants and my brothers, whom You want to be Your sons; my masters, whom You have ordered me to serve if I want to live with You and from You. But this Word of Yours would be a small thing if it only gave orders by speaking, and did not go ahead by doing. So I do this in deed and in word; I do this under Your wings — and in far too much danger, if my soul were not made subject to You under Your wings, and my weakness known to You. I am a small child, but my Father lives for ever, and my guardian is enough for me. For he is the one who both fathered me and defends me; and You Yourself are all my good — You, almighty, who are with me before ever I am with You. So to those You have ordered me to serve, I shall reveal not what I have been but what I now am and what I still am. And yet I do not pass judgement on myself either. This, then, is how I would like to be heard.

For it is You, Lord, who judge me; because although nobody knows what is in a man but the spirit of the man which is in him, there is nevertheless something in a man that not even the spirit in him knows. But You, Lord, know all of him, since You made him. And I, though I despise myself in Your sight and reckon myself dust and ashes, still know something of You that I do not know of myself. And truly, now we see through a glass darkly, and not yet face to face. So as long as I am away from You I am more present to myself than to You; and yet I know of You that You cannot suffer, while about myself I do not know which temptations I can resist and which I cannot. And there is hope, because You are faithful, and will not let us be tempted beyond what we are able to bear, but will with the temptation also make a way of escape so that we can bear it. So I will confess what I know about myself, and I will confess what I do not know about myself. And that is because what I do know about myself, I know by Your shining on me; and what I do not know about myself, I go on not knowing until my darkness is made like noon in Your face.

Not with any doubt but with a settled certainty do I love You, Lord. You struck my heart with Your word, and I loved You. And the heavens too, and the earth and everything in them, tell me on every side to love You, and never stop saying so to everybody, so that they are without excuse. But more deeply You will have mercy on whom You will have mercy, and have compassion on whom You have had compassion; otherwise the heavens and the earth are speaking Your praises into deaf ears.

But what do I love, when I love You? Not the beauty of bodies, nor the loveliness of the passing seasons; not the brightness of light, so welcome to our eyes; not the sweet melodies of songs of every kind; not the fragrant smell of flowers and ointments and spices; not manna and honey; not limbs that are pleasant to embrace. None of these is what I love when I love my God. And yet I do love a kind of light, and a melody, and a fragrance, and a food, and an embrace, when I love my God: the light and melody and fragrance and food and embrace of my inner man — where there shines for my soul what no space can contain, and there sounds what time cannot carry away, and there smells what no breeze scatters, and there tastes what no eating diminishes, and there clings what no satisfaction parts us from. That is what I love when I love my God.

And what is this? I asked the earth, and it answered, "I am not he"; and everything in it said the same. I asked the sea and the deeps and the living creatures that creep in them, and they answered, "We are not your God; look above us." I asked the moving air, and the whole air with its inhabitants answered, "Anaximenes was mistaken; I am not God." I asked the sky, the sun, the moon, the stars: "We are not the God you are looking for either," they said. And I said to all the things that stand around the doorway of my flesh, "You have told me about my God, that you are not he; tell me something about him." And they cried out in a loud voice, "He made us." My questioning of them was my attention to them, and their form and beauty were the answer. And I turned to myself and said to myself, "Who are you?" And I answered, "A man." And there present themselves to me in me a soul and a body, the one outside and the other within. Which of these should I look for my God by? I had looked for him in the body, from earth up to heaven, as far as I could send my messengers, the beams of my eyes. But the inner is the better, since it was to the inner as presiding judge that all the bodily messengers reported the answers of heaven and earth and everything in them, when they said, "We are not God; he made us." These things my inner man knew through the service of the outer: I the inner knew them, I, the mind, through the senses of my body. I asked the whole structure of the world about my God, and it answered, "I am not he; he made me."

Is this bodily shape not visible to everyone whose senses work? Then why does it not say the same thing to everybody? Animals large and small see it and cannot ask it, because they have no reason set over their senses to judge what the senses report. But men can ask, so that the invisible things of God are clearly seen and understood by the things that are made. Yet by loving those things men are made subject to them, and subjects cannot judge. Nor do the creatures answer people who ask, unless the people can judge. And the creatures do not change their voice — that is, their appearance — so as to look one way to one man and another way to another, if one merely sees and another sees and asks. Appearing the same way to both, it is dumb to one and speaks to the other. Or rather it speaks to both, and only those understand who compare the voice they receive from outside with the truth inside. For truth says to me, "Neither heaven nor earth nor any other body is your God." Their very nature says this to anyone who sees them: "We are a mass, and a mass is less in one part of itself than in the whole." Now I am speaking to you, my soul; you are my better part, for you give life to the mass of my body, which no body can give to a body. But your God is even to you the life of your life.

What then do I love, when I love my God? Who is he, above the summit of my soul? By my very soul I shall climb up to him. I shall go beyond that power by which I am joined to my body and fill its whole frame with life; for by that power I cannot find my God, since if I could, a horse or a mule with no understanding could find him, seeing it is the same power by which their bodies live too. There is another power, not only the one by which I animate my flesh but the one by which I give it sensation, which the Lord has built for me: ordering the eye not to hear and the ear not to see, but the eye to be what I see through and the ear what I hear through, and giving each of the other senses its own particular seat and function; which are many, and which I, being one mind, work through. I shall go beyond this power of mine too, for horses and mules have this as well, since they perceive through the body too.

So I shall go beyond this power of my nature also, climbing by degrees to the one who made me. And I come to the fields and vast palaces of memory, where the treasures of countless images are stored, brought in from things of all kinds perceived by the senses. There is stored up as well everything else we think, by enlarging or reducing or in any other way varying the things the senses have reached; and whatever else has been entrusted to it and laid up there, which forgetfulness has not yet swallowed and buried. When I go in there, I ask for whatever I want to be brought out, and some things come instantly, while others have to be looked for longer and are fetched, as it were, out of some more inward container. Others come rushing out in crowds, and while one thing is wanted and asked for they leap forward as if to say, "Perhaps it is me?" These I drive away with the hand of my heart from the face of my remembering, until what I want is unveiled and comes into view out of its hiding place. Other things come up readily in unbroken order as they are called for, the ones in front making way for the ones behind; and as they make way they are hidden from view, ready to come again whenever I want. All this happens when I recite something by heart.

There everything is kept, sorted and under general headings, each having come in by its own entrance: light and all the colours and shapes of bodies by the eyes; all kinds of sound by the ears; all smells by the entrance of the nostrils; all tastes by the mouth; and by the sensation of the whole body, what is hard or soft, hot or cold, rough or smooth, heavy or light, whether outside the body or inside it. All these that great harbour of the memory receives in its countless secret and indescribable folds, to be available and brought out when needed, each having come in by its own gate and been stored there. Nor do the things themselves come in; only the images of the things perceived are there ready for thought to recall. How those images are formed, who can say — though it is quite plain which sense brought each of them in and stored it. For even while I am sitting in darkness and silence, I can produce colours in my memory if I want, and tell black from white and any other colours I please; and sounds do not break in and disturb the image drawn in by my eyes that I am reviewing, though they are there too, lying dormant and stored away separately. For I call for those as well and they appear at once. And though my tongue is still and my throat silent, I can sing as much as I like; and those images of colours, which are there all the same, do not push in and interrupt when another store is called for, which flowed in through my ears. And so with the other things heaped in by the other senses: I recall them at will. I can tell the smell of lilies from violets while smelling nothing; and I prefer honey to sweet wine, and smooth to rough, while tasting and touching nothing at the time, only remembering.

All this I do inside, in that huge court of my memory. For there heaven and earth and sea are present to me, and everything I could think about in them, apart from what I have forgotten. There too I meet myself, and recall myself, and when and where I did what, and how I felt about it. There is everything I remember, either from my own experience or on somebody else's word. Out of the same store I continually combine fresh and fresh likenesses of things I have experienced, or believed on the strength of what I have experienced, with the past; and from these I infer future actions and events and hopes, and reflect on all of these as present. "I will do this or that," I say to myself in that great container of my mind, stocked with the images of so many great things — "and this or that will follow." "If only this or that could happen!" "God forbid this or that!" That is how I talk to myself; and while I talk, the images of everything I mention are there, out of the same treasury of memory. And I could not speak of any of them if the images were missing.
