Book Five: Faustus, Rome, and the Road to Milan

(Part 1 of 2)

Accept the sacrifice of my confessions from the ministry of my tongue, which You shaped and stirred up to confess to Your name. Heal all my bones, and let them say, Lord, who is like You? A man who confesses to You is not informing You what goes on inside him, since a closed heart does not shut out Your eye, and human hard-heartedness cannot push back Your hand: You dissolve it whenever You like, in pity or in vengeance, and nothing can hide from Your heat. But let my soul praise You, so that it may love You; and let it confess Your own mercies to You, so that it may praise You. The whole of Your creation never stops praising You and never falls silent: not the human spirit with its voice turned towards You, and not creation, living or lifeless, speaking through the voices of those who consider it. So our souls may rise up out of their weariness towards You, leaning on the things You have made and passing on to You, who made them wonderfully. There is refreshment, and real strength.

Let the restless and the godless leave You and run away — and still You see them, and divide the darkness. And look: the universe with them in it is beautiful, though they are foul. What harm have they done You? How have they disgraced Your rule, which is just and complete from heaven down to this lowest earth? Where did they run to, when they ran from Your presence? Where do You not find them? They ran so as not to see You seeing them, and so as to blunder blindly into You — because You abandon nothing You have made — so that the unjust should stumble against You and be justly hurt, pulling away from Your gentleness, tripping on Your uprightness, and falling on their own roughness. They do not know that You are everywhere, and that no place contains You, and that You alone are near even to those who take themselves far away from You. Let them turn round, then, and look for You; for You have not abandoned Your creation the way they have abandoned their creator. Let them turn round and look for You, and there You are, in their heart — in the heart of those who confess to You and throw themselves on You and weep on Your chest after all their rough roads. Then You gently wipe away their tears, and they weep the more, and rejoice in weeping; because it is You, Lord, not some man of flesh and blood but You, Lord, who made them, who remake them and comfort them. But where was I when I was looking for You? You were in front of me, and I had gone away from You; and I could not even find myself, let alone You.

I want to lay open before my God that twenty-ninth year of my life. There had come to Carthage a certain bishop of the Manichees called Faustus, a great trap of the devil, and many people were caught in it by the bait of his smooth talk. I admired that talk myself, but I could tell it apart from the truth of the things I was so anxious to learn; I was not so much interested in the serving-dish of his oratory as in the knowledge that this Faustus, so praised among them, was setting in front of me to feed on. Rumour had already announced him as an expert in every valuable branch of learning and finely skilled in the liberal arts. And since I had read and remembered a good deal of the philosophers, I compared some of their views with the long fables of the Manichees and found the philosophers more probable — even though they could get no further than judging this lower world, and could not by any means find out its Lord. For You are great, Lord, and You have regard for the humble, while the proud You watch from far off. You come near only to the contrite in heart, and are not found by the proud, not even if by ingenious skill they could number the stars and the sand, and measure the starry heavens, and track the courses of the planets.

For with the understanding and wit You gave them they investigate these things, and they have found out a great deal. They have predicted eclipses of those two great lights, the sun and the moon, many years in advance — the day, the hour, and how much would be covered — and their calculations did not fail, and it happened as they said. They wrote down the rules they had discovered, and those rules are read today; and from them others predict in what year and month and on what day of the month and at what hour of the day, and what fraction of its light, the moon or sun will be eclipsed. And so it happens, exactly as foretold. People who do not know the science marvel at this and are astonished; and those who do know it exult and are puffed up, and by an irreligious pride they go away from You and lose Your light. They foresee a failure of the sun's light so long beforehand, and do not see the failure of their own, which is present now. For they do not enquire religiously where the wit comes from with which they investigate all this. And when they find that You made them, they do not give themselves up to You to be kept safe, or sacrifice to You what they have made of themselves. They do not kill off their own soaring fancies, as birds of the air; nor their own diving curiosities, with which like the fishes of the sea they wander the unknown paths of the deep; nor their own indulgences, as beasts of the field — so that You, Lord, a consuming fire, might burn up those dead concerns of theirs and remake the men themselves immortally.

But they did not know the way, Your Word, by whom You made the things they count, and the men who do the counting, and the sense by which they perceive what they count, and the understanding out of which they count — and that of Your wisdom there is no counting. But the Only Begotten was himself made wisdom and righteousness and sanctification for us, and was counted among us, and paid tax to Caesar. They did not know this way, by which to come down to him from themselves and by him climb up to him. They did not know this way, and thought themselves high up among the stars and shining; and look, they fell to the earth, and their foolish heart was darkened. They say a great many true things about the created world; but Truth, the maker of that world, they do not seek reverently, and so they do not find him — or if they do find him, and know him to be God, they do not glorify him as God, nor are they thankful, but become empty in their imaginings and declare themselves wise, taking to themselves what is Yours. And thereby, with the most perverse blindness, they take pains to attribute to You what is their own, inventing lies about You, who are the truth, and exchanging the glory of the incorruptible God for an image made like corruptible man, and like birds and four-footed animals and creeping things — changing Your truth into a lie, and worshipping and serving the creature more than the creator.

Yet I did retain from these men many true things about the created world, and saw the reasons for them from calculations, from the sequence of the seasons, and from the visible evidence of the stars. And I compared these with the sayings of Manichaeus, who in his frenzy had written at enormous length on these subjects; and I could find no account of the solstices or the equinoxes or the eclipses of the greater lights, or anything else of the kind I had learned in the books of secular philosophy. And I was ordered to believe — and it did not match what had been established by calculation and by my own eyes, but was flatly contrary to it.

Does a man who knows these things please You just for that, Lord God of truth? Surely a man who knows all of them and does not know You is unhappy; and happy is the man who knows You, even if he knows none of them. And the man who knows both You and them is not the happier for them but only for You, if in knowing You he glorifies You as God and is thankful and does not become empty in his imaginings. For a man is better off who knows how to own a tree and give You thanks for the use of it, though he does not know how many feet high it is or how wide it spreads, than one who can measure it and count every branch and neither owns it nor knows or loves its creator. In the same way a believer, to whom all this world of wealth belongs, and who having nothing yet possesses everything by holding fast to You, whom all things serve — even if he does not know the circling of the Great Bear, it would be folly to doubt that he is in a better state than someone who can measure the sky and count the stars and weigh the elements, and neglects You, who have made all things in number, weight and measure.

But who told that Manichaeus to write about these subjects too, when skill in them is no part of true religion? For You have said to man: reverence — that is wisdom. Of these he might have been ignorant while knowing everything about the stars. But since he dared, most impudently, to teach these things while not knowing them, he plainly could have no knowledge of reverence. It is empty to make a profession of these worldly matters even when you do know them; but confession to You is reverence. So this wanderer talked at length about these things for this purpose: that when he had been shown up by people who had really learned them, it would become obvious what sort of understanding he had of the more obscure matters. For he did not want to be thought little of, but set out to persuade people that the Holy Spirit, the comforter and enricher of Your faithful, was personally inside him with full authority. So when he was caught teaching falsehoods about the heavens and the stars and the motions of the sun and moon — although these things do not belong to religious doctrine — his sacrilegious presumption became obvious enough, since he delivered things he not only did not know but had got wrong, with such a mad vanity of pride that he wanted them credited to himself as to a divine person.

For when I hear some Christian brother who is ignorant of these matters and mistaken about them, I can watch such a man holding his opinion patiently. I cannot see that any ignorance about the position or character of the physical creation can hurt him, so long as he believes nothing unworthy of You, Lord, the creator of everything. It does hurt him if he imagines it belongs to the substance of religious doctrine, and insists stubbornly on what he does not know. And yet even that weakness, in the infancy of faith, is carried by our mother, Love, until the newborn grows into a full-grown man and can stop being blown about by every wind of doctrine. But in a man who presumed to be the teacher, the source, the guide, the head of everyone he could so persuade — so that whoever followed him believed he was following not a mere man but Your Holy Spirit — who would not judge that such enormous madness, once it was convicted of teaching anything false, should be detested and utterly rejected? But at that stage I had not yet established for certain whether the changes between longer and shorter days and nights, and between day and night itself, and the eclipses of the greater lights, and everything else of the kind I had read about elsewhere, could possibly be explained consistently with what he said. If they somehow could be, it would still remain an open question for me whether they were so or not; but I might rest my belief on his authority, on account of his reputed holiness.

And for almost all those nine years in which I had been their disciple with an unsettled mind, I had been longing far too intensely for this Faustus to come. Whenever the rest of the sect that I happened to run into could not solve my objections on these matters, they still held out the coming of this Faustus, in conversation with whom these and any greater difficulties I might have would be cleared up most readily and abundantly. When he did come, I found him a man of agreeable conversation who could talk fluently and in better words — but still the very same things they were all in the habit of saying. What good was the neatest of wine-waiters to my thirst for a more precious drink? My ears were already sick of this sort of thing, and it did not seem better to me for being better said, or truer for being eloquent, or the soul wiser for the face being handsome and the language graceful. But the people who had held him out to me were poor judges of things, and so to them he seemed intelligent and wise because his words were pleasing. I noticed that another sort of people are suspicious even of the truth and refuse to accept it if it is delivered in a smooth and copious style. But You, my God, had already taught me by wonderful and secret ways, and I believe You taught me because it is the truth, and there is no teacher of truth besides You, wherever it may shine on us from. From You, then, I had learned by now that nothing ought to seem true because it is eloquently said, or false because the words come out awkwardly; nor again true because it is roughly delivered, or false because the language is rich; but that wisdom and folly are like wholesome and unwholesome food, and ornate or plain phrases are like court dishes and country dishes: either kind of food can be served up in either kind of dish.

So the greediness with which I had waited so long for this man was certainly delighted with his manner and his feeling in argument, and with the choice and readiness of the words he dressed his ideas in. I was delighted then, and along with many others, and more than they, I praised and extolled him. What troubled me, though, was that in the crowd of his listeners I was not allowed to put in the questions that were bothering me and talk them over with him privately. When I could, and began with my friends to get his ear at times when it was not unsuitable for him to discuss things with me, and brought forward the questions that were troubling me — I found him first of all entirely ignorant of the liberal arts, apart from grammar, and that only in an ordinary way. But because he had read some of Cicero's speeches, a very few books of Seneca, some poetry, and the few volumes of his own sect that were written in neat Latin, and because he practised speaking daily, he had picked up a certain eloquence, which was all the more pleasing and seductive for being guided by a good wit and a kind of natural grace. Is that not how it was, as I recall it, Lord my God, judge of my conscience? My heart is in front of You, and my memory, You who at that time were directing me by the hidden mystery of Your providence, and setting those shameful errors of mine in front of my face, so that I might see them and hate them.

For once it was clear that he was ignorant of the arts I had thought he excelled in, I began to despair of his opening up and solving the difficulties that puzzled me — though ignorant of all that, he might still have held the truths of religion, had he not been a Manichee. For their books are stuffed with long-winded fables about the heaven and the stars and the sun and moon; and I no longer thought him able to settle satisfactorily what I very much wanted settled: whether, comparing these things with the calculations I had read elsewhere, the account in the books of Manichaeus was preferable, or at least as good. When I proposed that this be considered and discussed, he modestly shrank from the burden. He knew that he did not know these things, and was not ashamed to admit it. He was not one of those talkers, many of whom I had put up with, who undertook to teach me these things and said nothing. This man had a heart that was not right towards You, and yet was not altogether treacherous to himself either. He was not entirely ignorant of his own ignorance, and he would not be rashly tangled up in an argument he could neither back out of nor get out of honourably. I liked him the better even for that. For the modesty of an honest mind is finer than the knowledge of the things I wanted; and that is how I found him in all the harder and subtler questions.

With my enthusiasm for the writings of Manichaeus blunted like this, and despairing all the more of their other teachers, seeing how the most famous of them had turned out on various points that puzzled me, I began working with him on the literature he was keen on too — and which as a lecturer in rhetoric I was at that time teaching to young men at Carthage. We read together either what he wanted to hear or what I judged suited his abilities. But all the efforts I had planned to make to rise in that sect came completely to an end once I knew the man; not that I detached myself from them altogether, but as one who could find nothing better, I settled for being content in the meantime with whatever I had happened to land in, unless something more eligible should happen to dawn on me. So this Faustus, who to so many had been a trap of death, had now, without wanting to or knowing it, begun to loosen the trap I was caught in. For Your hands, my God, in the secret purpose of Your providence, did not abandon my soul; and out of my mother's heart's blood, through the tears she poured out day and night, a sacrifice was being offered for me to You, and You dealt with me in wonderful ways. You did it, my God: for the steps of a man are ordered by the Lord, and he shall direct his way. How else shall we obtain salvation, but from Your hand, remaking what it made?

So You arranged that I should be persuaded to go to Rome and teach there what I had been teaching at Carthage. And how I was persuaded to this I shall not fail to confess to You, because here too the deepest recesses of Your wisdom and Your most present mercy to us must be considered and confessed. I did not want to go to Rome because higher pay and higher standing were promised me by the friends who urged it, though at the time those things did have an influence on me. My chief and almost only reason was that I heard the young men there studied more peaceably and were kept quiet under a more regular discipline, so that they did not go bursting into the classroom of a teacher whose pupils they were not, whenever they felt like it, and were not even admitted without his permission. Whereas at Carthage there is a thoroughly disgraceful and unruly licence among the students. They burst in insolently, and with almost frantic behaviour they wreck whatever order a man has established for the good of his pupils. They commit various outrages with a remarkable stupidity, punishable by law were custom not on their side — that same custom showing them to be the more wretched, since they now do as lawful what by Your eternal law will never be lawful, and they think they get away with it unpunished, when they are punished by the very blindness that makes them do it, and suffer incomparably worse than what they inflict. So the behaviour I would not adopt as a student, I was obliged to put up with in others as a teacher; and so I was glad to go where everyone who knew assured me that nothing of the kind happened. But You, my refuge and my portion in the land of the living: so that I should change my earthly dwelling for the salvation of my soul, You goaded me at Carthage to tear me away from it, and dangled attractions at Rome to draw me there — through men in love with a dying life, the one lot doing frantic things and the other lot promising empty ones. And to correct my steps You secretly used both their perversity and my own. For those who were disturbing my peace were blinded by a disgraceful frenzy, and those who were inviting me elsewhere had the taste of earth about them. And I, who hated real misery here, was over there looking for an unreal happiness.
