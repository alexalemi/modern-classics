Book Two: The Pear Tree

Now I want to call to mind my past foulness, and the bodily corruptions of my soul — not because I love them, but so that I may love You, my God. I do it for love of Your love, going back over my worst ways in the very bitterness of remembering them, so that You may grow sweet to me: sweetness that never fails, blissful and certain sweetness. I do it to be gathered up again out of the scattering in which I was torn to pieces, when I turned away from You, the one good, and lost myself among a multitude of things. For there was a time in my youth when I burned to be filled full with things down here, and I dared to run wild with all these shadowy loves; my beauty wasted away and I stank in Your sight — while pleasing myself, and anxious to be pleasing in the sight of men.

And what was it I took delight in, except loving and being loved? But I did not keep to the proper measure of love, mind to mind, which is the bright boundary of friendship. Out of the muddy craving of the flesh and the bubbling up of adolescence, a fog rose that clouded my heart over, so that I could not tell the clear brightness of love from the mist of lust. Both were boiling together in me confusedly and sweeping my unsteady youth over the cliff of unholy desires, sinking me in a pit of shameful things. Your anger had gathered over me and I did not know it. I had gone deaf from the clanking of the chain of my mortality, which was the punishment for my soul's pride; and I wandered further from You, and You let me alone, and I was thrown about and squandered and scattered and boiled over in my promiscuity — and You kept quiet, my slow-coming joy. You kept quiet then, and I wandered further and further from You, into more and more barren seedbeds of sorrow, with a proud despondency and a restless exhaustion.

If only somebody had brought my disorder into some kind of order then, and made good use of the fleeting beauty of these lowest things You have made — had set a limit to their pleasure, so that the tides of my youth might have broken on the shore of marriage, if they could not be calmed. If they could have been kept inside the purpose of raising a family, as Your law prescribes, Lord — You who shape the offspring of this our death, and are able with a gentle hand to blunt the thorns that were shut out of Your paradise. For Your all-powerfulness is not far from us, even when we are far from You. Otherwise I ought to have paid closer attention to the voice from the clouds: Nevertheless such shall have trouble in the flesh, but I spare you. And, It is good for a man not to touch a woman. And, He that is unmarried thinks of the things of the Lord, how he may please the Lord; but he that is married cares for the things of this world, how he may please his wife.

I should have listened to those words more carefully; and if I had made myself a eunuch for the kingdom of heaven's sake, I would have waited more happily for Your embrace. But I, poor wretch, foamed like a rough sea, following the rush of my own tide, abandoning You, and I overran every limit You had set — and I still did not escape Your whip. What mortal ever does? You were always with me, mercifully severe, sprinkling the bitterest additive over all my forbidden pleasures, so that I might go looking for a pleasure with nothing bitter in it. And where to find one I could not discover, except in You, Lord, who teach by pain, and wound in order to heal, and kill us so that we shall not die away from You. Where was I, and how far was I exiled from the delights of Your house, in the sixteenth year of the age of my flesh, when the madness of lust took charge of me — the madness human shamelessness gives free licence to, though Your laws do not — and I handed myself over to it completely? Meanwhile my family took no trouble to save me from falling by marrying me off. Their only concern was that I should learn to speak excellently and be a persuasive public speaker.

That year my studies were interrupted. I had come back from Madaura, a nearby town where I had gone to learn grammar and rhetoric, and money was being put together for a longer journey to Carthage — put together more by my father's determination than by his means, since he was only a poor freeman of Thagaste. Whom am I telling this to? Not to You, my God; but in Your presence I am telling it to my own kind, to whatever small part of the human race may come across these writings of mine. And why? So that anyone who reads this may consider out of what depths we have to cry to You. And what is nearer to Your ears than a confessing heart and a life of faith? Everybody praised my father for providing his son with everything necessary for a long journey for the sake of his studies, well beyond what he could afford; plenty of far better-off citizens did nothing of the kind for their children. And yet that same father had no concern at all about how I was growing towards You, or whether I was chaste — so long as I was fluent in speech, however barren I might be of Your cultivation, God, who are the one true and good owner of Your field, which is my heart.

But while I was living with my parents in that sixteenth year, out of school for a time because a period of idleness had been forced on us by my parents' narrow finances, the briars of unclean desire grew up over my head, and there was no hand to root them out. When my father saw me at the baths, growing towards manhood and full of a restless adolescence, he told my mother about it gladly, as though already anticipating grandchildren — delighting in that upheaval of the senses by which the world forgets You its creator and falls in love with what You made instead of with You, drunk on the invisible wine of its own self-will, turning aside and bowing down to the very lowest things. But in my mother's heart You had already begun Your temple and laid the foundations of Your holy dwelling, while my father was still only a catechumen, and a recent one. So she was startled into a holy fear and trembling; and though I was not yet baptised, she was afraid for me of the crooked paths walked by those who turn their backs to You and not their faces.

How wretched I am — and do I dare say that You kept quiet, my God, while I wandered further from You? Did You really keep quiet then? And whose were those words if not Yours, which You sang in my ears through my mother, Your faithful servant? Not one of them sank into my heart deep enough to act on. She wanted, and I remember her warning me privately with great anxiety, that I should not commit fornication, and above all should never touch another man's wife. This struck me as women's advice, which I should have been embarrassed to take. But it was Yours, and I did not know it; I thought You were silent and it was she who was speaking — and it was through her that You were not silent to me, and in her that I, her son, the son of Your handmaid, Your servant, despised You. But I did not know it, and I ran headlong, so blind that among my equals I was ashamed of not being shameless enough. I would hear them boasting of their disgraces, and the more degraded they were the more they boasted; and I took pleasure not only in the pleasure of the act but in the credit for it. What deserves blame except vice? But I made myself out worse than I was, so as not to be blamed; and where I had not sinned as far as the worst of them, I would claim to have done things I had not done, so as not to seem contemptible in proportion as I was innocent, or to count for less the chaster I was.

Look at the company I walked the streets of Babylon in, wallowing in its mud as though in a bed of spices and precious ointments. And to fasten me tighter to its very centre, my invisible enemy trod me down and led me astray, because I was easy to lead. Even my mother in the flesh — who had already fled from the centre of Babylon, though she still moved slowly through its outskirts — even she, while she urged me to be chaste, did not act on what her husband had told her about me by trying to keep within the bounds of marriage what could not be cut away altogether. She did not act on what she felt was already dangerous and would be more dangerous later. She did not, because she was afraid that a wife would be a weight and a hindrance to my prospects. Not the hopes of the world to come, which my mother rested in You, but the hopes of an education, which both my parents were far too eager for me to get: my father because he had next to no thought of You and only empty ideas about me; my mother because she reckoned that the usual course of study would be no obstacle to reaching You, and might even help. That is my guess, at least, recalling as best I can what my parents were like. Meanwhile the reins were let out for me far past any reasonable severity, to spend my time in amusement and even in dissipation, at whatever I felt like. And over all of it hung a fog, cutting me off, my God, from the brightness of Your truth; and my wickedness burst out as if from sheer overfeeding.

Theft is punished by Your law, Lord, and by the law written in human hearts, which not even wickedness can rub out. What thief will put up with another thief? Not even a rich thief will put up with one who steals out of need. And yet I wanted to steal, and did it, driven by no hunger and no poverty, but out of a surfeit of doing well and a pampered appetite for wrongdoing. For I stole something I had plenty of already, and better. Nor did I want to enjoy what I stole; my enjoyment was in the theft, in the sin itself. There was a pear tree near our vineyard, loaded with fruit, tempting neither in colour nor in taste. A gang of us worthless young men went to shake it down and rob it late one night, having dragged our games out in the streets till then, as our poisonous custom was. We carried off huge loads — not to eat, but to throw to the pigs, after tasting a few. And we did it purely because we liked doing what we were not allowed to do. Look at my heart, God, look at my heart, which You had pity on at the bottom of the bottomless pit. Now let my heart tell You what it was after down there, that I should be gratuitously evil, with no motive for wickedness except wickedness itself. It was foul, and I loved it. I loved my own ruin. I loved my own failing — not the thing I failed over, but the failing itself. My soul was foul, falling away from Your firmament into utter destruction, seeking nothing through the disgrace but the disgrace itself.

For there is an attraction in beautiful bodies, in gold and silver, in everything; and in the touch of the body there is a strong sympathy, and every other sense has its own object properly suited to it. Worldly honour has its charm too, and the power of winning and of mastery, which is also where the thirst for revenge comes from. But we may not leave You, Lord, or turn aside from Your law, in order to get any of it. The life we live here has its own enchantment as well, through a certain proportion of its own and a correspondence with all the beautiful things down here. Human friendship, too, is sweetly binding, because of the unity it makes out of many souls. It is over all these things, and things like them, that sin gets committed — when through an immoderate leaning towards these goods of the lowest order, the better and higher goods are abandoned: You, our Lord God, and Your truth and Your law. These lower things have their delights, but not like my God who made everything, for it is in him that the righteous delight, and he is the joy of the upright in heart.

So when we ask why a crime was committed, we do not believe the answer unless it appears that there might have been some desire to get one of the lower goods we mentioned, or some fear of losing it. They are beautiful and attractive, after all, though compared with the higher and blessed goods they are cheap and low. A man has murdered another. Why? He loved his wife, or his property; or he wanted to rob him to live on; or he was afraid of losing something to him; or, having been wronged, he was on fire for revenge. Would anybody commit a murder for no reason at all, simply enjoying the murdering? Who would believe it? Even of that furious and savage man who is said to have been gratuitously evil and cruel, a reason is given: so that, as he put it, his hand and his heart should not go soft through idleness. And to what end? So that by practising crime he might take the city and get office and power and money, and be free of his fear of the laws and of his difficulties from debts at home and from knowing what he had done. So not even Catiline loved his own crimes; he loved something else, and it was for that he committed them.

What then did wretched I love in you, my theft, my deed of darkness, in that sixteenth year of my life? Lovely you were not, since you were a theft. But are you anything at all, that I should be talking to you? The pears we stole were lovely, because they were Your creation, most lovely of all, creator of all, good God, God, the highest good and my true good. Those pears were lovely; but it was not the pears my wretched soul wanted, since I had plenty of better ones, and I picked these only in order to steal. Once picked, I threw them away; my whole feast in them was my own sin, which I was pleased to enjoy. If any of those pears did go into my mouth, what sweetened it was the sin. And now, Lord my God, I ask what it was in that theft that delighted me — and look, there is no loveliness in it at all. I do not mean the loveliness there is in justice and wisdom; nor the loveliness in the human mind and memory and senses and animal life; nor the way the stars are glorious and beautiful in their courses, or the earth and the sea, full of unborn life, replacing what decays by what is born. Not even that false and shadowy beauty that belongs to the vices that deceive us.

For pride imitates height, when You alone are God, high above everything. Ambition — what does it want but honour and glory, when You alone are to be honoured above everything, and are glorious for ever? The cruelty of the powerful wants to be feared: but who is to be feared except God alone, out of whose power what can be wrenched or withheld, and when, and where, and how, and by whom? The caresses of the promiscuous want to be counted as love — yet nothing is more tender than Your love, and nothing is loved more wholesomely than Your truth, brighter and more beautiful than anything. Curiosity puts on the appearance of a desire for knowledge, when You supremely know everything. Even ignorance and stupidity dress themselves up under the names of simplicity and harmlessness, since nothing is found more single than You, and what is less harmful, since it is the sinner's own works that damage him? Laziness would like to be at rest: but what steady rest is there apart from the Lord? Extravagance wants to be called plenty and abundance: but You are the fullness and the never-failing plenty of pleasures that do not go bad. Wastefulness offers a shadow of generosity: but You are the most overflowing giver of every good thing. Greed wants to own many things, and You own everything. Envy argues about who is better: what is better than You? Anger looks for revenge: who takes revenge more justly than You? Fear startles at whatever is strange and sudden and endangers what it loves, and takes thought for its safety: but to You what is strange or sudden, and who separates from You what You love? Where is unshakeable safety except with You? Grief wastes away over things it has lost, the delight of its desires, because it wants nothing taken away from it — as nothing can be taken from You.

This is how the soul is unfaithful: when she turns away from You, looking outside You for what she will not find pure and unspoiled until she comes back to You. So everyone who goes far away from You and lifts himself up against You is imitating You in a twisted way. But even by imitating You like that, they show that You are the creator of the whole of nature, and that there is nowhere to retreat to that is altogether away from You. So what did I love in that theft? In what way was I even corruptly and perversely imitating my Lord? Did I want, at least by stealth, to do the opposite of Your law, because by power I could not — so that being a prisoner I might act out a crippled liberty, doing with impunity what was not permitted me, a darkened likeness of Your all-powerfulness? Look at Your servant, running away from his Lord and catching a shadow. What rottenness, what a monstrous life, what a depth of death: could I like a thing only because it was not allowed?

What shall I give back to the Lord, that my memory can go over these things and my soul not be terrified by them? I will love You, Lord, and thank You, and confess to Your name, because You have forgiven me these enormous and hateful deeds of mine. I put it down to Your grace and Your mercy that You have melted my sins away like ice. And I put down to Your grace as well every evil I did not do — for what might I not have done, when I loved a sin for its own sake? I confess that all of it has been forgiven me: both the evils I committed by my own will, and the ones I did not commit because You guided me. What man is there who, weighing up his own weakness, dares to credit his purity to his own strength, so as to love You the less, on the grounds that he needed less of the mercy by which You forgive the sins of those who turn to You? If anyone was called by You and followed Your voice and avoided the things he reads me recalling and confessing about myself, let him not laugh at me: I was sick and was cured by the same doctor whose help kept him from being sick, or from being as sick. Let him love You for that as much and more, since he can see that I was brought back from so deep a wasting of sin by the very one who kept him from wasting the same way.

What did I get out of those things then, wretched man, that I am now ashamed even to remember? Above all out of that theft, which I loved for the theft's own sake — and it was nothing in itself, which makes me the more miserable for loving it. And yet I would not have done it alone. That is what I was then, as I remember it: alone I would never have done it. So did I love the company of my accomplices as well as the theft? Then it was not the theft alone I loved — or rather it was nothing else I loved, because that circumstance of the company was nothing either. What is the truth about this? Who can teach me but the one who lights up my heart and uncovers its dark corners? What is it that has come into my mind to ask and turn over and think about? If I had loved the pears I stole and wanted to enjoy them, I could have done it alone, if simply committing the theft would have been enough for my pleasure; I would not have needed to inflame the itch of my desire by the excitement of accomplices. But since my pleasure was not in the pears, it was in the offence itself, and the company of fellow sinners is what produced it.

What was this feeling, then? Certainly it was too foul, and it was my misfortune to have it. But what was it? Who can understand his own errors? It was the joke — the way it tickled our hearts to fool people who had no idea what we were doing and would have hated it. Why then was my delight the kind I could not take alone? Because nobody normally laughs alone. Normally nobody does; and yet laughter does sometimes overcome a man by himself, with nobody at all beside him, if something very funny strikes his senses or his mind. Yet I would not have done this alone; alone I would never have done it. Look, my God, at the vivid memory of my soul, here in front of You: alone I would never have committed that theft, in which what pleased me was not what I stole but the stealing — and stealing alone would not have pleased me, so I would not have done it. What an unfriendly friendship, an inexplicable seducer of the mind, a greed for doing harm out of fun and mischief, a thirst for other people's loss with no appetite for my own gain or revenge! But when somebody says, "Come on, let's do it," we are ashamed not to be shameless.

Who can untangle that twisted, knotted thing? It is foul; I hate to think about it or look at it. But it is You I long for, righteousness and innocence, beautiful and attractive to all clean eyes, and satisfying without ever cloying. With You is complete rest and a life nothing can disturb. Whoever comes into You comes into the joy of his Lord, and shall not be afraid, and shall be at his best in the one who is best of all. I fell away from You and wandered, my God, much too far astray from You, my support, in those days of my youth, and I became to myself a barren land.
