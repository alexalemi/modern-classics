Book Eight: The Garden at Milan

(Part 3 of 3)

Where does this monstrous thing come from, and why? Let Your mercy shine out so that I can ask, in case the hidden punishments of men and the darkest agonies of the sons of Adam may perhaps answer me. Where does this monstrous thing come from, and why? The mind commands the body and is obeyed instantly; the mind commands itself and meets resistance. The mind orders the hand to move, and there is such readiness that the order is barely distinguishable from the obedience — and yet the mind is mind and the hand is body. The mind orders the mind, its own self, to will, and it does not do it. Where does this monstrous thing come from, and why? It orders itself, I say, to will, and would not give the order unless it willed; and what it orders is not done. But it does not will completely, and so it does not order completely. It orders only so far as it wills; and the thing ordered is not done exactly so far as it does not will. For the will orders that there be a will — not another will, but itself. But it does not order completely, and so what it orders does not happen. If the will were complete it would not even order itself to be, because it would be already. So it is not monstrous to will partly and refuse partly; it is a disease of the mind, which does not stand up wholly, being lifted by truth and weighed down by habit. And that is why there are two wills: because one of them is not complete, and what the one lacks the other has.

Let them perish from Your presence, God, as empty talkers and seducers of the soul do perish — the people who notice that in deliberating there are two wills, and then claim there are two minds in us of two kinds, one good and one evil. They are the evil ones themselves while they hold these evil views; and they will become good when they hold the truth and agree with the truth, so that Your apostle can say to them: you were once darkness, but now you are light in the Lord. But they want to be light not in the Lord but in themselves, imagining the nature of the soul to be what God is; and so they are made a thicker darkness by a dreadful arrogance, since they have gone further back from You, the true light that lights every man who comes into the world. Watch what you say, and blush for it; come near to him and be enlightened, and your faces shall not be ashamed. When I was deliberating about serving the Lord my God now, as I had long meant to, it was I who willed and I who refused: I, myself. I neither willed completely nor refused completely. So I was at war with myself and torn apart by myself. And this tearing happened to me against my will, and pointed not to the presence of another mind but to the punishment of my own. So it was no longer I who was doing it, but the sin that lived in me: the punishment of a sin committed more freely, in that I was a son of Adam.

For if there are as many contrary natures as there are conflicting wills, there will be not two but many. If a man is deliberating whether to go to their meeting-house or to the theatre, these Manichees cry out, "There you are: two natures. One good, pulling this way; one bad, pulling back that way." Where else, they say, could this hesitation between conflicting wills come from? But I say both are bad: the one that pulls towards them just as much as the one that pulls back to the theatre. They do not believe the will that pulls towards them is anything but good. Then what if one of us were deliberating, and in the struggle of his two wills were torn between going to the theatre and going to our church? Would these Manichees not be at a loss for an answer? For either they must admit, which they would hate to do, that the will leading to our church is good, just as theirs is who have received their mysteries and are held by them; or they must suppose two evil natures and two evil souls in conflict in one man, and then what they say will not be true, that one is good and the other bad; or they must be converted to the truth and stop denying that when a man deliberates, one soul wavers between contrary wills.

So let them stop saying, when they see two conflicting wills in one man, that the conflict is between two contrary souls of two contrary substances from two contrary principles, one good and one bad. For You, the true God, disprove and check and convict them. Take a case where both wills are bad: a man deliberating whether to kill a man with poison or with a sword; whether to seize this piece of somebody else's property or that one, when he cannot have both; whether to buy pleasure with extravagance or keep his money out of greed; whether to go to the races or the theatre, if both are on the same day; or, thirdly, to burgle somebody's house, if he gets the chance; or, fourthly, to commit adultery, if he has the opportunity at the same time — all these coinciding at the same moment, and all equally wanted, and none of them possible at once. They tear the mind between four, or, given the vast variety of things people want, even more conflicting wills; and yet the Manichees do not claim there are that many different substances. And the same holds for good wills. For I ask them: is it good to take pleasure in reading the apostle? Is it good to take pleasure in a sober psalm? Is it good to discuss the gospel? They will answer, to each, that it is good. What then if all of them give equal pleasure, and all at the same moment? Do these different wills not pull the mind about while it deliberates which to choose? And yet they are all good, and are at odds until one is chosen, and the whole will, which was divided among many, is carried towards it. So too when eternity delights us from above, and the pleasure of a temporary good holds us down below, it is one and the same soul that does not will this or that with a whole will; and that is why it is torn apart with painful perplexities, setting one thing first because of the truth, and not setting the other aside because of habit.

So I was sick in the soul and tormented, accusing myself far more bitterly than usual, rolling and turning in my chain until it should be wholly broken — the chain that now held me only just, but did still hold me. And You, Lord, pressed on me in my inward parts with a severe mercy, redoubling the lashes of fear and shame, in case I should give way again and, not bursting that one slight remaining tie, it should recover its strength and bind me tighter. For I said to myself, "Let it be done now, let it be done now." And as I spoke I very nearly did it. I all but did it, and did not do it; and yet I did not sink back to where I had been, but held my ground close by and caught my breath. And I tried again, and fell a little less short, and a little less, and all but touched it and took hold of it — and still did not reach it, or touch it, or take hold of it, hesitating to die to death and live to life. The worse that I was used to had more power over me than the better that I was not used to; and the very moment when I was to become other than I was, the nearer it came to me the greater the horror it struck into me. And it did not strike me back or turn me away; it held me in suspense.

The sheerest trifles of trifles and vanities of vanities, my old mistresses, still held me. They tugged at the garment of my flesh and whispered softly, "Are you throwing us off? And from this moment shall we never be with you again? And from this moment shall this and that be forbidden you for ever?" And what was it they suggested in what I have called "this and that" — what did they suggest, my God? Let Your mercy turn it away from the soul of Your servant. What filth they suggested, what disgrace! And by now I heard them much less than half, since they were not showing themselves openly and contradicting me but muttering behind my back, as it were, and secretly plucking at me as I went away, to make me look round at them. And yet they did hold me up, so that I hesitated to tear myself free of them and shake them off and leap over to where I was being called — while a violent habit kept saying to me, "Do you imagine you can live without them?"

But by now it spoke very faintly. For on the side I had set my face towards, and trembled to go, there appeared to me the chaste dignity of Self-Control: serene, and cheerful without being loose, honestly enticing me to come and not to hesitate, and stretching out to receive and embrace me, her holy hands full of crowds of good examples. There were so many young men and girls there, a multitude of the young and of every age, dignified widows and old women who had never married; and Self-Control herself in all of them, not barren but a fruitful mother of children of joy, by You her husband, Lord. And she smiled at me with a persuasive mockery, as if to say, "Can you not do what these young men and these girls can do? Or can any of them do it in themselves, and not rather in the Lord their God? The Lord their God gave me to them. Why are you standing on yourself, and so not standing at all? Throw yourself on him; do not be afraid, he will not pull back and let you fall. Throw yourself on him without fear; he will catch you and heal you." And I blushed deeply, because I could still hear the muttering of those trifles and was hanging in suspense. And again she seemed to say: "Stop your ears against those unclean parts of you on the earth, so that they may be put to death. They tell you about delights, but not as the law of the Lord your God does."

This argument in my heart was self against self, and nothing else. And Alypius, sitting close beside me, silently waited to see how my unusual state of feeling would come out.

But when deep reflection had drawn up all my misery from the secret bottom of my soul and heaped it in the sight of my heart, a huge storm rose, bringing a huge downpour of tears. And so that I might let the whole of it out with the noises it naturally makes, I got up and left Alypius; solitude struck me as better suited to the business of weeping. So I went far enough off that even his presence would not be a weight on me. That was the state I was in, and he noticed something of it; for I suppose I had said something in which the sound of my voice was choked with tears, and had got up on that. So he stayed where we had been sitting, utterly astonished. I threw myself down somehow under a fig tree and gave my tears free rein; and the floods poured out of my eyes, an acceptable sacrifice to You. And I said a great deal to You — not in these words, but to this effect: And You, Lord, how long? How long, Lord? Will You be angry for ever? Do not remember our old wrongdoings — for I felt I was still held by them. And I sent up these sorrowful words: How long, how long? Tomorrow and tomorrow? Why not now? Why is there not an end of my uncleanness this very hour?

I was saying this and weeping in the bitterest contrition of my heart, when suddenly I heard a voice from a neighbouring house, a boy's or a girl's, I do not know which, chanting and repeating over and over: "Take up and read; take up and read." At once my expression changed, and I began to think as hard as I could whether children ever sing such words in any kind of game; and I could not remember ever hearing anything like it. So I checked the flood of my tears and got up, taking it to be nothing but a command from God to open the book and read the first chapter I found. For I had heard about Antony that he came in during a reading of the gospel and took what was being read as spoken to him: Go, sell everything you have and give to the poor, and you shall have treasure in heaven; and come, follow me. And by that oracle he was converted to You at once. So I hurried back to where Alypius was sitting, since that was where I had put down the volume of the apostle when I got up. I snatched it, opened it, and read in silence the passage my eyes first fell on: Not in rioting and drunkenness, not in debauchery and licentiousness, not in strife and envy; but put on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make no provision for the flesh to gratify its desires. I did not want to read any further, and there was no need: for instantly, as the sentence ended, it was as though a light of certainty flooded into my heart and all the darkness of doubt vanished away.

Then, putting my finger or some other marker in the book, I closed it, and with a calm face told Alypius. And he told me what had been going on in him, which I did not know. He asked to see what I had read. I showed him, and he looked further on than I had read, and I did not know what came next. What came next was, Receive the man who is weak in the faith. He applied it to himself and told me so. And he was strengthened by that message; and by a good resolve entirely in keeping with his character, in which he had always been far better than I was, he joined me without any of the upheaval and delay. From there we went in to my mother and told her; she was overjoyed. We described in order how it had happened; she leapt for joy and triumphed, and blessed You, who are able to do more than we ask or think. For she could see that You had given her more for me than she had been in the habit of begging for with her pitiful and sorrowful groaning. For You converted me to Yourself so completely that I looked for neither a wife nor any hope of this world, standing on that rule of faith where You had shown me to her in a vision so many years before. And You turned her mourning into a joy far richer than she had wanted, and far more precious and pure than she had once asked for in wanting grandchildren of my body.
