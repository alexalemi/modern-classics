Book Ten: Memory

(Part 5 of 5)

And You know how far You have already changed me, You who first healed me of the appetite for justifying myself, so that You might forgive all the rest of my wrongdoing, and heal all my weaknesses, and redeem my life from corruption, and crown me with mercy and pity, and satisfy my desire with good things. You who curbed my pride with reverence for You, and tamed my neck to Your yoke. And now I carry it and it is light to me, because that is what You promised and what You have done; and it truly was so, and I did not know it, when I was afraid to take it up.

But, Lord — You alone are Lord without pride, because You are the only true Lord and have no lord over You — has this third kind of temptation stopped in me too, or can it stop through this whole life? I mean, wanting to be feared and loved by men, for no other purpose than to get from it a joy which is no joy at all. It is a wretched life and a foul boastfulness. This above all is why men neither purely love nor purely fear You. And that is why You resist the proud and give grace to the humble; and why You thunder down on the ambitions of the world, and the foundations of the mountains shake. Since certain offices of human society make it necessary to be loved and feared by people, the enemy of our true blessedness presses hard on us, spreading his traps of "well done, well done" everywhere, so that when we snatch greedily at them we may be caught off guard, and part our joy from Your truth and settle it in the deceptions of men; and be pleased at being loved and feared, not for Your sake but in Your place. And so, having been made like him, he can have us for his own, not in the bonds of love but in the chains of punishment — he who planned to set his throne in the north, so that dark and frozen we might serve him, imitating You in a perverse and crooked way.

But we, Lord, are Your little flock; keep us for Your own, stretch Your wings over us and let us fly under them. Be our glory; let us be loved for Your sake, and let Your word be feared in us. A man who wants to be praised by men when You blame him will not be defended by men when You judge him, or rescued when You condemn him. But when it is not the sinner who is praised for the desires of his soul, nor the man who acts irreligiously who is called blessed, but a man is praised for some gift You have given him, and he takes more pleasure in the praise for himself than in having the gift he is praised for — then he too is praised while You disapprove; and the man who did the praising is better than the man praised. For the one took pleasure in a gift of God in a man, and the other was better pleased with the gift of a man than with the gift of God.

By these temptations we are attacked daily, Lord; we are attacked without a break. Our daily furnace is the human tongue. And here too You command us self-control. Give what You command, and command what You will. You know the groans of my heart on this matter, and the floods from my eyes. For I cannot find out how far I am cleansed of this plague, and I am very much afraid of my hidden sins, which Your eyes know and mine do not. In other kinds of temptation I have some means of examining myself; in this one, hardly any. In holding my mind back from the pleasures of the flesh and from idle curiosity, I can see how far I have got when I do without them, either by giving them up or by not having them, since then I can ask myself how much more or less troublesome it is not to have them. And riches, which are wanted so as to serve one or two or all three of these cravings — if the soul cannot tell whether it despises them while it has them, they can be thrown away so that it can test itself. But to be without praise, and test our powers that way, must we live badly, indeed so abandonedly and atrociously that nobody could know us without detesting us? What greater madness could be said or thought of? But if praise usually accompanies a good life and good works, and ought to, we should no more give up its company than give up the good life itself. And yet I do not know whether I can do well or badly without something, unless it is taken away.

What then do I confess to You about this kind of temptation, Lord? What, except that I am delighted by praise — but delighted more by truth itself than by praise? For if it were put to me whether I would rather be deranged and wrong about everything and praised by everybody, or consistent and quite settled in the truth and blamed by everybody, I know which I would choose. And yet I could wish that somebody else's approval did not even add to my joy over any good in me. But I admit it does add to it, and not only that: disapproval reduces it. And when I am troubled by this wretchedness of mine, an excuse occurs to me — and how much it is worth, You know, God, since it leaves me uncertain. For You have commanded us not only self-control, that is, what to hold our love back from, but righteousness too, that is, where to bestow it; and You have wanted us to love not only You but our neighbour as well. So often, when I am pleased by intelligent praise, I seem to myself to be pleased at my neighbour's competence or promise, or to be sorry for a fault in him when I hear him disparage either what he does not understand or what is good. For sometimes I am sorry at praise given to me, either when things are praised in me that I do not like in myself, or when smaller and slighter goods are valued more highly than they deserve. But again, how do I know whether I feel this way because I do not want the man praising me to disagree with me about myself — not because I am concerned for him, but because the same good things that please me in myself please me more when they please somebody else as well? For somehow I am not being praised when my judgement of myself is not being praised, since either things are praised that displease me, or things are praised more that please me less. So am I in doubt about myself in this matter?

Look: in You, truth, I see that I ought not to be moved by praise of myself for my own sake, but for my neighbour's good. And whether that is how it is with me I do not know. Here I know less about myself than about You. I beg You now, my God, reveal me to myself as well, so that I may confess to my brothers, who are to pray for me, where I find myself crippled. Let me examine myself again more carefully. If in the praise given me I am moved by my neighbour's good, why am I less moved when somebody else is unjustly disparaged than when it is me? Why am I more stung by an insult thrown at me than by one thrown just as unjustly at somebody else in front of me? Do I not know this either? Or is it in the end that I deceive myself, and do not do the truth before You in my heart and my tongue? Put this madness far from me, Lord, in case my own mouth becomes to me the sinner's oil that makes my head fat. I am poor and needy, and I am at my best when in hidden groans I displease myself and look for Your mercy, until what is lacking in my defective state is made new and complete, on into that peace which the eye of the proud does not know.

And yet the word that comes out of the mouth, and deeds that people know about, bring with them a most dangerous temptation through the love of praise, which canvasses and collects people's votes so as to establish a certain excellence of our own. It tempts even when I am condemning it in myself, on the very ground that I am condemning it; and it often boasts more emptily about its very contempt for empty boasting — so that it is no longer contempt for empty boasting that it boasts of, since it is not despising it while it is boasting of it.

Inside, too, inside there is another evil, arising out of a similar temptation, by which men become empty, pleasing themselves in themselves although they do not please others, or displease them, or do not care whether they please them. But in pleasing themselves they greatly displease You, not only by taking pleasure in things that are not good as though they were, but in Your good things as though they were their own; or if as Yours, then as though for their own merits; or even if as coming from Your grace, then not with a brotherly rejoicing but grudging that grace to others. In all these dangers and labours and the like You can see the trembling of my heart; and I feel my wounds being cured by You rather than never being inflicted by me.

Where have You not walked with me, truth, teaching me what to beware of and what to want, whenever I brought You what I could find down here and asked Your advice? With my outward senses, as far as I could, I surveyed the world, and observed the life my body has from me, and these senses of mine. From there I went into the recesses of my memory, those many spacious chambers wonderfully furnished with countless stores; and I considered it and stood aghast, being able to make out none of these things without You, and finding that none of them was You. Nor was I myself the one who found these things out — I who went over them all and worked to distinguish them and value each according to its worth, taking some things on the report of my senses and questioning others that I felt to be mixed up with myself, counting and distinguishing the reporters themselves, and in the great treasure house of my memory turning some things over, storing others up, drawing others out. Nor was I myself when I did this — that is, the power in me by which I did it was not I; and it was not You either, for You are the abiding light which I consulted about all these things, whether they were, and what they were, and how they were to be valued. And I heard You directing and commanding me. And this I often do; this delights me; and whenever I can get free of my necessary duties, this is the pleasure I fall back on. And in all these things I run over while consulting You, I can find no safe place for my soul except in You, where my scattered parts may be gathered and nothing of me may leave You. And sometimes You let me into a state of feeling deep inside me that is most unusual, rising to a strange sweetness, which, if it were completed in me, I do not know what there would be in it that did not belong to the life to come. But through my miserable encumbrances I sink back down into these lower things and am swept back by my old habit, and am held, and weep greatly, and am greatly held. So heavily does the burden of a bad habit weigh on us. Here I can stay and would not; there I would and cannot; and both ways I am miserable.

So I have considered the sicknesses of my sins under that threefold craving, and have called Your right hand to my help. For it was with a wounded heart that I looked at Your brightness, and, struck back, I said, "Who can get there? I am cast away from the sight of Your eyes." You are the truth presiding over everything; and I in my greed did not want to lose You but wanted to possess a lie along with You — just as nobody wants to tell a lie in such a way as to be ignorant of the truth himself. So I lost You, because You will not be possessed along with a lie.

Whom could I find to reconcile me to You? Was I to turn to the angels? With what prayers? With what sacraments? Many people trying to come back to You and unable to manage it by themselves have tried this, I hear, and fallen into a craving for strange visions, and been judged fit to be deceived. For being high-minded they looked for You by the pride of learning, puffing out their chests rather than beating them; and so by the agreement of their hearts they drew to themselves the princes of the air, fellow conspirators in their pride, by whom they were deceived through magical influences while looking for a mediator to be cleansed by — and there was none. For it was the devil, transforming himself into an angel of light. And it greatly enticed proud flesh that he had no body of flesh. For they were mortal and sinners; but You, Lord, to whom they proudly sought to be reconciled, are immortal and without sin. Now a mediator between God and man must have something like God about him and something like man; otherwise, if he were like man in both respects he would be far from God, and if like God in both he would be too unlike man — and either way he would not be a mediator. That deceitful mediator, then, by whom in Your secret judgements pride deserved to be deluded, has one thing in common with man, namely sin; another he would like to seem to have in common with God, and, not being clothed in mortal flesh, would boast of being immortal. But since the wages of sin is death, this he does have in common with men: that along with them he is condemned to death.

But the true mediator, whom in Your secret mercy You have shown to the humble and sent so that by his example they might learn that same humility, that mediator between God and man, the man Christ Jesus, appeared between mortal sinners and the immortal just one: mortal along with men, just along with God. So that, because the wages of righteousness is life and peace, he might by a righteousness joined to God cancel the death of sinners now made righteous — the death he chose to have in common with them. So he was shown to the holy men of old, so that they, through faith in his passion to come, as we through faith in it past, might be saved. For as man he was a mediator; but as the Word he was not in the middle between God and man, being equal with God, and God with God, and together one God.

How You have loved us, good Father, who did not spare Your only Son but handed him over for us who were ungodly! How You have loved us, for whom he who thought it no robbery to be equal with You was made obedient even to the death of the cross — he alone free among the dead, having the power to lay down his life and the power to take it up again; for us to You both victor and victim, and victor because victim; for us to You both priest and sacrifice, and priest because sacrifice; making us Your sons instead of Your servants by being born of You and serving us. So my hope in him is strong, that You will heal all my weaknesses through him who sits at Your right hand and pleads for us — otherwise I should despair. For my weaknesses are many and great, many and great; but Your medicine is mightier. We might have imagined Your Word to be far from any union with man, and despaired of ourselves, if he had not been made flesh and lived among us.

Frightened by my sins and by the weight of my misery, I had turned it over in my heart and made up my mind to run away into the wilderness. But You forbade me and gave me strength, saying: Christ died for all, so that those who live should no longer live for themselves but for him who died for them. See, Lord, I throw my care on You, so that I may live and consider the wonderful things of Your law. You know my lack of skill and my weaknesses; teach me and heal me. He, Your only Son, in whom all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge are hidden, has redeemed me with his blood. Let the proud not speak evil of me, because I think about my ransom, and eat and drink it and share it out; and being poor, I wanted to be filled from him, among those who eat and are satisfied — and they who seek the Lord shall praise him.
