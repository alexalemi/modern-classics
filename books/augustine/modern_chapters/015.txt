Book Eight: The Garden at Milan

(Part 1 of 3)

My God, let me remember with thanksgiving, and confess to You, Your mercies to me. Let my bones be soaked with Your love, and let them say to You: who is like You, Lord? You have broken my chains apart; I will offer You the sacrifice of thanksgiving. And how You broke them I shall now tell; and everyone who worships You, when they hear this, will say, "Blessed be the Lord, in heaven and on earth; great and wonderful is his name."

Your words had stuck fast in my heart, and I was fenced in on all sides by You. Of Your eternal life I was now certain, though I saw it in a riddle and as through a glass. I had stopped doubting that there is an incorruptible substance from which every other substance comes; and I no longer wanted to be more certain about You, but steadier in You. But as for my life in this world, everything was wavering, and my heart had to be purged of the old leaven. The Way, the saviour himself, pleased me well enough, but I still shrank from going through its narrowness. And You put it into my mind, and it seemed a good idea to me, to go to Simplicianus, who struck me as a good servant of Yours, and in whom Your grace shone. I had also heard that from his earliest youth he had lived entirely devoted to You. By now he was well on in years, and after so long a life spent following Your ways so eagerly, he seemed to me likely to have gathered a great deal of experience — and so he had. I wanted him to draw on that store and tell me, once I had laid out my anxieties in front of him, which was the best road for a man in my condition to walk in Your paths.

For I could see the church was full, and one man went this way and another that. And I was unhappy that I was leading a worldly life. Now that my old desires no longer inflamed me with hopes of honour and profit, it was a very heavy burden to carry so heavy a slavery. Compared with Your sweetness and the beauty of Your house, which I loved, those things gave me no more pleasure. But I was still held captive by the love of a woman; and the apostle did not forbid me to marry, although he advised something better, chiefly wishing that all men were as he was himself. But being weak, I chose the more indulgent place; and because of that one thing alone, I was tossed about in everything else, faint and worn out with withering cares, since in other matters I was forced against my will to conform to a married life which I was given over to and enslaved by. I had heard from the mouth of the truth that there are some eunuchs who have made themselves eunuchs for the kingdom of heaven's sake; but, he says, let the man who can take it, take it. Certainly all men are empty who are ignorant of God, and could not from the good things that are seen find out the one who is good. But I was no longer in that emptiness; I had got past it, and by the shared testimony of all Your creatures I had found You, our creator, and Your Word, God with You, and one God together with You, by whom You created everything. There is yet another kind of irreligion: knowing God and not glorifying him as God, and not being thankful. Into that I had fallen too, but Your right hand held me up and took me out of it, and You set me where I might recover. For You have said to man: reverence for the Lord — that is wisdom. And, Do not want to seem wise, because those who claimed to be wise became fools. But I had now found the fine pearl which I should have sold everything to buy, and I hesitated.

So I went to Simplicianus, who had been father to Ambrose, now a bishop, in receiving Your grace, and whom Ambrose truly loved as a father. To him I described the maze of my wanderings. But when I mentioned that I had read certain books of the Platonists, which Victorinus, once professor of rhetoric at Rome and, as I had heard, dead a Christian, had translated into Latin, he said how glad he was that I had not come across the writings of other philosophers, which are full of fallacies and deceptions, after the elements of this world, whereas the Platonists led in many ways towards belief in God and his Word. Then, to urge me towards the humility of Christ, hidden from the wise and revealed to little ones, he spoke of Victorinus himself, whom he had known very intimately at Rome; and about him he told me a story I shall not keep back, since it contains great praise of Your grace, to be confessed to You.

Here was an old man, deeply learned and skilled in the liberal arts, who had read and weighed so many works of the philosophers; the teacher of so many noble senators, who as a monument of his excellent service had both deserved and been given a statue in the Roman forum, which men of this world reckon a high honour. Up to that age he was a worshipper of idols and a sharer in the sacrilegious rites that almost all the nobility of Rome were devoted to, and he had inspired the people with a love of

	Anubis, the barking god, and all
	the monster gods of every kind, who fought
	against Neptune, Venus and Minerva:

gods Rome had once conquered and now worshipped, all of which the aged Victorinus had defended for so many years with thundering eloquence. And now he did not blush to be the child of Your Christ and a newborn baby at Your font, bending his neck under the yoke of humility and submitting his forehead to the disgrace of the cross.

Lord, Lord, who have bent the heavens and come down, and touched the mountains and made them smoke, by what means did You get Yourself into that man's heart? He used to read the holy Scripture, Simplicianus said, and studied and searched all the Christian writings most carefully, and would say to Simplicianus — not publicly, but privately and as a friend — "You should know that I am already a Christian." To which he answered, "I shall not believe it, and I shall not count you among the Christians, until I see you in the church of Christ." And the other would say, teasing, "Is it the walls, then, that make Christians?" He often said he was already a Christian, and Simplicianus as often gave the same answer, and the joke about the walls was as often repeated. For he was afraid of offending his friends, proud demon-worshippers, and he supposed the weight of their enmity would fall on him from the height of their Babylonian dignity, as from the cedars of Lebanon which the Lord had not yet brought down. But after he had gathered firmness by reading and hard thought, and had come to be afraid of being denied by Christ before the holy angels if he was now afraid to confess him before men — and thought himself guilty of a serious offence in being ashamed of the sacraments of the humility of Your Word, while not being ashamed of the sacrilegious rites of those proud demons, whose pride he had imitated and whose rites he had taken up — he became bold-faced against emptiness and shamefaced before the truth, and said suddenly and unexpectedly to Simplicianus, as Simplicianus himself told me: "Let us go to the church; I want to be made a Christian." And he, unable to contain himself for joy, went with him. And having been admitted to the first sacrament and become a catechumen, not long afterwards he gave in his name to be born again by baptism — Rome astonished, the church rejoicing. The proud saw it and were furious; they ground their teeth and melted away. But the Lord God was Your servant's hope, and he paid no attention to empty things and lying madness.

And at last, when the hour came for him to make his profession of faith — which at Rome those about to come to Your grace deliver from a raised place, in front of all the faithful, in a set form of words learned by heart — the presbyters, he said, offered Victorinus the chance to make his profession more privately, as was done for those likely to be alarmed by shyness. But he chose to profess his salvation in the presence of the holy crowd. "For it was not salvation that he had taught in his rhetoric, and he had professed that publicly. How much less, then, should he be afraid of Your gentle flock when pronouncing Your word, when he had not been afraid of a mad crowd while delivering his own?" So when he went up to make his profession, everybody who knew him whispered his name to his neighbour with a note of congratulation. And who there did not know him? A low murmur ran through all the mouths of the rejoicing crowd: Victorinus! Victorinus! There was a sudden burst of delight that they saw him, and a sudden hush so that they could hear him. He pronounced the true faith with an admirable boldness, and everyone wanted to draw him into their very heart; and by their love and joy they did draw him there, for those were the hands they drew him with.

Good God, what is it that happens in a man, that he should rejoice more over the salvation of a soul that had been despaired of, and freed from greater danger, than if there had always been hope for it or the danger had been less? For You too, merciful Father, rejoice more over one man who repents than over ninety-nine righteous people who need no repentance. And we hear with great gladness, every time we hear it, with what joy the sheep that had strayed is brought back on the shepherd's shoulders, and the coin is put back in Your treasury with the neighbours celebrating alongside the woman who found it. And the joy of the solemn service of Your house forces tears out of us, when it is read in Your house about Your younger son, that he was dead and is alive again, was lost and is found. For You rejoice in us, and in Your holy angels, holy through holy love. For You are always the same; and everything that does not stay the same and does not last for ever, You know for ever in the same way.

What then happens in the soul, when it is more delighted at finding or recovering the things it loves than if it had always had them? Other things testify to this too, and everything is full of witnesses crying out, "That is how it is." The victorious general celebrates his triumph — and he would not have conquered if he had not fought, and the more danger there was in the battle the more joy there is in the triumph. The storm throws the sailors about and threatens shipwreck; they all go pale at the approach of death; then the sky and the sea grow calm, and they are as overjoyed as they had been terrified. A friend is ill, and his pulse is alarming; everyone who wants him to recover is sick in mind along with him. He gets better, and though he cannot yet walk with his old strength, there is a joy that was not there before, when he walked about sound and strong. Even the pleasures of human life are things people get at by difficulties — not only difficulties that fall on us unlooked for and against our will, but trouble we deliberately choose and go looking for. Eating and drinking give no pleasure unless the pinch of hunger and thirst comes first. Heavy drinkers eat salty things to work up an uncomfortable heat, which the drink then quenches, and that gives pleasure. And it is the custom that a girl who is engaged should not be handed over at once, in case a husband should hold cheap a woman he had not sighed after as a fiancée.

This law holds in foul and cursed joy; it holds in permitted and lawful joy; it holds in the very purest perfection of friendship; it holds in the man who was dead and lived again, was lost and was found. Everywhere the greater joy is ushered in by the greater pain. What does this mean, Lord my God, when You are everlastingly a joy to Yourself, and some things around You rejoice in You for ever? What does it mean that this portion of things ebbs and flows like this, in turn displeased and reconciled? Is this their allotted measure? Is this all You have assigned them, when from the highest heavens to the lowest earth, from the beginning of the world to the end of the ages, from the angel to the worm, from the first motion to the last, You set each thing in its place and bring each about in its season, everything good after its kind? How high You are in the highest and how deep in the deepest! And You never leave, and we can scarcely get back to You.

Get up, Lord, and act; stir us up and call us back; kindle us and draw us; set us on fire, grow sweet to us; let us love now, let us run. Do not many people come back to You out of a deeper hell of blindness than Victorinus, and come near and are lit up, receiving that light — and those who receive it receive from You the power to become Your sons? But if they are less known to everybody, then even the people who know them rejoice less for them. For when many rejoice together, each of them also has a more abundant joy, because they set each other alight. And also because people known to many have more influence towards salvation, and lead the way for many to follow. And so those who went before them rejoice greatly in them too, because they are not rejoicing in them alone. Far be it that in Your tent the rich should be preferred to the poor, or the noble to the low-born; seeing that You have rather chosen the weak things of the world to confound the strong, and the low things of this world and the things despised You have chosen, and the things that are not, so that You might bring to nothing the things that are. And yet even that least of Your apostles, by whose tongue You sounded out these words, when through his campaign Paulus the proconsul had his pride conquered and was made to pass under the easy yoke of Your Christ, and became a subject of the great king — even he chose to be called Paul instead of his former name Saul, as evidence of so great a victory. For the enemy is more defeated in a man he has more hold over, and through whom he has hold of more people. And he has more hold over the proud, through their high birth, and through them of more people, through their authority. So the more the heart of Victorinus was valued, which the devil had held as an impregnable possession, and the tongue of Victorinus, that mighty and sharp weapon with which he had killed many, the more abundantly ought Your sons to rejoice — because our king has bound the strong man, and they saw his vessels taken from him and cleaned and made fit for Your honour, and become useful to the Lord for every good work.

But when that man of Yours, Simplicianus, told me this about Victorinus, I was on fire to imitate him — which was exactly why he had told it to me. And when he added how in the days of the emperor Julian a law was made forbidding Christians to teach the liberal arts or oratory, and how Victorinus obeyed that law and chose to give up the school of words rather than Your Word, by which You make the tongues of the dumb eloquent, he seemed to me not so much resolute as fortunate, in having found a chance to wait on You alone. And that was what I was sighing for — tied up as I was, not with somebody else's irons but by my own iron will. My will was held by the enemy, and out of it he had made a chain and bound me with it. For out of a twisted will came lust; and lust served became habit; and habit not resisted became necessity. By these links, joined together as it were, which is why I called it a chain, a hard slavery held me fast. But the new will that had begun to be in me, to serve You freely and to want to enjoy You, God, the only reliable pleasure, was not yet able to overcome my old wilfulness, which age had made strong. So my two wills, one new and one old, one of the flesh and one of the spirit, struggled inside me, and by their quarrelling they tore my soul apart.
