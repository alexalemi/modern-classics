Book Four: Friendship, Grief, and the Death of a Friend

(Part 1 of 2)

For nine years, from my nineteenth to my twenty-eighth, we lived seduced and seducing, deceived and deceiving, in one appetite after another: openly, through the disciplines they call liberal; secretly, through a religion with a false name. Proud on the one side, superstitious on the other, and empty on both. On the one side chasing the emptiness of popular applause, right down to theatre ovations and poetry prizes and contests for wreaths of grass, and the follies of public shows and the intemperance of desire. On the other, wanting to be cleansed of those pollutions by carrying food to the men they called the "elect" and the "holy", out of which, in the workshop of their stomachs, they were to forge angels and gods for us, who might then do the cleansing. These were the things I chased and practised with my friends, who were deceived by me and along with me. Let the arrogant mock me, and everyone who has not been struck down and knocked flat by You for the health of his soul, my God — I will still confess my own shame to You in Your praise. Bear with me, I beg You, and give me the grace to go over the wanderings of my past in my present memory, and to offer You the sacrifice of thanksgiving. For what am I to myself without You but a guide to my own downfall? What am I even at my best but a baby sucking the milk You give, and feeding on You, the food that does not perish? What sort of thing is any man, seeing he is only a man? Let the strong and the powerful laugh at us; let us, poor and needy, confess to You.

In those years I taught rhetoric, and, mastered by greed, sold a talkativeness for mastering others. And yet, Lord, You know, I preferred students who were honest, as honesty is reckoned; and I taught them tricks without any tricks of my own — tricks not to be used against an innocent man's life, though sometimes to save a guilty one's. And You, God, saw me from far off, stumbling on that slippery road and sending out a few sparks of good faith through a great deal of smoke; and I showed those sparks in the way I guided people who loved emptiness and went looking for lies, being their companion in it. In those years I had a woman — not in what is called lawful marriage, but one I had found in a wayward passion with no understanding in it. But only one, and I stayed faithful to her. And in my own case I learned the difference between the self-restraint of the marriage covenant, entered into for the sake of children, and the bargain of a lustful love, where children are born against their parents' wishes — though once born they compel love.

I remember too that when I had decided to enter for a prize at the theatre, some fortune-teller asked me what I would pay him to win. I detested and loathed those foul mysteries, and answered, "Even if the wreath were of imperishable gold, I would not let a fly be killed to win it for me." For he was going to kill living creatures in his sacrifices, and by those honours invite the demons to favour me. But I rejected this evil too, and not out of any pure love for You, God of my heart; for I did not know how to love You, not knowing how to conceive of anything beyond a material brightness. And does not a soul sighing after such fictions commit adultery against You, trusting in unreal things and feeding on wind? Still, I did not want sacrifices offered to demons on my behalf — while by that superstition I was sacrificing myself to them. For what else is feeding on wind but feeding them, that is, becoming their entertainment and their joke by going astray?

Those frauds they call astrologers, then, I consulted without hesitation, because they seemed to use no sacrifices and to pray to no spirit for their divinations — an art which Christian and true piety nevertheless consistently rejects and condemns. For it is a good thing to confess to You and say, Have mercy on me, heal my soul, for I have sinned against You; and not to abuse Your mercy as a licence to sin, but to remember the Lord's words, See, you are made whole; sin no more, in case something worse happens to you. All of that wholesome advice they work to destroy, saying, "The cause of your sin is fixed inevitably in heaven," and "This was the doing of Venus, or Saturn, or Mars" — so that a man, flesh and blood and proud rottenness, may be blameless, while the creator and orderer of heaven and the stars takes the blame. And who is he but our God? The very sweetness and wellspring of righteousness, who repay every man according to his works, and will not despise a broken and contrite heart.

There was in those days a wise man, very skilled in medicine and famous for it, who had with his own proconsular hand set the winner's wreath on my diseased head — though not as a physician, for this disease is cured only by You, who resist the proud and give grace to the humble. But did You fail me even through that old man, or hold back from healing my soul? For I got to know him better and hung on his conversation steadily, since although his words were simple they were vivid and lively and in earnest; and when he had gathered from my talk that I was devoted to the books of the horoscope-casters, he advised me kindly, like a father, to throw them away and not waste on such empty things the care and diligence that useful work needed. He said he had studied that art in his earliest years, meaning to take it up as the profession he would live by; that if he could understand Hippocrates, he could soon have understood a study like this; and that he had nevertheless given it up and turned to medicine, for no other reason than that he had found it completely false — and being a serious man he would not make a living by deceiving people. "But you," he said, "have rhetoric to keep yourself by, so you are following this by choice and not from necessity. All the more reason to believe me about it, since I worked to master it well enough to live by it and nothing else." When I asked him how so many true things could be predicted by it, he answered as best he could: that the force of chance, spread through the whole order of things, brought it about. For if when a man opens a book of poetry at random — a poet who was thinking and writing about something completely different — a line often turns up that fits the present business remarkably well, it would be no wonder if out of a man's soul, which does not know what is going on inside it, some higher instinct should produce an answer matching the affairs and actions of the person asking: by luck, not by art.

And this much, from him or through him, You conveyed to me and traced in my memory, for me to examine for myself later. But at the time neither he nor my dearest Nebridius, an exceptionally good young man with a holy fear in him, who laughed at the whole business of divination, could persuade me to throw it out. The authority of the writers still swayed me, and I had as yet found no certain proof, of the kind I was looking for, that would show beyond all doubt that what had been truly predicted by the people consulted was the result of chance and not of the star-gazers' art.

In the years when I first began teaching rhetoric in my home town, I had made a friend, too dear to me, out of a shared set of interests: a man of my own age, and like me in the first opening flower of youth. He had grown up with me from childhood, and we had been schoolfellows and playfellows. But he was not yet my friend in the way he became later, nor even then in the way that true friendship is; for friendship cannot be true unless You cement it together, in people who hold fast to You by the love which is poured into our hearts by the Holy Spirit, who is given to us. And yet it was all too sweet, ripened in the warmth of shared studies. For I had twisted him away from the true faith, which as a young man he had not thoroughly taken in, and towards those superstitious and destructive fables that my mother wept over me for. Now his mind went wrong along with mine, and my soul could not be without him. But look: You were close behind Your runaways, God of vengeance and fountain of mercies at once, turning us back to Yourself by remarkable means. You took that man out of this life when he had barely completed a single year of my friendship, a friendship sweeter to me than all the sweetness of that life of mine.

Who can recount all Your praises, which he has felt in his own single self? What did You do then, my God, and how unsearchable is the depth of Your judgements? For he was long and badly sick with a fever, and lay unconscious in a death sweat; and when his recovery was despaired of, he was baptised without knowing it. I meanwhile hardly cared, assuming that his soul would keep what it had got from me rather than what had been done to his unconscious body. But it turned out quite otherwise: he revived and recovered. As soon as I could speak to him — and I could as soon as he was able, since I never left him and we depended far too much on each other — I tried to joke with him, expecting him to join me in laughing at the baptism he had received while completely absent in mind and feeling, but had now been told about. But he recoiled from me as from an enemy, and with a remarkable and sudden independence told me that if I wanted to remain his friend I would stop talking to him like that. Astonished and confused, I held back everything I felt until he should get well and be strong enough for me to handle him as I wished. But he was taken away from my madness, to be kept safe with You for my consolation. A few days later, while I was away, he was attacked by the fever again, and died.

At this grief my heart went completely dark, and everything I looked at was death. My own country was a torture to me and my father's house a strange misery; and everything I had shared with him became, without him, an agony. My eyes looked for him everywhere and did not get him; and I hated every place, because he was not in it, and because they could not say to me, "He is coming," as they had when he was alive and away. I became a great riddle to myself, and I asked my soul why she was so sad and why she troubled me so badly — and she did not know what to answer. And if I said, Trust in God, she quite rightly did not obey me; because the dearest friend she had lost was, being a man, both truer and better than the phantom she was being told to trust. Only tears were sweet to me, for they had taken my friend's place in the dearest of my affections.

And now, Lord, these things are past and time has taken the edge off my wound. Let me learn from You, who are the truth, and bring the ear of my heart up to Your mouth, so that You may tell me why weeping is sweet to the wretched. Have You, though present everywhere, thrown our misery far away from You? You stay in Yourself, while we are thrown about among all sorts of trials. And yet unless we did our mourning in Your hearing, we should have no hope left. So where does sweet fruit come from, gathered out of the bitterness of life, out of groaning and tears and sighs and complaints? Is it hoping that You hear us that sweetens it? That is true of prayer, since there is a longing in prayer to come close to You. But is it also true of grief for something lost, and the sorrow I was drowning in then? For I did not hope he would come back to life, and I did not ask for that with my tears. I only wept and grieved. I was miserable, and I had lost my joy. Or is weeping really a bitter thing, which pleases us out of sheer disgust at what we used to enjoy, at the moment when we recoil from it?

But why am I talking about this? Now is no time for questions but for confessing to You. I was wretched, and every soul is wretched that is tied down by love of things that die. It is torn apart when it loses them, and only then does it feel the wretchedness it was in before it lost them. So it was with me then. I wept most bitterly and found my rest in bitterness. That was how wretched I was, and I held that wretched life dearer than my friend. For although I would gladly have changed it, I was still less willing to give it up than to give him up — indeed I do not know whether I would have given it up even for him, as the story goes, if it is not invented, about Pylades and Orestes, who would gladly have died for each other or together, since living apart was worse to them than death. But something had risen in me that was quite the opposite of this and that I cannot explain: I loathed living and was afraid to die, both at once. I suppose the more I loved him the more I hated and feared, as the cruellest of enemies, the death that had taken him from me; and I imagined it would make a quick end of everybody, since it had had power over him. That is how it was with me, I remember. Look at my heart, my God, look inside me; I remember it well, my hope, who cleanse me of the impurity of such loves, turning my eyes towards You and pulling my feet out of the trap. For I was amazed that other people, who are also going to die, were alive, when the man I had loved as if he would never die was dead; and I was more amazed still that I myself, who had been a second self to him, could be alive when he was dead. Somebody said of his friend, "You half of my soul" — and I felt that my soul and his soul had been one soul in two bodies; and so my life was a horror to me, because I did not want to live as a half. And that was perhaps why I was afraid to die: so that the man I had loved so much should not die completely.

What madness, not to know how to love people as people! What a fool I was then, unable to bear a human being's lot with any patience! I fretted and sighed and wept and was distracted, with no rest and no counsel. For I was carrying about a shattered and bleeding soul that could not bear to be carried by me — and where to set it down I could not find. Not in pleasant woods, not in games and music, not in scented gardens, not in elaborate dinners, not in the pleasures of the bed, and not, in the end, in books or poetry either. Everything looked ghastly, even the daylight. Whatever was not what he had been was disgusting and hateful — except groaning and tears, which were the only things I found any relief in. But when my soul was pulled away from those, an enormous weight of misery pressed me down. It should have been lifted up to You, Lord, for You to lighten it; I knew that, and I could not and would not — all the more since when I thought about You, You were nothing solid or substantial to me. For You were not Yourself but a phantom, and my error was my god. If I tried to unload my burden onto that, so that it might rest, it slid through the emptiness and came rushing back down on me; and I was left to myself as a miserable place where I could neither stay nor get away from. For where could my heart run to, away from my heart? Where could I run from myself? Where would I not follow myself? And yet I ran out of my own country, so that my eyes should look for him less, in places where they were not used to seeing him. And so I came from Thagaste to Carthage.

Time does not waste time; it does not roll idly by. Through our senses it works strange changes on the mind. Look: the days came and went, and by coming and going they brought other images and other memories into my mind, and patched me back together bit by bit with my old sort of pleasures, to which that grief of mine gave way. And what followed was not exactly other griefs, but the causes of other griefs. For how had that first grief got so easily into the deepest part of me, except that I had poured out my soul on dust, by loving a man who had to die as if he never would? What mainly restored and refreshed me was the comfort of other friends, with whom I loved what I was loving instead of You; and this was a huge fable and an extended lie, whose adulterous prodding defiled our souls as they lay itching in our ears. But that fable would not die for me, however many of my friends died. There were other things in them that took my mind more: talking and laughing together; doing each other kindnesses by turns; reading good books together; playing the fool together and being serious together; disagreeing occasionally without any ill feeling, as a man does with himself, and using the very rarity of those disagreements to season our far more frequent agreements; sometimes teaching and sometimes learning; longing impatiently for the ones who were away, and welcoming them with delight when they came. These signs and others like them, coming out of the hearts of people who loved and were loved in return, showing in the face and the tongue and the eyes and a thousand pleasing gestures, were fuel to melt our souls together and make one out of many.

This is what is loved in friends, and so loved that a man's conscience condemns him if he does not love someone who loves him, or does not love back the man who loves him, looking for nothing from him but the signs of his love. Hence that mourning when one of them dies, and the darkenings of sorrow, and the steeping of the heart in tears, and all sweetness turned bitter; and when the dying lose their life, the living die. Blessed is the man who loves You, and loves his friend in You, and his enemy for Your sake. He alone loses nobody dear to him, since everybody is dear to him in the one who cannot be lost. And who is that but our God, the God who made heaven and earth and fills them, because he created them by filling them? Nobody loses You except the man who leaves You; and the man who leaves You, where does he go or run to, except from You pleased to You displeased? For where does he not find Your law in his own punishment? And Your law is truth, and truth is You.

Turn us again, God of hosts, show us Your face, and we shall be whole. For wherever the human soul turns, unless it turns towards You, it is nailed to sorrows — even when it is nailed to beautiful things. And yet those things, outside You and outside the soul, would not exist unless they were from You. They rise and set; and by rising they begin, so to speak, to be; they grow, so as to become complete; and once complete they age and wither. Not all of them age, but all of them wither. So when they rise and press towards being, the faster they grow towards being, the faster they hurry towards not being. That is the law of them. That is the share You have allotted them, because they are parts of a thing that does not exist all at once, but by passing away and being succeeded makes up together the whole universe they are parts of. And in the same way our speech is completed by signs that make a sound — but this too is not finished unless one word passes away when it has sounded its part, so that another can follow. Out of all these things let my soul praise You, God, creator of everything; but let my soul not be nailed to these things with the glue of love, through the senses of the body. For they go where they were always going, into not being, and they tear her with sickly longings, because she longs to be, and loves to rest in what she loves. But in these things there is no resting place; they do not stay, they run. And who can follow them with the senses of the flesh? Who can even grasp them when they are close at hand? For the sense of the flesh is slow, because it is the sense of the flesh, and it is limited by that. It is enough for what it was made for, but it is not enough to hold things that are running their course from their appointed start to their appointed end. For in Your Word, by which they are created, they hear their sentence: this far and no further.
