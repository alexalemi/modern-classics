Book Three: Carthage, the Theatre, and the Manichees

(Part 2 of 2)

Nor did I know that true inward righteousness which judges not by custom but by the most upright law of God almighty — the law by which the ways of particular places and times were arranged to suit those times and places, while the law itself stays the same always and everywhere, not one thing here and another there. By that law Abraham and Isaac and Jacob and Moses and David were righteous, and so were all the others whom God's own mouth commended; and they were judged unrighteous by silly people, judging by human standards and measuring the moral customs of the whole human race by their own small habits. It is as if a man in an armoury, not knowing what fitted which part of the body, put leg-armour on his head and tried to wear a helmet on his feet and then complained that they did not fit. Or as if, on a day when business is publicly closed in the afternoon, somebody were angry at not being allowed to keep his shop open, when he had been open in the morning. Or as if in one house he saw a servant handling something the butler is not allowed to touch, or something permitted outdoors that is forbidden in the dining room, and were angry that in one house and one family the same thing is not allowed everywhere and to everybody. Such are the people who are irritated to hear that something was lawful for righteous men in the past and is not now; or that God, for particular reasons of time, commanded those men one thing and these another, when both were obeying the same righteousness. They can see in one man, one day, one household, that different things suit different parts and different seasons and different persons, and that what was lawful before a certain time is not lawful after it, and that a thing permitted in one corner is rightly forbidden and punished in another. Is justice, then, variable and changeable? No; but the times over which it presides do not flow evenly, because they are times. Men, whose days on earth are few, cannot use their senses to harmonise the causes of things in earlier ages and other nations, which they have no experience of, with the ones they do have experience of; whereas in a single body or day or family they easily see what suits each part and season and person. So they take exception to the one and submit to the other.

These things I did not know then and did not notice. They struck my eye from every side and I did not see them. I wrote verses, in which I could not put every foot everywhere but had to put different feet in different metres, and could not even put the same foot in every position within a single metre. Yet the art itself, by which I wrote, did not have different principles for these different cases: it took them all in together. Still I could not see how that righteousness, which good and holy men obeyed, contained all the things God had commanded far more excellently and loftily in one, and varied in no part at all — although in varying times it did not prescribe everything at once, but allotted and required what was right for each. And in my blindness I criticised the holy fathers, not only where they were using what was to hand as God commanded and inspired them, but even where they were foretelling things to come, as God was revealing through them.

Can it ever, anywhere, be unjust to love God with all your heart and all your soul and all your mind, and your neighbour as yourself? Therefore those foul offences that are against nature are to be detested and punished everywhere and always, such as the offences of the men of Sodom — which, if all nations committed them, would leave them all guilty of the same crime by the law of God, who did not make men to abuse one another in that way. For even the dealing that ought to be between God and us is violated when the nature he is the author of is polluted by a perversion of lust. But actions that are offences against human custom are to be avoided according to whatever customs prevail in each place, so that something agreed and settled by the custom or law of any city or nation may not be broken by the lawless whim of anyone, native or foreigner. For any part that does not harmonise with its whole is offensive. But when God commands something to be done that goes against the customs or agreements of a people, it must be done, even if they have never done it before; and if it has lapsed, it must be restored; and if it was never established, it must be established now. For if it is lawful for a king in the state he rules to command something nobody before him and he himself had never commanded, and obeying him cannot be against the common good of the state — indeed it would be against the common good if he were not obeyed, since obeying rulers is a general agreement of human society — how much more unhesitatingly ought we to obey God in everything he commands, the ruler of all his creatures? Just as among the powers in human society the greater authority is obeyed in preference to the lesser, so God must be obeyed above all.

The same goes for acts of violence, where there is a wish to hurt, by insult or injury; and these are done either for revenge, as one enemy against another; or for some profit that belongs to somebody else, as a robber does to a traveller; or to avoid some harm, against a man one is afraid of; or out of envy, as a less fortunate man treats a more fortunate one, or a man doing well treats one whose coming level with him he fears or resents; or simply for the pleasure of another's pain, as with spectators at gladiator shows, or people who jeer at and mock others. These are the main headings of wickedness, which spring from the appetite of the flesh, or of the eye, or for power — either singly, or two together, or all three; and so men live badly against the three and the seven, that instrument of ten strings, Your Ten Commandments, God, most high and most sweet. But what foul offence can there be against You, who cannot be defiled? What act of violence against You, who cannot be harmed? What You avenge is what men commit against themselves; because even when they sin against You they are acting wickedly against their own souls, and wickedness gives itself the lie — either by corrupting and perverting the nature You created and ordered, or by an immoderate use of what is allowed, or by burning for what is not allowed, in a use that is against nature. Or they are found guilty of raging in heart and tongue against You, kicking against the goads; or of bursting out of the fence of human society and boldly delighting in self-willed factions and splits, according to whatever they have to gain or to take offence at. And all this is done when You are abandoned, fountain of life, the one true creator and governor of the universe, and when some single false thing is picked out of what You made and loved, out of self-willed pride. So it is by a humble devotion that we come back to You; and You cleanse us of our bad habits, and are merciful to the sins of those who confess, and hear the groaning of the prisoner, and loosen the chains we made for ourselves — as long as we do not raise against You the horns of an unreal liberty, losing everything through greed for more, by loving our own private good more than You, the good of all.

Among these offences of foulness and violence, and so many other wickednesses, there are the sins of men who are on the whole making progress; and those who judge rightly criticise these by the standard of perfection while commending the men themselves, in hope of future fruit, as one does with the green blade of growing corn. And there are things that look like offences of foulness or violence and are yet not sins at all, because they offend neither You, our Lord God, nor human society: when, for instance, things suitable to a particular time are obtained for the service of life, and we cannot tell whether it was out of an appetite for possessing; or when things are punished by a constituted authority for the sake of correction, and we cannot tell whether it was out of an appetite for hurting. So many an action disapproved of in men's sight is approved by Your testimony, and many that men praise are condemned with You as witness — because the look of the action, the mind of the man doing it, and the unknown needs of the moment all vary independently. But when You suddenly command something unaccustomed and unimagined, even something You once forbade, and keep the reason for Your command hidden for the time, and it goes against the rules of some human society — who doubts that it must be done, since the human society that is just is the one that serves You? But blessed are those who know Your commands! For everything Your servants did was either to show something needed at the time, or to foreshadow what was to come.

Being ignorant of all this, I mocked those holy servants and prophets of Yours. And what did I gain by mocking them, except to be mocked by You, drawn on imperceptibly, step by step, into such follies as believing that a fig tree wept when its fruit was picked, and that the mother tree shed milky tears? And yet if some Manichaean saint had eaten that fig — picked by somebody else's guilt and not his own — and mixed it with his insides, he would breathe angels out of it; indeed particles of divinity would burst out at every sigh and groan of his prayer, particles of the most high and true God, which would have stayed imprisoned in that fig if they had not been set free by the teeth and stomach of some "Elect" saint. And I, poor wretch, believed that more mercy was owed to the fruits of the earth than to the men they were created for. For if some hungry man who was not a Manichaean asked for a piece, the mouthful given to him would be as good as condemned to death.

And You reached down Your hand from above and pulled my soul out of that deep darkness, while my mother, Your faithful servant, wept to You for me more than mothers weep at the bodily deaths of their children. For by the faith and the spirit she had from You she could see the death I was lying in, and You heard her, Lord. You heard her and did not scorn her tears, when they streamed down and watered the ground under her eyes wherever she prayed. Yes, You heard her. For where else did that dream come from, by which You comforted her, so that she let me live with her and eat at the same table in the house — which she had begun to shrink from, hating and detesting the blasphemies of my error? She saw herself standing on a wooden measuring rule, and a shining young man coming towards her, cheerful and smiling at her, while she was grieving and overwhelmed with grief. He asked her the reasons for her grief and her daily tears — asking, as such messengers do, in order to teach rather than to be taught — and when she answered that she was mourning my ruin, he told her to be easy, and to look and see that where she was, I was too. And when she looked, she saw me standing beside her on the same rule. Where did this come from, except that Your ears were turned towards her heart? Good and all-powerful one, who care for every one of us as if You cared for him alone, and for all of us as if we were one!

And where did this come from too: that when she had told me the vision, and I tried to bend it to mean that she should not despair of one day being what I was, she replied at once, without any hesitation, "No. It was not said to me, 'Where he is, there you will be,' but 'Where you are, there he will be.'" I confess to You, Lord, that as far as I can remember — and I have often spoken of this — Your answer through my waking mother moved me even more at the time than the dream itself: that she was not thrown by the plausibility of my false reading, and saw so quickly what was there to be seen, which I certainly had not noticed before she spoke. The dream had signalled, so long in advance, a joy for that holy woman which was to be fulfilled so long afterwards, and was given to console her present anguish. For nearly nine years went by in which I wallowed in the mud of that deep pit and the darkness of falsehood, often trying to get up and being knocked down harder. All that time that chaste, devout, level-headed widow — the kind You love — now more cheerful with hope, but relaxing not at all in her weeping and mourning, never stopped lamenting my case to You at every hour of her prayers. And her prayers came into Your presence; and still You let me be tangled and re-tangled in that darkness.

Meanwhile You gave her another answer, which I remember; for I am passing over a great deal, hurrying on to the things that press me harder to confess to You, and much of it I do not remember. You gave her another answer, then, through a priest of Yours, a bishop brought up in Your church and well read in Your books. When this woman begged him to be so good as to talk to me, refute my errors, unteach me the bad things and teach me the good ones — which he was in the habit of doing when he found people fit to receive it — he refused, wisely, as I realised later. He answered that I was still unteachable, being puffed up with the novelty of that heresy, and had already tied several unskilled people in knots with trick questions, as she had told him. "Just leave him alone for a while," he said. "Only pray to God for him. He will find out by his own reading what that error is and how great its impiety." At the same time he told her how he himself, as a small boy, had been handed over to the Manichees by his own misled mother, and had not only read but frequently copied out almost all their books, and had seen — without anybody arguing him out of it — how much that sect was to be avoided, and had avoided it. When he had said this and she was still not satisfied but pressed him harder, with pleading and many tears, to see me and talk to me, he was a little annoyed at her persistence and said, "Go your way, and God bless you; it is not possible that the son of these tears should perish." And she took that answer, as she often mentioned when talking with me afterwards, as though it had sounded out of heaven.
