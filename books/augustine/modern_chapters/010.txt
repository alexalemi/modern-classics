Book Six: Ambrose, Alypius, and the Struggle to Believe

(Part 2 of 3)

I panted after honours, money, marriage — and You laughed at me. In these desires I went through the bitterest frustrations, and You were the kinder the less You allowed anything to grow sweet to me that was not You. Look at my heart, Lord, who wanted me to remember all this and confess it to You. Let my soul hold fast to You, now that You have pulled it free of the sticky birdlime of death. How wretched it was! And You kept irritating the feeling of its wound, so that it might leave everything else and turn to You, who are above everything and without whom nothing would exist at all — turn, and be healed.

How miserable I was then, and how You dealt with me to make me feel my misery, on the day when I was preparing to deliver a speech in praise of the emperor, in which I was going to tell a great many lies, and be applauded for lying by people who knew I was lying. My heart was panting with all these anxieties and boiling with the fever of thoughts that were eating me up. And going along one of the streets of Milan I noticed a poor beggar, with a full stomach, I suppose, joking and laughing. And I sighed, and spoke to the friends with me about the many sorrows of our own madness. For by all the efforts I was labouring at then, dragging the burden of my own wretchedness along under the goad of desire and making it heavier by dragging it, we were only hoping to arrive at the very cheerfulness that beggar had arrived at before us — and which we might never reach at all. What he had got with a few coins begged for, I was scheming after by many a laborious twist and turn: the joy of a temporary happiness. He certainly did not have the true joy — but with those ambitions of mine I was looking for one much less true. And he was certainly cheerful and I was anxious; he had no cares and I was full of fears. But if anyone asked me whether I would rather be cheerful or afraid, I would answer cheerful. And if he asked whether I would rather be what that man was or what I then was, I would choose to be myself, worn out with cares and fears — and that would be a bad judgement. For was it the truth? I ought not to have preferred myself to him on the grounds of being better educated, since I got no joy from my education but used it to please people; and not to instruct them, simply to please them. That is why You broke my bones with the rod of Your correction.

So away from my soul with those who say to her, "It makes a difference what a man's joy comes from. That beggar's joy came from drink; you wanted joy from glory." What glory, Lord? One that is not in You. For just as his was no true joy, so that was no true glory, and it overturned my soul the more. He would sleep off his drunkenness that same night; I had slept and got up with mine, and was going to sleep and get up with it again, for how many days You know, God. Yes, it does make a difference what a man's joy comes from; I know it, and the joy of a faithful hope lies incomparably beyond such emptiness. And in fact even then he was beyond me: he was genuinely the happier man, and not only because he was thoroughly soaked in cheerfulness while I was gutted with anxieties, but because he had got his wine by wishing people well, while I was looking for empty, swollen praise by lying. I said a good deal to this effect to my friends then, and I often noticed in them how things stood with me; and I found things were going badly with me, and grieved over it, and so doubled the trouble. And if any success smiled on me, I could not be bothered to snatch at it, since it flew away almost before I could get hold of it.

These were the things we lamented together, we who were living as friends; but I spoke about them mostly and most intimately with Alypius and Nebridius. Alypius was born in my own town, of leading people there, and was younger than I was. He had studied under me both when I first lectured in our town and afterwards at Carthage, and he loved me very much because I struck him as kind and learned, and I loved him for his great promise in virtue, which was conspicuous enough in one so young. But the whirlpool of Carthaginian habits, where those idle entertainments are hotly followed, had drawn him into the madness of the racetrack. And while he was being wretchedly tossed about in it, and I, teaching rhetoric there, had a public school, he was not yet attending my classes, because of some ill feeling that had arisen between his father and me. So I had found out how fatally besotted he was with the races, and was deeply grieved that he looked likely to throw away — or had already thrown away — such great promise; and yet I had no way of advising him, or of reclaiming him by some kind of pressure, either through a friend's kindness or a teacher's authority. For I assumed he thought of me as his father did. But he did not; and putting aside his father's attitude in the matter, he began to greet me, and to come into my lecture room now and then, listen a little, and go.

But I had forgotten to work on him, so that he should not ruin so good a mind through a blind, headlong appetite for empty amusements. But You, Lord, who steer the course of everything You have created, had not forgotten him, who was one day to be among Your children, a priest and a minister of Your sacrament. And so that his reform might plainly be credited to You, You brought it about through me, without my knowing it. For one day, as I sat in my usual place with my pupils in front of me, he came in, greeted me, sat down and set his mind on what I was discussing. I happened to have a passage in hand, and while I was explaining it a comparison with the chariot races occurred to me as likely to make what I wanted to convey pleasanter and plainer, seasoned with a biting mockery of those the madness had enslaved. God, You know that I was not thinking then of curing Alypius of that infection. But he took the whole thing to himself and thought I had said it purely for his sake. And where another man would have taken it as grounds for offence at me, that right-minded young man took it as grounds for being offended at himself, and for loving me more fiercely. For You had said it long ago, and put it in Your book: Rebuke a wise man and he will love you. But I had not rebuked him. You had — You who use everyone, knowing and unknowing, in the order You Yourself know, and that order is just. You made burning coals out of my heart and my tongue, to set light to the hopeful mind that was wasting away, and cure it. Let anyone keep quiet in Your praises who does not consider Your mercies, which confess to You out of the depths of my soul. For at that speech he burst out of the pit he had deliberately plunged into, and been blinded in by its wretched amusements; and he shook his mind free by a strong act of self-command, and all the filth of the racetrack flew off him, and he never went there again. After this he prevailed on his unwilling father to let him be my pupil. His father gave way and gave in. And Alypius, beginning to attend me again, was caught up in the same superstition I was, loving in the Manichees that show of self-restraint which he took to be genuine — whereas it was a senseless and seducing restraint, trapping precious souls that could not yet reach the depths of virtue and were easily taken in by the surface of a shadowy and counterfeit one.

Without giving up the secular career his parents had charmed him into, he had gone ahead of me to Rome to study law; and there he was carried away by an incredible eagerness for the gladiator shows, incredibly. He had been utterly opposed to such spectacles and detested them. Then one day he happened to be met by a number of his acquaintances and fellow students coming from dinner, and with the familiar violence of friends they hauled him — furiously refusing and resisting — into the amphitheatre, during those cruel and deadly shows. He protested as they went: "You can drag my body to that place and sit me down in it, but can you make me turn my mind or my eyes to those shows? I shall be absent while present, and so I shall beat you and them together." Hearing this, they took him along all the same, perhaps wanting to test that very thing, whether he could do what he said. When they got there and had taken whatever seats they could, the whole place was ablaze with that savage entertainment. But he shut the gateway of his eyes and forbade his mind to go out after such evil — and if only he had stopped his ears too! For in the fighting, when one man fell, the enormous roar of the whole crowd struck him hard; and overcome by curiosity, and as though prepared to see whatever it was and despise it and rise above it, he opened his eyes. And he was struck with a deeper wound in his soul than the man he had wanted to look at was struck in his body; and he fell more wretchedly than the man whose fall had raised the great shout that came in through his ears and unlocked his eyes, opening the way for a soul to be struck and beaten down — a soul that was bold rather than resolute, and all the weaker for having relied on itself, when it should have relied on You. For as soon as he saw the blood he drank in savagery along with it; and he did not turn away, but fixed his gaze and swallowed the frenzy without knowing it, and took delight in that guilty fight and was drunk on the bloody entertainment. He was no longer the man who had come in, but one of the crowd he had come into — a true companion of the men who had brought him. Why say more? He watched, he shouted, he caught fire, he carried away with him a madness that would goad him to come back, not only with the friends who first dragged him there but ahead of them, and dragging others in. And yet You pulled him out of it with the strongest and most merciful hand, and taught him to have confidence not in himself but in You. But that came later.

Even now, though, it was being stored up in his memory as a medicine for later. So was this. When he was still studying under me at Carthage, and was going over at midday in the marketplace what he had to recite by heart, as students do for practice, You allowed him to be arrested by the market officers as a thief. I imagine, our God, that You allowed it for no other reason than that a man who was going to turn out so great should begin to learn already that in judging cases a man must not be condemned by another man out of rash credulity. For as he was walking up and down by himself in front of the court, with his notebook and pen, a young man, a lawyer, the real thief, secretly brought a hatchet in, got as far as the lead gratings that fence in the silversmiths' shops without Alypius noticing him, and began to cut away the lead. But the noise of the hatchet was heard, and the silversmiths below began to stir and sent men to arrest whoever they found. Hearing their voices, he ran off, leaving his hatchet behind, afraid of being caught with it. Alypius, who had not seen him come in, was aware of him going, and saw how fast he made off. Wanting to know what the matter was, he went into the place — and there he found the hatchet, and was standing over it, wondering and examining it, when the men who had been sent found him alone with the hatchet in his hand, the noise of which had startled them and brought them there. They seized him and hauled him away, and gathering the people of the marketplace round them, boasted of having caught a notorious thief; and so he was taken off to be brought before the judge.

But this was as far as Alypius's lesson was to go. For at once, Lord, You came to the help of his innocence, of which You alone were the witness. As he was being led off either to prison or to punishment, they were met by an architect who had the chief responsibility for the public buildings. They were especially glad to run into him, since he was the man who usually suspected them of stealing the goods that went missing from the marketplace; now they could show him at last who was committing these thefts. But he had several times seen Alypius at the house of a certain senator whom he often went to pay his respects to; and recognising him at once, he took him aside by the hand, asked what the cause of such a disaster was, heard the whole story, and told everyone there, amid a great deal of uproar and threatening, to come with him. So they came to the house of the young man who had done it. In front of the door was a boy young enough to be likely to give the whole thing away, not realising it could harm his master; and he had gone with his master to the marketplace. As soon as Alypius remembered him he told the architect, who showed the boy the hatchet and asked whose it was. "Ours," he said at once — and being questioned further, he revealed everything. So the crime was transferred to that house, and the crowd who had begun to jeer at Alypius were put to shame; and the man who was to be a minister of Your word, and to examine many cases in Your church, went away with more experience and better instructed.

So I had found him at Rome, and he clung to me by the strongest of ties and went with me to Milan, both so as not to leave me and so as to practise something of the law he had studied — more to please his parents than himself. There he had sat three times as an assessor, with an incorruptibility that others found astonishing, while he found it more astonishing that anyone could prefer gold to honesty. His character was tested besides not only with the bait of greed but with the goad of fear. At Rome he was assessor to the count of the Italian treasury. There was at that time a very powerful senator to whom many people owed favours and many others owed fear. He wanted, by his usual authority, to have something allowed him that the laws did not allow. Alypius resisted. A bribe was offered; he despised it with his whole heart. Threats were made; he trampled on them. Everyone was amazed at so unusual a spirit, which neither wanted the friendship nor feared the enmity of a man so great and so hugely famous for his countless ways of doing good or harm. And the judge himself, whose adviser Alypius was, although he too was unwilling that it should be allowed, would not openly refuse, but put the matter off onto Alypius, claiming that Alypius would not let him do it — when in truth if the judge had done it, Alypius would have left. On one point connected with his studies he was nearly seduced: that he might have books copied for him at official rates. But consulting justice, he changed his mind for the better, reckoning the fairness that stopped him more profitable than the power that would have allowed it. These are small things; but he that is faithful in little is faithful also in much. And nothing can be empty that has come out of the mouth of Your truth: If you have not been faithful in the unrighteous Mammon, who will trust you with true riches? And if you have not been faithful in what belongs to another man, who shall give you what is your own? Being such a man, he was at that time attached to me, and wavered along with me about what course of life was to be taken.
