LE MORTE D'ARTHUR — MODERNIZATION STRATEGY
Sir Thomas Malory, finished 1469-70; printed by William Caxton, 1485
Source: Standard Ebooks, after A. W. Pollard's 1900 text (Caxton in
modernised spelling, his omissions supplied from Wynkyn de Worde's 1498)

This is a scholarly modernization of a canonical fifteenth-century English
text. The source is ALREADY ENGLISH, and its spelling has already been
modernised by Pollard. What remains is the grammar, the vocabulary and,
above all, the false friends. The precedent is bunyan/, not quixote/.


1. WHAT THIS BOOK IS

Malory was a Warwickshire knight who wrote the book in prison (he says so,
twice) at the end of the Wars of the Roses, out of "certain books of
French" and two English poems, and died before it was printed. Caxton
found the manuscript, divided it into twenty-one Books and 504 chapters
(his preface says 507; see section 9), wrote a one-line rubric for every
chapter, and gave it the title of its last section. It is the book through
which English has known King Arthur ever since: Tennyson, T. H. White,
Steinbeck and every film come out of it.

It is not a novel. It is a cycle: one long arc (Arthur's begetting, his
kingdom, the Round Table at its height, the Grail quest that empties it,
the love of Lancelot and Guinevere that splits it, the war that ends it)
carrying dozens of knights' adventures that interlace, break off ("Now
leave we Sir Tristram, and turn we unto Sir Lancelot") and resume. The
nearest precedents in this project are the story cycles: decameron/ and
nights/ for the interlace, quixote/ for the scale.

Malory's own eight "tales", which Caxton's Books cut across:
  Books I-IV      The tale of King Arthur (his rise; Balin; the wedding
                  and the Round Table; Merlin's end; Morgan's plot)
  Book V          Arthur and the Emperor Lucius (the war with Rome)
  Book VI         Sir Lancelot du Lake
  Book VII        Sir Gareth (Beaumains)
  Books VIII-XII  Sir Tristram de Liones (with La Cote Male Taile,
                  Lamorak, Palomides, Dinadan; XI-XII also carry
                  Galahad's begetting and Lancelot's madness)
  Books XIII-XVII The Quest of the Holy Grail
  Books XVIII-XIX Lancelot and Guinevere (the poisoned apple, the Fair
                  Maid of Astolat, Meliagaunce, the healing of Sir Urry)
  Books XX-XXI    The Death of Arthur

Word counts (body text, excluding rubrics): I 17.3k, II 10.3k, III 8.0k,
IV 17.0k, V 9.0k, VI 12.2k, VII 24.5k, VIII 26.0k, IX 29.5k, X 62.9k,
XI 8.9k, XII 7.6k, XIII 12.0k, XIV 5.4k, XV 3.3k, XVI 9.6k, XVII 14.9k,
XVIII 19.1k, XIX 9.7k, XX 17.0k, XXI 9.5k. Total 333,882; with the
504 rubrics and Caxton's preface the source files hold ~346k.


2. WHY IT NEEDS A MODERNIZATION, AND WHAT KIND

Malory is not HARD in the Hobbes or Gibbon way. His sentences are short
and his syntax is the simplest in English literature: "and then... and
so... and there..." There is nothing to unstack. The ROADMAP measured his
archaism at 15.8 per 1,000, and that is the visible half: in the body
text, thou 1,097, thee 623, thy 387, ye 2,871, hath 313, art 138,
hast 127, anon 371, wit (as in "wit ye well") 494, hight 111, wist 158.
A reader trips on these and knows it.

The invisible half is the case for this volume, exactly as in bunyan/:
FALSE FRIENDS. "Worship" (292x, plus worshipful/worshipfully 67x) means
HONOUR AND RENOWN, not adoration, and a reader who meets "he won great
worship" reads religion into a tournament. "Wood" means MAD. "Lusty"
means VIGOROUS. "Gentle" means WELL-BORN. "Villain" means a PEASANT.
"Pavilion" is a TENT. "Harness" is ARMOUR. "Quick" means ALIVE. "Let"
usually means CAUSED ("let make a tomb" = had a tomb made) and sometimes
HINDER. "Sad" means STEADFAST. "Cheer" is a face, a mood, or hospitality.
"Or" means BEFORE. "An" means IF. The full list is section 5. A reader
walks past every one of these confidently wrong, and that is the reader
this edition is for.

And there is a third obstacle peculiar to Malory: THERE IS NO QUOTATION
PUNCTUATION AT ALL. Speech is run into narration ("Well, said Merlin, I
know whom thou seekest"), whole exchanges sit inside one paragraph, and
Malory slides from reported into direct speech in mid-sentence. Modern
punctuation is one of the things CLAUDE.md says to modernize, and here it
is the single largest gain in readability. See section 6.


3. THE VOICE: PLAINNESS AND PACE ARE THE STYLE

Malory's signature is the paratactic, fast, flat sentence. Deaths take one
clause. Marvels are reported in the same tone as breakfast. The emotion
comes from the plainness: "Then there was weeping and dolour out of
measure." The great passages (the passing of Arthur, Ector's lament,
Lancelot's last sight of Guinevere) are great because nothing is dressed
up. The modern version must KEEP THAT. Concretely:

- KEEP the "and"-chains and "then... and so..." linkage. Do not
  subordinate them into "After X had done Y, Z, who was..." periods. A
  run of short clauses joined by "and" is the voice, and it is perfectly
  readable modern English. You MAY break a very long "and"-chain into two
  or three sentences where a modern reader would lose the thread; start
  the next one with "And" or "Then" or "So" as Malory does.
- DO NOT ADD: no adjectives he did not write, no interior feeling he did
  not state, no scene-setting, no motives. When Malory says "and so he
  died", we say that.
- DO NOT INTENSIFY. "Smote off his head" is "struck off his head", not a
  spray of blood. Fights are reported in his formulae (they "lashed
  together", "traced and traversed", "hurtled together like two boars")
  and the formulae stay, modernized but recognisable, every time.
- KEEP HIS NARRATOR. "As the French book saith", "as the book telleth",
  "now leave we X and turn we to Y", "here endeth...", "to make short
  tale" are Malory talking to us, and they stay as real sentences:
  "as the French book says", "Now we leave Sir Tristram and turn to Sir
  Lancelot", "to cut a long story short". The interlace formula is the
  book's structure, not a tic (the bunyan dream-frame rule).
- REGISTER: plain, a little formal in direct address between knights
  ("Fair knight", "Sir", "my lord"), never slangy. Contractions are fine
  in dialogue and in brisk narration; avoid modern idioms that date or
  jar ("okay", "guy", "no way"). The target reads like a good modern
  translation of a medieval romance: clean, quick, dignified.
- PACE: a 600-word Caxton chapter should still read in two minutes.

Two registers to keep distinct:
  (a) the adventure books (I-XII): brisk, often funny (Dinadan is a real
      comic character; Kay is a running joke; Beaumains' damsel insults
      him for a whole Book) — play the comedy straight, as in bunyan;
  (b) the Grail books (XIII-XVII): slower, allegorical, full of hermits
      explaining dreams. The hermits' explanations are long and they stay
      long; this is the book's theology and it is not padding.
And the last four Books (XVIII-XXI) are tragedy, in the same plain
voice, which is why they work.

stay as name-parts. "Sir" and "Dame" stay.

SAME-NAME TRAPS, which will produce fluent wrong prose if misread:
  - TWO ECTORS. Sir Ector, Arthur's foster-father and Kay's father
    (Book I), and Sir Ector de Maris, Lancelot's half-brother (Book VI
    on, and the speaker of the great lament in XXI.13). Never confuse
    them; keep "de Maris" wherever Malory has it.
  - SEVERAL ELAINES. Arthur's half-sister (I.2); Elaine, daughter of
    King Pelles, mother of Galahad (XI-XII); Elaine le Blank, the Fair
    Maid of Astolat (XVIII). Malory distinguishes them by context;
    don't merge them.
  - TWO ISOLDES: Isolde the Fair (Mark's queen) and Isolde of the White
    Hands (Tristram's wife in Brittany).
  - "the Lady of the Lake" is several women over the book (one is killed
    by Balin in II.3; Nimue later holds the role). Follow the source.
  - Gawain's brothers are Agravain, Gaheris, Gareth, and their half-
    brother Mordred; Lot and Morgause are their parents. Their feud with
    Pellinore's family (Lamorak, Percival, Tor) runs the whole book.


5. FALSE FRIENDS AND ARCHAISMS — THE WORD LIST

THE FALSE FRIENDS (a modern reader will NOT know to stop):
  worship (292)      honour, renown, glory ("win worship" = win honour);
                     NEVER religious unless the context is God
  worshipful(ly)     honourable, honourably, renowned
  disworship         dishonour, disgrace
  wood (79)          mad, wild with rage ("wood wroth" = mad with rage)
  lusty              vigorous, lively, eager; of May, "joyful". Not
                     sexual. ("a lusty knight" = a fine vigorous knight)
  gentle / gentleman well-born, noble; a gentleman is a man of rank
  villain            a peasant, a low-born man; a churl. Insult by
                     class, not by crime
  pavilion (66)      tent
  harness (88)       armour
  quick              alive ("quick and dead")
  let (460, mostly)  CAUSED, had done ("let make a cry" = had a
                     proclamation made); occasionally HINDER ("without
                     let"); never "allow" unless it means allow
  sad                steadfast, sober, serious
  cheer (120)        expression, mood; or welcome, hospitality ("made
                     him great cheer" = gave him a warm welcome)
  adventure (183)    a chance happening, a marvel met with on a quest;
                     "by adventure" = by chance
  deliver            free, release; as adjective, nimble, agile
  dress (135)        prepare, set in order, turn ("dressed him to Sir
                     Tristram" = squared up to / turned toward)
  press (27)         the thick of the crowd or of the fighting
  rescue             as now, but also "rescue" as noun = a relieving force
  mischief           misfortune, harm, distress (stronger than now)
  kind / kindly      nature / natural, by nature
  noise              rumour, report, fame
  suffer             allow, permit; endure
  meat               food, a meal
  presently          (rare here) at once
  fellowship (183)   a company of knights; the Round Table as a body
  avoid              leave, go away from
  stint              stop
  nice               (rare) foolish
  courage            heart, spirit, desire
  avail              profit, benefit
  array             condition, state, clothing
  debonair          gentle, gracious, kindly
  jealous           as now, but also zealous, anxious
  recreant          a knight who yields and begs for his life: the
                    deepest disgrace; "recreant" may stay as a term if
                    the sentence makes it plain ("yield, recreant" ->
                    "yield and admit yourself beaten"; keep the shame)
  paramour / leman  lover; carries no necessary sneer in Malory
  wit (494)         to know; "wit ye well" = "believe me", "you may be
                    sure", "know this"; never "wit" as humour
  courteous         courtly, refined in manners; ok as is

THE PLAIN ARCHAISMS (ordinary friction; they go):
  thou/thee/thy/thine/ye -> you, your (see section 6 on thou)
  hath, doth, art, hast, -eth, -est -> modern forms
  anon -> at once, immediately     hight -> was called / was named
  wist / wot -> knew / know        or / or ever -> before
  an / and (= if) -> if            but if -> unless
  fain -> gladly                   lief / liefer -> willing / rather
  wroth -> angry                   ware -> aware
  espied -> saw, spotted, noticed  withal / therewithal -> with that
  nigh -> near                     yede -> went
  mickle -> much                   certes -> certainly
  sith -> since                    ere -> before
  smote -> struck                  brast -> burst
  gat -> got / fathered (a child)  brake -> broke
  slew -> slew or killed (both are modern; "slew" suits combat)
  rede -> advice                   bourder -> jester
  truage -> tribute                brachet -> hound, little hound
  paynim -> pagan                  passing (263) -> exceedingly, very
  divers -> various, several       dole / dolour -> grief, sorrow
  stour -> fight                   foin -> thrust
  sore -> badly, severely, hard ("sore wounded" = badly wounded)
  hurtle -> crash, collide         bushment -> ambush
  siege (seat) -> seat (but "the Siege Perilous" stays)
  me list / me lust -> I wish, I care to
  do me to wit -> let me know      ado -> trouble; "had ado" = fought
  Jesu -> Jesus                    Mary / Marry -> keep as oaths if clear

ARMS AND CHIVALRY: keep the real technical words a fourteen-year-old can
picture: joust, tournament, lance/spear, shield, helm (or helmet),
hauberk (mail shirt; gloss once), sword, squire, page, dwarf, damsel,
quest, siege (of a castle). "Overthrew him" = knocked him off his horse;
say so where it isn't obvious. "Horse and man" stays. "Avoided his
horse" = got clear of his horse. "Rashed off his helm" = tore off.

SE's endnotes and glossary (reference/notes.txt, reference/glossary.txt)
give Pollard's glosses of rarer words, by file. They are a crib.


6. PUNCTUATION AND PARAGRAPHING — MODERNIZED

- QUOTATION MARKS: every piece of direct speech gets straight double
  quotes, with the speech tag set off in modern style:
     Well, said Merlin, I know whom thou seekest
  -> "Well," said Merlin, "I know who you're looking for."
- MALORY SLIDES FROM REPORTED TO DIRECT SPEECH mid-sentence ("he
  promised her that he would meet with Sir Dinadan... And there shall I
  make ready for you"). Open the quote where the speaker's own words
  begin, adding a short tag ("he said") only where the shift would
  otherwise confuse. Do not convert his reported speech into direct
  speech, or the reverse; follow him.
- PARAGRAPHS: a new paragraph for each new speaker in a dialogue
  exchange, as in any modern novel. This is the one place the paragraph
  count should go UP. Malory's long narrative paragraphs may otherwise
  stay as they are.
- Letters, inscriptions and epitaphs: inside quotes, in the sentence.
  Latin is kept where it is famous and translated in the same sentence
  (see section 8). Tab indentation is NOT used (there is no verse in
  the book), and nothing is ever set in all capitals.

THOU AND YOU. Malory's knights switch between thou (to an inferior, an
intimate, or an enemy being insulted) and ye (to an equal or superior).
Modern English cannot do this with pronouns, and "thou" in a modern
sentence is costume. Render all of it as "you" and carry the social
temperature in the WORDS: a knight who drops to "thou" to insult another
gets a curt, contemptuous sentence ("Defend yourself, traitor!"), not a
polite one. The Pusey/Augustine reasoning (whose-archaism-is-it) does not
apply here because the archaism IS the author's; it goes for the
ordinary reason, that it now reads as fancy dress.

OATHS: "by my head", "by the faith of my body", "Jesu defend me", "so
God me help", "Mary" stay as oaths in modern dress ("God help me",
"Jesus forbid", "on my life").


7. SENSITIVE CONTENT — MEASURED REGISTER, NEITHER SANITIZED NOR
   SENSATIONALIZED

Malory is a fifteenth-century knight writing about knights. The book is
full of killing, and its sexual plots turn on deception and on women
treated as property. This edition renders all of it as MALORY'S account,
in his own flat register, without editorial comment and without
softening (the Verne rule: we do not agree with him and we do not correct
him; a note is the wink the rule forbids). Specific cases:

- THE BEGETTING OF ARTHUR (I.1-2). Uther wants another man's wife; she
  refuses him and flees; he makes war on her husband; Merlin disguises
  Uther as the husband, who is killed that same night; Uther lies with
  Igraine, who believes he is her husband. Render exactly that, in
  Malory's plain terms ("lay with", "slept with"); Igraine's refusal,
  her flight and her private grief ("she mourned privily and held her
  peace") are all in the source and all stay. No modern labelling,
  no euphemism that hides the deception.
- THE BEGETTING OF MORDRED (I.19-20). Arthur sleeps with Morgause, Lot's
  wife, not knowing she is his half-sister; Merlin then tells him the
  child will destroy him. Same register. Then THE MAY-DAY CHILDREN
  (I.27): Arthur has every child born on May Day put to sea, and the
  ship is wrecked. Malory reports it in two sentences and does not
  defend it. Neither do we.
- Galahad's begetting (XI.2-3): Elaine and her nurse use an enchanted
  cup to make Lancelot believe she is Guinevere. Same treatment as
  Uther's deception, mirrored; render it as plainly.
- Lancelot and Guinevere. MALORY IS RETICENT and his reticence is a
  famous choice ("whether they were abed or at other manner of
  disports, me list not hereof make no mention, for love that time was
  not as is now-a-days", XX.3). Keep the reticence. Never make the
  affair more explicit than he does.
- WOMEN AS PRIZES. Damsels are won, given, wagered and sometimes killed
  (Gawain beheads a lady by accident, III.7; Pellinore ignores a lady's
  cry and finds her dead, III.12-15; a castle's custom bleeds maidens,
  II.13). Malory often judges these (the oath of the Round Table, III.15,
  is partly written in response). Render the deeds and the judgements
  both, as he gives them.
- VIOLENCE. Report in his formulae, flat. No gore he did not write; no
  softening of what he did ("clave his head to the teeth" stays as
  "split his head to the teeth"). Executions by burning (Guinevere,
  twice) are threatened in the plainest terms; keep them so.
- SARACENS, PAYNIMS, TURKS. "Saracen" is kept: it is Palomides' identity
  and a real medieval word with a real referent. "Paynim" -> "pagan".
  "Miscreants" (XXI.13, of the Turks) -> "unbelievers": the word now
  means "wrongdoers" and would give a false meaning. The crusading
  ending (Bors, Ector and the others die fighting in the Holy Land "upon
  a Good Friday for God's sake") stays exactly as Malory gives it.
- CLASS CONTEMPT ("villain", "churl", the old saw in X.61, "whoso be
  ruled by a villain born... the same villain shall destroy all the
  gentlemen about him"): Malory's subject is a knightly class talking
  about itself. Keep it; it is the author's argument.
- SUICIDE (II.6, II.17 and others): reported plainly, as in the source.

The test throughout is the project's: does the reader LOSE information,
or GAIN a false one? (sensitive-content-tests). Here the answer is
almost always to keep; ask the orchestrator before cutting anything.


8. FAMOUS PASSAGES — LOCKED WORDING

These must survive near-verbatim. The exact strings checked by verify.py
are in must_contain.txt; the full locked renderings are in
running_notes.txt and must be used as given.

  I.5    The sword in the stone. The inscription in gold letters: "Whoever
         pulls this sword out of this stone and anvil is the true-born
         king of all England." (Caxton: "Whoso pulleth out this sword of

  I.25   Excalibur and the Lady of the Lake ("an arm clothed in white
         samite, that held a fair sword in that hand").
  XVIII.25  "The month of May": Malory's essay on true love; it ends,

         therefore she had a good end."
  XXI.5  The passing of Arthur: Bedivere twice hides Excalibur and
         lies; the third time "there came an arm and a hand above the

         trust left in me to trust in, for I will go into the vale of
         Avalon to be healed of my grievous wound."
  XXI.7  "Hic jacet Arthurus, rex quondam, rexque futurus": KEEP THE
         LATIN, then English in the same sentence: "Here lies Arthur,
         the once and future king." (Pollard spells "jacet"; keep that.)
  XXI.13 Sir Ector de Maris' lament over Lancelot ("thou were the
         courteoust knight that ever bare shield..."), locked in full in
         running_notes.txt. The anaphora ("And you were... that
         ever...") is the passage; keep every clause, in order.
  XXI.13 Malory's prayer: "pray for me while I am alive, that God send
         me good deliverance, and when I am dead, I pray you all pray for
         my soul."
  000    Caxton: "Do after the good and leave the evil" -> "Follow the
         good and leave the evil, and it will bring you to good fame and
         renown."

Name-phrases that are part of the language and must appear: the sword in
the stone (the phrase need not be Malory's, but the scene must), the

9. STRUCTURE, HEADINGS, AND WHAT CAXTON DID

- Caxton's rubrics are part of the work (since 1485 they have been the
  book's table of contents) and Caxton's preface explicitly claims them.
  They are rendered as the chapter headings, modernized in the same
  voice, in SENTENCE CASE, keeping his "How X did Y" shape:
     Caxton: How Uther Pendragon made war on the duke of Cornwall, and
             how by the mean of Merlin he lay by the duchess and gat
             Arthur.
     Ours:   Chapter 2: How Uther Pendragon made war on the Duke of
             Cornwall, and how through Merlin he lay with the duchess and
             fathered Arthur
  All 504 are written in titles.json in advance and given to agents
  verbatim. Agents never invent or "improve" a heading.
- Each Book opens with a divider ("Book Seven: Sir Gareth, Whom Sir Kay
  Called Beaumains") drawn from Caxton's own summary of the Books in
  his preface. The divider comes from the manifest; agents NEVER write
  it into a file.
- Caxton's "Explicit liber primus" lines at Book ends are translated:
  "Here ends the first book." / "Here ends the eighteenth book, and here
  follows the nineteenth."
- Caxton's preface says 507 chapters; there are 504. Three of his final
  chapter numbers were wrong: Book IV skips XX (Pollard's note: "Misnumbered
  XX by Caxton"), Book VII misnumbers XXV as XXVI ("setting the numeration
  wrong to the end of the book"), and Book I's XXVIII is the preface's
  slip (Caxton printed 27). Pollard renumbered; we follow Pollard.
  check.py asserts this exactly.
- The book's final chapter (XXI.13) holds Malory's own explicit and
  prayer and then Caxton's colophon. Both are translated, both stay in
  that file, in that order.


10. RATIO AND COMPLETENESS

EN->EN modernization runs close to 1:1. Bunyan landed 1.03, Grimm 0.99,
Burton's Nights 1.04. Malory's archaisms are mostly the same length as
their replacements ("anon" -> "at once" adds a word; "wit ye well that"
-> "believe me," loses one), and quotation marks add nothing to the word
count. Expect 0.95-1.10 per file; run verify with
    python3 verify.py malory --min-ratio 0.85 --max-ratio 1.3
Under 0.92 in a single file means something was summarised; read it.
Because the files are short (median ~600 words), one dropped sentence
moves the ratio a visible amount: that is a feature.

THE WORST FAILURE MODE HERE IS NOT SUMMARY BUT DEDUPLICATION. Malory
repeats himself on purpose: tournament after tournament, the same
formulae ("and there he smote down Sir X, and Sir Y, and Sir Z"), lists
of thirty knights by name. Every list stays complete, every repeated
formula stays repeated. The lists of names are the book's roll of honour.
