Chapter 4: How Sir Percival saw many men-at-arms carrying a dead knight, and how he fought against them

"When the king had made his prayers in this way, he heard a voice that said, 'Your prayers are heard, for you shall not die until he has kissed you. And when that knight comes, the clearness of your eyes will come back, and you will see plainly, and your wounds will be healed, and before then they shall never close.' And this happened to King Evelake, and this same king has lived this holy life these three hundred years, and men say the knight who will heal him is at the court. Sir," said the good man, "I beg you, tell me what knight you are, and whether you are of King Arthur's court and of the Round Table."

"Yes, truly," he said, "and my name is Sir Percival de Galis."

And when the good man understood his name he rejoiced greatly over him.

And then Sir Percival left and rode until the hour of noon. And in a valley he met some twenty men-at-arms, who were carrying on a bier a knight who had been killed. And when they saw Sir Percival they asked him where he came from.

And he answered, "From the court of King Arthur."

Then they all cried out at once, "Kill him!"

Then Sir Percival struck the first to the ground, with his horse on top of him. And then seven of the knights struck on his shield all at once, and the rest killed his horse so that he fell to the ground. So they would have killed him or taken him, if the good knight Sir Galahad, with the red arms, had not come into those parts by chance. And when he saw all those knights against one knight he cried, "Spare me that knight's life!" And then he turned toward the twenty men-at-arms as fast as his horse could carry him, with his spear in rest, and struck the foremost to the ground, horse and man. And when his spear was broken he set his hand to his sword, and struck on the right hand and on the left so that it was a marvel to see, and at every stroke he struck one down or put him to shame, so that they would fight no more but fled into a thick forest, and Sir Galahad followed them.

And when Sir Percival saw him chase them like that, he grieved greatly that his horse was gone. And then he knew well that it was Sir Galahad.

And then he cried aloud, "Ah, fair knight, wait, and let me give you thanks, for you have done a great deal for me."

But Sir Galahad rode on so fast that at last he passed out of his sight. And Sir Percival went after him on foot as fast as he could, crying out. And then he met a yeoman riding on a small riding horse, who led in his hand a great steed blacker than any bear.

"Ah, fair friend," said Sir Percival, "as surely as I will ever do anything for you, and be your true knight at the first need you ask of me, lend me that black steed, so that I may overtake a knight who is riding ahead of me."

"Sir knight," said the yeoman, "I beg you to excuse me from that, for I cannot do it. For believe me, the horse belongs to such a man that if I lent it to you or to any man, he would kill me."

"Alas," said Sir Percival, "I never had so great a sorrow as I have had at losing that knight."

"Sir," said the yeoman, "I am very sorry for you, for a good horse would suit you well; but I dare not give you this horse unless you take him from me."

"That I will not do," said Sir Percival.

And so they parted; and Sir Percival sat down under a tree, and grieved beyond measure. And as he sat there, a knight came riding on the horse that the yeoman had been leading, and he was fully armed.
