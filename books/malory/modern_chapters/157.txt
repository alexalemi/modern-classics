Chapter 3: How Sir Tristram was sent to France with a tutor named Gouvernail, and how he learned to play the harp, to hawk, and to hunt

And then he appointed a gentleman who was well learned and well taught, and his name was Gouvernail; and then he sent young Tristram with Gouvernail into France, to learn the language, and good manners, and deeds of arms. And Tristram was there more than seven years. And then, when he could speak the language well, and had learned all that he could learn in that country, he came home again to his father, King Meliodas. And so Tristram learned to be a harper beyond all others, so that there was none like him in any country, and so in his youth he set himself to learn the harp and instruments of music.

And afterwards, as he grew in might and strength, he worked always at hunting and hawking, more than any gentleman we ever heard read of. And as the book says, he devised the proper horn calls for the beasts of the hunt (the great game of venery, the lesser beasts of the chase, and every kind of vermin), and all these terms of hawking and hunting we still have. And that is why the book of hunting and hawking is called the book of Sir Tristram. So, it seems to me, all gentlemen who bear ancient coats of arms ought by rights to honour Sir Tristram for the fine terms that gentlemen have and use, and will use until the Day of Judgement, so that by them, in a way, all men of honour can tell a gentleman from a yeoman, and a yeoman from a peasant. For the man who is well-born will be drawn to well-born ways, and will follow the customs of noble gentlemen.

So Sir Tristram lived in Cornwall until he was big and strong, eighteen years old. And then King Meliodas had great joy of Sir Tristram, and so did the queen, his wife. For ever after, all her life, because Sir Tristram had saved her from the fire, she never hated him again, but loved him always, and gave Tristram many great gifts; for people of every rank loved him, wherever he went.
