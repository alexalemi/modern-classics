Thomas Bulfinch (1796–1867) was a Boston man, the son of the architect Charles Bulfinch, and a graduate of Harvard. He spent most of his working life as a clerk in a Boston bank, and spent his evenings on the classics. The Age of Fable, published in 1855, was the result, and he dedicated it to Longfellow. He followed it with The Age of Chivalry in 1858 and Legends of Charlemagne in 1863. After his death the three were bound together as Bulfinch's Mythology, and for a century that was how most English-speaking readers first met the Greek and Roman myths.

His preface says exactly what he meant the book to do. It was not for scholars. It was for a reader of English poetry who kept meeting Niobe, the Parcae and the judgment of Paris in Milton and Byron and did not know who they were. So he retold the stories, mostly from Ovid and Virgil, in plain prose, and after almost every one he quoted the English poets who had used it, from Spenser to Longfellow. The quotations are the point of the book: each myth is followed into the poetry that borrowed it. The later chapters go beyond Greece and Rome, to Egyptian, Hindu and Norse mythology and to the Druids, mostly from the scholarship of his day.

Three things will seem strange now. The gods have their Roman names, Jupiter and Juno and Minerva, because those were the names the English poets used; the first chapter gives the Greek names beside them. Bulfinch also says plainly that he left out stories and parts of stories "offensive to pure taste and good morals," so a good deal of Ovid is missing or quietly softened. And the chapters on the East describe other religions as a Boston reader of 1855 would have understood them. All of this is Bulfinch's, and it stands as he wrote it.

This is a restored edition, and it gives Bulfinch's own text of 1855. The transcription it is built from follows a revised edition of 1913. That edition replaced his preface with one of its own, respelled many words, and changed a number of readings. This edition compared it word by word with two scanned copies of the first edition, and wherever both copies agree it follows them. It restores the dedication to Longfellow and the 1855 preface. It keeps the list of Proverbial Expressions, the Latin tags the chapters point to, and names the chapter each one belongs to in place of a page number. The 1913 plates, maps and glossary, which were not Bulfinch's, are left out, and so is his index. The edition also adds modern typesetting, an ebook, and this introduction.
