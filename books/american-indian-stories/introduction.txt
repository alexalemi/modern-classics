Zitkala-Ša published American Indian Stories in Washington in 1921, near the end of twenty years of writing and public work. The first three pieces appeared in the Atlantic Monthly in 1900 and tell her own life: a childhood with her mother on the Yankton reservation in South Dakota; being taken east at the age of eight by missionaries who promised a land of red apples, to a boarding school where her long hair was cut; and the years when she taught at an Indian school herself and came to doubt what such schools were for. The Great Spirit is her 1902 Atlantic essay "Why I Am a Pagan" under a new title. The Trial Path, The Soft-Hearted Sioux and A Warrior's Daughter first appeared in Harper's and Everybody's, and the book's acknowledgments page names them all. It names no earlier home for the rest.

By 1921 she was living in Washington, married to Raymond Bonnin and signing herself Gertrude Bonnin, and working for Indian rights. She had been an officer of the Society of American Indians. The last piece, America's Indian Problem, is a political essay, and most of it quotes a 1915 report on the management of the Indian Bureau. Three years later, in 1924, Congress made citizens of all American Indians born in the United States who were not citizens already.

A few things are worth knowing before starting. "Paleface" is her plain word for white people. The "iron horse" is the train. The Dakota words she keeps are mostly names, and the one footnote glosses Taku Iyotan Wasaka. Subsections in the autobiographical pieces are numbered, most with a short title of their own.

Copies of the 1921 printing do not all agree. In at least one, the missionary's candies on the train are followed by two sentences about the trouble they caused later, and The Great Spirit ends on the last line of the 1902 essay: "If this is Paganism, then at present, at least, I am a Pagan." Other copies lack both and end the essay on a new paragraph about the robe of the Great Spirit. This edition follows those copies, which is also the text Project Gutenberg transcribed.

This is a restored edition: her words are unchanged. The Gutenberg text was compared word by word with two scans of the 1921 book. Where both copies print a period spelling that Gutenberg modernised, such as intrust and gayly, this edition goes back to print; the printer's plain misprints stay corrected. The epigraph and the acknowledgments page, which Gutenberg left out, are restored, and the publisher's advertisement at the back is not. It also adds modern typesetting, a proper ebook, and this introduction.
