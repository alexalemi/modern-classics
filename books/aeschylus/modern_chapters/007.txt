Agamemnon
(Part 2 of 3)

Chorus.
	Who was it that gave her that name,
	a name so completely, so exactly true?
	Was it someone we cannot see,
	who knows ahead of time what is fated,
	and steered a tongue to hit the mark by luck?
	Helen. The bride the spear fought for,
	the woman two armies quarrelled over.
	And the name fits her. She was hell to ships,
	hell to men, hell to the city.
	Out from behind her delicate, costly curtains
	she sailed on the breath of a giant west wind,
	and after her came the hunters,
	a great crowd of men carrying shields,
	following the vanished track of her oars
	to the place where she had run her boat ashore
	on the leafy banks of the Simois,
	to a quarrel that would end in blood.

Chorus.
	And to Troy that marriage brought
	a mourning that deserved its name,
	driven home by Wrath, who finishes what she starts.
	In later time she collected the debt
	for the dishonoured table,
	for Zeus who shares the hearth,
	from the men who raised their voices
	loud in honour of the bride,
	the wedding song it fell to the bridegroom's kin
	to sing that day.
	Priam's old city has learned a new song since.
	Now, I think, she groans out loud,
	a long lament, and calls Paris
	the man whose marriage was a curse.
	She has borne a life of total ruin,
	a life of lament,
	for the pitiful blood of her people.

Chorus.
	So a man once raised a lion cub in his house,
	taken from the milk it wanted,
	still hungry for the breast.
	In the first days of its life it was gentle,
	loving with the children,
	a delight to the old.
	It was held in their arms, often,
	like a newborn baby,
	bright-eyed, turning to the hand that fed it,
	fawning, because its belly made it fawn.

Chorus.
	But time went on, and it showed the nature
	it had from its parents.
	It paid back the ones who raised it:
	unasked, it made itself a feast,
	a ruin of slaughtered sheep,
	and the house was fouled with blood,
	a grief the household could not fight,
	a great slaughter, many killed.
	A priest of Ruin, sent by a god,
	and the house itself had raised it.

Chorus.
	And so, I would say, there came to the city of Troy
	what looked like a spirit of windless calm,
	a soft ornament of wealth,
	a gentle arrow from the eyes,
	the flower of love that bites the heart.
	Then she swerved aside
	and brought the marriage to a bitter end,
	a bad neighbour, bad company,
	rushing in on the sons of Priam
	with Zeus, god of guest and host, as her escort:
	a Fury who made the brides weep.

Chorus.
	There is an old saying among men,
	spoken long ago and handed down:
	that a man's good fortune, once it is full grown,
	has children and does not die childless,
	and that out of good luck
	a family sprouts a misery that is never satisfied.
	But I stand apart from the others. I think alone.
	It is the godless deed that breeds more after it,
	and the children look like their parents.
	A house that keeps to straight justice
	has fine children always. That is its fate.

Chorus.
	Old Outrage loves to breed
	a young Outrage in bad men,
	sooner or later, when the appointed day of birth comes,
	and with it a spirit no one can fight or make war on,
	unholy Recklessness,
	and black Ruins for the house,
	the image of their parents.

Chorus.
	But Justice shines in smoky houses,
	and honours the life that keeps to the line.
	The gold-plated halls where hands are dirty
	she leaves, with her eyes turned away,
	and goes to what is clean.
	She does not worship the power of wealth
	stamped with a false mark by men's praise,
	and she steers everything to its end.

(Agamemnon comes in on a chariot, with a large escort of soldiers.
Cassandra rides in the chariot behind him, silent.)

Chorus.
	Come then, my King, sacker of Troy,
	son of Atreus,
	how am I to greet you? How am I to honour you
	without overshooting, without falling short
	of the right measure of thanks?
	Many men put the look of a thing before the truth
	and step over the line of justice.
	Everyone is ready to groan beside the man who is down,
	but no bite of grief ever reaches his liver.
	And they force their unsmiling faces into smiles
	and pretend to share in other people's joy.
	But a good shepherd knows his flock,
	and a man's eyes cannot fool him
	when they fawn on him with watery friendship
	and pretend it comes from a loyal mind.
	I will not hide it from you: back then,
	when you sent the army out for Helen's sake,
	you were painted in my eyes in very ugly colours,
	a man not steering the helm of his mind well,
	buying courage for dying men
	with sacrifices.
	But now, not from the surface of my heart, and not without love,
	I say it: the work was worth it to the men who finished it well.
	And in time, by asking, you will learn
	which of your citizens kept the city justly
	while you were gone, and which did not.

Agamemnon.
Argos first, and the gods of this land. It is right that I greet
them first. They helped me home, and they helped me take the justice
I took from Priam's city. For the gods did not listen to pleas from
anyone's tongue. They cast their votes, with no divided opinion, into
the urn of blood: death for the men of Troy, and destruction for the
city. To the other urn Hope came near, but no hand filled it. The
city is taken, and even now you can tell it by the smoke. The storms
of Ruin are still alive there. The ash is dying with the city, and as
it dies it breathes out rich fumes of wealth.

For this we owe the gods a gratitude that never forgets, since we
threw the net of our anger around the city, and for the sake of one
woman the Argive beast ground the city into dust: the horse's brood,
the people carrying shields, that made its leap as the Pleiades were
setting. It vaulted the tower, a lion that eats raw meat, and lapped
its fill of royal blood.

That is my opening, to the gods, and I have drawn it out at length.
As for what you feel, I heard it, and I remember it. I say the same,
and you have an advocate in me. Few men are born with this: to honour
a friend's good fortune without envy. The poison of ill will sits on
the heart and doubles the load of the man who has caught the disease.
He is weighed down by his own troubles, and he groans to see happiness
next door. I can speak from knowledge, for I know the mirror of
companionship well: the men who seemed so very devoted to me were the
ghost of a shadow. Only Odysseus, the very man who did not want to
sail, once he was yoked, pulled beside me willingly in harness. I say
this of him whether he is dead or alive.

For the rest, matters of the city and the gods: we will call a full
assembly, put them to open debate, and take counsel. Where things are
going well, we must see how they may stay well for a long time. And
where some healing drug is needed, we will burn or cut, with kind
intentions, and try to turn away the harm of the disease.

Now I will go into my halls, to my house and its hearth, and first I
will give the gods my right hand in greeting. They sent me out and
they have brought me back. And since Victory has followed me, may she
stay with me, and hold firm.

(Clytemnestra comes out of the palace. Behind her are slave women
carrying purple cloths.)

Clytemnestra.
Men of the city, elders of Argos here before me, I will not be
ashamed to tell you how I love my husband. In time, shyness dies away
in people. I have not learned this from others: I will tell you my own
hard life, all the years this man was under the walls of Troy.

First, for a woman to sit at home alone, apart from her man, is a
monstrous evil. She hears rumour after spiteful rumour. One messenger
comes, and then another brings a worse blow than the last, shouting it
to the house. If this man had taken as many wounds as the reports that
ran home like water through a channel, he would have more holes in him
than a net. If he had died as often as the stories said, he would be a
second Geryon, with three bodies, and could boast of taking on a
triple cloak of earth, a great deal of it above him, for of the earth
below him I say nothing, dying once for each of his shapes. Because of
spiteful rumours like these, other people had to loosen from my neck,
more than once, the noose I had hung from above and was caught in, by
force.

That is why our boy is not standing here beside me as he should,
Orestes, who holds the pledges of my faith and yours. Do not be
surprised at it. He is being raised by a good ally, our friend by the
spear, Strophius of Phocis, who warned me of trouble from two sides:
your danger under Troy, and the chance that the people, in a roar of
lawlessness, might throw down the council, since it is in men's nature
to kick a man harder once he is down. An excuse like that carries no
trickery.

Clytemnestra.
As for me, the rushing springs of my tears have run dry. There is not
a drop left. My eyes are sore from late nights, weeping over the
beacon-lights set for you that were never attended to. In my dreams I
would be woken by the thin whine of a gnat, and I had seen more
suffering happen to you than the time I slept could hold.

Now I have borne all that, and with a heart free of grief I can call
this man the watchdog of the fold, the forestay that saves the ship,
the pillar footed firm under a high roof, a father's only son, land
appearing to sailors past all hope, the loveliest daybreak after a
storm, a spring of running water to a thirsty traveller. It is sweet
to escape every kind of necessity. Those are the greetings I think he
deserves. And let envy stay far away. We have put up with many evils
before now.

And now, my dear one, step down from this chariot. But do not set your
foot on the bare ground, my lord, the foot that trampled Troy. Women,
what are you waiting for? You were given the task of spreading cloth
over the ground where he walks. Let his path be purple at once, so
that Justice may lead him into a home he never hoped to see. The
rest, a mind that sleep does not overcome will set in order justly,
with the gods' help, as it is destined.

Agamemnon.
Daughter of Leda, guardian of my house, your speech matched my
absence: you drew it out at length. But fitting praise is a prize that
should come from other people. And do not pamper me like a woman, and
do not gape at me with grovelling shouts from the ground as if I were
some barbarian, and do not make my path a thing for envy by spreading
cloth on it. That is how we honour the gods. For a mortal to walk on
embroidered beauty is never, to my mind, a thing without fear. I tell
you to honour me as a man, not a god. Footcloths and embroideries:
Rumour shouts the difference between them. Not to think badly is the
gods' greatest gift. Call a man blessed only when he has ended his
life in the prosperity he loves. If I can act like this in
everything, I have good confidence.

Clytemnestra.
Come now, tell me this, and do not go against what you really think.

Agamemnon.
What I think, be sure of it, I will not corrupt.

Clytemnestra.
If you had been afraid, would you have vowed to the gods to do this?

Agamemnon.
Yes, if any man ever would. I would have declared it as my vow,
knowing well what I said.

Clytemnestra.
And what do you think Priam would have done, if he had won what you
have won?

Agamemnon.
He would have walked on the embroidered cloth. I am quite sure of
it.

Clytemnestra.
Then do not be ashamed of what people will say.

Agamemnon.
And yet the voice of the people has great power.

Clytemnestra.
Yes, but the man nobody envies is a man nobody admires.

Agamemnon.
It is not a woman's part to be hungry for a fight.

Clytemnestra.
But for the fortunate, it is fitting even to be beaten.

Agamemnon.
And you, do you value winning this quarrel so much?

Clytemnestra.
Give in. Yield the victory to me, but of your own free will.

Agamemnon.
Well, if that is what you want, let someone quickly unlace my boots,
the slaves that serve my feet as I walk. And as I walk on these purple
cloths of the gods, may no envious eye strike me from far off. I feel
a great shame at ruining the house with my feet, spoiling wealth,
spoiling weaving bought with silver. So much for that. This foreign
girl, take her into the house kindly. God looks with favour from far
away on a master who is gentle in his power. Nobody puts on the yoke
of slavery by choice. She is the pick of the flower of many treasures,
the army's gift to me, and she came with me. Now, since I have been
brought round to obey you in this, I will go into the halls of my
house, walking on purple.

Clytemnestra.
There is the sea, and who will drain it dry? It breeds an ever-fresh
ooze of purple, worth its weight in silver, to dye our clothes. And
the house has enough of this, my lord, thanks to the gods. This house
does not know how to be poor. I would have vowed the trampling of many
cloths, if the oracle had ordered it for this house, when I was
working out a ransom for this life. While the root lives, the leaves
come back to the house and spread their shade against the dog star. So
now that you have come to the hearth of your home, you are a sign that
warmth has come in winter. And when Zeus makes wine from the bitter
unripe grape, then already there is coolness in the house, when the
master, the perfect man, walks his own halls again.

(Agamemnon walks over the purple cloth into the house.)

Zeus, Zeus who brings things to their end, bring my prayers to their
end. And take care of what you mean to bring to an end.

(She goes into the house after him.)

Chorus.
	Why does this terror keep hovering,
	standing guard before my heart,
	my heart that reads the signs?
	Why does my song play the prophet,
	unasked, unpaid?
	Why can I not spit it out
	like a dream too hard to read,
	and let a confidence I can trust
	sit on the throne of my mind?
	Time has heaped the sand of the beach
	over the stern-cables thrown ashore
	when the army took ship and set out for Troy.

Chorus.
	I have seen their homecoming with my own eyes.
	I am my own witness.
	But still my heart, self-taught, inside me,
	sings the dirge of the Fury, with no lyre,
	and cannot hold the whole of hope's welcome courage.
	My insides are not talking nonsense.
	My heart is spinning in whirlpools
	that come to their end,
	against a mind that has good reason to fear.
	I pray that what I expect
	falls out a lie
	and never comes to its end.

Chorus.
	Truly, great health does not know when to stop.
	Disease is its neighbour,
	leaning on the shared wall.
	And a man's fortune, sailing straight,
	strikes on a hidden reef.
	But if fear throws overboard a part of the cargo,
	the goods he owns,
	with a well-measured swing of the sling,
	the whole house does not go down,
	overloaded with grief,
	and the hull is not swamped.
	And truly Zeus gives with both hands,
	and the yearly furrows
	kill the disease of hunger.

Chorus.
	But a man's black blood, once it has fallen
	to the ground in front of him, and he is dead,
	who can sing it back by any charm?
	The one man who knew the right way
	to bring back the dead:
	did Zeus not stop him, as a warning?
	If one fate set by the gods
	did not hold back another fate
	from getting more than its share,
	my heart would have run ahead of my tongue
	and poured all this out.
	As it is, it mutters in the dark,
	sore, and with no hope
	of ever unwinding anything in time,
	while my mind is on fire.

(Clytemnestra comes out of the palace again.)

Clytemnestra.
Go inside, you too. I mean you, Cassandra. Zeus, without anger, has
made you a sharer in the holy water of this house, standing with many
other slaves beside the altar of the god who guards its wealth. Get
down from this chariot, and do not be too proud. Even Alcmene's son,
they say, was once sold, and put up with eating the bread of a slave.
And if necessity tips the scale this way, there is much to be thankful
for in masters whose wealth is old. Those who reap a rich harvest
they never hoped for are cruel to their slaves in everything, and
beyond the measure. From us you get what custom allows.

Chorus.
She has finished speaking to you, and plainly. You are caught inside
the nets of fate. Perhaps you will obey, if you are going to
obey. Perhaps you will not.

Clytemnestra.
Well, unless she twitters in some unknown barbarian tongue, like a
swallow, my words are reaching inside her mind, and I am winning her
round.

Chorus.
Go with her. What she says is the best of what is left to you. Do as
she says. Leave your seat in the chariot.

Clytemnestra.
I have no time to waste out here at the door with this woman. The
sheep are already standing at the central hearth for the slaughter,
for a joy we never hoped we would have. As for you, if you are going
to do any of this, do not waste time. But if you do not understand
and are not taking in what I say, then instead of your voice, make a
sign with your barbarian hand.

Chorus.
The stranger seems to need an interpreter, a clear one. She is like a
wild animal, just caught.

Clytemnestra.
She is mad. She listens to her own sick mind. She has come here
leaving a city just taken, and she does not know how to carry the bit
until she has foamed her rage away in blood. I will not throw more
words at her and be insulted.

(Clytemnestra goes back into the house.)

Chorus.
I will not be angry with her. I pity her. Come, poor woman, leave the
chariot empty. Give in to this necessity, and put on the new yoke.

Cassandra.
	Otototoi, popoi, da!
	Apollo! Apollo!

Chorus.
Why are you crying otototoi over Apollo? He is not the kind of god
who has anything to do with mourning.

Cassandra.
	Otototoi, popoi, da!
	Apollo! Apollo!

Chorus.
Again she calls on the god with ill-omened cries, a god who has no
business standing by at a lament.

Cassandra.
	Apollo, Apollo,
	god of the roads, my destroyer. Your name says it.
	You have destroyed me, and it cost you nothing, this second time.

Chorus.
She is going to prophesy, it seems, about her own troubles. The god's
gift stays in her mind, slave though she is.

Cassandra.
	Apollo, Apollo,
	god of the roads, my destroyer.
	Ah, where have you brought me? To what kind of house?

Chorus.
To the house of the sons of Atreus. If you do not understand that, I
am telling you. And you will not say it is a lie.

Cassandra.
	No. To a house that hates the gods,
	a house that has seen too much: kin killing kin,
	heads cut off,
	a slaughterhouse for men, a floor spattered with blood.

Chorus.
The stranger seems to have a nose like a hound's. She is on the
scent, and she will find the blood.

Cassandra.
	Here is the evidence I trust:
	those babies, crying over their own slaughter,
	their roasted flesh eaten by their father.

Chorus.
We had heard of your fame as a prophet. But we are not looking for
any prophets here.

Cassandra.
	Oh, popoi, what is she plotting?
	What is this new grief? Great, great
	is the evil she is plotting in this house,
	unbearable to the people who are its own, past any cure.
	And help stands far away.

Chorus.
These prophecies I know nothing about. But those others I understood.
The whole city is shouting them.

Cassandra.
	Oh, wretched woman, you will do it, then?
	The husband who shares your bed:
	you wash him bright in the bath, and then, how do I say the end?
	It will be soon. Hand after hand
	she stretches out, reaching.

Chorus.
I still do not follow. Now, after riddles, I am helpless before
oracles with a film over their eyes.

Cassandra.
	E, e! Papai, papai! What is this that shows itself?
	Is it some net of Hades?
	No. The net is the woman who shares his bed,
	who shares the guilt of murder.
	Let the pack that never has enough of this family
	scream in triumph over a sacrifice
	that should be answered with stoning.

Chorus.
	What Fury is this you are calling
	to raise its cry over this house?
	Your words do not brighten me.
	The blood runs back to my heart, yellow as saffron,
	the way it does for a man struck down,
	when it ends together with the last rays of his life.
	And Ruin comes fast.

Cassandra.
	Ah, ah, look, look! Keep the bull
	away from the cow! She has caught him in the robe,
	her trick with the black horn,
	and she strikes. He falls in the vessel full of water.
	It is a cauldron of treacherous murder I am telling you of.

Chorus.
	I would not boast that I am a fine judge of oracles,
	but I make this out to be some evil.
	From oracles what good word
	ever comes to men?
	Through evil, the wordy arts of prophets
	bring men to learn fear.

Cassandra.
	Oh, oh, the bad luck of my wretched fate!
	It is my own suffering I cry out now, poured on top of the rest.
	Why have you brought me here, poor thing that I am?
	For nothing, except to die with him. What else?

Chorus.
	Your mind is out of itself, some god is carrying you,
	and you cry over yourself
	a song that is no song, like that brown bird
	who never has enough of crying, ah, in her wretched heart,
	moaning Itys, Itys, a life
	that flowers on both sides with grief,
	the nightingale.

Cassandra.
	Oh, oh, the fate of the clear-voiced nightingale!
	The gods wrapped her in a feathered body
	and gave her a sweet life, without tears.
	What waits for me is the split of a two-edged blade.

Chorus.
	Where do they come from, these rushing, god-driven pains
	that mean nothing?
	Why do you beat out the terrible things in a song
	of ugly screams and high straining notes?
	Where did you learn the landmarks on this road of prophecy,
	a road of evil words?

Cassandra.
	Oh, the marriage, the marriage of Paris, death to the ones he loved.
	Oh, Scamander, the river my fathers drank from.
	Once, poor wretch, I was raised beside your banks,
	and grew up there.
	Now beside Cocytus and the banks of Acheron
	I think I will soon be singing my prophecies.

Chorus.
	What is this word you have said, far too clear?
	A newborn child could hear it and understand.
	I am struck through, as by a bloody fang,
	by your bitter fate, as you cry your small sad cries,
	things that break me to hear.

Cassandra.
	Oh, the pains, the pains of my city, destroyed to the last.
	Oh, my father's sacrifices before the walls,
	the herds killed, the cattle from the pasture,
	and they were no cure at all,
	they could not stop the city suffering what it has.
	And I, my mind on fire, will soon be thrown to the ground.

Chorus.
	These words follow the ones before.
	Some god who means you harm
	falls on you with all his weight
	and makes you sing of sufferings, groaning, full of death.
	And the end of it I cannot find.
