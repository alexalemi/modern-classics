The Persians
(Part 2 of 2)

Chorus.
	Zeus, King. Now you have destroyed
	the army of the Persians,
	so proud of themselves, so many men,
	and buried the city of Susa and Agbatana
	under a black cloud of grief.
	Women everywhere are tearing at their veils
	with soft hands,
	and the tears run down and soak
	the dresses at their breasts. They share the pain.
	And the wives of Persia, gentle even in their wailing,
	who long to see the men they were only just yoked to,
	have given up the soft sheets of the marriage bed,
	the pleasure of their pampered youth,
	and mourn with cries that can never have enough.
	And I too take up the fate of the men who are gone
	and raise, as is proper, a song of many sorrows.

Chorus.
	Now the whole land of Asia groans,
	emptied of its men.
	Xerxes led them out, popoi,
	Xerxes lost them, totoi,
	Xerxes bungled everything
	with his ships on the sea.
	Why was Darius, in his time,
	so free of harm when he was over us,
	master of the bow to his people,
	the leader the men of Susa loved?

Chorus.
	Soldiers and sailors alike,
	the ships led them out, the dark-eyed ships
	with wings of linen, popoi,
	the ships lost them, totoi,
	the ships, with the ramming that destroys everything,
	and the hands of the Ionians.
	And the King himself, we hear,
	only just escaped
	across the flat plains of Thrace
	and its roads of bitter winter.

Chorus.
	And the ones who were the first to die, pheu,
	left behind because they had no choice, Oh,
	are dragged back and forth along the shore of Cychreia, oa.
	Groan, and bite your lips,
	and roar out heavily
	a grief to reach the sky, oa.
	Stretch out the miserable voice
	into a long, wretched howl.

Chorus.
	Torn by the whirling water, pheu,
	they are gnawed, Oh, by the voiceless children
	of the sea that nothing stains, oa.
	A house robbed of its man mourns him,
	and parents with no children left,
	old men wailing over pain the gods sent, oa,
	are hearing now the whole of it.

Chorus.
	And the peoples across the land of Asia
	will not be ruled by Persian law much longer,
	nor go on paying tribute
	because a master makes them,
	nor fall face down on the ground
	in awe. The King's strength
	is destroyed.

Chorus.
	Men's tongues are no longer
	under guard. The people are let loose
	to say whatever they like,
	now that the yoke of power is broken.
	And the island of Ajax, washed round by the sea,
	its fields soaked in blood,
	holds all that was Persia.

(The Queen comes back on foot, without her chariot and her finery, with
attendants carrying the offerings.)

Queen.
My friends, anyone who has had experience of misfortune knows how it is
with people: when a wave of trouble comes over them, they are frightened
of everything. But when the god is sending a fair wind, they trust that
the same god will blow their luck the same way for ever. For me, now,
everything is full of fear. Before my eyes the gods appear, set against
me, and in my ears a noise is shouting that has no healing in it. That
is how badly this disaster has shaken me and frightened my mind. So I
have come back from the house by this road with no chariot and none of
my earlier finery, bringing offerings to soften the heart of my son's
father, the things that soothe the dead: white milk, good to drink, from
an unblemished cow; bright honey, the drop the flower-worker bee
distils; running water from a virgin spring; and this, unmixed, the
drink from a wild mother, the shining juice of an old vine. And here too
is the sweet-smelling fruit of the pale olive, which spends its whole
life in leaf; and flowers plaited together, the children of the earth
that bears everything. Come, my friends: sing your hymns over these
offerings to the dead, and call up the spirit of Darius, while I send
these honours down for the earth to drink, for the gods below.

Chorus.
	Royal lady, whom the Persians honour,
	you send your offerings down to the chambers under the earth,
	and we will ask, with hymns,
	the escorts of the dead
	to be kind to us down there beneath the ground.
	Come then, holy gods of the world below,
	Earth, and Hermes, and you, King of the dead,
	send his soul up from underneath into the light.
	For if he knows of any further cure for our troubles,
	he alone of mortals can tell us where they end.

Chorus.
	Does he hear me, the blessed one,
	the King who was a god's equal,
	as I send up in barbarian speech,
	confused and thick with grief,
	these cries of every misery, these harsh, broken sounds?
	Or must I shout out
	the whole of my wretched pain?
	Does he hear me, down there?

Chorus.
	Earth, and you others,
	you leaders of the dead below,
	consent, and let the proud spirit
	come out of his house,
	the god of the Persians, born in Susa.
	Send him up: a man whose like
	the earth of Persia never covered yet.

Chorus.
	Yes, the man was loved, and the mound is loved,
	because what it hides is a heart we loved.
	Hades, you who send the dead back up,
	Hades, let him come,
	our divine lord Darius. Oh.

Chorus.
	For he never lost his men
	in wars that ruin and destroy.
	The Persians called him
	the counsellor whose counsel came from god,
	and he was that,
	because he steered the army well. Oh.

Chorus.
	Master, old master,
	come, come here.
	Come up to the very top of the mound,
	lifting the saffron-dyed slipper on your foot,
	showing the crest
	of the royal tiara.
	Come, father Darius, who did no harm. Oi.

Chorus.
	So that you can hear
	of griefs that are new and will last for ever,
	master of our master, appear.
	A fog like the fog of the Styx has settled over us,
	for the young men of the land
	are dead now, all of them.
	Come, father Darius, who did no harm. Oi.

Chorus.
	Aiai, aiai.
	You whose death your friends wept for so bitterly,
	why, master, master,
	why has this double blunder, wept for on every side,
	come over your land?
	Every three-banked ship this country had
	is gone. Ships that are ships no more, no more.

(The ghost of Darius rises out of the tomb, in his royal robes and
tiara.)

Darius.
Faithful of the Faithful, men who were young when I was, old men of
Persia, what is this trouble the city is in? The ground groans. It is
beaten, it is scored with feet. I see my wife beside my tomb and I am
afraid, though I received her offerings kindly. And you stand close to
the tomb and wail, and with high cries that call up the dead you call
me, pitifully. It is not easy to get out. For one reason above all: the
gods under the earth are better at taking than at letting go. Still, I
have power among them, and I have come. But hurry, so that no one can
blame me for the time I take. What is this new, heavy evil on the
Persians?

Chorus.
	I am in awe, and cannot look at you.
	I am in awe, and cannot speak to your face,
	from the old dread of you.

Darius.
Well, since I came up from below because your wailing persuaded me, do
not make a long story of it. Be short. Speak, and go through all of it,
and put your awe of me aside.

Chorus.
	I am afraid to do what you want.
	I am afraid to speak to your face
	and say things hard to say to the people I love.

Darius.
Well, since the old fear in your minds stands in your way: you, my noble
wife, the aged partner of my bed, stop these tears and this wailing and
tell me something clear. Human suffering can come to human beings; that
is how it is. Many evils come out of the sea and many out of the land
for mortals, if their life is stretched out long enough.

Queen.
You who outdid every mortal in prosperity, with a fortunate fate: as
long as you looked on the rays of the sun, you lived a life the Persians
envied, a happy life, like a god. And now I envy you for dying before
you saw the depth of our evils. You will hear the whole story in a short
time, Darius. The Persian state is sacked and wrecked, in a word.

Darius.
How? Did some thunderbolt of plague fall on the city? Or civil war?

Queen.
Neither. Near Athens, the whole army has been destroyed.

Darius.
Which of my sons led the army there? Tell me.

Queen.
Hot-headed Xerxes. He emptied the whole flat continent of its men.

Darius.
Was it on foot or by sea that the poor fool made this mad attempt?

Queen.
Both. There were two fronts, two armies.

Darius.
But how did a land army as large as that get across?

Queen.
He yoked the strait of Helle with engines, and made himself a road.

Darius.
He did that? He shut the great Bosporus?

Queen.
He did. Some god must have had a hand in the plan.

Darius.
Pheu. Some great god came on him, so that he could not think straight.

Queen.
Yes. You can see how it ended, what an evil he brought about.

Darius.
And how did it go for them, that you groan over them like this?

Queen.
The fleet was beaten, and its defeat destroyed the land army.

Darius.
So the whole people has been wiped out by the spear, utterly?

Queen.
So much so that the whole city of Susa groans that it has no men.

Darius.
Oh, popoi. That fine defence, that army that stood by us.

Queen.
And the people of Bactria are gone, wiped out, not even an old man left.

Darius.
Poor wretch. What a youth of allies he has destroyed.

Queen.
And Xerxes, alone, deserted, they say, with not many men...

Darius.
Ended how, and where? Is there any hope of safety?

Queen.
Reached, to his joy, the bridge that yokes the two lands.

Darius.
And is safe on this continent? Is that certain?

Queen.
Yes. The report is clear and it holds. There is no argument about it.

Darius.
Pheu. So the oracles have been carried out quickly, and it is on my son
that Zeus has brought down the end of what was foretold. I trusted that
the gods would take a long time to bring it about. But when a man is in
a hurry himself, the god joins in. Now a spring of evils seems to have
been found for everyone I love. And it was my son who did it, not
knowing what he did, in the boldness of youth. He hoped to hold the
sacred Hellespont with chains, as if it were a slave, the Bosporus, the
god's own flowing stream. He changed the shape of the strait, threw
hammered shackles around it, and made a great road for a great army. He
was a mortal, and he thought he could master all the gods, Poseidon too.
That was not good judgement. Was that not a sickness of the mind that
had hold of my son? I am afraid that the great wealth I laboured for
will be plunder for whoever gets to it first.

Queen.
Hot-headed Xerxes learned that lesson by keeping company with bad men.
They kept telling him that you won great wealth for your children with
your spear, while he, for want of manhood, played the warrior at home
and added nothing to his father's fortune. He heard taunts like that
many times from bad men, and he planned this road, and this army
against Greece.

Darius.
And so they have done a deed, the greatest deed, one that will never be
forgotten, one that has emptied this city of Susa by its fall as nothing
ever did before, since the day Lord Zeus granted this honour: that one
man should rule all of Asia, the land that feeds the flocks, with the
sceptre of command in his hand. Medus was the first leader of the army.
Another, his son, finished his work, because his mind held the tiller of
his heart. Third after him came Cyrus, a fortunate man. He ruled and
made peace for everyone who was his. He won the people of the Lydians
and the Phrygians and drove the whole of Ionia under him by force, and
no god hated him for it, because he was born with good sense. Fourth,
the son of Cyrus commanded the army. Fifth, Mardus ruled, a disgrace to
his country and its ancient throne; brave Artaphrenes killed him by a
trick, in the palace, with the men he trusted, whose duty it was. Sixth
was Maraphis, and seventh, Artaphrenes. And I drew the lot I wanted, and
I marched out many times with a great army. But I never threw an evil
this size on the city. Xerxes, my son, is young, and thinks like a young
man, and does not remember what I told him. You know this well, men of
my own age: all of us who have held this power could not be shown, put
together, to have done so much harm.

Chorus.
Then what, lord Darius? Where are you steering your words to? After
this, how can we, the people of Persia, do best?

Darius.
If you do not march against the land of the Greeks, not even if the
Median army is larger than theirs. The land itself is their ally.

Chorus.
What do you mean? How is it their ally?

Darius.
It kills with hunger any army that is far too large.

Chorus.
But we will send out a picked force, trim and easy to supply.

Darius.
Not even the army that is left in the land of Greece now will win its
way safely home.

Chorus.
What do you mean? Is the whole barbarian army not crossing the strait of
Helle out of Europe?

Darius.
Few out of many, if the oracles of the gods are to be trusted at all,
looking at what has happened now; for when they come true, it is not
some and not others. It is all of them. And if that is so, he is leaving
behind a picked mass of his army, persuaded by empty hopes. They are
waiting where the Asopus waters the plain with its streams, a welcome
fattening for the soil of the Boeotians. There the highest of evils is
waiting for them to suffer, the payment for outrage and godless
thoughts. They came to the land of Greece and did not scruple to rob the
images of the gods and burn their temples. Altars are wiped out, and the
seats of the gods have been torn up from their bases, roots and all, and
thrown down in a heap. So they did evil, and they are suffering no less,
and more is to come; the spring of their evils is not dried up yet, it
is still bubbling up. So great will be the clotted mess of slaughtered
blood on the ground at Plataea under the Dorian spear that the heaps of
the dead will tell the eyes of men, without a word, down to the third
generation, that a mortal must not think too high. For outrage comes
into flower, and the crop it ripens is Ruin, and from that it reaps a
harvest of nothing but tears. Look at these punishments, and remember
Athens and Greece, and let no one of you, despising the luck he has and
lusting after more, pour away great wealth. Zeus stands over thoughts
that boast too much and punishes them; he corrects with a heavy hand. So
teach him, with sensible warnings, the man who needs to learn to keep
himself in hand, to stop offending the gods with his boasting
recklessness. And you, aged mother, whom Xerxes loves, go into the
house, take whatever clothing is fit for him, and go to meet your son.
For the grief of these evils has ripped his embroidered robes to shreds
all about his body. Soothe him gently with words. He will listen to you,
I know, and to no one else. I am going down now, into the dark under the
earth. And you, Elders, farewell. Even in evil times, give your hearts
some pleasure every day, since wealth is no use at all to the dead.

(The ghost of Darius sinks back into the tomb.)

Chorus.
It hurts to hear it, so many sufferings for the barbarians, now and
still to come.

Queen.
God, how many evils and pains come over me. And this misfortune bites
the hardest: to hear of the shame my son is wearing on his body, the
rags that wrap him. I will go, and take clothing from the house, and try
to meet my son. I will not desert the one I love most in his trouble.

(The Queen goes out.)

Chorus.
	Oh, popoi. What a great and good life we had,
	a life under good government,
	when the old King,
	who provided everything, who did no harm,
	whom no one could fight,
	Darius, equal to a god, ruled the land.

Chorus.
	First, we showed the world armies to be proud of,
	and laws, strong as towers,
	kept everything straight,

	unworn, unhurt,

Chorus.
	And how many cities he took

	without stirring from his own hearth:
	the Acheloan cities
	by the Strymonian sea, that neighbour
	the Thracian farms,

Chorus.
	and outside the lake, the cities on the mainland,

	obeyed him as their King;
	and the ones that boast of the broad strait of Helle,
	and the deep-set Propontis,
	and the mouth of the Pontus;

Chorus.
	and the islands along the sea's headland,
	washed round by the water,
	lying close to this land of ours:
	Lesbos, and Samos planted with olives,
	Chios and Paros, Naxos, Myconos,

Chorus.
	and he ruled the islands out in the sea,
	between the two shores,
	Lemnos, and the seat of Icarus,
	and Rhodes and Cnidus,
	and the Cyprian cities, Paphos,
	and Soli, and Salamis,
	whose mother city is now
	the cause of all this groaning.

Chorus.
	And the rich cities, full of men, of the Greeks
	in the Ionian allotment,
	he ruled by his own will,
	and he had, at his call, the tireless strength
	of armoured men
	and allies from every people mixed together.
	But now, past all argument,
	we bear the reverse, the gods turned it,
	beaten down hard in war
	by blows off the sea.

(Xerxes comes in on foot, with only a few attendants, his robes torn to
rags and an empty quiver on his back.)

Xerxes.
	Io.
	Wretched man that I am, I have met this hateful fate,
	that gave no sign at all of its coming.
	How savagely the god has stamped
	on the race of the Persians. What is to become of me?
	The strength has gone out of my legs
	as I look at these old men of the city.
	Zeus, I wish that the fate of death
	had covered me too
	with the men who are gone.

Chorus.
	Otototoi, King, for the good army,
	for the great honour of Persian rule,
	for the fine order of men
	that the god has cut down now.
	The land cries aiai for its own young men,
	killed by Xerxes, who has crammed Hades
	full of Persians. Many men have walked down to Hades,
	the flower of the country,
	masters of the bow, a thick crowd,
	ten thousand at a time, and are gone.
	Aiai, aiai, for the strong men we trusted.
	And the land of Asia, King of the earth,
	terribly, terribly,
	is bent down onto its knee.

Xerxes.
	Here I am, oioi, a thing to wail over,
	a wretch. I was born to be an evil
	to my race and my father's land.

Chorus.
	To greet you home
	I will send up a cry of bad omen,
	an ill-tuned shout,
	the howl of a Mariandynian mourner,
	I will send it, send it,
	a wail of many tears.

Xerxes.
	Let it go, the harsh voice,
	full of grief, all lament.
	The god has turned round against me now.

Chorus.
	I will let it go, all lament,
	in honour of your sufferings
	and the blows the sea dealt,
	the city's grief for its children.
	I will scream out, scream out
	a wail of many tears.

Xerxes.
	It was the Ionians who took them,
	the Ionian War, fenced with ships,
	that gave the strength to the other side
	and mowed the dark plain of the sea
	and the shore of ill luck.

Chorus.
	Oioioi. Cry out, and learn everything.
	Where is the rest of the crowd of your friends?
	Where are the men who stood beside you,
	men like Pharandaces,
	Susas, Pelagon, and Dotamas, and Agdabatas,
	Psammis, and Susiscanes,
	who left Agbatana behind him?

Xerxes.
	I left them dead,
	fallen from a Tyrian ship,
	drifting on the shores
	of Salamis, knocked
	against the hard beach.

Chorus.
	Oioioi. Cry out. Where is your Pharnuchus,
	and good Ariomardus?
	Where is Seualces the prince,
	or Lilaeus of the noble father,
	Memphis, Tharybis, and Masistras,
	Artembares and Hystaechmas?
	This I ask you again.

Xerxes.
	Io, io, moi, moi.
	They looked on ancient Athens,
	the hateful city, and all with one stroke of the oar,
	eh, eh, the poor wretches lie gasping on the land.

Chorus.
	And your Eye, the faithful Eye,
	who counted the Persians for you
	ten thousand, ten thousand at a time,
	Alpistus, son of Batanochus,
	of the line of Sesames, of Megabates,
	and Parthus, and great Oebares,
	did you leave them, leave them?
	Oh, oh, oh, for the wretched men.
	You are telling the noble Persians of evils beyond evil.

Xerxes.
	Io, io, yes,
	you remind me of the longing for my good companions,
	you stir what cannot be forgotten, hateful, evil beyond evil.
	The heart inside my body shouts, shouts.

Chorus.
	And there are others too that we miss:
	the commander of ten thousand Mardian men,
	Xanthis, and warlike Anchares,
	and Diaexis and Arsaces,
	masters of horse,
	Cegdadatas and Lythimnas,
	and Tolmus, who never had enough of the spear.
	I am amazed, amazed
	that they are not following
	behind your wheeled tent.

Xerxes.
	They are gone, the men who led the army.

Chorus.
	Gone, oi, and without a name.

Xerxes.
	Ie, ie. Io, io.

Chorus.
	Io, io, you gods,
	you made an evil no one hoped for,
	plain to see, like the look in Ruin's eyes.

Xerxes.
	We are struck, what a blow, for all time.

Chorus.
	We are struck. It is plain enough.

Xerxes.
	New misery, new misery.

Chorus.
	We met the Ionian seamen
	and our luck was bad.
	The race of the Persians is unlucky in war.

Xerxes.
	How not? An army so large,
	and I am struck down, wretched.

Chorus.
	And what is not? Everything the Persians had is destroyed.

Xerxes.
	Do you see this, what is left of my robe?

Chorus.
	I see, I see.

Xerxes.
	And this, that held the arrows

Chorus.
	What is this you say was saved?

Xerxes.
	a treasure-house for shafts?

Chorus.
	Little enough, out of so much.

Xerxes.
	We are stripped of defenders.

Chorus.
	The Ionian people do not run from the spear.

Xerxes.
	Too brave by far. And I saw a disaster I never expected.

Chorus.
	You mean the fleet, turned and routed?

Xerxes.
	I tore my robe when the evil fell.

Chorus.
	Papai, papai.

Xerxes.
	And more than papai.

Chorus.
	Yes, for double and triple

Xerxes.

Chorus.
	And our strength is cut short

Xerxes.

Chorus.

Xerxes.

Chorus.

Xerxes.

Chorus.
