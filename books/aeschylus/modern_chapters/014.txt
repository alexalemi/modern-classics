Prometheus Bound
(Part 2 of 2)

Prometheus.
I will tell you plainly everything you want to know, no riddles
woven into it, in simple words, the way it is right to open your
mouth to friends. You are looking at Prometheus, who gave fire to
mortals.

Io.
You, who showed yourself the common benefactor of the human race,
poor suffering Prometheus, what is this the punishment for?

Prometheus.
I have only just stopped weeping over my own troubles.

Io.
Then will you not do this one favour for me?

Prometheus.
Say what it is you are asking. You can learn anything from me.

Io.
Tell me who clamped you into this ravine.

Prometheus.
The plan was Zeus's. The hand was Hephaestus's.

Io.
And what crimes are you paying for?

Prometheus.
I have made enough clear to you already. That is all.

Io.
Then tell me this instead: where does my wandering end? How long
does a wretched girl like me have to go on?

Prometheus.
For you, not knowing is better than knowing.

Io.
Do not hide from me what I am going to suffer.

Prometheus.
It is not that I begrudge you the gift.

Io.
Then why do you hold back from telling me everything?

Prometheus.
There is no grudge in it. I am afraid of shattering your mind.

Io.
Do not take more care of me than I want taken.

Prometheus.
Since you insist, I have to speak. Listen, then.

Chorus.
Not yet. Give me my share of the pleasure too. Let us hear about her
sickness first, and let her tell us herself the disasters that have
ruined her life. Then she can learn from you what struggles are still
to come.

Prometheus.
Io, it is your job to do them this kindness, all the more since they
are your father's sisters. And weeping and wailing over your bad luck
is time well spent when the people listening are going to give you a
tear for it.

Io.
I do not see how I can refuse you. You will hear everything you want
to hear, in plain words. And yet even telling it shames me: the storm
the gods sent, the ruin of my body, and where it came from, swooping
down on me, poor wretch.

Night after night, visions came into my room, into a girl's room, and
coaxed me with smooth words. "Lucky, lucky girl, why stay a virgin so
long, when the greatest marriage of all is yours for the taking? Zeus
has been warmed by the arrow of desire for you, and wants to take
love's pleasure with you. Child, do not kick away the bed of Zeus.
Go out to Lerna, to the deep meadow, to your father's sheep and
cattle pens, so that the eye of Zeus can get some relief from its
longing."

Dreams like that held me every night, and I was miserable, until at
last I dared to tell my father about the dreams that came walking in
the dark. He sent messenger after messenger to Pytho and to Dodona,
to find out what he had to do or say to please the gods. They came
back reporting oracles with shifting mouths, oracles that meant
nothing, worded so that no one could make them out. Then at last a
clear message came to Inachus, plainly charging him, plainly telling
him, to push me out of his house and out of my country, to roam loose
to the farthest edges of the earth. And if he would not, a fiery
thunderbolt would come from Zeus and wipe out his whole family.

He believed those oracles of Apollo. He drove me out and barred his
doors against me, against his will and against mine. But the bridle
of Zeus was on him, and it forced him to do it. At once my body and
my mind were twisted out of shape, and I grew horns, as you see, and
a gadfly with a sharp mouth stung me, and I went leaping, mad, to the
sweet stream of Cerchnea and the spring of Lerna. And a herdsman born
from the earth, Argus, hot-tempered and never cooling, came after me,
watching every step I took with his crowd of eyes. A sudden death
that no one expected took his life from him. But I, stung by the fly,
lashed by the gods' whip, am driven from land to land.

You have heard what happened. If you can say what struggles are still
left, tell me. And do not pity me and warm me with lies. I say that
made-up words are the most shameful sickness there is.

Chorus.
	Ah, ah. Keep it away. Oh.
	Never, never did I think
	words so strange would come to my ears,
	or that sufferings so hard to look at,
	so hard to bear,
	pain and filth and terror,
	would strike my soul with a two-pointed goad.
	Oh, oh, Fate, Fate,
	I shudder to see what has been done to Io.

Prometheus.
You are groaning too soon. You are full of fear. Wait until you have
learned the rest as well.

Chorus.
Speak. Teach us. When someone is sick, it is sweet to know clearly
beforehand what pain is still coming.

Prometheus.
You got your first request out of me easily enough. You wanted to
hear her tell the story of her own struggle first. Now hear the rest,
the sufferings this young woman still has to endure at Hera's hands.
And you, seed of Inachus, throw my words into your heart, so that you
learn where your road ends.

First, from here, turn yourself towards the rising sun and walk
across plains that have never seen a plough. You will come to the
Scythian nomads, who live in woven houses raised high off the ground
on wagons with good round wheels, and carry bows that shoot a long
way. Do not go near them. Keep your feet close to the shore, where
the sea groans on the rocks, and pass on out of their country. On
your left hand live the Chalybes, who work iron. You must watch out
for them. They are savages, and strangers cannot approach them. Then
you will reach the river Hybristes, the Violent, and it does not lie
about its name. Do not cross it. It is not an easy river to cross.
Go on until you come to Caucasus itself, the highest of all
mountains, where the river blows its strength out of the very brows
of the peak. You must climb over those summits, which are neighbours
of the stars, and take the road south. There you will come to the
army of the Amazons, who hate men. One day they will settle
Themiscyra on the river Thermodon, where the rough jaw of Salmydessus
opens on the sea, a hateful host to sailors, a stepmother to ships.
The Amazons will guide you on your way, and gladly. Then, right at
the narrow gates of the lake, you will come to the Cimmerian isthmus.
Leave it behind you with a bold heart, and cross the channel of
Maeotis. And for all time mortals will tell the great story of your
crossing, and it will be named after you: the Bosporus, the Cow's
Ford. Then you will leave the soil of Europe and come to the
continent of Asia.

Well? Does it not seem to you that the tyrant of the gods is violent
in everything alike? He is a god, he wanted to sleep with a mortal
girl, and he threw these wanderings on her. Girl, you have found a
bitter suitor for your hand. And the words you have just heard, you
must understand, are not even the introduction.

Io.
Oh, oh, oh. Ee, ee.

Prometheus.
Crying out again? Groaning again? What will you do when you learn
the evils that are still to come?

Chorus.
You mean there are more sufferings still to tell her?

Prometheus.
A whole stormy sea of them, a sea of ruinous misery.

Io.
Then what do I gain by living? Why did I not throw myself off this
rough rock at once, dash myself on the ground and be free of all my
troubles? Better to die once than to suffer badly every day of my
life.

Prometheus.
Then you would find my struggles hard to bear. It is not fated for me
to die. Death would have been a release from my sufferings. As it is,
there is no end set to my troubles until Zeus falls from his tyranny.

Io.
What? Is Zeus really going to fall from power one day?

Prometheus.
You would be glad, I think, to see that happen.

Io.
How could I not be, when it is Zeus who makes me suffer?

Prometheus.
Then you may know it for a fact: it will.

Io.
Who will strip him of the tyrant's sceptre?

Prometheus.
He will. Himself, and his own empty-headed plans.

Io.
How? Tell me, if there is no harm in telling.

Prometheus.
He will make a marriage that one day he will be sorry for.

Io.
To a goddess, or a mortal? If it can be said, say it.

Prometheus.
Why ask to whom? That cannot be spoken aloud.

Io.
Is it his wife who will throw him off his throne?

Prometheus.
She will bear a son who is stronger than his father.

Io.
And is there no way for him to turn this fate aside?

Prometheus.
None. Unless I were set free from these chains.

Io.
Then who is going to free you, against the will of Zeus?

Prometheus.
It has to be one of your own descendants.

Io.
What did you say? A child of mine will free you from your suffering?

Prometheus.
The third generation after ten more.

Io.
Now your prophecy is getting too hard to read.

Prometheus.
Then do not try to learn the whole of your own suffering either.

Io.
Do not hold out a gift to me and then snatch it back.

Prometheus.
I will make you a present of one of two stories.

Io.
Which two? Show me them, and let me choose.

Prometheus.
I am giving you the choice. Either I tell you clearly the rest of
your sufferings, or I tell you who will set me free.

Chorus.
Do her the one favour and me the other. Do not refuse my request.
Tell her the rest of her wandering. Tell me who will set you free.
That is what I long to hear.

Prometheus.
Since you are set on it, I will not refuse to declare everything you
want to know. To you first, Io, I will describe your wandering, all
the tossing about that is still to come. Engrave it on the tablets
of your mind, where memory keeps its records.

When you have crossed the stream that divides the two continents,
turn towards the flaming east, where the sun walks. You will cross
the roaring sea until you reach the Gorgon plains of Cisthene, where
the daughters of Phorcys live, three ancient girls shaped like swans,
who share one eye between them and have one tooth. The sun never
looks down on them with his rays, and the moon never sees them by
night. Near them live their three winged sisters, the Gorgons, with
snakes for hair, whom the human race hates, and no mortal who has
looked at them has drawn another breath. That is one guard-post I am
warning you about.

Now hear about another sight, an ugly one. Watch out for the hounds
of Zeus that never bark, the griffins with their sharp beaks, and for
the one-eyed army of the Arimaspians, who ride on horses and live
around the stream of Pluto's river, whose waters run with gold. Do
not go near them. Then you will come to a far country, a dark-skinned
people who live beside the springs of the sun, where the river
Aethiops runs. Go along its banks until you reach the great drop,
where the Nile sends down his holy, sweet stream from the Bybline
mountains. He will lead you on to the three-cornered land of the
Nile, the land Nile makes, where at last, Io, it is fated that you
and your children will found your far-off colony.

If any of this is stammered and hard for you to make out, go back
over it and learn it clearly. I have more spare time than I want.

Chorus.
If there is anything left, or anything you passed over, that you can
tell her about her wandering and its ruin, say it. But if you have
told everything, then do us the favour we asked in our turn. You
remember it, I think.

Prometheus.
She has heard her whole journey to its end. But so that she knows
she has not been listening to me for nothing, I will tell her what
she went through before she came here, and give her that as proof
that my words are true. I will leave out the great crowd of details
and go straight to the last stretch of your wandering.

When you reached the Molossian plains and the high ridge that stands
round Dodona, where the oracle and the seat of Thesprotian Zeus are,
and that wonder no one believes in, the talking oaks, you were hailed
by them, brightly and with no riddle in it at all, as the one who
was going to be the famous wife of Zeus. Does any of that flatter
you? From there the gadfly stung you, and you rushed along the coast
road to the great gulf of Rhea, and from there you are tossed back
the way you came, wandering in reverse. And for all time to come,
know this for certain, that inlet of the sea will be called the
Ionian Sea, a memorial of your journey to the whole human race.

Those are the signs I give you that my mind sees further than what
has been shown. The rest I will tell to you and to her together,
coming back onto the track of my earlier story.

Prometheus.
There is a city called Canobus at the very edge of the land, right at
the mouth of the Nile, on the silt it lays down. There Zeus will give
you back your senses, stroking you with a hand that means no harm,
and only touching you. And from that touch of Zeus you will bear a
child and name him for it: dark Epaphus, the Touched One, who will
harvest all the land the broad-flowing Nile waters.

Five generations after him, a family of fifty daughters will come
back to Argos, and not by their own choice: they will be running from
marriage to their cousins, their own blood. And the cousins, their
hearts fluttering, like hawks left not far behind the doves, will
come hunting a marriage that should not be hunted. But the god will
begrudge them the women's bodies. Pelasgian soil will take them in,
after a War made for killing men is fought by women, and the men are
beaten by a daring that keeps watch by night. For each wife will rob
her husband of his life, dipping a two-edged sword in his throat. May
love come to my enemies like that.

But one of the daughters will be charmed by desire, and will not kill
the man who shares her bed. The edge will go off her resolve. Of two
names she will choose to be called the weaker one: coward rather than
murderess. And she will give birth in Argos to a line of kings.

It would take a long speech to go through all this clearly. But from
that seed there will be born a bold man, famous with the bow, who
will set me free from these sufferings. That is the prophecy my
mother told me, Themis, the Titan born long ago. How, and by what
means, would take a long story to tell, and you would gain nothing by
learning it.

Io.
	Eleleu, eleleu.
	Again the spasm, again the madness
	that hammers the brain, it heats me,
	the gadfly's barb, forged in no fire,
	stings me,
	and my heart kicks at my ribs in terror.
	My eyes roll and spin,
	I am carried out of my course
	by the raging blast of frenzy,
	I have lost the mastery of my tongue,
	and muddy words beat wildly
	against the waves of hateful ruin.

(Io runs off, driven by the gadfly, and does not come back.)

Chorus.
	Wise, he was wise, the man
	who first weighed this in his mind
	and put it into words with his tongue,
	that to marry in your own rank is far the best,
	and that a man who works with his hands
	should not set his heart on marrying
	among people gone soft with money
	or people swollen with their great birth.

Chorus.
	Never, never, you Fates,
	you powerful ones,
	may you see me made
	a partner of the bed of Zeus,
	never may I come near
	a bridegroom from the sky.
	For I shudder when I look at Io,
	a virgin no man loved,
	worn down like this
	by Hera's cruel wandering, by pain.

Chorus.
	For me, when a marriage is level,
	there is no fear in it. I am not afraid.
	But never let the love of the greater gods
	fix me with the eye no one escapes.
	That is a war that cannot be fought,
	a road with no way out and no way through,
	and I do not know what would become of me.
	For I cannot see
	how I could escape the mind of Zeus.

Prometheus.
And yet Zeus, stubborn as his heart is, will be humbled. The marriage
he is getting ready to make will throw him out of his tyranny and off
his throne into nothing. And then the curse his father Cronus laid on
him, the curse he spoke as he fell from his ancient throne, will be
fulfilled to the last word. No god can show him the way out of those
troubles except me. I know what is coming and I know how.

So let him sit there, confident, trusting in the crashes he makes up
in the air, shaking his fire-breathing bolt in his hands. None of it
will keep him from falling, dishonoured, a fall past bearing. Such is
the wrestler he is getting ready now against himself, a wonder that
cannot be fought, who will find a flame stronger than the lightning
and a mighty crash that outroars the thunder, and will shatter the
trident, Poseidon's spear, that plague of the sea that shakes the
earth. And when he has tripped over that evil, he will learn how far
apart it is to be a ruler and to be a slave.

Chorus.
It is only what you wish for that your tongue is putting on Zeus.

Prometheus.
I say what will come to pass, and what I want as well.

Chorus.
Are we really to expect that someone will be master over Zeus?

Prometheus.
Yes. And he will have a neck-load of pains harder to carry than
these of mine.

Chorus.
How are you not afraid, flinging out words like that?

Prometheus.
What should I fear, when I am not fated to die?

Chorus.
But he could give you a struggle even more painful than this one.

Prometheus.
Let him do it. I expect everything.

Chorus.
The wise are the ones who bow down to Necessity, the goddess no one
escapes.

Prometheus.
Worship, pray, fawn on whoever holds the power at the time. I care
less than nothing for Zeus. Let him act, let him hold power for this
short time of his, however he likes. He will not rule the gods for
long. But look. Here I see the runner of Zeus, the errand boy of the
new tyrant. He has come to announce something new, no doubt.

(Hermes comes in, the messenger of Zeus, with the wand and the
winged sandals of his office.)

Hermes.
You. The clever one. Bitter beyond all bitterness. The one who
sinned against the gods by handing out their honours to creatures
that live for a day. The thief of fire. I am talking to you. The
Father orders you to say what marriage this is you keep boasting
about, the one that is going to throw him out of power. And none of
it in riddles: set out every single point as it stands. And do not
make me come twice, Prometheus. You can see that Zeus is not softened
by tricks like yours.

Prometheus.
That is a pompous speech, and full of self-importance, as you would
expect from a servant of the gods. You are new, and you hold new
power, and you really think you live in a stronghold that grief can
never reach. Have I not seen two tyrants thrown out of it already?
And I will see the third, the one who is lord now, thrown out too,
most shamefully and most quickly. Do you think I tremble in front of
these new gods and cower down? I am a long way from that. All the way
from it. Now hurry back along the road you came by. You will learn
nothing you are asking me about.

Hermes.
It was wilfulness exactly like this that ran you into this harbour of
misery before.

Prometheus.
I would not trade my bad luck for your slavery. Be clear about that.

Hermes.
Better, I suppose, to be a slave to this rock than to be born the
trusted messenger of Father Zeus.

Prometheus.
That is how outrage should be answered: with outrage.

Hermes.
You seem to be enjoying your present situation.

Prometheus.
Enjoying it? I hope I see my enemies enjoying themselves like this.
And I count you among them.

Hermes.
What? You blame me too, somehow, for your disasters?

Prometheus.
In one word: I hate every god that I did good to and that repays me
with evil, against all justice.

Hermes.
I hear you. You are sick with no small madness.

Prometheus.
Sick I may be, if it is a sickness to hate your enemies.

Hermes.
You would be unbearable if things were going well for you.

Prometheus.
Oh.

Hermes.
Oh? That is a word Zeus does not know.

Prometheus.
But Time, as he grows old, teaches everything.

Hermes.
And yet you, at least, have not learned yet to keep yourself in hand.

Prometheus.
No. Or I would not be talking to you, an underling.

Hermes.
It seems you will say nothing of what the Father wants.

Prometheus.
Of course. I owe him so much. I ought to pay him back the favour.

Hermes.
You mock me as if I were a child.

Prometheus.
And are you not a child, and stupider than a child, if you expect to
learn anything from me? There is no torture and no trick by which
Zeus will make me speak this, until these chains that shame me are
loosened. So let him throw his blazing flame. Let him stir up
everything and shake it with white-winged snow and thunder under the
earth. None of it will bend me into saying by whose hand he is fated
to fall from his tyranny.

Hermes.
Look, now, and see if this course seems likely to help you.

Prometheus.
It was looked at long ago, and decided.

Hermes.
Bring yourself, you fool, bring yourself at last to think straight
about the sufferings you are in.

Prometheus.
You are wasting your breath on me, as if you were talking a wave
round. Never let it enter your head that I will turn woman-hearted
out of fear of what Zeus intends, and beg the one I hate most, with
my hands turned up like a woman's, to free me from these chains. I
am the whole width of the world from that.

Hermes.
I think I am going to say a lot and say it for nothing. Nothing I ask
softens you. Nothing wears you down. Like a colt fresh under the yoke
you bite the bit and fight against the reins. But the cleverness you
are so violent about is a feeble thing. Wilfulness, on its own, in a
man who does not think straight, has less strength than nothing at
all.

Think, if you will not be persuaded by my words, what a storm, what a
triple wave of evils is going to break on you, with no escape. First
the Father will tear this jagged ravine apart with thunder and the
flame of lightning, and hide your body, and the rock will hold you in
its stony arms. And when you have finished a long stretch of time,
you will come back up into the light. And then the winged hound of
Zeus, the blood-red eagle, will tear your body greedily into great
rags, a guest who comes to dinner uninvited and stays the whole day,
and will feast on your liver until it is black with his gnawing. Do
not expect any end to that agony until some god appears to take over
your sufferings, and is willing to go down into Hades, where there is
no light, and into the black depths of Tartarus.

Think about it, then. This is no invented boast. It is said all too
truly. The mouth of Zeus does not know how to lie: he brings every
word to pass. So look around you, and think, and never believe that
wilfulness is better than good counsel.

Chorus.
To us, at least, what Hermes says does not seem out of place. He is
telling you to let go of your wilfulness and go looking for the good
counsel that is wisdom. Do as he says. It is shameful for a wise man
to go on being wrong.

Prometheus.
	I knew all that before he came
	bawling his message at me.
	And for an enemy to suffer
	at the hands of enemies is no disgrace.
	So let it come.
	Let the two-edged curl of fire be thrown at me,
	let the air be torn
	with thunder and the spasm of wild winds,
	let the blast shake the earth from its foundations,
	roots and all,
	let the waves of the sea in their rough surge
	pile up over the paths of the stars in heaven,
	and let him lift my body
	and hurl it down to black Tartarus
	in the hard whirlpools of Necessity.
	Whatever he does, he cannot kill me.

Hermes.
	These are the plans and these are the words
	you hear from men whose minds are struck.
	What is missing, what does his prayer lack,
	to make it raving? Where does his madness slacken?
	But you, at least, you who share
	the labour of his sufferings,
	get away from this place, somewhere, quickly,
	before the bellow of the thunder
	that nothing softens stuns your minds.

Chorus.
	Say something else. Give me advice
	I could actually take.
	That word you swept past me just now
	is not a thing I can stand.
	How can you order me to practise cowardice?
	Whatever he has to suffer, I want to suffer with him.
	I have learned to hate traitors,
	and there is no sickness
	I spit out more than that one.

Hermes.
	Then remember what I am telling you now,
	and when you are caught in the hunt of ruin
	do not blame your luck,
	and never say that Zeus
	threw you into a suffering you could not foresee.
	No. You did it to yourselves.
	You knew. It was not sudden, it was not secret.
	Your own stupidity will tangle you
	in the net of ruin, the net with no way out.

(Hermes goes out. The storm begins.)

Prometheus.
	Now it is deed and no longer word.
	The earth is rocking.
	The thunder's echo bellows up from the deep,
	the curls of lightning flash out, all fire,
	whirlwinds spin the dust,
	the blasts of all the winds leap at each other
	and show their fight, breath against breath,
	and the sky is churned into the sea.
	This is the blow that Zeus sends against me,
	openly, to make me afraid.
	O holiness of my mother,
	O sky, wheeling the light that everyone shares,
	you see me. You see what injustice I suffer.

(Thunder and lightning. The rock splits and Prometheus goes down
into the earth, and the daughters of Oceanus go down with him.)
