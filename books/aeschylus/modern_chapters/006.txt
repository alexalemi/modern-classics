Agamemnon
(Part 1 of 3)

(Argos, at night, before the palace of the sons of Atreus. A Watchman
lies on the flat roof, looking out towards the hills.)

Watchman.
I ask the gods for release from this work, this watch that has run a
whole year. All that time I have lain up here on the roof of the sons
of Atreus, propped on my elbow like a dog, and I have come to know the
whole gathering of the night stars, and the bright lords that bring
winter and summer to men, the stars that stand out in the sky, when
they set and when they rise. And now I am watching for the signal of
the torch, the gleam of fire that will carry word from Troy, the news
that the city has fallen. That is the order of a woman whose heart
hopes like a man's and plans like a man's.

So I keep this bed of mine, a bed that wanders through the night, wet
with dew, a bed no dreams come to, because fear stands beside it
instead of sleep, and I cannot shut my eyes properly and sleep. And
whenever I think I will sing, or hum a tune, cutting myself a dose of
song as a cure for sleep, then I weep instead, groaning for the luck
of this house, which is not run as well as it used to be. But tonight,
let there be a lucky release from these labours. Let the fire of good
news show out of the dark.

(A fire flares up on the far hills.)

Oh, welcome, lamp of the night, showing a light like day, and the
setting up of many dances in Argos to give thanks for this stroke of
luck. Iou, iou! I give the sign, loud and clear, to Agamemnon's wife:
up from her bed, and as fast as she can, raise the cry of joy through
the house to welcome this torch, if the city of Ilium is truly taken,
as that beacon blazing there announces. And I will dance the opening
steps myself. My masters' dice have fallen well, and I am counting it
as my own throw: this beacon-watch has thrown me three sixes.

Well, may it happen that the master of the house comes home, and that
I hold his dear hand in this hand of mine. The rest I keep quiet
about. A great ox has stepped on my tongue. The house itself, if it
could find a voice, would tell the story plainest. As for me, I choose
to speak to those who know. For those who do not know, I have
forgotten.

(The Watchman goes down into the house. The old men of Argos, the
Chorus, come in below.)

Chorus.
	This is the tenth year since Priam's great opponent at law,
	King Menelaus, and Agamemnon with him,
	the strong yoked pair of Atreus' sons,
	honoured by Zeus with two thrones and two sceptres,
	put out from this land
	with a thousand ships full of Argives,
	an army to back their claim,
	screaming a great war-cry out of their hearts
	the way vultures do
	when, in wild grief for their young,
	they wheel high above the nest,
	rowing with the oars of their wings,
	the long labour of guarding the chicks
	lost, all of it.
	And someone on high, Apollo,
	or Pan, or Zeus, hears the shrill, sharp
	bird-cry of these lodgers in his sky
	and sends a Fury on the ones who broke the law,
	late, but she comes.
	Just so the stronger one, Zeus, god of guest and host,
	sends the sons of Atreus against Paris,
	all for a woman with many husbands,
	and he will lay many wrestling-bouts, limb-heavy,
	the knee driven into the dust,
	the spear-shaft snapped in the first rites,
	on Danaans and Trojans alike.
	It is where it is now.
	It is being brought to its appointed end.
	Not by burning under, not by pouring under,
	not by tears will a man charm away
	the stiff anger of sacrifices that will not take fire.
	But we, who cannot pay our way with old flesh,
	left behind when that army went to help,
	stay here, steering a strength like a child's
	on our walking sticks.
	For the young marrow, king inside the breast,
	is like an old man's: Ares is not in his place.
	And great old age, its leaves already drying,
	walks its three-footed roads,
	no stronger than a child,
	a dream that wanders about in daylight.
	But you, Tyndareus' daughter,
	Queen Clytemnestra,
	what is it? What is new? What have you heard,
	what message has persuaded you
	to send round the order for sacrifice?
	All the gods that hold the city,
	the high gods, the gods under the earth,
	the gods of the sky and the gods of the marketplace,
	their altars are blazing with gifts,
	and here and there and everywhere a flame
	rises high as the sky,
	drugged with the soft, honest coaxing
	of holy oil, the royal offering
	brought up from the palace's inner store.
	Tell us what you can of this,
	and what is lawful to tell,
	and be the healer of this worry,
	which now turns to dark thoughts,
	and now the hope that shines gentle from the sacrifices
	pushes back the care that never gets its fill,
	the grief that eats the heart.

Chorus.
	I have the right to sing of the sign that met the men on the road,
	the lucky sign of power, of grown men in their strength,
	for still the gods breathe into me
	persuasion, the strength of song, the age that grew up with me:
	how the twin-throned power of the Achaeans,
	the two commanders of one mind over the young men of Greece,
	was sent with spear and avenging hand
	against the Trojan land by the rushing bird,
	the king of birds to the kings of the ships,
	one black, one white behind,
	appearing near the palace on the spear-hand side,
	perched where everyone could see them,
	feeding on a hare, heavy with the young inside her,
	caught in her last run.
	Sing sorrow, sing sorrow, but let the good win out.

Chorus.
	And the army's trusted prophet, seeing the two sons of Atreus,
	two in temper as well, knew the eaters of the hare
	for the leaders who sent the army out, and read it like this:
	"In time this march takes Priam's city,
	and all the herds the people own before its towers
	Fate will plunder by force.
	Only let no jealousy from the gods darken
	the great bit forged for the mouth of Troy, the army,
	and strike it down before its time.
	For holy Artemis pities, and hates
	the winged hounds of her father
	that sacrificed the poor cowering thing
	with her young, before the birth.
	She loathes the eagles' feast."
	Sing sorrow, sing sorrow, but let the good win out.

Chorus.
	"Lovely goddess, so kind
	to the dewy cubs of savage lions,
	so delighted in the suckling young
	of every beast that roams the fields,
	grant that what these signs mean comes true,
	the omen of the birds, lucky on the right hand, but with a fault in it.
	And I call on Apollo the Healer:
	let her not raise against the Danaans
	long winds that hold the ships and will not let them sail,
	pressing for another sacrifice, one with no law, one no one may eat,
	a builder of quarrels born into the family,
	a thing that fears no husband. For there waits at home,
	terrible, rising up again, a keeper of the house full of tricks,
	a Wrath that remembers, that avenges a child."
	Such were the fates Calchas screamed out, along with great good,
	from the birds on the road, for the royal house.
	And in tune with it,
	Sing sorrow, sing sorrow, but let the good win out.

Chorus.
	Zeus, whoever he is,
	if that is the name he likes to be called,
	that is what I call him.
	I have weighed everything
	and I have nothing to set beside him,
	nothing but Zeus, if I am truly to throw
	this useless weight off my mind.

Chorus.
	Not the one who was great before,
	swollen with a daring that would fight anyone:
	he will not even be spoken of. He was, and is gone.
	And the one who came after him
	met his thrower, three falls, and is gone.
	But whoever gladly shouts the victory song for Zeus
	will hit the mark of understanding, all of it.

Chorus.
	Zeus, who set mortals on the road to thinking,
	who made it the law that stands:
	learning through suffering.
	In sleep, in front of the heart,
	the pain that remembers drips,
	and against their will men come to their senses.
	It is a violent kindness, somehow, from the gods
	who sit at the helm on their solemn bench.

Chorus.
	And so, at that time, the older leader of the Achaean ships,
	blaming no prophet,
	bending with the winds of luck that beat on him,
	when the Achaean people were weighed down
	by no sailing and empty bellies,
	holding the shore across from Chalcis
	at Aulis, where the tide roars back and forth,

Chorus.
	the winds came down from the Strymon,
	winds of bad idleness, of hunger, of bad anchorage,
	that set men wandering, that spared neither ships nor cables,
	and doubled the length of the waiting
	and wore the flower of the Argives down by rubbing.
	And when the prophet screamed out to the chiefs
	a cure for the bitter storm
	heavier still than the storm, and named Artemis as its cause,
	so that the sons of Atreus struck the ground with their sticks
	and could not hold back their tears,

Chorus.
	then the older king spoke and said:
	"It is a heavy fate not to obey,
	and heavy if I cut down my child, the glory of my house,
	staining a father's hands at the altar
	with streams of a slaughtered girl's blood.
	Which of these is without evil?
	How can I desert the fleet
	and fail my allies?
	For them to want, with passion beyond passion,
	a sacrifice to stop the winds, a virgin's blood,
	is their right. May it turn out well."

Chorus.
	When he had put on the yoke-strap of Necessity,
	a wind shifting in his mind,
	impious, unclean, unholy,
	from that moment he changed, and would think anything, dare anything.
	For men are made bold by the wretched madness
	that plans shame and is the first cause of pain.
	He steeled himself, then, to be the sacrificer of his daughter,
	to help a war fought to avenge a woman,
	a first rite for the ships.

Chorus.
	Her prayers, her cries of "Father,"
	her girl's life: the judges who loved war
	counted them as nothing.
	Her father said a prayer and told the servants
	to lift her over the altar like a goat,
	as she fell forward with all her heart into her robes,
	to lift her up bodily, and to hold her lovely mouth
	with a guard against any word
	that might curse the house,

Chorus.
	by force, by the bit, by a strength that gave her no voice.
	Her saffron dress poured onto the ground,
	and she struck each of the men who were sacrificing her
	with a shot from her eyes that begged for pity,
	standing out like a figure in a painting, wanting to speak,
	because many times, in her father's hall at the good tables,
	she had sung, and with a pure voice, untouched by any man,
	lovingly she had honoured her dear father's hymn
	at the third libation, the prayer for good luck,

Chorus.
	What came after that I did not see, and I do not tell.
	The arts of Calchas do not go unfulfilled.
	Justice tips her scales so that those who suffer learn.
	The future you will hear when it happens. Until then, let it go.
	It comes to the same as grieving ahead of time.
	It will come clear with the light of dawn.
	And for what follows, may the outcome be good,
	as she wishes it, the one who stands nearest to our lord,
	the single guard and wall of this Apian land.

(Clytemnestra comes out of the palace.)

Chorus.
I have come, Clytemnestra, out of respect for your power. It is right
to honour the wife of the ruling man when the man's throne stands
empty. Whether you have heard something good, or heard nothing and
are sacrificing on hopes of good news, I would gladly hear it. And if
you keep silent, no one will blame you.

Clytemnestra.
Good news, as the proverb has it: may Dawn be born from her mother
Night. You will hear of a joy greater than any you hoped to hear. The
Argives have taken Priam's city.

Chorus.
What did you say? The word ran past me. I could not believe it.

Clytemnestra.
Troy belongs to the Achaeans. Am I speaking clearly?

Chorus.
Joy is creeping over me, and it is calling out my tears.

Clytemnestra.
Yes. Your eye gives you away. You mean well by us.

Chorus.
But what is the proof? Do you have evidence of this?

Clytemnestra.
I do. Why would I not? Unless a god has cheated me.

Chorus.
Is it the persuasive visions of dreams you are honouring?

Clytemnestra.
I would not take the fancies of a dozing mind for fact.

Chorus.
Then has some rumour without wings fattened your hopes?

Clytemnestra.
You are finding fault with my wits as if I were a little girl.

Chorus.
And how long ago was the city sacked?

Clytemnestra.
In the night, I tell you, that has just now given birth to this
light.

Chorus.
And what messenger could get here so fast?

Clytemnestra.
Hephaestus, sending out a bright blaze from Ida. Beacon sent beacon
here, fire relayed by courier fire. Ida to the Hermes crag on Lemnos.
Third from the island, the great torch was taken up by the peak of
Athos, the mountain of Zeus; and rising high, high enough to ride on
the back of the sea, the strength of the travelling beacon went on
joyfully, the pine-wood shining gold like a sun, and passed the blaze
on to the lookouts of Macistus. And the man there did not delay, was
not beaten stupidly by sleep, and did not neglect his share of the
message: far off, the beacon's light came to the streams of Euripus
and signalled to the watchmen on Messapion. They answered with a
blaze and passed it on, setting fire to a heap of old heather. The
lamp, strong, not dimmed yet, leaped the plain of Asopus like a bright
moon to the crag of Cithaeron and woke another relay of the courier
fire. The watch there did not refuse the far-sent light but burned
more than they were told; the light shot across the Gorgon-eyed lake,
and reaching Mount Aegiplanctus it urged on the ordinance of fire: no
delay. They light it with unstinting force and send up a great beard
of flame, so that it crossed, blazing, even the headland that looks
down on the Saronic strait. Then it swooped, and came to the peak of
Arachnaeus, the lookout next door to our city. And then it lands on
this roof of the sons of Atreus, this light, whose grandfather is the
fire on Ida.

Those were my rules for the beacon-runners, one runner taking the
light from the next, like a torch-race, and the winner is the one who
ran first and last. That is the proof and the sign I give you: my
husband sent me word from Troy.

Chorus.
Lady, I will pray to the gods again in a moment. But I would like to
hear this story straight through from beginning to end and marvel at
it, so tell it once more.

Clytemnestra.
The Achaeans hold Troy today. I think a cry that will not mix is
rising in the city. Pour vinegar and oil into the same jar and you
would say they stand apart, no friends. So you can hear the voices of
the taken and the takers, two separate fortunes. Some are thrown over
the bodies of husbands and brothers, children over the old fathers who
gave them life, and they wail for the death of their dearest from a
throat that is no longer free. And the others: the night's wandering
labour after battle sets them down hungry at whatever breakfast the
city has, with no order of rank about who goes where, but as each man
drew his lot from luck. They are already living in the captured
houses of Troy, out of the frosts and dews of the open sky, and like
happy men they will sleep the whole night through with no guard.

If they honour properly the gods who hold the city, the gods of the
conquered land, and the seats of the gods, then the takers will not
be taken in their turn. But let no lust fall on the army first, to
plunder what they must not, beaten by greed. They still have to make
the turn for home, run the second leg of the double course, and get
safely back. And if the army comes home without any offence to the
gods, the pain of the dead might still wake up, if no sudden evil
strikes first. That is what you hear from me, a woman. But may the
good win out, with no one in two minds about seeing it. Of many
blessings, I have chosen to enjoy this one.

Chorus.
Lady, you speak with good sense, like a sensible man. Now that I have
heard your trustworthy proofs I am getting ready to address the gods
properly. The labour has earned a thanks worth the giving.

Chorus.
	Zeus the king, and friendly Night,
	who won us great glory,
	you who threw over the towers of Troy
	a net with no gaps in it, so that no one, grown man
	or child, could leap over
	the great dragnet of slavery,
	the ruin that catches everything.
	Great Zeus, god of guest and host, I stand in awe of him,
	who did this, who kept his bow aimed at Paris
	so long, so that the arrow should not fall short
	nor fly past the stars
	for nothing.

Chorus.
	"The stroke of Zeus": they can say it.
	This much you can track down:
	he did as he decided. Someone said
	the gods do not bother to care about men
	who trample the grace of untouchable things.
	That man was impious.
	It is shown now, what comes of daring what must not be dared,
	when men breathe bigger than is just,
	and their houses swell past all reason,
	past the best measure. Let there be enough,
	and no harm in it, so that a man
	with good sense can be satisfied.
	For there is no wall in wealth
	to protect a man who, glutted,
	has kicked the great altar of Justice
	out of sight.

Chorus.
	Wretched Persuasion forces him on,
	the unbearable child of Ruin, who plans ahead.
	Every remedy is useless. It is not hidden;
	the damage shows, a light with a terrible shine.
	Like bad bronze rubbed and knocked,
	he shows black when he is put to the test,
	since he is a child chasing a bird on the wing,
	and he has put a stain on his city no one can bear.
	No god hears his prayers,
	and the man who keeps company with these things,
	the unjust man, they pull down.
	Such was Paris, who came
	to the house of the sons of Atreus
	and shamed the table where he was a guest
	by stealing a wife.

Chorus.
	She left to her own people the clash of shields,
	the churning of spears, the arming of ships,
	and carrying to Ilium destruction for a dowry,
	she went lightly through the gates,
	daring what should not be dared. And the prophets of the house
	groaned loud and said:
	"Oh, oh, the house, the house and its princes!
	Oh, the bed and the tracks where she lay and loved her husband!"
	You can see him: silent, dishonoured, not reproaching,
	sitting apart in bitter grief.
	In his longing for the woman over the sea
	a ghost will seem to rule the house.
	The grace of beautiful statues
	is hateful to the husband;
	in the emptiness of their eyes
	all Aphrodite is gone.

Chorus.
	Grieving shapes come in dreams
	and bring a joy that is useless,
	useless, for when a man thinks he sees good things,
	the vision slips through his hands and is gone,
	never to come back, on wings, down the paths of sleep.
	These are the griefs at home, at the hearth,
	and there are griefs that go far beyond them:
	in every house of all who set out together
	from the land of Greece
	a grief no heart can stand shows plain.
	Many things touch the liver.
	A man knows whom he sent away;
	but instead of men,
	urns and ashes
	come home to each man's house.

Chorus.
	Ares, who trades bodies for gold
	and holds the scales in the battle of spears,
	sends back from Ilium to the families
	a heavy dust out of the fire, wept over with hard tears,
	filling the neat urns with ashes
	in exchange for men.
	They groan and praise him: this one,
	how well he knew battle; that one,
	fell well in the killing,
	for another man's wife.
	Somebody snarls that under his breath,
	and grief with resentment in it creeps against
	the sons of Atreus, who brought the case.
	And others, there, around the wall,
	fine-looking men, hold graves in the earth of Ilium;
	the enemy's soil has covered the men who took it.

Chorus.
	Heavy is the talk of citizens with anger in it.
	It pays the debt of a curse the people have voted.
	My worry waits to hear
	something hidden under the night.
	The gods are not blind to men who kill many.
	The black Furies, in time,
	take the man who is lucky without justice
	and wear him down, turn his luck, and dim him,
	and once he is among the unseen there is no help.
	To be praised beyond measure is heavy;
	the thunderbolt is thrown from the eyes of Zeus.
	I choose a prosperity nobody envies.
	Let me not be a sacker of cities,
	nor let me be taken myself and see
	my life in another man's hands.

Chorus.
	From the beacon of good news
	the quick report runs through the city.
	But is it true? Who knows?
	Or is it some lie from a god?
	Who is so childish, so struck out of his wits,
	that he lets his heart catch fire
	at fresh news from a flame,
	and then, when the story changes, breaks down?
	It fits a woman's command
	to give thanks for a thing before it appears.
	Too ready to believe, a woman's word
	spreads fast over its borders;
	but it dies fast too,
	the glory a woman proclaims.

Chorus.
We will soon know about these light-bringing torches and beacon
watches and fires relayed one to the next, whether they are true, or
whether this pleasant light came like a dream and tricked our minds.
I see a herald coming up from the shore, shaded with olive branches.
And the dust, mud's thirsty sister, right beside him, tells me this:
he will not be dumb, and he will not signal to you by lighting a fire
of mountain wood and sending up smoke. He will either come out and
say "rejoice," and more than the beacon said, or the opposite of
that, and I refuse to say it. Let good be added to the good that has
already shown itself. Whoever prays otherwise for this city, let him
reap the harvest of his own mind's mistake himself.

(The Herald comes in from the shore, with olive branches in his hair.)

Herald.
Oh, soil of my fathers, land of Argos! In the light of the tenth year
I have come back to you. Many hopes broke; I have caught hold of one.
I never expected to die here in the land of Argos and have my share
of the grave I love best. Now greetings, land, and greetings, light
of the sun, and Zeus, highest lord of the country, and the Pythian
lord, no longer shooting his arrows at us. You were hostile enough to
us beside the Scamander. Now be our saviour again, and our healer,
lord Apollo. All the gods of the gathering-place, I address them, and
my own patron, Hermes, dear herald, whom heralds revere; and the
heroes who sent us out: take back kindly what the spear has left of
the army.

Oh, halls of kings, dear roofs, solemn seats, and you gods who face
the sun: if ever before, then now, after so long, receive the king
with honour, with shining eyes. For he has come bringing light in the
darkness to you and to all these people here, King Agamemnon. Give
him a good welcome; it is right, for he has dug Troy up with the
mattock of Zeus who brings justice, and the ground has been worked
over with it. The altars are gone and the seats of the gods, and the
seed of the whole land is dying out. Such a yoke he threw round the
neck of Troy, the king, the elder son of Atreus, a man the gods have
blessed, and he has come home, the most worthy of honour of all men
living. For Paris and the city that paid with him cannot boast that
what they did was greater than what they suffered. Convicted of rape
and of theft, he lost his plunder and mowed his father's house down to
the ground, land and all, in total destruction. The sons of Priam
have paid double for their crimes.

Chorus.
Herald of the Achaeans, from the army, greetings and joy.

Herald.
Joy, yes. I will no longer argue with the gods if they want me dead.

Chorus.
Was it longing for this land of your fathers that wore you down?

Herald.
So much that my eyes are filling with tears of joy.

Chorus.
Then it was a sweet sickness you caught.

Herald.
How so? Teach me, and I will master what you mean.

Chorus.
You were struck with longing for people who longed for you back.

Herald.
You mean this land missed the army that missed it?

Chorus.
So much that I often groaned from a darkened heart.

Herald.
Where did this gloom come from, this hatred weighing on your spirit?

Chorus.
For a long time now I have kept silence as my medicine against harm.

Herald.
How? With the princes away, were there people you feared?

Chorus.
So much that now, in your own words, even death would be a great
kindness.

Herald.
Yes, because it has ended well. Over such a long time, some of it you
could say went smoothly, and some of it was open to blame. Who but
the gods goes without pain the whole length of a lifetime? If I told
you the hardships, the bad quarters, the narrow gangways, the
wretched bedding, what did we not groan over? What did we not get as
our share of the day? And then on land there was worse still to hate.
Our beds were under the enemy's walls, and out of the sky and up from
the meadows the dews drizzled down on us, a steady ruin to our
clothes, making our hair a nest for lice. And if someone told of the
winter that killed the birds, the unbearable snow Ida sent down, or
the heat, when the sea at noon fell asleep on a bed with no wave and
no wind, why grieve over that? The trouble is past. It is past, and
for the dead so past that they will never care even to get up again.

Why count the spent men on the tally? Why should the living feel pain
at bad luck long gone? I say a long goodbye to our misfortunes. For
us, what is left of the Argive army, the gain wins, and the loss does
not tip the scale against it. So it is right that we boast to this
light of the sun, as we fly over sea and land: "The Argive expedition
took Troy at last and nailed up these spoils on the houses of the
gods throughout Greece, a glory for all time." When men hear such
things they must praise the city and its generals; and the grace of
Zeus that brought this about will get its honour. You have the whole
story.

Chorus.
Your words have won and I do not deny it. The old are always young
enough to learn properly. But this news most properly concerns the
house and Clytemnestra, and it makes me rich along with them.

(Clytemnestra comes out of the palace again.)

Clytemnestra.
I raised my cry of joy long ago, when the first night messenger of
fire came, telling of the capture and the overthrow of Ilium. And
somebody sneered at me and said: "On the strength of beacons you
believe Troy is sacked now? How like a woman, to let her heart lift."
Talk like that made me look as if I had lost my way. Still, I went on
sacrificing, and in the woman's way, here and there all over the
city, they raised the cry of joy in the seats of the gods, lulling the
sweet-smelling flame that eats the offerings.

And now, why do you need to tell me more? I shall hear the whole
story from the king himself. And I should hurry to receive my
honoured husband as well as I can when he comes home. For what light
is sweeter for a woman to see than this: to open the gates for her
husband when the god has brought him safe home from the war? Take
this message to my husband: come as fast as he can, the darling of
the city; and when he comes, may he find in his house a wife as
faithful as the one he left, a watchdog of the house, good to him, an
enemy to those who wish him ill, and the same in everything else,
having broken no seal in all this length of time. Of pleasure from
another man, or of any talk of scandal, I know no more than I know
the dipping of bronze.

(Clytemnestra goes back into the palace.)

Herald.
A boast like that, when it is loaded with truth, is no shame for a
noble woman to shout out.

Chorus.
That is how she spoke to you, and you have taken it in: a fine
speech, for those who read it clearly. But tell me, herald. I am
asking about Menelaus. Has he come back safe with you, the beloved
power of this land?

Herald.
There is no way I could tell a lie so beautiful that the people I
love would enjoy it for long.

Chorus.
Then how could you manage to say something both good and true? When
those two split apart, it is hard to hide.

Herald.
The man has vanished from the Achaean fleet, himself and his ship. I
am not lying.

Chorus.
Did he put out from Ilium in plain sight, or did a storm, a burden
shared by all, snatch him away from the fleet?

Herald.
You have hit the mark like a champion archer. A long pain, and you
have said it in a word.

Chorus.
Alive or dead? What did the other sailors say about him?

Herald.
Nobody knows enough to report it clearly, except the Sun, who feeds
everything that grows on the earth.

Chorus.
Then how do you say the storm came on the fleet, by the anger of the
gods, and how did it end?

Herald.
It is not right to stain a day of good words with a tongue that
brings bad news. The honour of the gods is a separate thing. When a
messenger with a grim face brings a city the disasters it prayed
against, the army fallen, one wound for the whole city, the public
wound, and many men cursed out of many houses by the double whip that
Ares loves, a ruin with two spears, a bloody pair in one harness: when
he is loaded with that kind of suffering, that is the time to sing
this hymn of the Furies. But when he comes as the bearer of good
news, of things saved, to a city rejoicing in its good fortune, how
am I to mix the good with the bad, telling of a storm that fell on
the Achaeans, and not without the gods' anger?

For fire and sea, the bitterest of enemies before, swore an oath
together and showed their good faith by destroying the wretched
Argive army. In the night the evil rose, and the waves ran badly. The
Thracian winds smashed the ships against each other; rammed by force,
horn against horn, in the hurricane and the driving rain, they went
off and vanished, spun round by an evil shepherd. When the bright
light of the sun came up, we saw the Aegean sea blooming with the
corpses of Achaean men and the wreckage of ships. As for us and our
ship, its hull unharmed, somebody stole us out of it, or begged us
off, some god, not a man, with his hand on the tiller. Luck the
saviour sat on our ship and was willing, so that we neither took the
surge of the waves at anchor nor ran aground on rocky land. Then,
having escaped a Hades of the sea, in the white daylight, not trusting
our luck, we grazed our thoughts on the new suffering: the army worn
out and badly battered.

And now, if any of them is still breathing, they speak of us as lost,
and why would they not? We think the same of them. May it turn out for
the best. As for Menelaus, first and most of all, expect him to come.
If some ray of the sun finds him alive and seeing, by the devices of
Zeus, who does not yet want to wipe out the family, there is some hope
he will come home again. So much you have heard; and know that what
you have heard is the truth.
