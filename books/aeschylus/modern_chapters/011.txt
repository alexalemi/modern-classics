The Eumenides
(Part 1 of 2)

(Delphi, before the temple of Apollo. The Priestess comes out to open
the day's prophecy.)

Priestess.
First of all the gods, in this prayer, I give the place of honour to
Earth, the first prophet. After her, Themis, who was the second to sit
on this seat of prophecy, her mother's seat, so the story goes. Third,
by allotment, with Themis willing and no one forcing her, another
Titan sat here, a child of Earth, Phoebe. And she gave it as a
birthday gift to Phoebus, Apollo, who carries her name as his own. He
left the lake and the ridge of Delos, put in at the shores of Athena
where the ships come and go, and came to this land and to his seat on
Parnassus. The sons of Hephaestus, the men of Athens, built him a road
and escorted him with great reverence, taming a land that had been
wild. And when he arrived, the people paid him high honour, and so did
Delphus, the lord who steers this country. Zeus filled his mind with
the inspired skill and set him on this throne, the fourth prophet. And
Apollo speaks for Zeus his father.

These are the gods I put first in my prayers. And Athena of the
Forecourt, who stands before the temple, has the place of honour in my
words. I worship the Nymphs, where the Corycian rock is hollow, loved
by the birds, where the spirits come and go. Dionysus holds that
ground, and I do not forget him, not since the day the god led his
Bacchants to war and stitched up a death for Pentheus like a hunted
hare. I call on the springs of the Pleistus, on the power of Poseidon,
and on Zeus the highest, who brings all things to their end. And then
I take my seat on the throne as prophet. May they grant me now an
entrance far better than any before. And if any Greeks are here, let
them come in by lot, in their turn, as the custom is. I prophesy as
the god leads me.

(She goes into the temple. A moment later she comes back out on her
hands and knees.)

Priestess.
Horrible to tell, horrible to see with my own eyes. What is inside has
sent me back out of the house of Apollo so weak that I cannot stand,
cannot walk upright. I am running on my hands, not on my legs. A
frightened old woman is nothing. She is a child again.

I was going in to the inner shrine, all hung with wreaths, and I saw a
man on the navel stone, a man the gods must loathe, sitting in the
seat of a suppliant, his hands dripping blood, holding a sword just
drawn and an olive branch cut from the top of the tree, wreathed
properly with a great tuft of wool, white fleece. That much I can tell
you clearly. And in front of this man an astonishing company of women
was asleep, sitting on the thrones. Not women. Gorgons, I would say.
No, not even Gorgons: I cannot compare them to any picture of Gorgons.
I saw a painting once of the creatures carrying off the feast of
Phineus. These have no wings, that I could see. They are black, and
altogether disgusting. They snore, and the breath that comes out of
them is not to be gone near. Out of their eyes drips a foul liquid.
And their dress is not fit to bring before the statues of the gods or
into the houses of men. I have never seen the tribe this company comes
from, nor the land that can boast it reared this brood without paying
for it, and did not weep for the labour afterwards.

What happens from here is the business of the master of this house,
mighty Apollo himself. He is a healer and a prophet, a reader of
signs, and for other men he cleans the pollution out of their houses.

(She goes. The inside of the temple is seen. Orestes sits at the navel
stone with the Furies asleep on the seats around him, and Hermes
stands behind. Apollo comes out from the inner shrine and stands
beside Orestes.)

Apollo.
No, I will not betray you. I am your guardian to the end, standing
close beside you or far off, and I will not go soft on your enemies.
Look at them now, these mad women, caught. They have dropped asleep,
these loathsome girls, these ancient old-woman children, whom no god,
no man, no beast ever goes near. They were born for evil and nothing
else, because evil darkness is what they live in, and Tartarus under
the earth, hated by men and by the gods of Olympus. All the same: run,
and do not go soft. They will drive you the length of the mainland and
over all the ground a wanderer treads, over the sea and through the
island cities. Do not tire too soon, brooding over this labour like a
herdsman over his cattle. Come to the city of Athena, sit down there,
and take her ancient image in your arms. There we will have judges of
your case, and words that charm, and we will find a way to release you
from these troubles for good. For it was I who persuaded you to take
your mother's life.

Orestes.
Lord Apollo, you know how not to do wrong. And since you know that,
learn not to neglect me either. Your strength to do good is a
guarantee.

Apollo.
Remember: do not let fear beat your mind. And you, Hermes, my own
blood, my father's son, watch over him. Be true to your name and be
his guide. Shepherd this suppliant of mine, for Zeus honours this
right of the outcast, and send him out among men with the good luck of
a good escort.

(Apollo goes back in. Orestes leaves, with Hermes guiding him. The
Ghost of Clytemnestra rises among the sleepers.)

Ghost of Clytemnestra.
Sleep, would you? Hey! What use are sleepers to me? It is your doing
that I am dishonoured like this among the other dead. Because of the
ones I killed, the reproach never lets up among the dead, and I wander
in disgrace. I tell you, I carry the heaviest blame down there because
of them. And after suffering so terribly at the hands of my nearest
and dearest, not one of the powers is angry for my sake, though I was
butchered by a son's hands, a mother-killer's hands. Look at these
gashes in my heart. Look where they came from. The mind asleep has
bright eyes; in the daytime the fate of mortals cannot be seen ahead.
You have lapped up plenty of mine, I tell you: wineless libations,
sober offerings to soothe you, and the solemn feasts of the night that
I used to burn on the hearth of fire, at an hour no other god shares.
And I see all of it trampled underfoot. He has slipped away and gone,
like a fawn, and lightly too. Out of the middle of your nets he
sprang, and he made a great mocking face at you as he went. Hear me.
It is my life I am pleading for. Wake up and think, goddesses under
the earth. It is a dream that calls you now, and the dream is
Clytemnestra, and I am she.

Chorus.
(The sleeping women whine, the way a dog whines in its sleep.)
Mmm. Mmm.

Ghost of Clytemnestra.
Whine away. But the man is gone, far away in flight. He has friends,
and they are not like mine.

Chorus.
(The whining goes on.)
Mmm. Mmm.

Ghost of Clytemnestra.
You sleep too deep. You have no pity for my suffering. Orestes, who
murdered me, his mother, has got away.

Chorus.
(The whine turns into a moan.)
Ohh. Ohh.

Ghost of Clytemnestra.
You moan, you doze. Will you not get up? Now! What work were you ever
made for except doing harm?

Chorus.
(The moaning goes on.)
Ohh. Ohh.

Ghost of Clytemnestra.
Sleep and hard labour, the two true conspirators, have drained the
terrible snake of her strength.

Chorus.
(Two sharp whines together, and then words, still asleep.)
Get him. Get him, get him, get him. Look out.

Ghost of Clytemnestra.
You are hunting a beast in a dream, and you yelp like a dog that never
lets the work out of its mind. What are you doing? Up! Do not let
weariness beat you. Do not let sleep soften you into forgetting my
pain. Let my reproaches sting you in the liver. They are just, and to
those who keep themselves in hand a just reproach is a goad. Send your
bloody breath after him like a wind, dry him up with the heat of it,
the fire out of your bellies. After him. Wither him with a second
chase.

(The Ghost is gone. The Furies wake, one rousing the next.)

Chorus.
Wake up. Wake her, as I am waking you. Still asleep? Up. Kick sleep
off you, and let us see if any of this beginning was for nothing.

Chorus.
	Oh, oh, pupax! We have suffered, friends.
	I have suffered so much, and all for nothing.
	We have suffered a pain hard to bear, oh, popoi,
	an evil past carrying.
	The beast has slipped out of the nets and gone.
	Sleep beat me, and I have lost my prey.

Chorus.
	Oh, son of Zeus, you are a thief.
	Young, you have ridden down the old powers,
	honouring your suppliant, a godless man,
	bitter to the parents that bore him.
	You stole away a mother-killer, and you a god.
	Who will say there is any justice in that?

Chorus.
	The reproach that came to me out of my dreams
	struck me like a charioteer
	with the goad gripped in the middle,
	under the heart, under the liver.
	I can feel it, the heavy, the crushing cold
	of the public scourger's cruel whip.

Chorus.
	This is what they do, the younger gods,
	ruling past all justice
	from a throne that drips blood
	about its foot, about its head.
	I can see the navel stone of the earth
	standing thick with a terrible pollution of blood.

Chorus.
	He is a prophet, and he has fouled his own hearth,
	stained his inner shrine with pollution,
	of his own urging, at his own bidding,
	honouring mortal things against the law of the gods
	and rotting the allotments that are older than he is.

Chorus.
	He is a grief to me too, and he will not get that man loose.
	Even if he runs under the earth, he is never set free.
	Polluted as he is, he will find
	another avenger out of his own blood
	coming down on his head.

(Apollo comes out of the inner shrine, his bow in his hand.)

Apollo.
Out! I order you. Out of this house, quickly. Get out of my prophetic
shrine, or you will take a winged, glittering snake, shot from a
golden bowstring, and bring up black foam in your agony, foam from the
men you have fed on, vomiting the clots of blood you sucked out of
them. This house is no place for you to come near. Your place is where
the sentences are beheading and gouged-out eyes and cut throats, where
boys have their manhood ruined by the destruction of the seed, where
there is maiming and stoning, and where men fixed on a stake under the
spine moan long and pitifully. Do you hear what kind of feast you
love, and why the gods spit you out? The whole shape of your bodies
tells it. Creatures like you should live in the cave of a lion that
drinks blood, not rub your filth off on everyone near you in this
place of oracles. Get out, you goats with no herdsman. There is no god
who loves a flock like you.

Chorus.
Lord Apollo, hear us in our turn. You are not partly to blame for
this. You alone did all of it, and the whole blame is yours.

Apollo.
How so? Draw your speech out that far.

Chorus.
Your oracle told the stranger to kill his mother.

Apollo.
My oracle told him to take payment for his father. What of it?

Chorus.
And then you undertook to receive the fresh blood.

Apollo.
Yes, and I told him to turn to this house for cleansing.

Chorus.
And then you abuse us, the escort that brought him here?

Apollo.
Because it is not fitting for you to come into this house.

Chorus.
But this is the duty assigned to us.

Apollo.
What duty is that? Go on, boast of your fine privilege.

Chorus.
We drive mother-killers out of their homes.

Apollo.
And what about a wife who kills her husband?

Chorus.
That would not be the murder of her own blood by her own hand.

Apollo.
Then you make nothing of the pledges of Hera, who makes a marriage
complete, and of Zeus. You count them dishonoured and worthless. And
Aphrodite is thrown aside, dishonoured, by that argument of yours, and
from her come the dearest things mortals have. The bed a man and a
woman are fated to share is greater than an oath, and Justice stands
guard over it. If, then, when one of them kills the other, you are so
slack that you take no payment and turn no angry eye on them, I say it
is unjust of you to hound Orestes out of his home. One case, I see,
you take very much to heart; the other, plainly, you handle far more
gently. But the goddess Athena will oversee the trial of this.

Chorus.
I will never let that man go. Never.

Apollo.
Then chase him, and pile up more trouble for yourself.

Chorus.
Do not cut my privileges short with your talk.

Apollo.
I would not take your privileges if they were offered to me.

Chorus.
No, because you are called great in any case, at the throne of Zeus.
But I have a mother's blood leading me on. I will hunt that man down
for my case, and I am on his trail now.

Apollo.
And I will stand by my suppliant and rescue him. A man who turns to a
god for cleansing has a terrible anger, among mortals and gods alike,
if the god betrays him of his own free will.

(The Furies go out after Orestes. Apollo goes back into his shrine.
The scene is now Athens, before the temple of Athena. Orestes comes in
and takes hold of the ancient image of the goddess.)

Orestes.
Lady Athena, I have come at Apollo's command. Receive kindly a man
under a curse. I am not one who still needs cleansing, and my hand is
not unclean. The edge is off my guilt already; it has been rubbed away
at other houses and on the travelled roads of men. I have crossed land
and sea alike, keeping the commands of Apollo's oracle, and now I come
to your house and your image, goddess. Here I will keep my place and
wait for the end of my trial.

(The Furies come in, scattered, one after another, hunting his trail
like hounds.)

Chorus.
There. Here is the clear sign of the man. Follow the hints of the
informer who has no voice. The way a hound tracks a wounded fawn, we
track him by the drops of blood. My lungs are heaving with all this
man-killing labour. I have herded every corner of the earth, and over
the sea I came flying without wings, chasing him, as fast as any ship.
And now he is here somewhere, cowering. The smell of human blood
smiles at me.

Chorus.
	Look. Look again.
	Look everywhere, so that the mother-killer
	does not slip away in secret
	with his debt unpaid.
	There he is, and he has found protection again:
	his arms twisted round the image of the immortal goddess,
	he wants to go to trial for what he owes.
	But that cannot be. A mother's blood on the ground
	is hard to gather back. Papai,
	the wet of it, poured into the earth, is gone.
	You must pay it back from your living body:
	let me suck the red pulp out of your limbs.
	From you I will take my feeding, a drink hard to swallow.
	I will dry you up alive and drag you down below,
	so that you pay, agony for agony, for the mother you murdered.
	And you will see every other mortal who has sinned,
	against a god or a guest
	or the parents he owes his love to,
	getting exactly what his justice deserves.
	For Hades is a great auditor of mortals
	under the earth,
	and he watches everything and writes it on the tablets of his mind.

Orestes.
I have been taught by suffering, and I know many rites of cleansing. I
know where it is right to speak and where to keep silent, and in this
matter a wise teacher ordered me to speak. The blood is going to sleep
and fading from my hand. The pollution of a mother's murder has been
washed out; while it was still fresh it was driven off at the hearth
of the god Apollo by the cleansing sacrifice of pigs. It would be a
long story from the beginning, how many people I have visited without
any harm coming to them from my company. Time cleans everything as it
grows old along with it. So now, with a clean mouth, I call in all
reverence on Athena, the lady of this land, to come and help me.
Without a spear she will win me, and my land, and the people of Argos,
as a faithful and true ally for all time. Wherever she is, in some
part of the Libyan country, by the waters of Triton, the stream where
she was born, standing at her ease or striding into action to help the
people she loves, or surveying the plain of Phlegra like a bold
commander, let her come, for a god hears even from far away, and set
me free from this.

Chorus.
No. Not Apollo, not the strength of Athena can save you. You will go
to ruin, neglected, never learning where in your heart joy lives, a
bloodless thing for the powers below to feed on, a shadow. You do not
answer me. You spit my words away, you who were fattened up for me and
consecrated to me? Alive you will feed me, and not cut down at any
altar. Now you will hear the hymn that binds you.

Chorus.
	Come, then, let us join hands for the dance,
	since we have decided
	to show our hateful music
	and tell how our company
	deals out its allotted work among men.
	We believe we are straight in our justice.
	The man who holds out clean hands,
	no anger of ours creeps up on him;
	he goes through life unharmed.
	But the man who sins, like this one,
	and hides his bloody hands,
	we come against him to the end,
	true witnesses for the dead,
	collectors of the blood he owes.

Chorus.
	Mother who bore me, Mother Night,
	hear me. You bore me to be payment
	for the blind and for the seeing.
	The son of Leto is making me nothing:
	he is taking this cowering thing away from me,
	this hare, who is the proper offering
	to wash out a mother's blood.

Chorus.
	Over our victim we sing this song:
	a madness, a wandering of the wits,
	a wrecking of the mind,
	the hymn of the Furies,
	a spell to bind the mind, without the lyre,
	a withering for mortals.

Chorus.
	This is the lot that Fate, who pierces straight through,
	spun for us to hold for good:
	when mortals bring rash murder
	down on their own kin,
	we go with them until they go under the earth,
	and even dead
	they are not very free.

Chorus.
	Over our victim we sing this song:
	a madness, a wandering of the wits,
	a wrecking of the mind,
	the hymn of the Furies,
	a spell to bind the mind, without the lyre,
	a withering for mortals.

Chorus.
	These lots were fixed on us at our birth.
	The immortals must keep their hands off us,
	and none of them eats at our table;
	and I have no share, no portion, in the pure white robes.

Chorus.
	For I chose the overthrow of houses,
	whenever War, made tame in the home,
	kills one of its own.
	We run down that man,
	and strong as he is
	we wear him down to nothing for the fresh blood.

Chorus.
	We are eager to take these cares off another's hands,
	to let the gods off the work I do,
	so that none of it ever comes to a hearing before them;
	for Zeus has ruled that this tribe, dripping blood and hateful,
	is beneath his notice and his company.

Chorus.
	For I chose the overthrow of houses,
	whenever War, made tame in the home,
	kills one of its own.
	We run down that man,
	and strong as he is
	we wear him down to nothing for the fresh blood.

Chorus.
	Men's proud thoughts, so high under the open sky,
	melt and shrink to nothing under the earth, dishonoured,
	at our attack in our black robes
	and the spiteful dancing of our feet.

Chorus.
	Yes, I leap from high above
	and bring down the full weight of my foot,
	heavy in the fall,
	legs that trip even the fast runner,
	a ruin no one can bear.

Chorus.
	And as he falls he does not know it, so witless is the harm;
	such a darkness of pollution hovers over the man,
	and a rumour full of groaning tells
	of a dark mist over his house.

Chorus.
	Yes, I leap from high above
	and bring down the full weight of my foot,
	heavy in the fall,
	legs that trip even the fast runner,
	a ruin no one can bear.

Chorus.
	For it stays. We are good at plotting,
	and we finish what we start;
	we remember evil deeds, we are revered,
	and mortals cannot talk us round.
	We drive at our allotted work,
	unhonoured, unrespected, set apart from the gods
	in a light with no sun in it,
	a road of rocks for the seeing and the dim-eyed alike.

Chorus.
	What mortal, then, does not stand in awe
	and fear of this,
	when he hears from me the law
	that Fate decreed and the gods gave in full?
	My ancient privilege stays with me still.
	I meet with no dishonour,
	though my station is under the earth
	in a darkness the sun never reaches.

(Athena comes in, wearing the aegis.)

Athena.
From far away I heard a voice calling me, from the Scamander, where I
was taking possession of the land that the captains and chiefs of the
Achaeans assigned to me, a great share of the spoils their spears won,
root and branch, to be mine for ever, a choice gift to the sons of
Theseus. From there I came, driving my tireless feet, no wings, the
folds of my aegis whistling in the wind, this chariot yoked to colts
in their prime. I see a strange new company in my land, and I am not
afraid, but it is a wonder to my eyes. Who in the world are you? I ask
you all together: you, the stranger sitting at my image, and you, who
are like no race ever sown on this earth, never seen among goddesses
by the gods, and not shaped like anything mortal either. But to speak
ill of a neighbour who has done no wrong is far from just, and Right
stands well away from it.

Chorus.
You will hear it all in brief, daughter of Zeus. We are the children
of Night, and we last for ever. In our homes under the earth we are
called the Curses.

Athena.
I know your family now, and the names you go by.

Chorus.
And you will soon learn my office.

Athena.
I would, if someone told it plainly.

Chorus.
We drive murderers out of their homes.

Athena.
And where does the killer's flight end?

Chorus.
Where joy is nowhere the custom.

Athena.
And you are screaming this man on into that kind of flight?

Chorus.
Yes. Because he thought it right to be his mother's murderer.

Athena.
Was he forced to it by something else, or afraid of someone's anger?

Chorus.
Where is there a goad sharp enough to drive a man to kill his mother?

Athena.
There are two parties here, and I have heard half the case.

Chorus.
But he will not accept an oath, and he will not give one.

Athena.
You want to be called just more than you want to act justly.

Chorus.
How so? Teach me. You are not poor in cleverness.

Athena.
I say that oaths must not let injustice win.

Chorus.
Then examine him, and judge a straight judgment.

Athena.
So you would turn the decision of the charge over to me?

Chorus.
How could we not? We honour you: you are worthy, and you come of
worthy parents.

Athena.
And what do you want to say to this, stranger, in your turn? First
tell me your country, your family and what has happened to you, and
then defend yourself against this charge, if it really is trust in the
justice of your case that keeps you sitting here holding my image,
beside my hearth, a solemn suppliant in the manner of Ixion. Give me
an answer I can understand, to all of this.

Orestes.
Lady Athena, first I will lift a great worry out of your last words. I
am not a suppliant who needs cleansing, and I did not sit down at your
image with pollution on my hand. I will give you strong proof of that.
The law is that a man stained with murder is barred from speech until
a man who can cleanse blood has sprinkled him with the blood of a
new-born animal cut in sacrifice. Long ago, at other houses, I was
cleansed that way, with animals and with running streams. So I say
that worry is out of the way. As for my family, you will soon hear how
it stands. I am an Argive, and my father, whom you are right to ask
about, was Agamemnon, the marshal of the men who sailed in the ships,
the man with whom you made Troy, the city of Ilium, no city at all. He
did not die well when he came home. My black-hearted mother killed
him, after she had wrapped him in a cunning net, a net that stands
witness to this day to the murder in the bath. And I came home, after
all the years of exile before it, and I killed the woman who bore me.
I will not deny it. It was killing paid for killing, for the father I
loved. And Apollo shares the blame for it with me. He warned me of
pains that would goad my heart if I did not do this to the ones who
were guilty. Whether I acted justly or not, you judge. Whatever comes
to me at your hands, I will accept it.

Athena.
The matter is too big for any mortal who thinks he can judge it. And
it is not right even for me to decide a case of murder with the quick
anger of the Furies behind it. Especially since you came to my house a
suppliant fully prepared, clean and harmless, and so I respect you,
since you bring my city no harm. But these women have an office that
is not easily sent away, and if they do not win their case, the venom
of their resentment will fall on the ground afterwards, an unbearable,
unending sickness in the land. So it stands. Either way, whether I let
them stay or drive them out, brings disaster, and I have no way
through it. But since this matter has fallen here, on me, I will
choose judges of murder, bound by oath, and I will set up this court
for all time. You two, call your witnesses and your proofs, sworn
evidence to support your case. And I will pick the best of my citizens
and come back, so that they can decide this matter truly, after they
have sworn an oath to say nothing against justice.

(Athena goes out.)

Chorus.
	Now the new laws overturn everything,
	if the case of this mother-killer,
	his justice and his crime together, wins.
	From now on this one deed
	will fit every mortal's hand to recklessness.
	Many wounds, real wounds,
	struck by their own children,
	are waiting for parents
	in the time to come.

Chorus.
	No anger of ours, the maenads who watch mortals,
	will creep up on such deeds any more;
	I will let loose every kind of death.
	One man will hear from another,
	each telling his neighbour's troubles,
	and ask when the labour will ease or end,
	and some poor wretch
	will offer comfort that means nothing,
	remedies that hold no weight.

Chorus.
	Let no one struck by disaster
	call out any more
	and cry this word:
	Justice! Thrones of the Furies!
	Some father, some mother
	fresh in her grief,
	may cry those words and weep,
	now that the house of Justice
	is falling down.

Chorus.
	There is a place where fear is good,
	and it should sit
	as a watchman over the mind.
	It pays
	to learn to keep yourself in hand under the strain.
	But who, if he raised no fear
	in his heart at all,
	city or mortal alike,
	would still bow to Justice?

Chorus.
	Praise neither the life with no master
	nor the life ground down under a master.
	God gives the power to the middle in everything,
	and watches over each thing
	in its own way.
	I say a word in proportion:
	Outrage is truly the child
	of a godless heart;
	but from health of mind
	comes the happiness everyone loves
	and everyone prays for.

Chorus.
	And this I say to you, for all time:
	honour the altar of Justice.
	Do not see profit
	and kick it over with a godless foot;
	punishment will follow.
	The appointed end waits.
	So let a man put first
	the reverence he owes his parents,
	and respect
	the stranger he welcomes
	under his roof.

Chorus.
	The man who is just by his own will, and not by force,
	will not go without happiness;
	he will never be wiped out altogether.
	But the man who dares the other way, I say,
	who trespasses and hauls a great cargo
	heaped up without justice,
	will strike his sail in time, by force,
	when trouble takes him and the yardarm splinters.

Chorus.
	He calls out, and nobody hears,
	in the middle of the whirlpool he cannot fight.
	The god laughs at the hot-headed man,
	seeing him, who boasted this would never happen,
	limp with troubles he cannot help
	and failing to top the crest of the wave.
	He has driven the happiness of his whole life
	onto the reef of Justice,
	and he dies unwept, unseen.
