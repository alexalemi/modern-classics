Seven Against Thebes
(Part 1 of 2)

(Thebes, on the citadel, before the statues of the city's gods. The city
is under siege. Eteocles comes in to a crowd of citizens.)

Eteocles.
Citizens of Cadmus's city. The man who guards the ship of state from the
stern, with his hand on the tiller and never letting his eyelids close
in sleep, has to say what the moment needs. If we come through this
well, the credit goes to a god. But if disaster comes, and may it never
come, then Eteocles is the one name that will be sung all over the city
by every citizen, in a rising roar of blame and the wailing that goes
with it. May Zeus the Defender live up to his name and defend the city
of the Cadmeans from that.

As for you, all of you: the boy who has not yet reached the peak of his
youth, and the man whom time has carried past it but who still keeps a
big strong growth of body, and every man in his prime, each as fits him.
You must come to the help of the city now, and of the altars of the gods
of this land, so that their honours are never wiped out. Help your
children, and the Earth, your mother, the nurse who loves you most. When
you were small and crawling on her kind ground she took on the whole
burden of raising you, and she raised you to be householders who carry a
shield, and to be faithful to her in this hour of need.

And up to this day the god's scales have tipped our way. All the time we
have been penned in behind our towers, the war has mostly gone well for
us, and that is the gods' doing. But now the prophet, the herdsman of
birds, who works without the fire of sacrifice, sorting the oracle birds
with his ears and his mind by an art that does not lie, this master of
prophecy says the greatest Argive assault yet is being debated in the
night and planned against the city. So all of you, to the battlements
and the gates of the towered walls. Move. Run, in full armour. Man the
breastworks, take your stand on the platforms of the towers, hold your
posts where the gates open out, and keep your courage up. Do not be too
frightened of this mob of foreigners. The god will bring it to a good
end. I have sent out scouts myself, men to watch their army, and I trust
them not to waste the journey. Once I have heard them, no trick will
catch me out.

(The Scout comes in, running.)

Scout.
Eteocles, best of the Cadmeans, my lord, I have come back from the army
out there with sure news. I saw what they did with my own eyes. Seven
men, fierce captains of companies, cut a bull's throat over a
black-rimmed shield, dipped their hands in the bull's blood, and swore
by Ares, by Enyo, and by Terror who loves blood, that either they would
tear this city down to its foundations and gut the town of the Cadmeans
by force, or die and knead this earth into mud with their own blood. And
they were hanging keepsakes of themselves on Adrastus's chariot, for
their parents at home, and crying as they did it. But not one word of
pity came out of their mouths. Their iron hearts were blazing with
courage and breathing hard, like lions with war in their eyes.

And the news of this has not been slowed by any hesitation of mine. I
left them drawing lots, to decide which gate each man would lead his
company against, as chance assigned him. So pick the best men in the
city and post them at the gate exits, quickly. The Argive army is
already close, in full armour, coming on and raising dust, and white
foam from the horses' lungs is spattering the plain in drops. You are
the good helmsman of this ship. Make the town fast before the squalls of
Ares break over it. The wave of their army is roaring in over dry land.
Seize the fastest moment there is for this. For the rest, I will keep a
faithful daylight watch with my eyes, and with the clear word I bring
you will know what is happening outside the gates and come to no harm.

(The Scout goes out.)

Eteocles.
Zeus, and Earth, and gods who hold this city, and Curse, the Fury of my
father, so strong: do not tear my city up by the roots, do not let it be
ripped out and destroyed and taken by the enemy, a city that speaks the
language of Greece, with its houses and its hearths. Never let this free
land, this city of Cadmus, be held under the yoke of slavery. Be our
strength. What I ask is for all of us, I think. A city that does well
honours its gods.

(Eteocles goes out with the citizens. The Chorus, the young women of
Thebes, come rushing in, in terror.)

Chorus.
	I cry out in fear, great pains, great cries.
	The army is loose. It has left its camp,
	and here it comes, a flood of men, the horsemen out in front.
	The dust rising into the sky tells me so,
	a messenger with no voice, but clear, and true.
	And now the plains of my own country
	ring under the hooves and push the roar into my ear.
	It flies, it rumbles,
	like water no one can fight, crashing down a mountain.
	Oh, oh,
	gods and goddesses, the evil is rushing on us,
	shout it back over the walls.
	The army of the white shields is on its feet,
	ready, running at the city at full stretch.
	Who will save us then? Which of the gods
	or goddesses will stand by us?
	Shall I throw myself down
	before the old statues of my fathers' gods?
	Oh, blessed ones on your firm seats,
	now is the time to hold on to your statues.
	Why do we wait, and groan?
	Do you hear the shields clash, or don't you?
	If not now, when shall we hold up
	robes and garlands and pray?
	I see the clash. That is not the clatter of one spear.
	What will you do, Ares, old lord of this land?
	Will you betray your own?
	Oh, god of the golden helmet, look, look
	at the city you once loved so well.

Chorus.
	Gods who guard the city, all of you, come, over the land.
	Look at this band of girls on our knees,
	begging to be spared slavery.
	A wave of men with plumes slanting on their helmets
	is boiling round the town, driven by the blasts of Ares.
	Zeus, father, who brings all things to their end,
	keep us, whatever it costs, from capture by the enemy.
	The Argives are circling the fortress of Cadmus.
	Terror of their weapons of war shakes us,
	and through the horses' jaws
	the bridles whine of murder.
	Seven proud men, the pick of the army,
	in spear-shaking harness, at the seven gates
	take their stands, as the lot fell to them.

Chorus.
	And you, Zeus-born power who loves the fight,
	be the saviour of the city, Pallas.
	And you, lord of horses, ruler of the deep,
	Poseidon, with your weapon that strikes fish,
	give us release from fear, give us release.
	And you, Ares, oh, oh, guard the city
	that carries Cadmus's name; show that you care for it, out in the open.
	And Aphrodite, first mother of our race,
	keep this off us. We come from your blood.
	Crying to you with prayers a god must hear,
	we come close.
	And you, Wolf-lord Apollo, be a wolf
	to the enemy army, and give them groan for groan.
	And you, girl born of Leto,
	Artemis, our own, make your bow ready.

Chorus.
	Ah, ah, ah, ah,
	I hear the rattle of chariots round the city.
	Lady Hera!
	The hubs screamed under the loaded axles.
	Artemis, our own, ah, ah, ah, ah,
	the air is going mad with shaken spears.
	What is happening to our city? What is coming?
	Where is the god leading us, and to what end?

Chorus.
	Ah, ah, ah, ah,
	a hail of stones is coming at the tops of the battlements.
	Apollo, our own!
	At the gates the clang of bronze-bound shields.
	Son of Zeus, from whom comes
	the holy end of war, the deciding of the battle.
	And you, blessed queen Onca, before the city,
	shield your seven-gated home.

Chorus.
	Oh, gods who can do everything,
	oh, you gods and goddesses who bring things to their end,
	guardians of the towers of this land,
	do not betray a city worn out by the spear
	to an army that speaks another tongue.
	Hear us, hear us girls, as is right,
	our prayers, our hands stretched out.

Chorus.
	Oh, spirits who love us,
	stand round the city and set it free,
	show how you love this town.
	Think of the people's holy offerings,
	and as you think of them, help.
	The city loves its sacrifices and its rites.
	Remember them for me.

Eteocles.
I ask you, you unbearable creatures: is this the best way? Is this how
the city gets saved? Is this what gives heart to an army penned in
behind its towers, you throwing yourselves down in front of the statues
of the gods who hold the city and howling and shrieking, the thing every
sane person hates? May I never share a house with the female race, not
in bad times and not in the good times we love. When a woman has the
upper hand her boldness is more than anyone can live with, and when she
is frightened she is a worse evil still, for house and city both. Look
at you now. Running this way and that in flight, you have shrieked a
spineless cowardice into the citizens. The enemy outside the gates could
not ask for better help, and we are being sacked from inside by our own
people. That is what you get when you live with women. And if anyone
will not listen to my command, man, woman, or whatever is in between,
the vote against them is death, and there is no escaping the people's
stones. What happens outside is the man's business. No woman is to make
decisions about it. Stay inside and do no harm. Did you hear me or not?
Or am I talking to the deaf?

Chorus.
	Dear son of Oedipus, I was afraid when I heard
	the rattle, the rattle of the crashing chariots,
	when the axle-boxes screamed as the wheels went round,
	and the steering gear in the horses' mouths,
	the fire-forged bits, sang out.

Eteocles.
And so? Did a sailor ever find a way to safety by running from the stern
to the prow while the ship was labouring in the open sea?

Chorus.
	No. I ran to the gods, to their ancient statues,
	trusting the gods, when the deadly blizzard,
	the snowstorm of stones, roared at the gates.
	That was when fear lifted me up to pray to the blessed ones
	to hold their strength over the city.

Eteocles.
Pray that the wall keeps out the enemy's spear. That is the gods'
business, yes. But they say the gods of a captured city leave it.

Chorus.
	Never in my lifetime may this gathering of gods
	leave us. Never let me see this city
	overrun, and an army
	setting it alight with the enemy's fire.

Eteocles.
Call on the gods, but do not plan badly while you do it. Obedience is
the mother of Success, and the wife of Safety. That is the saying.

Chorus.
	It is. But the strength of a god is higher still,
	and often, in bad times, it lifts a helpless man
	up out of hard misery,
	even with the storm clouds hanging over his eyes.

Eteocles.
Slaughtered beasts and offerings to the gods, when we test the enemy:
that is men's work. Yours is to keep silent and stay inside the house.

Chorus.
	It is through the gods that we live in a city no one has conquered,
	and that the wall holds off the crowd of men who hate us.
	What is there in that to resent?

Eteocles.
I do not grudge you honouring the race of the gods. But so that you do
not turn the citizens into cowards, be calm, and stop being so terribly
afraid.

Chorus.
	I heard a strange clashing, all mixed together,
	and I came, shaking with fear, up to this citadel,
	our honoured seat of worship.

Eteocles.
Then if you hear of men dying or wounded, do not snatch at the news with
wailing. That is what Ares feeds on: the blood of men.

Chorus.
But I do hear it. Horses, snorting.

Eteocles.
Then hear it, but not so loudly.

Chorus.
The town is groaning from the ground up, as if they were all round us.

Eteocles.
It is enough that I am making the plans for that.

Chorus.
I am afraid. The battering at the gates is getting worse.

Eteocles.
Will you be quiet, and say none of this in the city?

Chorus.
Gods gathered here, do not betray our walls.

Eteocles.
Damn you. Will you not bear this in silence?

Chorus.
Gods of my city, do not let slavery be my lot.

Eteocles.
You are making a slave of me, and of the whole city, yourself.

Chorus.
Zeus almighty, turn your bolt on the enemy.

Eteocles.
Zeus, what a breed you have given us in women.

Chorus.
A wretched one, like men whose city is taken.

Eteocles.
Words of bad omen again, with your hands on the statues?

Chorus.
I have no courage left, and fear is snatching at my tongue.

Eteocles.
If you would only grant me one small thing I ask.

Chorus.
Say it, as fast as you can, and I will know as fast.

Eteocles.
Be quiet, you poor wretch. Do not frighten your own people.

Chorus.
I am quiet. I will suffer what is fated, with the others.

Eteocles.
I take those words of yours in place of the ones before. And one thing
more: get away from the statues, and make the better prayer, that the
gods fight on our side. Listen to my prayers, and then raise the holy
cry of triumph, the cry of good omen, the Greek custom of shouting over
a sacrifice, which gives courage to our friends and unties their fear of
the enemy.

To the gods who hold this country's city, the ones who live in the plain
and the ones who watch over the market place, and to the springs of
Dirce and the water of Ismenus, I say this: if things go well and the
city is saved, we will redden the gods' hearths with the blood of sheep
and slaughter bulls for the gods. That is my vow. And I will hang
trophies in their holy houses, the enemy's clothing, spoils torn from
them by the spear. I will hang them up in front of the temples, the
garments of our enemies.

Pray to the gods like that, not with your love of groaning, not with
wild, useless panting. You will not escape what is fated one bit the
more for it. I am going to post six men, and myself the seventh, to face
the enemy in the grand manner at the seven exits in the walls, before
fast-running messengers and hurrying words arrive and set us on fire
with need.

(Eteocles goes out.)

Chorus.
	I obey. But fear will not let my heart sleep.
	Worries, neighbours to my heart,
	keep fanning the terror
	of the army all round the walls,
	the way a dove, trembling all over,
	fears for the young in her nest
	because of the snakes, bad bedfellows.
	Some of them march against the towers
	with all their people, all their host.
	What will become of me?
	Others are hurling jagged stones
	at the citizens caught on every side.
	Gods born of Zeus, by every means,
	save the city and the army
	born of Cadmus.

Chorus.
	What ground will you take in exchange for this,
	what better country, if you hand over to the enemy
	this deep-soiled land,
	and the water of Dirce, the most nourishing drink
	of all the drinks that Poseidon
	who holds the earth
	and the children of Tethys pour out?
	So, gods who hold the city,
	throw onto the men outside the towers
	the doom that destroys men,
	the ruin that makes them drop their weapons,
	and win glory for these citizens.
	Be the saviours of the city,
	stay firm in your seats,
	for these shrill, wailing prayers.

Chorus.
	It is pitiful, to throw a city as old as this
	down to Hades, the spear's prey,
	a slave, crumbling to dry ash,
	sacked without honour by an Achaean man
	because the gods willed it;
	and the women, taken, led away,
	ah, ah, young and old alike,
	dragged by the hair like horses,
	their clothes torn off round them.
	The city screams as it empties out,
	the plunder wailing in every voice as it dies.
	I shudder at the heavy fate before it comes.

Chorus.
	And it is a thing to weep for, that girls just ripe
	should be picked raw, before the wedding rites,
	and go the hateful road away from home.
	What? I say the dead man does better than they do.
	For when a city is beaten down,
	ah, ah, many and miserable are the things it suffers.
	Man drags man away, or kills him, or carries fire.
	The whole town is smeared with smoke.
	And Ares breathes over it, raging,
	the breaker of peoples, fouling everything holy.

Chorus.
	Roaring through the town, and against the town
	a towered net closing in.
	Man goes down before man, by the spear.
	Bloody bleating,
	the wails of babies fresh from the breast,
	still at the nipple.
	And looting, blood-brother to the running to and fro.
	Loaded man runs into loaded man,
	empty man calls to empty man,
	wanting a partner in it,
	and none of them wants less, none wants an equal share.
	From this you can guess the rest.

Chorus.
	Fruit of every kind, fallen to the ground,
	hurts to find, a bitter sight
	for the housekeepers' eyes.
	Heaped, jumbled, the gifts of the earth
	are swept away on worthless waves.
	And the young slave women, new to suffering:
	a wretched bed, a prisoner's bed,
	a lucky man's, an enemy's, a stronger man's,
	that is their hope now, the coming of the night's due,
	a helper to the tears and all the grief.

(The Scout is seen coming from one side, Eteocles from the other, both
in a hurry.)

First Half-Chorus.
The scout from the army, I think, is bringing us some fresh news,
friends. Look how fast his legs are swinging as they carry him on his
errand.

Second Half-Chorus.
And here is our lord himself, the son of Oedipus, just in time to hear
the messenger's report. Haste is spoiling his stride, too.

Scout.
I can tell you the enemy's dispositions exactly, and which gate each man
drew by lot. Tydeus is already roaring at the Proetid gate. But the
prophet will not let him ford the Ismenus. The sacrifices are not coming
out well. And Tydeus, raving, greedy for the fight, is bellowing like a
snake that hisses at midday, and lashing the wise prophet, the son of
Oecles, with the insult that he fawns on death and on battle out of
cowardice. Shouting things like that, he shakes the three shadowing
plumes, the mane of his helmet, and under his shield bells beaten out of
bronze ring out fear. On his shield he has this proud sign: a sky worked
in metal, blazing with stars, and bright in the middle of the shield the
full moon, eldest of the stars, the eye of night. Raving like that in
his boastful armour he roars along the river bank, in love with the
fight, like a horse straining at the bit and panting, waiting for the
trumpet's call. Who will you set against him? Who can be trusted to
stand at the Proetid gate when the bars are drawn?

Eteocles.
No man's finery would make me tremble, and signs do not make wounds.
Plumes and bells do not bite without a spear. And this night you speak
of on his shield, glittering with the stars of heaven: perhaps his folly
will turn prophet for someone. If night falls on his eyes as he dies,
then for the man who carries it this arrogant sign will be rightly and
justly named, and he will have prophesied his own outrage against
himself. Against Tydeus I will post the trusty son of Astacus as
defender of this gate, a man of very good birth, who honours the throne
of Shame and hates arrogant talk. He is slow to do anything disgraceful,
and he does not like to be a coward. His root springs from the sown men,
the ones Ares spared, and he is very much a man of this soil. His name
is Melanippus. Ares will decide the outcome with his dice. But Justice,
who shares his blood, is sending him out, and rightly, to keep the enemy
spear from the mother who bore him.

Chorus.
	May the gods give our champion good luck,
	since he goes up in a just cause
	to fight for the city. But I tremble
	to see the bloody deaths
	of men who die for their own.

Scout.
May the gods give him that luck. Capaneus drew the Electran gate,
another giant, bigger than the one I told you of, and his boasting is
beyond what a man should think. He threatens terrible things against our
towers, and may chance not fulfil them. He says he will sack the city
whether the god wants it or not, and that not even the Quarrel of Zeus,
striking the ground in front of him, would stand in his way. The
lightning and the thunderbolts he compares to the heat of noon. For a
sign he has a naked man carrying fire, and the torch he holds as his
weapon blazes in his hands, and in gold letters he says: I will burn the
city. Against a man like that, send someone. Who will stand up to him?
Who will wait for that boaster without trembling?

Eteocles.
Here too, gain breeds gain. When men think empty thoughts, the tongue
turns out to be their true accuser. Capaneus threatens, ready to act,
dishonouring the gods, and giving his mouth its exercise in idle joy he
sends up to heaven, a mortal man, loud swelling words for Zeus to hear.
I trust that the fire-bearing thunderbolt will come to him with justice,
and that it will be nothing like the heat of the noonday sun. Against
him, big-mouthed as he is, a man of burning spirit is posted: the
strength of Polyphontes, a guard you can trust, with the goodwill of
Artemis who stands before the gate, and of the other gods. Tell me of
another, and the gate he drew.

Chorus.
	Death to the man who boasts so big against the city.
	May the thunderbolt's shaft stop him
	before he bursts into my house
	and with his arrogant spear
	drives me out of my girlhood room.

Scout.
Now I will tell you of the man who drew the next gate. The third lot
leaped out of the upturned bronze helmet for Eteoclus, the third man, to
throw his company at the Neistan gate. He is wheeling his mares,
snorting in their head-straps, wanting to fall on the gate. Their
muzzles whistle in a barbarian way, filled with the breath of their
proud nostrils. And his shield is not modestly decorated: an armoured
man climbs the rungs of a ladder up an enemy tower, meaning to sack it.
And this one too shouts, in the syllables of written letters, that not
even Ares could throw him off the wall. Against this man too send
someone you can trust to keep the yoke of slavery from this city.

Eteocles.
I would send this man now, and with luck, too. In fact he is already
sent, his boast in his hands: Megareus, Creon's seed, of the race of the
sown men. He will not back away from the gate for fear of the thunder of
maddened horses snorting. Either he will die and pay the land in full
for his rearing, or he will take two men and the city on the shield, and
dress his father's house with the spoils. Boast of another, and do not
grudge me the telling.

Chorus.
	I pray for him to have good luck,
	oh, champion of my home,
	and for them, bad luck.
	As they talk their great talk against the city
	with a mind gone mad,
	so may Zeus the Punisher look on them in anger.

Scout.
Another, the fourth, has the gate next to Onca Athena, and stands there
with a shout: Hippomedon, the shape and huge stamp of him. He spun a
great threshing floor, I mean the circle of his shield, and I shuddered.
I will not say otherwise. The man who worked the sign on it was no cheap
craftsman: it is Typhon, sending out of his fire-breathing mouth black
smoke, the flickering sister of fire, and the rim of the hollow-bellied
shield is nailed all round with coils of snakes. He himself raised the
war cry, and possessed by Ares he raves for the fight like a maenad,
with terror in his eyes. Guard well against the attack of a man like
that. Terror is already boasting at the gate.

Eteocles.
First, Onca Pallas, who lives close to the city, next to the gate, and
hates outrage in a man, will keep him off like a cold snake from her
nestlings. And Hyperbius, the trusty son of Oenops, is chosen to meet
him man to man, willing to find out his fate in the crisis that chance
brings, a man with no fault in his body, in his spirit, or in the
handling of his arms. Hermes has matched them well. Man will meet man as
enemy, and the gods on their shields will clash as enemies too. One has
fire-breathing Typhon. On Hyperbius's shield Father Zeus sits upright
with a flaming bolt in his hand. And no one has ever yet seen Zeus
beaten. That is how the gods' favour lies. We are on the side of the
winners, they on the side of the beaten, if Zeus is stronger than Typhon
in a fight. It is likely the men will fare as their gods do, and by the
reading of the sign Zeus will be Hyperbius's saviour, since Zeus happens
to be on his shield.

Chorus.
	I am sure of it: the man who carries Zeus's enemy on his shield,
	the unloved body of the earth-born spirit,
	an image hated by mortals
	and by the long-lived gods,
	will fling his head down in the dust before the gate.
