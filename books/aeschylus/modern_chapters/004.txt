The Suppliants
(Part 1 of 2)

(The shore of Argos, near a mound where the images of the city's gods
stand together. Fifty young women come in from the sea, dark-skinned and
dressed in Egyptian linen, each carrying a branch wound with white wool.
Their father Danaus is with them.)

Chorus.
	Zeus, god of the suppliant, look kindly
	on our company, that put to sea
	from the fine-sanded mouths of the Nile.
	We have left the land of Zeus,
	whose pastures run up against Syria, and we are fugitives,
	not because any city voted to banish us
	for blood on our hands,
	but by our own choice. We are running from men.
	We hate the marriage the sons of Aegyptus want,
	and we call it what it is: impious.
	Danaus, our father, head of our councils,
	head of our revolt, moved the pieces on the board
	and picked, out of all the griefs on offer,
	the one with most honour in it:
	to run, headlong, over the salt waves,
	and put in at the land of Argos.
	For that is where our race began, and we are proud of it:
	from the heifer the gadfly tormented,
	from the touch and the breath of Zeus.
	What country could we come to
	kinder than this one,
	with these in our hands, the suppliant's only weapons,
	branches wreathed in wool?
	City, land, clear water,
	gods on high, and you gods below,
	heavy in your punishments, who keep the graves,
	and Zeus the Saviour, called on third,
	who guards the houses of righteous men:
	receive this company of women as your suppliants,
	and let the land breathe respect on us.
	But that swarm of men, that crowd of outrage born of Aegyptus,
	before they set one foot on this marshy shore,
	drive them out to sea, them and their fast-oared ship,
	and there let them meet the whirlwind,
	the storm that beats down, the thunder and lightning,
	the rain-heavy winds on a wild sea.
	Let them die out there,
	before they ever climb into beds that Right forbids them,
	before they take their uncle's daughters, us,
	against our will, and call us theirs.

Chorus.
	Now I call on the calf of Zeus,
	our champion from across the sea,
	the son of the cow our ancestress,
	the cow who grazed among the flowers,
	the child of the breath of Zeus.
	Zeus touched her. Her time came to term
	and gave the child his name, and the name was right:
	she bore Epaphus, the Touched One.

Chorus.
	I call on him.
	And now, here in the grassy places
	where our ancient mother pastured,
	I will remember what she suffered long ago,
	and I will show the people of this land
	sure proofs of it.
	Some of it they will not expect, but it will be seen to be true.
	Anyone who hears the whole story through
	will know it for the truth.

Chorus.
	And if there is someone near, a native of this place,
	who reads the cries of birds,
	when he hears our pitiful lament
	he will think he is hearing the voice
	of Metis, Tereus' wife, the pitiable one,
	the nightingale the hawk drives before it,

Chorus.
	who, shut out from her green leaves,
	grieves and cries for the haunts she loved,
	and puts together the song of her child's death:
	how he died by her own hand,
	killed by his own mother,
	when he met her unnatural rage.

Chorus.
	So I too, who love to lament,
	in the Ionian way,
	tear at my soft cheek
	that the Nile's sun has ripened,
	and at a heart that has never learned to weep.
	I gather the flowers of grief,
	afraid, wondering whether we have friends here at all,
	whether there is one person who will take up the cause
	of this flight of ours from the land of mists.

Chorus.
	But gods of our race, hear us.
	Look well at what is just.
	Do not let the young have all they want
	against what is due. Hate outrage, be quick to hate it,
	and you will be just toward marriage.
	Even for men beaten in war and running from it
	there is an altar, a defence against harm,
	the awe that the gods command.

Chorus.
	May Zeus make it come out well, truly well.
	The desire of Zeus is not easy to hunt down.
	It blazes everywhere,
	even in the dark, with black fortune,
	for the peoples of the earth.

Chorus.
	It lands safe, and not on its back,
	whatever thing the head of Zeus has nodded into being.
	The paths of his mind
	run thick and shadowed,
	and no eye can follow them.

Chorus.
	He hurls men down from the high towers of their hopes
	into utter destruction.
	He arms no violence against them.
	Everything the gods do is done without effort.
	Seated, he thinks his thought,
	and from where he sits, somehow, it is done,
	all of it, there on his holy throne.

Chorus.
	So let him look at human outrage
	and see how it grows young again,
	an old stock sprouting new shoots
	because of this marriage they want with us,
	with minds that no advice can turn,
	a mad purpose for its goad
	that nothing can escape,
	and ruin waiting, which it will know too late, when it is deceived.

Chorus.
	Such sufferings I cry out in my grief,
	shrill and deep, and the tears fall with them,
	ieh, ieh,
	the wailing that belongs at funerals.
	Alive, I sing my own dirge.

Chorus.
	I call on the hill country of Apia to be kind to me.
	Land, you understand my foreign speech well enough.
	Again and again I fall on my Sidonian veil
	and tear it, and the linen rips to rags.

Chorus.
	When things go well, and death keeps its distance,
	the gods get their sacrifices, freely and in full.
	Oh, oh,
	oh, troubles too tangled to read.
	Where will this wave carry me?

Chorus.
	I call on the hill country of Apia to be kind to me.
	Land, you understand my foreign speech well enough.
	Again and again I fall on my Sidonian veil
	and tear it, and the linen rips to rags.

Chorus.
	The oar and the house of timber
	stitched with linen rope, that kept the sea out,
	carried me here on the winds and no storm touched me.
	I have no complaint.
	But in time to come let the Father
	who sees everything
	make the ending kind:

Chorus.
	that the great seed of our honoured mother
	escape the beds of men, ah, ah,
	unmarried, untamed.

Chorus.
	And let the pure daughter of Zeus,
	who keeps her holy face steady and safe,
	look on me, willing, as I am willing.
	Angry at this hunt, with all her strength,
	a virgin, let her come to a virgin's rescue,
	and set me free:

Chorus.
	that the great seed of our honoured mother
	escape the beds of men, ah, ah,
	unmarried, untamed.

Chorus.
	And if she will not, then we,
	a race burned black by the sun,
	will go with our branches
	to the Zeus of the earth below,
	the Zeus who hosts more guests than any,
	the Zeus of the dead,
	and we will get there by the noose,
	if the gods of Olympus will not hear us.

Chorus.
	Ah, Zeus. It is Io's poison,
	a wrath from the gods that hunts us down.
	I know your wife's spite. It conquered heaven.
	After a harsh wind comes the storm.

Chorus.
	And then will Zeus not be caught
	by charges he cannot answer,
	for dishonouring the child of the cow,
	the child he made himself, long ago,
	and turning his face away
	from our prayers now?
	Let him hear, from high up, when we call him.

Chorus.
	Ah, Zeus. It is Io's poison,
	a wrath from the gods that hunts us down.
	I know your wife's spite. It conquered heaven.
	After a harsh wind comes the storm.

Danaus.
Children, you have to keep your heads. You came here with a father who
keeps his: an old man you can trust, and the captain of your ship. Now
that we are on land I have thought ahead, and I want you to write what I
say on the tablets of your minds and keep it there.

I see dust, an army's messenger that makes no sound. The axles are
turning in their sockets, and they are not quiet. I can make out a crowd
of men with shields and spears, and horses, and curved chariots. Most
likely the rulers of this land have heard from their scouts and are
coming to look at us. Whether the man who set this expedition moving
means no harm, or has been sharpened to a cruel rage, it is better in
every way, girls, to sit down at this mound, where the gods of the land
stand gathered together. An altar is stronger than a fortress wall. It
is a shield that cannot be broken.

So go, as fast as you can. Hold your branches wreathed in white wool,
the emblems of Zeus who respects the suppliant, solemnly, in your left
hands. Answer the strangers in the language of respect, of grief, of
sheer need, as newcomers should, and tell them plainly that this flight
of yours has no blood on it. Above all, let there be nothing bold in
your voices. Let nothing shameless come from your faces: modest brows,
quiet eyes. Do not run ahead of them in talk and do not drag behind. The
people here are very easily offended. And remember to give way. You are
a stranger in need, a fugitive. Bold tongues do not suit the weak.

Chorus.
Father, you speak good sense, and you are speaking to daughters who have
some. I will take care to remember these wise orders of yours. And may
Zeus, the father of our race, look on us.

Danaus.
May he look, indeed, and with a kind eye.

Chorus.
I would like to be sitting close beside you now.

Danaus.
Then do not waste time. Let the plan become action.

Chorus.
Zeus, pity our labours. Do not let us be destroyed.

Danaus.
If he wills it, all this will end well.

Danaus.
And now call on this bird of Zeus here too.

Chorus.
We call on the saving rays of the sun.

Danaus.
And on pure Apollo, a god who was once himself an exile from heaven.

Chorus.
He knows what that fate is. He may feel for mortals.

Danaus.
May he feel for us indeed, and stand beside us, ready to help.

Chorus.
Which of these gods should I call on next?

Danaus.
I see a trident here, the sign of its god.

Chorus.
He sent us here safely. Let him receive us safely on his land.

Danaus.
And here is Hermes, another god, as the Greeks set him up.

Chorus.
Then let him bring good news to free women.

Danaus.
Honour the altar that all these lords share. Sit down on holy ground
like a flock of doves in terror of hawks, birds of the same feather, kin
and enemies at once, who would defile their own blood. If a bird eats a
bird, how can it be pure? And if a man marries a woman who does not want
him, taking her from a father who does not want to give her, how can he
be pure? Not even in Hades, when he is dead, will a man who has done
that escape the charge of outrage. There too, so people say, another
Zeus sits in judgement on wrongdoing among the dead, and passes the last
sentence. Watch carefully, and answer in the way I told you, so that
this business comes out a victory for you.

(The King of Argos comes in with armed men.)

King.
Where has this crowd come from, that we are speaking to? There is
nothing Greek in the way you dress: you lounge in barbarian robes and
thick, close-woven headbands. This is not the clothing of the women of
Argos, or of anywhere in Greece. And how you dared to come to this
country without fear, with no heralds to announce you, no one here to
sponsor you, and no guides, that is a wonder to me. And yet I see
branches laid beside you in front of the gathered gods, the kind that
suppliants carry by custom. On that one point alone Greece can make a
confident guess. About everything else it would be fair to go on
guessing, if there were not a voice here present to tell me.

Chorus.
About our clothes you have spoken nothing but the truth. But I, how am I
to address you? As a private citizen, as a spokesman carrying the sacred
staff, or as the ruler of the city?

King.
On that point, answer me and speak without fear. I am Pelasgus, son of
Palaechthon, whom the earth itself brought forth, and I rule this land.
The race of the Pelasgians, who farm this ground, takes its name from
me, its king, as is right. All the country that the pure Strymon runs
through, on the side towards the setting sun, is mine. I set my borders
at the land of the Perrhaebians, at the parts beyond Pindus near the
Paeonians, and at the mountains of Dodona. The edge of the wet sea cuts
my land short. Everything on this side of those lines, I rule.

And the ground itself, this plain of Apia, has carried that name from
long ago in honour of a healer. Apis, healer and prophet, son of Apollo,
came from Naupactus on the far shore and cleaned this land of monsters
that were killing men, creatures that the earth itself, stained with the
pollution of old bloodshed, had sent up in her anger: a colony of
swarming snakes, a hateful thing to live beside. Apis cut those plagues
out and set us free of them, and did it without a fault, and the land of
Argos paid him back by remembering him in its prayers.

Now you have my account of myself. Declare your own ancestry and go on
from there. But our city does not care for long speeches.

Chorus.
Our story is short and clear. We claim to be Argives by birth, the seed
of a cow blessed in her child. And every word of it is true. I will fix
it to the story, point by point.

King.
Strangers, what you are telling me is past believing: that your race is
from Argos. You look far more like the women of Libya, and nothing at
all like the women born here. The Nile might raise a plant like you. And
there is the Cyprian stamp, struck on the female form by male craftsmen,
and it looks like yours. I hear too that there are nomad women who ride
camels as if they were horses, on padded saddles, in a country next door
to the Ethiopians. And if you were armed with bows I would certainly
have taken you for Amazons, the women who live without men and eat raw
meat. But teach me, and I will understand better how your birth and your
seed come from Argos.

Chorus.
Is there a story that once, here in the land of Argos, Io kept the keys
of Hera's house?

King.
There certainly is. The tradition is very strong.

Chorus.
And is there some story too that Zeus lay with a mortal woman?

King.
There is. And that embrace was no secret from Hera.

Chorus.
How did this quarrel between the royal pair end?

King.
The goddess of Argos turned the woman into a cow.

Chorus.
And while she was a cow with horns, did Zeus not come near her?

King.
So they say, in the shape of a bull that mounts cows.

Chorus.
And what did the strong wife of Zeus do about that?

King.
She set the all-seeing one to watch the cow.

Chorus.
What all-seeing herdsman with a single cow do you mean?

King.
Argus, the son of Earth. Hermes killed him.

Chorus.
What else did she devise against the unlucky cow?

King.
A sting that drives cattle and keeps them moving.

Chorus.
The people by the Nile call it the gadfly.

King.
So it drove her out of the land on a long, long run.

Chorus.
Everything you have said fits my story exactly.

King.
And so she came to Canobus, and to Memphis.

Chorus.
And Zeus laid his hand on her, and fathered a son by the touch.

King.
Then who claims to be the calf of Zeus, born of the cow?

Chorus.
Epaphus, and the name is true: he is called after the touch that set her
free.

Chorus.
Libya, who reaps the largest portion of the earth.

King.
And what other offspring of hers can you name?

Chorus.
Belus, who had two sons and was the father of my father here.

King.
Now tell me this very wise name of his.

Chorus.
Danaus. And he has a brother with fifty sons.

King.
Open up his name too. Do not hold it back.

Chorus.
Aegyptus. Now you know our ancient line. Act on it, and raise up this
company, which is Argive.

King.
I think you do have a share in this land from long ago. But how did you
bring yourselves to leave your father's house? What stroke of fortune
came down on you?

Chorus.
Lord of the Pelasgians, the troubles of human beings come in many
colours. You will not find two feathers of pain the same. Who would have
guessed that a family related to you from old times would run here, on a
flight no one expected, and put in at Argos in terror, because it
loathed a marriage bed?

King.
Why do you say you have come as suppliants to these gods gathered here,
with those fresh-cut branches wreathed in white?

Chorus.
So that I do not become a slave to the sons of Aegyptus.

King.
Because you hate them? Or are you saying it would be against the law?

Chorus.
Who would buy their masters from among their own kin?

King.
That is how families grow their power.

Chorus.
Yes, and when things go badly it is easy to get rid of a wife.

King.
Then how am I to do my duty by the gods where you are concerned?

Chorus.
By not handing us back to the sons of Aegyptus when they ask for us.

King.
That is a heavy thing you ask: to take on a new war.

Chorus.
But Justice stands over the men who fight beside her.

King.
Yes, if she had a share in the business from the beginning.

Chorus.
Then respect the city's stern, the altar, wreathed as you see it with
our branches.

King.
I shudder, looking at these seats in the shadow of the branches.

Chorus.
But the anger of Zeus, god of suppliants, is heavy.

Chorus.
	Son of Palaechthon, lord of the Pelasgians,
	hear me with a kind heart.
	Look at me, your suppliant, a fugitive, running in circles,
	like a heifer that wolves are chasing over sheer rocks,
	where she trusts the herdsman's strength
	and lows to tell him her trouble.

King.
I see this company of the gathered gods nodding in the shade of
fresh-cut branches. Let this business of our city's guests bring no
harm. Let no quarrel come on the city out of nowhere, unforeseen and
unprepared for. The city has no need of that.

Chorus.
	Let Right, the suppliant's goddess, daughter of Zeus who portions out,
	look on this flight and see that it brings no harm.
	And you, old in wisdom, learn from someone younger:
	if you respect the one who turns to you for help,
	you will not go short. The gods accept offerings
	from a man who is pure.

King.
It is not at the hearth of my own house that you sit. If the city is
polluted as a whole, let the people as a whole work out the cure. I will
make no promise before I have shared all this with every citizen.

Chorus.
	You are the city. You are the people.
	You are a ruler no one judges.
	You rule the altar, the hearth of the land,
	with the single vote of your nod.
	On a throne with a single sceptre
	you decide every matter. Guard against pollution.

King.
Pollution on my enemies. But I cannot help you without harm. And it is
not wise, either, to dishonour these prayers. I have no way out. Fear
holds my mind: to act, or not to act, and take what fortune gives.

Chorus.
	Look up at the one who watches from above,
	the guardian of mortals worn out with trouble,
	who sit at their neighbours' doors
	and do not get the justice the law owes them.
	The anger of Zeus, god of suppliants, stays,
	and the sufferer's cries will not soften it.

King.
If the sons of Aegyptus have power over you by the law of your own city,
claiming to be your nearest kin, who would want to stand against them?
You must plead by the laws of the home you fled that they have no
authority over you at all.

Chorus.
	Never, never let me fall into the hands
	of men and their power. I steer by the stars
	and I mark out my escape from a marriage
	that sickens my heart: flight. Take Justice for your ally
	and give the judgement that reveres the gods.

King.
The judgement is not easy. Do not make me the judge. I have said it
already: even though I rule, I will not do this without the people, or
one day, if something goes wrong, they will say, "You honoured
foreigners, and you destroyed your city."

Chorus.
	Zeus, kin to both sides, looks down on this,
	tipping his scale to one side or the other,
	giving, as is right, wrong to the wicked
	and holy things to those who keep the law.
	When the weights are balanced so evenly,
	why are you sorry to do what is just?

King.
We need deep thought here, thought that saves, an eye that sees clearly
and is not too drunk with wine, to go down like a diver into the depths,
so that this comes out without harm, first for the city and then for
ourselves, and ends well; so that no fight over hostages fastens on us,
and so that we do not hand you over, settled as you are in the seats of
the gods, and bring in to live with us the god who destroys everything,
the heavy avenger, who does not set a man free even in Hades, even dead.
Do you really think there is no need of thought that saves?

Chorus.
	Think, and be,
	with all justice, our reverent
	protector. Do not betray the fugitive,
	driven from far away
	by a godless expulsion.

Chorus.
	Do not see me dragged
	from these seats of many gods
	as a prize, you who hold all the power in the land.
	Recognise the outrage of these men
	and guard against the anger.

Chorus.
	Do not bring yourself to see your suppliant
	hauled from the images of the gods by force,
	against justice, like a horse by the headband,
	with hands seizing my robes
	of many-coloured weave.

Chorus.
	Know this: whichever way you settle it,
	your children and your house
	are left to pay the debt
	in full, measure for measure.
	Think about this. The power of Zeus is just.

King.
I have thought about it. And this is where I run aground. Against one
side or the other I am forced to take up a great war. There is no way
round it. The hull is bolted tight, as if the shipwrights' windlasses
had drawn it in. There is no ending anywhere without pain.

When property is plundered from a house, more can come, by the grace of
Zeus who guards a man's goods, a fresh cargo bigger than the loss. And
when a tongue has shot its arrows wide of the mark, one speech can heal
the wound another made, a soothing word for the bitter pain that stirs
the heart. But to keep kindred blood from being shed, there must be
sacrifice, and many victims must fall to many gods, to heal the harm
before it comes. Truly, I have come deep into this quarrel, and I am the
loser by it. I would rather be ignorant of trouble than an expert in it.
But let it go well, against my expectation.

Chorus.
Hear now the last of my many respectful words.

King.
I hear you. Speak. It will not escape me.

Chorus.
I have breast-bands and belts to gather up my robes.

King.
Such things go well with women, no doubt.

Chorus.
Out of these, then, understand me, there is a fine device.

King.
Tell me what words you are about to utter.

Chorus.
If you will not give this company some pledge it can trust—

King.
What does your device of belts accomplish for you?

Chorus.
To decorate these images with new tablets.

King.
That is a riddle. Say it plainly.

Chorus.
To hang ourselves from these gods, as fast as we can.

King.
I have heard a word that is a whip across my heart.

Chorus.
You understand. I have given you eyes to see it clearly.

King.
Yes, and on every side there are things hard to wrestle down. A mass of
troubles is coming at me like a river. I have put out onto a bottomless
sea of ruin with no easy crossing, and there is no harbour from trouble
anywhere. If I do not pay you this debt, the pollution you speak of is
beyond any arrow's range. And if I stand in front of my walls against
your own blood, the sons of Aegyptus, and fight it out to the end, how
is that not a bitter price to pay: men's blood staining the ground for
the sake of women? And yet I have to respect the anger of Zeus who
guards the suppliant. Among mortals there is no fear higher than that.

You, old father of these girls, take these branches in your arms at once
and lay them on the other altars of our country's gods, so that every
citizen sees the sign that you have come as suppliants, and no word of
blame is thrown at me. The people love to find fault with those in
charge. And it may be that someone, seeing this and pitying it, will
hate the outrage of that male expedition, and the people will be better
disposed to you. Everyone feels goodwill towards the weaker side.

Danaus.
This is worth a great deal to us: to have found a protector who respects
us. But send escorts and guides with me, natives of the country, so that
we can find the altars in front of the temples of the gods who keep your
city, and their seats, and go through the town in safety. Our shape is
not like yours. The Nile does not raise the same stock as the Inachus.
Take care that boldness does not give birth to fear. Men have killed a
friend before now, not knowing him.

King.
Go with him, men. The stranger speaks well. Lead him to the city's
altars, the seats of the gods. And do not talk at length with anyone you
meet on the way, while you are bringing this seafarer to sit at the
hearths of the gods.

(Danaus goes out with the King's men.)

Chorus.
You have told him what to do, and let him go with his orders. But what
about me? What am I to do? Where do you set my safety?

King.
Leave your branches here, the sign of your trouble.

Chorus.
There. I leave them, at your word and at your hand's signal.

King.
Now move down into this open grove around the sanctuary.

Chorus.
But how can a grove that is not holy ground protect me?

King.
We will not hand you over to be snatched away by birds of prey.

Chorus.
But what if it is to men more hateful than cruel snakes?

King.
Speak well of what may come, when you are spoken to so well.

Chorus.
It is no wonder if fear in the mind makes me hard to bear with.

King.
Fear that goes too far is always beyond control.

Chorus.
Then cheer my heart, with deeds as well as words.

King.
Your father will not leave you alone for long. I am going now to call
together the people of this land and make the community friendly to you,
and I will teach your father what he ought to say. So stay here, and
pray to the gods of this country for what your heart longs for, while I
go to arrange it. May persuasion go with me, and the luck that gets
things done.

(The King goes out with his attendants.)
