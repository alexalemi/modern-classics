Seven Against Thebes
(Part 2 of 2)

Scout.
May it be so. Now the fifth. He is posted at the fifth gate, the North
Gate, right beside the tomb of Amphion, Zeus's son. He swears by the
spear in his hand, which he trusts and honours more than any god and
more than his own eyes, that he will sack the city of the Cadmeans in
spite of Zeus. That is what he says, this beautiful shoot of a
mountain-ranging mother, this man who is half boy still. The down is
only now creeping across his cheeks, the first thick hair coming up
as his youth comes on. But the spirit he brings up to the gate is
savage, nothing like a girl's, for all that his name says girl, and
the eye in his head is a Gorgon's. And he does not stand at the gate
without a boast of his own. On his shield of beaten bronze, the round
wall he holds in front of his body, he was swinging the shame of our
city: the Sphinx, the eater of raw flesh, fastened on with bolts, her
body bright and hammered up out of the metal, and under her she
carries a man, one of the Cadmeans, so that as many missiles as
possible will be thrown at that man. He has not come to haggle over
the fighting, it seems, or to disgrace the long road he travelled.
Parthenopaeus, the Arcadian. Such is the man: a resident foreigner
paying Argos back for a fine upbringing, and he threatens these
towers with things I pray no god brings about.

Eteocles.
If only they got from the gods exactly what they intend, they and
those unholy boasts of theirs. Then they would die, all of them,
utterly and badly. For this one too, the Arcadian you speak of, there
is a man with no boast in him, whose hand sees what has to be done:
Actor, brother of the man I named before. He will not
let a tongue with no deeds behind it come flooding in through the gate
and swell our troubles. He will not let a man in who carries the image
of that hateful biting beast on an enemy shield. She will turn on the
man who carries her, from the outside in, when she meets the thick
hammering of blows under the city wall. If the gods are willing, what
I say may turn out true.

Chorus.
	The words go through my chest.
	The hair on my head stands up straight
	as I hear the huge threats of these huge talkers,
	these unholy men. Oh, let the gods
	destroy them here, in our land.

Scout.
The sixth I would name is the most level-headed man there and the
best of them in courage, a seer: mighty Amphiaraus. He is posted at
the Homoloid Gate, and he is heaping abuse on mighty Tydeus. Killer of
men. Wrecker of the city. Argos's great schoolmaster in evil. The man
who calls up the Fury. Slaughter's servant. Adrastus's counsellor in
all this wickedness. And then he turned his eyes up to heaven and
spoke to your own brother, mighty Polyneices, your mother's son, and
twice, at the end, he broke the name in two and called him by it,
Poly-neices, Much-Strife. This is what he said, word for word:

"Is this really a deed the gods love? Is it a fine thing to hear
about, a fine thing to tell the men who come after us, that you sacked
the city of your fathers and the gods of your own blood, and threw a
foreign army at them? What justice is there in drying up the spring
that is your mother? Your fatherland, taken by the spear because you
were in a hurry: how is it ever going to be your ally? As for me, I
will fatten this soil, a seer buried under enemy ground. Let us fight.
I do not expect a shameful death."

That is what the seer said, calmly, holding his shield of solid
bronze, and there was no device on its circle at all. He does not want
to seem the best. He wants to be it, and he reaps the deep furrow that
runs through his mind, where the good plans grow. Send wise and brave
men to row against that one, that is my advice. A man who reveres the
gods is a man to be afraid of.

Eteocles.
Oh, the bad luck of it, the omen that yokes a just man to the most
godless of men. In every business there is nothing worse than bad
company. Its crop is not worth carrying home: the field of ruin
grows death for its harvest. A god-fearing man boards a ship with a
crew of hotheads bent on some crime, and he goes down with that race
of men the gods spit on. Or he is a just man among fellow citizens who
hate strangers and have forgotten the gods, and he is caught in the
same net as them, though he does not deserve it, and struck down by
the god's whip that falls on everyone alike. So it is with the seer,
the son of Oecles: a level-headed, just, good, god-fearing man, a
great prophet, mixed in with godless, loud-mouthed men against his own
judgement, men marching out in a long procession that is a long way
back. If Zeus is willing, he will be dragged down with them. My own
guess is that he will not even attack the gate. Not because he has no
heart or a weak will, but because he knows he has to die in this
battle, if the oracles of Apollo Loxias are to bear fruit. And that
god likes either to say nothing or to say the thing that counts. Even
so, I will post a man against him: mighty Lasthenes, a gatekeeper who
hates strangers. He has an old man's mind in a body in its prime, a
quick-footed eye, and a hand that is not slow to drive its spear into
whatever shows past the edge of a shield. But it is the gift of a god
when mortals succeed.

Chorus.
	Hear our just prayers, gods, and bring them to pass.
	Let the city come through this safe.
	Turn the hard work of the spear back
	on the men who have invaded our land.
	Let Zeus strike them outside the walls
	with his thunderbolt and kill them.

Scout.
Now the seventh, at the seventh gate: your own brother, and the fate
he prays for and calls down on this city. He prays that he will climb
our towers and be proclaimed king over the land, shout the war-cry of
victory over a captured city, and then meet you hand to hand. And
having killed you, he prays to die beside you. Or, if you live, to pay
you back for dishonouring him, for driving him out, with the same
coin: exile. That is what
mighty Polyneices is shouting, and he calls on the gods of his birth,
the gods of his fathers' land, to look down on his prayers and see
every one of them granted. He carries a newly made shield, a
perfect circle, with a double device cleverly fastened on it. A man in
armour, worked in beaten gold, is led along by a woman walking ahead
of him with modest steps. She says she is Justice, and the letters on
the shield say it too: "I will bring this man home, and he will have
his city and the run of his father's house again." Those are the
inventions on their shields. Now decide for yourself, quickly, whom
you think to send. You will never be able to fault this man for what
he reported. What you must decide for yourself is how to steer the
city.

(The Scout goes out.)

Eteocles.
Oh, my family, driven mad by the gods and hated by them, house of
Oedipus, all tears. Oh no. Now my father's curses come true. But it is
not right for me to weep or to wail, in case an even harder grief is
born out of this one. As for the man whose name fits him so well,
Polyneices, I mean, we will soon know where that device on his shield
ends up, whether golden letters babbling on a shield, with a wandering
mind behind them, are going to bring him home. If Justice, Zeus's
unmarried daughter, had been with him in what he did and what he
thought, that might have happened. But
not when he came out of the darkness of his mother's womb, not while
he was nursed, not when he was a boy growing up, not when the beard
first gathered on his chin, not once did Justice look at him and judge
him worth her while. And I do not think she is standing beside him
now, while he does his own fatherland this violence. She would be
false to her own name, and justly so, if she kept company with a man
who would dare anything. Trusting in that, I will go and stand against
him. I myself. Who has a better right? Commander against commander,
brother against brother, enemy against enemy, I will take my stand.
Quick, bring my greaves, my armour against spear and stone.

Chorus.
No, son of Oedipus, dearest of men, do not become the twin in temper
of the man with the worst of names. It is enough that Cadmeans come to
grips with Argives. That blood can be washed clean. But two brothers
of one blood killing each other: that is a pollution that never grows
old.

Eteocles.
If a man has to suffer something bad, let it at least be without
shame. That is the only profit there is among the dead. But when a
thing is bad and shameful together, you will find no glory in it to
speak of.

Chorus.
	What are you so hungry for, child?
	Do not let the spear-mad ruin that fills the heart
	carry you away. Throw out this evil desire
	while it is still a beginning.

Eteocles.
Since the god is driving the business so hard, let the whole race of
Laius, which Phoebus hates, go down the wind, its lot the wave of
Cocytus.

Chorus.
	A raw-biting desire is goading you too far,
	to bring off a killing of men
	whose fruit is bitter: blood the gods forbid.

Eteocles.
Yes, my own dear father's curse, hateful, wretched, sits on my dry,
unweeping eyes and says: the gain comes first, and then the death.

Chorus.
	But do not let it drive you. No one will call you a coward
	for coming out of this with your life and your honour.
	Will the Fury in her black goat-skin not leave the house
	when the gods take a sacrifice from your hands?

Eteocles.
The gods have already stopped caring about us, somehow. The only gift of
ours they admire is our destruction. So why should I go on fawning on a
deadly fate?

Chorus.
	Now, while it stands right beside you.
	Because the spirit may turn, in time,
	with a change of heart, and come on a gentler wind.
	Right now it is still boiling.

Eteocles.
Yes, it boiled over: the curses of Oedipus. And the visions that came to
me in my sleep were too true, dividing up my father's property.

Chorus.
Listen to women, even though you do not like to.

Eteocles.
Say something that can be done. And do not make it long.

Chorus.
Do not go yourself, not on that road to the seventh gate.

Eteocles.
I am whetted sharp. You will not blunt me with words.

Chorus.
And yet a victory, even a cowardly one, is honoured by god.

Eteocles.
A man in armour has no business hugging that saying to himself.

Chorus.
But do you want to reap your own brother's blood?

Eteocles.
When the gods give evils, you cannot run away from them.

(Eteocles goes out to the seventh gate.)

Chorus.
	I shudder at the goddess who wrecks a house,
	the goddess who is not like other gods,
	the prophet of evil who is never wrong,
	the Fury a father prayed for:
	that she will carry out the raging curses
	of Oedipus, whose mind was hurt.
	This strife that kills his sons is driving her on.

Chorus.
	A stranger is dealing out their inheritance,
	a Chalybian settler out of Scythia,
	a bitter divider of goods, savage-hearted iron.
	He has shaken the lots for the land they will live in,
	as much as the dead can hold,
	and no share in the wide plains.

Chorus.
	And when they have died, each by the other's hand,
	each cutting the other down,
	and the dust of the earth has drunk
	their black clotted blood,
	who could find a cleansing for them?
	Who could wash them? Oh,
	new troubles of this house
	mixed in with the old evils.

Chorus.
	I am speaking of the old crime,
	the crime whose punishment came fast
	and has stayed on into the third generation:
	when Laius, against Apollo,
	who told him three times
	at his Pythian oracle at the navel of the earth
	that he could save the city
	if he died without children,

Chorus.
	was overpowered by his own foolish longing
	and fathered his own death,
	Oedipus, who killed his father,
	who sowed his mother's holy field,
	the field where he himself was grown,
	and endured a root of blood.
	Madness brought that pair together,
	a bride and groom out of their minds.

Chorus.
	It is like a sea of evils driving its wave on.
	One wave falls, and the sea lifts another,
	three-crested, and it crashes and boils
	around the stern of the city.
	In between, the defence stretches thin,
	no wider than a wall.
	I am afraid the city will go down
	along with its kings.

Chorus.
	Because when a curse spoken long ago comes true,
	the settling of accounts is heavy,
	and once the deadly thing exists, it does not pass by.
	When the wealth of men who live by their gain
	grows too fat,
	it has to go overboard, from the stern.

Chorus.
	What man did the gods ever admire so much,
	the gods, and the people who share the city's hearth,
	and the crowded gathering of mortals,
	as they honoured Oedipus then,
	when he took out of our land
	the plague that snatched men away?

Chorus.
	But when he came to himself
	and understood, poor wretch, his terrible marriage,
	he could not bear the pain. With a maddened heart
	he did two evil things at once.
	With the hand that killed his father
	he struck out his own eyes,
	which he loved more than his children.

Chorus.
	And on his sons he loosed savage words,
	angry at the way they kept him, aiai,
	curses from a bitter tongue:
	that one day they would divide his goods
	with iron in their hands.
	And now I tremble
	that the Fury, on her quick feet, is bringing it about.

(The Scout comes back from the walls.)

Scout.
Take heart, you daughters your mothers raised. This city has escaped
the yoke of slavery. The boasts of those mighty men have fallen to the
ground. The city is in fair weather. Under all the blows of the waves
she has not taken on a drop of water. The wall holds, and we barred
the gates with champions we could trust, one man against one man. At
six of the gates all is well. But the seventh: the
holy lord Apollo, the leader of sevens, took the seventh gate for
himself, and on the children of Oedipus he finished the old folly
of Laius.

Chorus.
What new thing has happened to the city, on top of all the rest?

Scout.
The city is saved. But the kings, born of the same seed—

Chorus.
Who? What are you saying? Fear of your news is driving me out of my
mind.

Scout.
Be sensible now, and listen. The sons of Oedipus—

Chorus.
Oh, I am wretched. I am a prophet of these evils.

Scout.
Beyond any argument, beaten down into the dust—

Chorus.
They are lying out there? It is heavy. But tell it anyway.

Scout.
The men are dead, by their own hands, each killing the other.

Chorus.
So with brothers' hands they slaughtered each other, both at once?

Scout.
Yes, so alike was the fate that took them both, too much alike. It is
that fate, all by itself, that is using up this ill-starred family.
There is cause here for joy and for tears. Joy, because the city is
doing well. Tears, because its two masters, the two generals, have
divided up the whole of their inheritance with hammered Scythian iron.
They will own exactly as much land as they take when they are buried,
swept away in misery, just as their father's curses said. The city is
saved. But the earth has drunk the blood of the two
kings born of the same seed, each murdered by the other.

(The Scout goes out.)

Chorus.
	Great Zeus, and you gods who hold the city,
	you who truly guard these towers of Cadmus,
	which do I do?
	Shout for joy and raise the women's cry
	because the city is safe and unharmed,
	or weep for the war-lords,
	miserable, ill-starred, and childless,
	who lived up to their names,
	men of much strife,
	and died of their godless intent?

Chorus.
	Black curse on the race of Oedipus,
	curse that has come to its end,
	a cold evil is falling round my heart.
	I have made a song for the tomb,
	like a wild woman of Dionysus,
	hearing of corpses dripping blood,
	dead by a miserable fate.
	It is a bird of bad omen,
	this music played to the spear.

Chorus.
	It has done its work, it did not fall short,
	the prayer a father spoke.
	The plans of Laius, made in disbelief, held their force.
	And I am anxious for the city:
	what the gods have said does not go blunt.
	Oh, men of many groans,
	you have done a thing past belief.
	Sorrows to scream at have come, and not in words.

(The bodies of the two brothers are carried in.)

Chorus.
	It is plain to see. The Scout's report is here in front of us.
	Double troubles, twin men killed by each other,
	a double share of suffering, complete.
	What can I say?
	What else but this: pain on pain
	sits at this house's hearth.
	Come, friends, on the wind of grief,
	row with your hands about your heads,
	the stroke that sends the dead on their way,
	the stroke that always carries across Acheron
	the black-sailed ship of pilgrims with no sail spread,
	to the shore where Apollo never walks,
	the sunless shore that takes in everyone,
	the land no one can see.
	But here they come to their bitter task,
	Antigone and Ismene,
	to keen for their two brothers.
	There is no doubt in my mind that from their lovely,
	deep-folded breasts they will send up
	a grief worthy of the dead.
	But it is right that we, before their voices,
	shriek out the ugly hymn of the Fury
	and then sing the victory-song
	that Hades loves and we hate.
	Oh, unluckiest in your brothers of all women
	who fasten a belt about their dress,
	I cry, I groan, and there is no pretence in it:
	this shrill lament comes straight from my heart.

Chorus.
	Oh. Oh. Men of wrong minds,
	whom your own people could not persuade
	and no evil could wear down,
	you captured your father's house
	with the spear, in your misery.
	Misery, yes. They found miserable deaths,
	and outraged their house to find them.

Chorus.
	Oh. Oh. You who were set to knock down
	the walls of your own home,
	who had eyes for one bitter thing, sole rule,
	you are reconciled now,
	with iron.
	And all too truly the queenly Fury
	of your father Oedipus has brought it to pass.

Chorus.
	Struck through the left side,
	struck, yes,
	through the flanks that grew in one womb.
	Aiai, the god in it.
	Aiai, the curses,
	death answering death.
	Struck clean through, you say,
	in their bodies and in their house,
	by a rage that has no voice,
	by the doom their father's curse called down,
	a doom that did not divide them.

Chorus.
	And the groaning runs right through the city.
	The towers groan.
	The ground that loved these men groans.
	But the property stays, for the ones who come after,
	the property through which these ill-fated men,
	through which the quarrel came,
	and death, the end of it.
	Quick-tempered, they shared out the goods
	so that each got an equal share.
	The one who reconciled them is not blameless
	to those who loved them,
	and there is no grace in Ares.

Chorus.
	Struck down by iron, this is how they are,
	and struck down by iron, what waits for them?
	Someone might ask that. What waits for them
	is their share of their father's tomb.
	Our loud, shrieking, tearing grief
	sends them on their way from this house,
	a groan for our own selves, a pain of our own,
	sick at heart, with no joy in it,
	weeping real tears from the heart,
	the heart that wastes away as I weep
	for these two lords.

Chorus.
	And it can be said over these wretched men
	that they did much to the citizens,
	and much to the ranks of all the foreigners
	who died by the crowd in the fighting.
	Ill-starred, beyond every woman
	who is called a mother, is the woman who bore them.
	She took her own child for her husband
	and bore these two,
	and this is how they ended,
	killing each other with hands
	born of the same seed.

Chorus.
	Of the same seed, yes, and both destroyed,
	in a parting with no love in it,
	in strife gone mad,
	at the end of their quarrel.
	Their hatred has stopped.
	Their lives are mixed together
	in the earth that runs with their blood.
	Now they truly are of one blood.
	Bitter, the settler of their quarrels,
	the stranger from across the sea,
	the iron whetted sharp and rushed out of the fire.
	Bitter too was Ares, the wicked divider of goods,
	who made their father's curse come true.

Chorus.
	They have their share, poor wretches,
	their allotment of the griefs the gods gave.
	Under their bodies
	the wealth of earth will have no bottom.
	Oh, you have crowned your family
	with troubles, like a garland.
	At the last the Curses have raised their war-cry,
	the shrill tune, over a family
	routed and put to flight on every side.
	A trophy to Ruin stands at the gate
	where they struck each other down,
	and the god, having mastered them both,
	stopped there.

(Antigone stands by the body of Polyneices, Ismene by the body of
Eteocles. The two sisters sing in answer to each other.)

Antigone.
You struck, and were struck.

Ismene.
You killed, and died.

Antigone.
With the spear you killed—

Ismene.
With the spear you died—

Antigone.
Misery in the doing.

Ismene.
Misery in the suffering.

Antigone.
Let the wailing come.

Ismene.
Let the tears come.

Antigone.
You lie there dead—

Ismene.
Who did the killing.

Antigone.
	Ah.

Ismene.
	Ah.

Antigone.
	My mind is mad with grief.

Ismene.
	The heart groans inside me.

Antigone.
	Oh. Oh. You, whom I mourn for everything.

Ismene.
	And you, wretched in everything.

Antigone.
	By one of your own you were destroyed.

Ismene.
	And one of your own you killed.

Antigone.
	Double to tell—

Ismene.
	Double to see—

Antigone.
	these griefs, brother beside brother.

Ismene.
	Brothers' sorrows beside brothers'.

Chorus.
	Oh, Fate, hard giver of hard gifts,

Antigone.

Ismene.

Antigone.
	Sorrows hard to look at—

Ismene.

Antigone.

Ismene.

Antigone.

Ismene.

Antigone.

Ismene.
	Wretched suffering.

Antigone.

Chorus.
	Oh, Fate, hard giver of hard gifts,

Antigone.
	You know her now. You crossed through her—

Ismene.
	And you learned no later than he—

Antigone.
	when you came back to the city,

Ismene.
	yes, to row against this one with your spear.

Antigone.
	Deadly to tell.

Ismene.
	Deadly to see.

Antigone.
	Oh, the pain—

Ismene.
	Oh, the evils—

Antigone.
	for house and land.

Ismene.
	And above all for me.

Antigone.
	And further still, for me.

Ismene.

Antigone.
	Oh, most mourned of all men.

Ismene.
	Oh. Oh. Possessed by Ruin.

Antigone.
	Oh. Oh. Where in the earth shall we lay them?

Ismene.

Antigone.
	Oh. Oh. A grief to lie beside their father.

(A Herald comes from the city.)

Herald.
I am to announce what the people's councillors of this city of Cadmus
have resolved and decreed. Eteocles here,
for his loyalty to this land, is to be buried in a kindly grave dug
in its soil. He hated the enemy and chose to die in the city. He kept
faith with the shrines of his fathers, and he died without a stain on
him, in the place where it is a fine thing for the young to die. That
is what I was ordered to say about him. But his brother, this corpse
of Polyneices, is to be thrown outside the city unburied, for the
dogs to tear. He would have been the destroyer of the Cadmeans' land,
if one of the gods had not stood in the way of him with his brother's
spear. Even dead, he will carry the guilt of the gods of his fathers,
whom he dishonoured when he threw a foreign army at this city to take
it. So this is his reward, so the council rules: to be buried without
honour, under the birds of the air. No hands are to heap a mound over
him, no one is to pay him the respect of shrill wailing, and he is to
be denied the honour of being carried to the grave by the people who
loved him. That is what the Cadmeans' council has decreed.

Antigone.
And I have something to say to the rulers of the Cadmeans. If no one
else is willing to help me bury this man, I will bury him myself, and
take the risk of burying my own brother, and I am not ashamed of this
defiance, this refusal to obey the city. There is something to be
feared in the womb we shared, born as we were from a wretched mother
and an unlucky father. So, my soul, share his evils willingly, though
he had no will in it. Be alive with the dead, one heart with my
brother. No hollow-bellied wolf is going to tear the flesh off this
man. Let no one imagine it. I will find him a grave and a burial, even
though I am a woman, carrying the earth in the fold of my linen dress,
and I will cover him with my own hands. Let no one decree otherwise.
Take heart. I will find a way to get it done.

Herald.
I order you not to force the city's hand like this.

Antigone.
I order you not to waste your proclamations on me.

Herald.
A people that has just escaped disaster can be harsh.

Antigone.
Be harsh, then. This man will not go unburied.

Herald.
What? A man the city hates, and you will honour him with a grave?

Antigone.
The gods finished settling their account with him long ago.

Herald.
No. Not until he put this land in danger.

Antigone.
He was treated badly, and he gave back bad for bad.

Herald.
But he did it to all of us, not to one man.

Antigone.
Strife is the last of the gods to end an argument. I am going to bury
him. Stop making speeches.

Herald.
Follow your own counsel, then. But I forbid it.

(The Herald goes out.)

Chorus.
	Oh. Oh.
	Great boasters, wreckers of families,
	you Fates, you Furies, who have destroyed
	the race of Oedipus like this, from the root,
	what is to become of me? What do I do? What plan do I make?
	How can I bring myself neither to weep for you
	nor to walk beside you to your tomb?
	But I am afraid. I turn away
	in terror of the citizens.
	You, at least, will have many mourners.
	But he, poor wretch, goes off unwept,
	with one sister's song for his whole dirge.
	Who could go along with that?

First Half-Chorus.
	Let the city do what it likes, or not,
	to those who weep for Polyneices.
	We will go and bury him with her,
	we will walk beside his body.
	This grief belongs to the whole race,
	and what the city praises as just
	is one thing one day and another the next.

Second Half-Chorus.
	And we will go with this one,
	as the city and justice together approve.
	Because after the blessed gods and the strength of Zeus,
	it was this man, more than any other,
	who kept the city of the Cadmeans from capsizing,
	from going under
	in a wave of foreign men.

(All go out, the two processions taking the two bodies in opposite
directions.)
