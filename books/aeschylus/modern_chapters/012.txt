The Eumenides
(Part 2 of 2)

(Athena comes in with a herald and the judges she has chosen, and a
crowd of citizens behind them. The judges take their seats. Orestes
stands in the place set aside for the accused, with the Furies facing
him.)

Athena.
Herald, make the proclamation and hold the crowd back. Let the
Etruscan trumpet, the one that pierces the ear, fill with human breath
and send its high note out over the people. While this council chamber
fills, it is right for everyone to keep silent and to learn my
ordinances: the whole city, for all the time that is to come, and these
two parties here, so that their case may be judged well.

(Apollo comes in and takes his stand beside Orestes.)

Chorus.
Lord Apollo, rule over what is yours. Tell us what share you have in
this business.

Apollo.
I have come to give evidence, because this man is by law a suppliant
of mine and a guest at my hearth, and I am the one who cleansed him of
the killing. And I have come to plead his case myself. The
responsibility for his mother's death is mine. Athena, bring in the
case, and settle it as you know how.

Athena.
The word is yours. I open the case. The prosecutor speaks first and
tells it from the beginning; that is the right way to teach us what
happened.

Chorus.
We are many, but we will be brief. Answer word for word, taking your
turn. Say first: did you kill your mother?

Orestes.
I killed her. There is no denying that.

Chorus.
One fall already, of the three that win a wrestling match.

Orestes.
You are boasting over a man who is not down yet.

Chorus.
Still, you have to tell us how you killed her.

Orestes.
I will tell you. With a drawn sword in my hand, I cut her throat.

Chorus.
Who persuaded you? On whose advice?

Orestes.
The oracles of this god here. He is my witness.

Chorus.
The prophet told you to kill your mother?

Orestes.
Yes, and to this moment I have no complaint against my luck.

Chorus.
If the vote takes hold of you, you will soon be talking differently.

Orestes.
I trust him. And my father will send help from his grave.

Chorus.
Trust in the dead, then, you who killed your mother.

Orestes.
I do. She was tainted twice over. Two pollutions were on her.

Chorus.
How? Explain that to the judges.

Orestes.
She killed her husband, and in killing him she killed my father.

Chorus.
And so you are alive, and she is free of it. Her death has cleared her.

Orestes.
Then why did you not hunt her into exile while she was alive?

Chorus.
The man she killed was not her blood.

Orestes.
And I am my mother's blood?

Chorus.
How else did she feed you, murderer, inside her body, under her belt?
Do you disown the closest blood there is, your mother's?

Orestes.
Now give your evidence, Apollo. Explain for me whether I had justice
on my side when I killed her. That I did it, exactly as it was done, I
do not deny. But whether this blood was shed justly or not, in your
judgment, is for you to decide, so that I can tell the court.

Apollo.
I will speak to you, Athena's great court, with justice, and being a
prophet I will not lie. Never once, from my seat of prophecy, have I
said a thing about a man, a woman or a city that Zeus, the father of
the Olympians, did not order me to say. Learn how strong this plea of
justice is, and take my advice: follow my father's will. No oath is
stronger than Zeus.

Chorus.
Zeus, you say, gave you this oracle: to tell Orestes here to avenge
his father's murder and to pay no honour at all to his mother?

Apollo.
Yes. It is not the same thing at all: a noble man killed, a man
honoured with the sceptre that Zeus gives, and killed by a woman. Not
with the darting arrows of a bow shot from far off, the way an Amazon
kills, but in the way you are about to hear, Pallas, and you men who
sit to divide this case with your votes. She welcomed him home from
the campaign, where he had done better, for the most part, than anyone
had hoped, and she welcomed him with kind words. Then, as he stepped
from the bath, at the very edge of it, she flung a robe over him like
a tent, fettered her husband in the endless embroidered folds of that
robe, and hacked him down. That is the death I set before you: the
death of a man honoured by everyone, the commander of the fleet. And I
have described the woman as I have so that the people appointed to
decide this case may feel the sting of it.

Chorus.
Zeus, by your account, gives the higher honour to a father's death.
Yet Zeus himself put his old father Cronus in chains. How is that not
the opposite of what you say? Judges, I call you to witness that you
heard it.

Apollo.
You beasts that everyone hates, you things the gods loathe! Chains can
be undone. There is a cure for that, and plenty of ways to be set
loose. But once the dust has drunk up a man's blood, once he is dead,
there is no standing up again. For that my father has made no charms,
though he turns everything else upside down and sets it back in place
without so much as a hard breath.

Chorus.
Then look at what your plea for his acquittal means. He poured his
mother's blood, his own blood, out on the ground, and after that he is
going to live in his father's house in Argos? What public altars can he
use? What clan will let him wash his hands in the water where the
brotherhood washes its hands?

Apollo.
I will answer this too, and see how right I am. The mother is not the
parent of the thing that is called her child. She is only the nurse of
the seed that was newly sown in her. The one who mounts her is the
parent. She keeps the young shoot safe, a stranger keeping a stranger's
property, if no god damages it. And I will show you proof of what I
say. There can be a father without a mother. The witness is standing
right here: the daughter of Olympian Zeus, who was never nursed in the
dark of a womb, and yet a shoot such as no goddess could ever bear. For
my part, Pallas, in everything else as I know how, I will make your
city and your people great, and I sent this man to your hearth as a
suppliant so that he would be faithful to you for all time, so that
you would gain him and those who come after him as allies, goddess,
and so that it would stand for ever: the descendants of these men
keeping faith with the pledges made today.

Athena.
Shall I now order these men to cast an honest vote according to their
judgment? Has enough been said?

Chorus.
Every arrow we have has been shot. I am waiting to hear how the
contest is decided.

Athena.
Well? And you two, how do I arrange this so that neither of you can
blame me?

Apollo.
You have heard what you have heard. As you cast your ballots, friends,
keep the oath sacred in your hearts.

Athena.
Hear my ordinance now, people of Attica, as you judge the first case
ever tried for spilled blood. From this day on, and for ever, the
people of Aegeus will have this council of judges.

This hill of Ares, where the Amazons pitched their tents and camped
when they came with an army out of hatred for Theseus, and built this
new high-towered city against his city, tower against tower, and
sacrificed to Ares, which is how this rock got its name, the Hill of
Ares: on this hill the reverence of the citizens, and fear, which is
reverence's kinsman, will hold them back from doing wrong by day and
by night alike, as long as the citizens themselves do not pollute the
laws with foul streams. Foul clear water with mud and you will never
find a drink.

Neither anarchy nor a master: that is what I advise my citizens to
keep and to respect, and not to throw fear out of the city altogether.
What man who fears nothing does what is right? Stand in awe, and
rightly, of a majesty like this, and you will have a wall for your
land and a saving power for your city such as no people on earth
possesses, not among the Scythians, not in the land of Pelops. I found
this council here: untouched by greed, worthy of reverence, quick to
anger, a guard for the land that stays awake over the sleeping.

That was my advice to my citizens, stretched out over the future. Now
you must rise, take up a ballot, and decide the case, respecting your
oath. I have said what I have to say.

(The judges rise and go one by one to the urns while the Furies and
Apollo argue.)

Chorus.
And I advise you: this company of ours is heavy for a land to bear. Do
not dishonour it in any way.

Apollo.
And I order you to fear the oracles, mine and Zeus's, and not to make
them barren.

Chorus.
Blood is not your business, yet you show respect for a bloody deed.
From now on the oracles you give will be unclean.

Apollo.
Then was my father mistaken in his judgment when Ixion, the first man
ever to kill, came to him as a suppliant?

Chorus.
Talk on. But if I do not get justice in this case, I will settle on
this land again, and I will be heavy on it.

Apollo.
You have no honour among the gods, the young or the old. I will win.

Chorus.
You did the same sort of thing in the house of Pheres. You persuaded
the Fates to make mortals free from death.

Apollo.
Is it wrong to do good to a man who worships you, above all when he
needs it?

Chorus.
You were the one who broke up the ancient allotments. You tricked the
old goddesses with wine.

Apollo.
And you, when in a moment you have lost this case, will spit your
venom, and it will not hurt your enemies at all.

Chorus.
Since you, a young god, ride me down, an old woman, I will wait to
hear the verdict. I have not decided yet whether to be angry with this
city.

Athena.
This part is mine: to give the final judgment. And I add this vote to
Orestes' side. No mother gave birth to me. In everything, except for
marriage, I am for the male, with all my heart, and I am altogether my
father's child. So I will not give more weight to the death of a woman
who killed her husband, the guardian of the house. Orestes wins, even
if the votes come out equal. Turn the ballots out of the urns, as fast
as you can, you judges who were given that task.

(The ballots are turned out and counted.)

Orestes.
Phoebus Apollo, how will it be decided?

Chorus.
Night, black Mother, are you watching this?

Orestes.
Now it is the end of a rope for me, or I go on seeing the light.

Chorus.
And for us it is ruin, or we keep our honours and go on.

Apollo.
Count the ballots exactly as they fall, friends. Be in awe of doing
wrong as you divide them. When judgment is missing, great pain
follows; and one ballot, cast, has set a house back on its feet.

Athena.
This man is acquitted of the charge of blood. The count of the
ballots is equal.

(Apollo goes.)

Orestes.
Pallas, saviour of my house! I had lost my father's land, and you have
given me a home in it again. Now some Greek will say: the man is an
Argive again and lives on his father's property, by the grace of
Pallas and of Apollo, and of the third, the Saviour who brings
everything to its end, who looked with respect on my father's death
and saves me, though he sees these advocates of my mother's.

I am going home now, but first I swear an oath to this land and to
your people, for the whole of time to come: no captain of my country
will ever come here bringing a well-armed spear against them. If any
of them break the oath I am swearing now, I myself, in my grave by
then, will make it fail. I will make their marches dispirited and
their roads full of bad omens, so that they regret the effort. But
while they keep to the straight road and honour this city of Pallas
for ever with an allied spear, I will be all the kinder to them.

And so goodbye, you and the people who guard your city. May you get a
grip on your enemies that they cannot slip, a grip that saves you and
brings victory to your spear.

(Orestes goes out. The Furies turn on Athena in fury at the verdict.)

Chorus.
	Oh, you younger gods, you have ridden down the old laws,
	you have snatched them out of my hands.
	And I, dishonoured, wretched, heavy with anger,
	on this land, ah,
	I will let venom go, venom for my grief,
	out of my heart, a drip on the ground
	that nothing can bear. Out of it
	a blight with no leaf and no child,
	oh Justice, will race across the plain
	and throw its stains on the land to kill men.
	I groan. What shall I do?
	The citizens laugh at me.
	Unbearable, what I have suffered.
	Oh, great is the grief of the daughters of Night,
	the unlucky ones, dishonoured and mourning.

Athena.
Listen to me: do not take it with heavy groaning. You have not been
defeated. The case came out with equal votes, honestly, with no
dishonour to you. But clear evidence from Zeus was there, and the one
who gave the oracle was himself the one who testified that Orestes
should take no harm for doing what he did. So do not be angry. Do not
hurl your heavy rage on this land or make it barren, letting loose the
drops of your divine spite, savage spearpoints that eat the seed. I
promise you, in all justice, that you will have a seat and deep
places in this righteous land, sitting on shining thrones beside your
hearths, and these citizens will honour you.

Chorus.
	Oh, you younger gods, you have ridden down the old laws,
	you have snatched them out of my hands.
	And I, dishonoured, wretched, heavy with anger,
	on this land, ah,
	I will let venom go, venom for my grief,
	out of my heart, a drip on the ground
	that nothing can bear. Out of it
	a blight with no leaf and no child,
	oh Justice, will race across the plain
	and throw its stains on the land to kill men.
	I groan. What shall I do?
	The citizens laugh at me.
	Unbearable, what I have suffered.
	Oh, great is the grief of the daughters of Night,
	the unlucky ones, dishonoured and mourning.

Athena.
You are not dishonoured. So do not, goddesses, in the excess of your
anger, make a land of mortals into a place no charm can cure. I too
trust in Zeus, and why should I need to say it? I alone of the gods
know the keys of the house where his thunderbolt is kept under seal.
But there is no need of that. Let me persuade you. Do not fling the
words of a reckless tongue at this land, so that everything that bears
fruit fails to prosper. Put the black wave's bitter fury to sleep,
since you will have your share of honour and a house beside mine. And
when you have the first fruits of this wide land for ever, offerings
made for the birth of children and for the rite of marriage, you will
praise what I said.

Chorus.
	That I should suffer this, ah,
	I with my ancient mind, to live under the earth,
	ah, a thing disowned, a pollution.
	I breathe fury, I breathe out all my rage.
	Oh, oh, earth, ah!
	What is this creeping into me, what pain in my side?
	Hear my anger, mother
	Night. The gods have stripped my old honours
	from me with tricks that cannot be fought,
	and brought me to nothing.

Athena.
I will bear with your anger. You are older, and in that you are far
wiser than I am. But Zeus gave me a mind too, and not a bad one. If
you go off to some foreign land, you will fall in love with this one.
I tell you that now. Time, flowing on, will bring these citizens more
and more honour, and you, with your honoured seat by the house of
Erechtheus, will get more from the processions of men and women than
you could ever win from other mortals anywhere.

So do not throw into my country the whetstones of blood that sharpen
the young against each other, damage in the guts, a madness like
drunkenness without the wine. Do not tear the heart out of my citizens
as if they were fighting cocks and plant war among them, war between
kin, bold against each other. Let war be outside the door, and let
there be plenty of it, for the man with a terrible love of glory in
him. But a fight between birds in one house I will not call a war.
This is what you can choose from me: do good, receive good, be
honoured well, and have your share in this land the gods love most.

Chorus.
	That I should suffer this, ah,
	I with my ancient mind, to live under the earth,
	ah, a thing disowned, a pollution.
	I breathe fury, I breathe out all my rage.
	Oh, oh, earth, ah!
	What is this creeping into me, what pain in my side?
	Hear my anger, mother
	Night. The gods have stripped my old honours
	from me with tricks that cannot be fought,
	and brought me to nothing.

Athena.
No, I will not grow tired of telling you the good that is on offer,
so that you can never say that you, an ancient goddess, were thrown
out of this land by me, a younger one, and by the mortal guardians of
my city, dishonoured, an exile from this soil. But if you hold
Persuasion sacred, the sweetness and the spell of my tongue, then you
would stay. And if you will not stay, then it would not be just for
you to bring down on this city any wrath, any rage, any harm to its
people. It is open to you to hold land here by right, with full
honour for all time.

Chorus.
Lady Athena, what place do you say I will have?

Athena.
One free of every pain and every misery. Accept it.

Chorus.
Say I have accepted. What honour waits for me?

Athena.
That no house will thrive without you.

Chorus.
You will do this? You will make me that strong?

Athena.
Yes. We will set the fortunes of those who worship you standing
upright.

Chorus.
And you will give me a pledge for all time?

Athena.
I have no need to say what I will not do.

Chorus.
Your spell is working on me, I think. I am letting my anger go.

Athena.
Then stay in the land, and you will win friends.

Chorus.
What blessings, then, do you tell me to sing over this land?

Athena.
Those that look to a victory with nothing evil in it: blessings from
the earth, from the waters of the sea, from the sky; that the winds
blow on the land in bright sunshine; that the fruit of the earth and
the herds flow in abundance for my citizens and never fail with time;
and that the seed of men be kept safe. And be more generous to those
who honour the gods, because I, like a man who tends his plants, love
this race of just men and would keep it free of grief. That is yours
to give. For my part, I will not put up with this city going without
honour among men, this city that wins in the glorious contests of war,
where Ares kills.

Chorus.
	I will accept a home with Pallas.
	I will not dishonour a city
	that Zeus the all-powerful and Ares
	hold as a fortress of the gods,
	the jewel that guards the altars of the gods of Greece.
	For it I pray,
	prophesying in kindness,
	that life's good fortune come rushing up
	out of the earth in plenty
	under the bright glare of the sun.

Athena.
	This I do, with a glad heart, for these citizens:
	I have settled here among them
	powers that are great and hard to please.
	Everything that happens to men
	has fallen to them to manage.
	The man who has never felt their weight
	does not know where the blows of life come from.
	His fathers' sins drag him
	before them. Destruction says nothing,
	and however loud he shouts,
	its hateful anger grinds him to dust.

Chorus.
	Let no wind blow that damages the trees,
	I speak my favour now,
	no scorching heat that blinds the buds
	and crosses over its own border,
	no fruitless, creeping, endless plague.
	Let the earth feed the flocks
	so they thrive and drop twin lambs
	at the appointed time, and let the rich yield
	of the earth pay honour
	to the lucky gift of the gods.

Athena.
	Do you hear this, guards of my city,
	what she is bringing to pass?
	The Lady Fury has great power
	with the immortals and the gods under the earth,
	and among men, openly and to the end,
	they get their way: for some, songs,
	for others a life blurred with tears.

Chorus.
	I forbid the deaths that cut men down
	before their time.
	Give lovely girls a life with a husband,
	you who have the power, you Fates,
	goddesses, sisters of ours by one mother,
	powers who deal out what is straight,
	who have a share in every house,
	whose weight is felt at every season
	in your just visitations,
	most honoured of gods, everywhere.

Athena.
	That they are bringing this to pass
	for my land, with good will, makes me glad.
	And I love the eyes of Persuasion,
	that she watched over my tongue and my mouth
	against these women when they refused so savagely.
	But Zeus of the assembly has won.
	Our rivalry in doing good
	is the winner, for all time.

Chorus.
	Civil strife, never satisfied with evil,
	I pray that it never roars
	in this city.
	Let the dust not drink
	the black blood of citizens
	and then, in a rage for revenge,
	snatch greedily at the city's ruin,
	killing for killing.
	Let them give joy for joy
	with a mind for what they share,
	and hate with one heart.
	That is the cure for many things among men.

Athena.
	See, they are finding their way
	to the road of kind words.
	Out of these terrifying faces
	I see great profit for these citizens.
	Be kind to the Kindly Ones, always,
	honour them greatly, and you will be seen
	by all to keep your land and city
	on the straight road of justice.

Chorus.
	Farewell, farewell, in the wealth your fate has given you.
	Farewell, people of the city,
	who sit close to Zeus,
	friends of the virgin he loves,
	learning good sense at last.
	Under Pallas's wings you are,
	and her Father stands in awe of you.

Athena.
	Farewell to you too. I must go ahead
	to show you your chambers
	by the sacred light of these escorts.
	Go, and with these solemn sacrifices
	speed down under the earth.
	Hold back what would ruin the land
	and send up what profits it,
	so that the city wins.
	And you, children of Cranaus who hold this city,
	lead the way for these new settlers.
	And may the citizens have a good mind
	for the good that is done to them.

Chorus.
	Farewell, farewell, and again I say it,
	all of you in the city,
	gods and mortals both,
	who keep the city of Pallas.
	Show respect to me in my new home among you,
	and you will not blame
	the chances of your lives.

Athena.
I approve the words of your prayers, and I will escort you by the
light of blazing torches to the places below and under the earth, with
the attendants who guard my image, as is their duty. The eye of the
whole land of Theseus is coming out: a glorious company of children,
of women, and a band of old women. Do them honour in robes dyed
crimson, and let the light of the fire go forward, so that this kindly
company of guests in our land may show itself, from now on, in the
good fortune of good men.

(The procession forms with torches, and the Furies, robed in crimson,
are led away towards their new home under the earth. The Escort sings
as it goes.)

Escort.
	Go to your home, great ones, lovers of honour,
	ancient children of Night, under this kindly escort.

Escort.
	Keep a holy silence, people of the land.
	Under the age-old hollows of the earth,
	with honours and sacrifices, revered on every side,
	keep a holy silence, all the people.

Escort.
	Gracious and of one mind with the land,
	come this way, holy ones, and enjoy
	the fire-eaten torch along the road.
	Raise the cry now, over our song.

Escort.
	Peace for all time, between these new settlers
	and Pallas's people. Zeus who sees everything
	and Fate have come down together to seal it.
	Raise the cry now, over our song.
