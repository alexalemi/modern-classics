The Libation Bearers
(Part 1 of 2)

(Argos, years after the murder of Agamemnon. His tomb stands in the
open ground before the palace. Orestes comes in with his friend
Pylades and lays a lock of his hair on the grave.)

Orestes.
Hermes of the underworld, you who watch over the powers your father
handed down to you: be my saviour, fight at my side, I am asking you.
I have come back to this land. I have come home from exile. Here on
the mound of this tomb I call to my father to hear me, to listen.
Here is a lock of hair for Inachus, the river of my home, in thanks
for the years it raised me, and here is a second one, for grief.
Because I was not here to cry over your death, father, and I never
stretched out my hand when they carried your body to the grave.

What is this I am looking at? What is this crowd of women coming
this way, so plain to see in their black cloaks? What kind of trouble
should I read into it? Has some new blow fallen on the house? Or
would I be right to guess that they are carrying offerings to my
father, drink poured out to soothe the dead below? It can be nothing
else. Yes, that is Electra, I think, my own sister, walking with
them, and her bitter grief marks her out. Zeus, let me pay back my
father's death, and be my willing ally.

Pylades, let us stand out of the way, so I can find out for certain
what these women have come here to pray for.

(Orestes and Pylades step back out of sight. Electra comes in with
the women of the house, slaves, who carry the offerings for the
grave.)

Chorus.
	Sent out from the house, I have come
	to carry the offerings, and my hands beat the time,
	sharp blow on blow.
	My cheek shows it, torn red,
	where the nail has cut fresh furrows,
	and all my life long my heart feeds on crying.
	Grief has ripped the linen to rags,
	the weaving of my dress is torn
	across the breast, the cloth
	struck down by blows of fortune
	that no one laughs at.

Chorus.
	For Terror, with his hair standing on end,
	the dream-prophet of this house,
	breathing anger out of sleep,
	screamed in the dead of night
	from the innermost rooms,
	a cry of fear that fell heavy
	on the women's quarters.
	And the readers of dreams,
	speaking for the god, under oath,
	cried out that the dead under the earth
	are furious, that they blame,
	that they rage at the ones who killed them.

Chorus.
	With that kind of thanks, a thankless thanks,
	to turn the evil away,
	Oh, Earth, my mother,
	the godless woman sends me here.
	I am afraid to let the words out of my mouth.
	What ransom is there for blood once it has fallen on the ground?
	Oh, hearth of pure misery,
	Oh, house dug up to its foundations!
	Sunless, hated by men,
	the dark covers this house
	since its master was killed.

Chorus.
	The awe of majesty that nothing could fight,
	nothing could tame, nothing could break in war,
	that went in through the ears and the hearts of the people,
	stands aside now.
	And people are afraid.
	Success: that, among men, is a god,
	and more than a god.
	But the scales of Justice keep watch.
	For some the blow comes fast, in daylight;
	for some, pain waits and ripens
	in the twilight between;
	and some an empty night holds fast.

Chorus.
	Because of blood drunk down by the earth that nursed us,
	the murder that wants payment has set hard
	and will not drain away.
	Ruin, an agony, splits the guilty man
	till he swells with a sickness that fills him whole.

Chorus.
	For the man who touches a bride's bed that is not his
	there is no cure. All the rivers,
	flowing in one course to wash the blood
	from the polluted hand, run for nothing.

Chorus.
	And I, since the gods threw a necessity around my city
	and led me out of my father's house
	into a slave's lot,
	I must approve what my masters do,
	just and unjust, forcing my heart down,
	and master my bitter hatred.
	But under my veil I weep
	for the senseless fate of my lord,
	frozen through with hidden grief.

Electra.
You slave women who keep this house in order: since you are here
beside me, walking with me in this procession of prayer, advise me
about this. What should I say as I pour these offerings of mourning?
How do I find kind words? How do I pray to my father? Do I say I
bring them from a loving wife to a loved husband, from my mother? I
have not got the nerve for that, and I do not know what I should say
as I pour this offering onto my father's tomb. Or do I say the words
people usually say, "Repay with good the ones who send these
honours," a gift, that is, to match their crimes? Or do I pour them
out in silence and dishonour, the way my father died, for the earth
to drink, and then walk back the way I came, like someone throwing
out the rubbish after a purification, tossing the bowl away without
turning my eyes? Share this decision with me, friends. We share a
hatred in this house. Do not hide your thoughts in your heart out of
fear of anyone. What is fated waits for the free man and for the one
whom another's hand rules over. If you have something better to say
than this, say it.

Chorus.
I honour your father's tomb as if it were an altar, and since you
tell me to, I will say what my heart says.

Electra.
Say it, as you honour my father's grave.

Chorus.
Pour, and speak good words for the people who mean us well.

Electra.
And which of the people I love do I call by that name?

Chorus.
Yourself first, and then anyone who hates Aegisthus.

Electra.
Then I am to make this prayer for myself and for you?

Chorus.
You see it now. Work the rest out yourself.

Electra.
Then who else do I add to our side?

Chorus.
Remember Orestes, even though he is far from home.

Electra.
Good. That is the best reminder you could have given me.

Chorus.
Now, for the guilty ones who did the killing, remember —

Electra.
What do I say? Teach me, I have never done this. Show me the form.

Chorus.
That someone come for them, a god or a man —

Electra.
A judge, do you mean, or an avenger?

Chorus.
Say it plainly: someone who will kill them in return.

Electra.
And is it right for me to ask the gods for that?

Chorus.
How could it not be right, to pay back an enemy with harm?

Electra.
Greatest of heralds, of the world above and the world below, Hermes
of the underworld, help me. Carry my call to the spirits under the
earth, the ones who keep watch over my father's house, and tell them
to hear my prayers. And call Earth herself, who gives birth to
everything, and rears it, and takes its swelling harvest back again.
And I, as I pour this water to the dead, call on my father and say:
pity me, and pity Orestes, whom I love. How are we to be masters in
our own house again? Because as things stand we have been sold, we
are drifting, thanks to the woman who bore us, and she has taken
another man in exchange for you: Aegisthus, who shared in your
murder. I am no better than a slave. Orestes is driven out of his
inheritance, and the two of them are wallowing in the winnings of
your work, and the arrogance of it is enormous. That Orestes may
come home, with some luck on his side, I pray to you, father. Hear
me. And for myself, grant that I turn out far more decent than my
mother, and my hand far cleaner.

Those are the prayers for us. For our enemies I say this: father,
let your avenger appear, and let the killers be killed in their turn,
as Justice demands. I set that in the middle of my prayer for good,
speaking this curse against them. But to us, send up good things
from below, with the help of the gods and Earth and Justice who
brings victory.

(Electra pours the offerings out over her father's grave.)

Those are my prayers, and over them I pour these offerings. It is
your part, by custom, to crown them with your cries, and raise the
song for the dead.

Chorus.
	Let the tear fall, splashing as it drops,
	for our lord who has fallen,
	onto this rampart against evil,
	this ward against the pollution
	the good turn from and pray away,
	now the offerings are poured.
	Hear me, hear me, my honoured lord,
	out of the dark where your mind lies.
	Otototototoi.
	Let some man come, strong with the spear,
	to set the house free,
	an Ares, with the Scythian bow bending back in his hands,
	shaking its arrows in the fight,
	swinging a sword hilt-to-hand at close quarters.

Electra.
My father has his offerings now, the earth has drunk them. But here is
something new. Share it with me.

Chorus.
Say it. My heart is dancing with fear.

Electra.
I see a lock of hair here, cut off and left on the tomb.

Chorus.
A man's? Or some girl's, with the deep belt at her waist?

Electra.
That is easy for anyone to guess.

Chorus.
How so? Let the old learn from the young.

Electra.
There is no one who could have cut it but me.

Chorus.
True. The ones who should have mourned him with their hair are his
enemies.

Electra.
And look, to my eye it is very like, the same feather as —

Chorus.
Like whose hair? That is what I want to know.

Electra.
Very like our own, to look at.

Chorus.
Then can it be a secret gift from Orestes?

Electra.
It is his curls it looks like most.

Chorus.
And how did he dare to come here?

Electra.
He sent it. He cut his hair and sent it, to honour his father.

Chorus.
What you say gives me no less to cry over, if he is never going to set
foot on this land again.

Electra.
Over my heart too a wave of bitterness has broken, and I am struck as if
a spear had gone right through me. Out of my eyes fall thirsty drops,
nothing holds them back, a winter flood, as I look at this lock of hair.
How can I expect that any other person in this town owns it? And she
certainly did not cut it, the murderess, my mother, whose feeling for
her children is godless and does not deserve the name of mother. But as
for me, how can I say outright that this is a gift to me from Orestes,
the dearest man alive? Hope is fawning on me.

Electra.
Oh. If only it had a voice, and could speak kindly, like a messenger, so
that I would not be torn two ways and shaken, but it would tell me
plainly to spit this lock out, if it was cut from a head I hate, or, if
it came from my own blood, it could grieve with me, an ornament on this
tomb, an honour to my father. But we call on the gods, who know what
storms are spinning us round, like sailors. And if we are meant to reach
safety, from a small seed a great trunk can grow. And look, footprints,
a second piece of proof, the same shape as mine, matching my own feet.
Yes, here are the outlines of two pairs of feet, his own, and some
companion who travelled with him. The heels, the lines of the tendons,
measured out, come to exactly the same as my own tracks. I am in labour
with this. It is destroying my mind.

(Orestes comes out from where he was hidden and stands in front of
her.)

Orestes.
Pray for the rest as well. Tell the gods your prayers have been
answered, and ask that what comes next goes well.

Electra.
Why? What have I got now, by the will of the gods?

Orestes.
You have come face to face with what you prayed for so long.

Electra.
And what person do you know that I was calling on?

Orestes.
I know you were crying out for Orestes, again and again.

Electra.
Then how has my prayer been answered?

Orestes.
Here I am. Do not look for anyone closer to you than me.

Electra.
Is this some trick you are weaving around me, stranger?

Orestes.
Then I am spinning plots against myself.

Electra.
No. You want to laugh at my misery.

Orestes.
Then at my own too, if I laugh at yours.

Electra.
Am I to call you Orestes, then? Is it really you?

Orestes.
You are looking at the man himself, and you are slow to know me. Yet
when you saw this lock of mourning hair, and when you were tracking my
footprints, your heart took wing and you were sure you saw me. Look. Put
the hair back where it was cut from, your own brother's hair, and see
how it matches my head. And look at this weaving, the work of your own
hands, the strokes of the batten, and the hunting scene in the design.
Hold yourself together. Do not let joy knock your mind loose. I know
that the people closest to us are bitter enemies to us both.

Electra.
Oh, dearest of all to your father's house, the hope we wept for, the
seed that saves the line, trust your own strength and you will win your
father's house back. Oh, face I love, that has four shares of love in it
for me. I have to call you father; and the love I owe a mother falls to
you, because her I hate, with every right; and the love for my sister,
slaughtered without pity; and you were my faithful brother, the one
person who honoured me. May Power and Justice, and Zeus with them, the
third, greatest of all, stand at your side.

Orestes.
Zeus, Zeus, look down on what is happening here. See the brood of an
eagle whose father is dead, dead in the coils, in the twisting knots of
a terrible snake. The orphans are pinched with starving hunger, because
they are not grown enough to bring their father's kill back to the nest.
That is what you see when you look at me, and at her, at Electra,
children robbed of their father, both of us driven out of the same
house. If you destroy the nestlings of a father who sacrificed to you
and honoured you so highly, where will you find another hand like his to
give you the rich feast? Destroy the eagle's brood, and you will never
again send signs that men can believe; and if this royal trunk is left
to wither, it will not serve your altars on the days when oxen are
slaughtered. Look after it. From something small you could raise this
house to greatness again, though it looks now as if it has fallen flat.

Chorus.
Children, saviours of your father's hearth, quiet, so that no one hears
you, children, and carries all this to the ones in power for the
pleasure of talking. May I see them dead one day, in the pitch, in the
ooze of the flame.

Orestes.
Apollo's oracle will not betray me, it is too strong. It ordered me to
run this risk to the end, and cried out to me at the top of its voice,
naming ruin after ruin, storms of it, to chill the warm blood under my
liver, if I do not go after the men who killed my father, and kill them
the same way they killed him, made wild as a bull by a penalty no money
can pay. He said that I myself would pay for it with my own dear life,
and suffer a great deal first, and none of it pleasant. Because he
showed men what the anger of the dead under the earth does to the
living, and he named the plagues: sores that climb onto the flesh with
savage jaws and eat away what a man was born with, and white hair
sprouting on the diseased place. And he spoke of other attacks the
Furies make, set in motion by a father's blood: the dark arrow of the
powers below, loosed when kin who fell cry out for vengeance, and
madness, and empty terrors in the night, that harass a man and drive
him, a man who sees clearly and yet has to feel his way in the dark, and
hound him out of his city, his body flayed by a scourge of beaten
bronze. And for such a man, the god said, there is no share in the
wine-bowl, no drop of the friendly offering; his father's anger, unseen,
bars him from the altar; no one takes him in, no one shelters him; and
in the end, dishonoured by everyone, without a friend, he dies, badly,
wasted to a husk by a death that spoils him whole. Oracles like that:
must I not trust them? And even if I do not trust them, the thing has to
be done. Many wishes come together into one: the god's commands, and my
great grief for my father, and the pinch of having nothing, which
presses too, and this: that my fellow citizens, the most famous men
alive, who uprooted Troy with glory in their hearts, should not be the
subjects of two women. Because his heart is a woman's. If not, he will
soon find out.

Chorus.
	You great Fates, by the power of Zeus,
	let this end the way
	Justice is now turning.
	For a word of hatred let a word of hatred
	be paid. That is the debt collected,
	and Justice shouts it as she collects.
	For a murderous blow let a murderous blow
	be paid. The doer must suffer.
	That is the saying, three generations old.

Orestes.
	Father, unhappy father,
	what can I say, what can I do,
	to reach you on a fair wind from far away,
	there where your bed holds you,
	a light to set against your dark?
	And yet a song of grief that honours the sons of Atreus,
	the old masters of this house,
	is called a kindness, all the same.

Chorus.
	Child, the hungry jaw of the fire
	does not tame the mind of the dead man.
	Afterwards he shows his anger.
	The dying man is wailed over,
	and the man who harmed him is shown up.
	A just cry of grief for fathers, for parents,
	once it is raised full and wide,
	goes searching everywhere.

Electra.
	Hear us then, father, in turn,
	our grief and all our tears.
	Two children, over your tomb,
	sing you the dirge.
	Your grave has taken them in
	as suppliants and exiles alike.
	What of this is good? What is free of evil?
	Is ruin not past wrestling?

Chorus.
	And yet, out of all this, a god who wished it
	could still set a song with a brighter sound.
	Instead of the dirge at the graveside,
	a hymn of victory in the king's own halls
	could bring home the friend, a new wine, freshly mixed.

Orestes.
	If only under the walls of Troy,
	father, some Lycian's spear
	had cut you down and killed you!
	You would have left a good name in your house,
	and for your children, in the street,
	a life people turned to look at,
	and had a tomb heaped high
	in a land across the sea,
	a weight your house could bear.

Chorus.
	Loved there below by the men you loved,
	the ones who died well,
	standing out under the earth,
	a lord held in awe,
	right hand to the greatest kings down there,
	the kings of the dead.
	Because you were a king, while you lived,
	one of the men who fill the lot of death
	with their hands, and hold the staff men obey.

Electra.
	No, not under the walls of Troy,
	father, not killed there
	and buried by the crossing of Scamander
	with the rest of the army the spear cut down.
	Sooner let the ones who killed him
	be brought down the same way, by their own people,
	so that someone far off, who knows nothing
	of the trouble we have here,
	hears the news of their deadly end.

Chorus.
	What you say, child, is better than gold,
	greater than any great luck, greater than the Hyperboreans'.
	You can wish it, and it costs nothing.
	But the double lash is coming down now,
	I can hear it strike: the ones who help us
	are already under the earth,
	and the hands of the ones in power, hateful men, are unholy.
	It has gone the children's way.

Orestes.
	That went through my ear
	straight as an arrow.
	Zeus, Zeus, who send up from below
	the ruin that punishes late,
	on the hard, reckless hand of men —
	and yet it is a parent it will be paid to.

Chorus.
	Let it be mine to raise the shout, the shrill
	cry of triumph over the man
	as he is struck, over the woman
	as she dies. Why hide
	what hovers over my heart all the same?
	Before the prow of my heart
	a sharp wind blows, anger,
	rancorous hate.

Electra.
	And when will Zeus, strong with both hands,
	bring his fist down, oh, oh,
	and split their heads?
	Let the land have its pledge.
	After injustice I demand justice.
	Hear me, Earth, and you honoured powers below.

Chorus.
	It is the law: drops of murdered blood
	spilled on the ground demand
	more blood. Slaughter shouts for the Fury,
	who comes from those killed before
	bringing ruin on top of ruin.

Orestes.
	Popoi, you royal powers below,
	look, you Curses of the dead, strong Curses,
	look at what is left of the sons of Atreus,
	helpless, thrown out of their house,
	dishonoured. Which way can we turn, Zeus?

Chorus.
	And again my heart shakes
	as I hear this pitiful cry.
	One moment I have no hope,
	and my insides go black
	at the words I hear.
	Then hope lifts me again to strength,
	pushes the pain away
	and shows me a fair face.

Electra.
	What could we say that would hit the mark
	better than the pain we have suffered from the woman who bore us?
	Let her fawn. It will not be soothed.
	Like a wolf's, raw-hearted,
	the temper we got from our mother will not be coaxed.

Chorus.
	I beat my breast to a Persian dirge,
	the way a wailing woman from Cissia does it,
	and you could see the hands reaching out
	and raining down blows, thick and fast,
	from above, from high above, one side then the other,
	till the strokes rang on my battered, miserable head.

Electra.
	Oh, oh, cruel,
	mother who would dare anything, at that cruel funeral
	you dared to bury a king without his people,
	a husband without mourning,
	and no one wept.

Orestes.
	Nothing but dishonour, all of it. Oh.
	And will she not pay for dishonouring my father,
	with the help of the gods,
	with the help of my own hands?
	Let me only take her life, and then let me die.

Chorus.
	And he was mutilated, you should know this.
	The woman who buried him like that
	did it on purpose, to make his death
	a weight your life could not bear.
	Now you have heard your father's shame, his suffering.

Electra.
	You tell how my father died. And I was kept out of the way,
	dishonoured, counted as nothing.
	Shut up in a back room like a vicious dog,
	I let the tears run, which came more easily than laughter,
	and poured out my grief where no one could see.
	Hear that, and write it on your heart.

Chorus.
	Yes, let it bore through your ears
	to a mind that stands quiet and firm.
	That is how things are.
	What comes next, you must be eager to learn yourself.
	You must go into the fight with a will that does not bend.

Orestes.
	I am speaking to you, father. Stand with the people who are yours.

Electra.
	And I add my voice to his, in tears.

Chorus.
	And all of us together take up the cry.
	Hear us. Come up into the light.
	Stand with us against our enemies.

Orestes.
	Ares will meet Ares, Justice will meet Justice.

Electra.
	Oh gods, judge our plea justly, as justice asks.

Chorus.
	A shiver creeps over me as I hear these prayers.
	What is fated has been waiting a long time,
	but it can come to people who pray.

Chorus.
	Oh, the trouble bred in the blood,
	and the bloody stroke of Ruin
	that jars against the music.
	Oh, the griefs, unbearable, past all groaning.
	Oh, the pain that will not be stopped.

Chorus.
	The house has a cure to dress these wounds,
	not from others, not from outside,
	but from itself, from its own people,
	through raw, bloody strife.
	This is the hymn to the gods under the earth.

Chorus.
	Hear it, then, blessed powers below,
	hear this prayer, and gladly send
	your help to the children, for victory.

Orestes.
Father, you died a death no king should die. I ask you: give me the
mastery of your house.

Electra.
And I ask you for this, father: let me escape, once I have brought
great destruction down on Aegisthus.

Orestes.
Do that, and men will set the feasts for you that custom owes the
dead. If not, you will sit without honour while the others are fed
on the rich, savoury smoke of the burnt offerings the earth
receives.

Electra.
And I, when I marry, will pour offerings to you out of my whole
inheritance from my father's house, and before anything else I will
give this tomb of yours the place of honour.

Orestes.
Earth, send up my father to watch me fight.

Electra.
Persephone, grant us victory, and make it beautiful.

Orestes.
Remember the bath, father, where they robbed you of your life.

Electra.
And remember how they thought up a new kind of net for you.

Orestes.
You were caught, father, in fetters that no smith forged.

Electra.
And shamefully, in a robe they had plotted over.

Orestes.
Do taunts like these wake you, father?

Electra.
Are you lifting that head we love, and holding it upright?

Orestes.
Either send Justice to fight beside the people who are yours, or let
us get the same grip on them that they got on you, if you want to
win in your turn after losing.

Electra.
And hear this last cry of mine, father, as you look at these
nestlings crouched on your tomb. Pity your children, the female and
the male together, and do not let this seed of the house of Pelops
be wiped out. Because that way you are not dead, even though you
died. Children keep a man's name alive after death, like the corks
that carry a net and hold the flax cord up out of the deep. Hear us.
It is for you that we are grieving like this. Honour what we say and
you save yourself.

Chorus.
There is nothing to fault in that. You have drawn the plea out long
enough to pay this tomb the honour it never had, when no one mourned
it. For the rest, since your mind is set straight on the deed, act
now, and put your luck to the test.

Orestes.
It will be done. But it is not off the track to ask how she came to
send these offerings, and what she was thinking of, honouring too
late a wound that nothing can heal. A miserable kindness to send to
a dead man who cannot feel it. I cannot make it out. The gifts are
smaller than the crime. Pour out everything you own to pay for one
man's blood and the effort is wasted. That is how the saying goes.
If you know, tell me. I want to hear it.

Chorus.
I know, child. I was there. It was dreams, and terrors that walk at
night, that shook her, and made her send these offerings, godless
woman that she is.

Orestes.
And did you find out what the dream was, so that you can tell it
properly?

Chorus.
She dreamed she gave birth to a snake. That is her own account.

Orestes.
And where does the story end? What is the point of it?

Chorus.
She laid it down in swaddling clothes, as if it were a child.

Orestes.
What food did it want, the newborn thing with the fangs?

Chorus.
In the dream she offered it her own breast.

Orestes.
And her nipple was surely not left unwounded by that loathsome
thing?

Chorus.
No. It sucked out a clot of blood along with the milk.

Orestes.
This is not empty. This dream means a man.

Chorus.
Then she screamed in her sleep and woke in terror, and all through
the house lamps that had been blinded by the dark flared up to
comfort our mistress. And after that she sent these offerings of
mourning, hoping they would be a cure cut clean through her
troubles.

Orestes.
Then I pray to this earth and to my father's grave that this dream
comes true in me. I read it, and it fits at every joint. If the
snake came out of the same place I came from, and was wrapped in my
swaddling clothes, and opened its mouth around the breast that fed
me, and mixed a clot of blood into the sweet milk, and she screamed
with fear at the pain of it, then she must die by violence, because
she has nursed a monster, a thing of horror. I am the snake. I turn
snake, and I kill her, just as this dream says.

Chorus.
I take you as my reader of this omen. Let it be so. For the rest,
tell your friends their parts. Tell some what to do and others what
to leave alone.

Orestes.
The story is simple. My sister goes inside, and I tell her to keep
this agreement of ours hidden, so that the people who killed an
honoured man by a trick will be caught by a trick in their turn, and
die in the same net. That is what Apollo, lord Apollo, decreed, the
prophet who has never yet told a lie.

I will come to the outer gate looking like a stranger, with all the
gear of a traveller, and Pylades here comes with me, a guest and
friend of the house, sworn to it by the spear. We will both put on
the speech of Parnassus, copying the accent of the Phocian tongue.
And suppose none of the doorkeepers gives us a cheerful welcome,
because the house is in the grip of an evil god: then we will wait
there, so that anyone walking past the house will think it over and
say, "Why is Aegisthus shutting his door on a man who asks for
shelter, if he is at home and knows about it?"

But if I do get across the threshold of the outer gate, and find
that man sitting on my father's throne, or if he comes to meet me
face to face and lifts his eyes and drops them again, then be sure
of this: before he can say, "Where is the stranger from?", I will
have him dead, with my quick bronze through him. And the Fury of
murder, who has never gone short of slaughter, will drink her third
drink, blood unmixed with water.

Now you, Electra, keep careful watch on everything inside the house,
so that all this fits together tight. And you women, I advise you to
carry a tongue that says only what is right: silence where silence
is needed, and the words the moment calls for. As for the rest, I
call on the god who spoke to me to turn his eyes this way and set
the sword-fight straight for me.

(Orestes, Pylades and Electra go out.)
