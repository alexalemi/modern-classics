William Strunk Jr. (1869–1946) taught English at Cornell University for more than forty years. In 1918 he had a short handbook printed at his own expense for the students in his composition course, and revised it the following year. This edition reproduces the version Harcourt, Brace and Howe published in New York in 1920, which carries both of those earlier copyrights on its back. It is Strunk's book alone. The version most readers know is a later one, revised and enlarged in 1959 by E. B. White, who had been one of his students at Cornell in 1919.

The book is short because Strunk thought a short book was the only kind a student would use. He explains in the first chapter that it gives three rules for the comma instead of a score or more, and one for the semicolon, because those four cover nearly every sentence a student will write. The rest, he says, is better learned from an instructor looking at the student's own work. What follows is a chapter of rules for usage, a chapter of principles of composition, a few matters of form, an alphabetical list of words and expressions commonly misused, a list of words often misspelled, and a set of exercises. The rules are numbered so that a teacher could write a number in the margin of a paper instead of an explanation. Rule 13 is "Omit needless words."

Most of the teaching is done by examples, and many of them come in pairs: a faulty sentence and the same sentence put right. Strunk also quotes Stevenson, Hazlitt, Browning, Wordsworth and Coleridge, whose Ancient Mariner appears in the exercises in two versions, from 1798 and 1800, to show what punctuation can change.

A few things will read as dated. The writer is always "he." Some spellings have changed since 1920, such as "to-day." A few of the usages Strunk objects to are now standard. His reading list in the first chapter names style manuals of his day. These are part of the book's record of its time and are left as he wrote them.

This is a restored edition, and Strunk's words are unchanged. In the printed book the paired examples stand in two columns, the fault on the left. A page that reflows has no columns, so each pair is set here as two lines, the fault first and the correction beneath it. The entries of the fourth and fifth chapters, printed in bold, are set in italic. Each numbered rule has its own heading, so the rules can be found from the Contents. Nine misprints corrected by the Project Gutenberg transcribers stay corrected. The text was compared word by word with a scan of Cornell's copy of the 1920 printing, which is the copy that transcription was made from. The edition also adds modern typesetting, an ebook, and this introduction.
