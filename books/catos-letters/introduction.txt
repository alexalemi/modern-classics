In 1720 the South Sea Company collapsed. It had taken on much of the British national debt in exchange for a monopoly on trade to South America, its shares had been talked up to roughly ten times their value, and when the price broke, thousands of investors were ruined and several ministers and directors turned out to have been paid in stock. That autumn a weekly letter began appearing in the London Journal, signed Cato, demanding that the directors be punished and asking who had protected them. It ran, later in the British Journal as well, until July 1723: one hundred and thirty-eight letters.

Cato was two men, John Trenchard and Thomas Gordon. Trenchard was a wealthy country gentleman with a long record of writing against standing armies; Gordon was a Scot who did much of the actual writing and outlived him. Each letter is signed with a single initial, G for Gordon or T for Trenchard, and that is how they divided the credit. Trenchard died a few months after the series ended.

The name was the argument. Cato the Younger killed himself at Utica rather than accept a pardon from Julius Caesar, and for eighteenth-century readers he was the standing figure for a republic refusing one man's power. Plutarch's life of him is in this collection, in Roman Lives.

What began as a financial scandal turned into a general theory, and that is what the letters became famous for. Power always encroaches and must be watched by people who assume the worst. Freedom of speech is not one liberty among others but the thing that protects all the rest, so that where a man cannot call his tongue his own he can scarcely call anything else his own. Government is made by men, for men, and holds nothing except what was handed to it. Trenchard and Gordon were arguing about British politics, and the men who used them hardest were American: colonial printers reprinted them for fifty years, and a sixteen-year-old Benjamin Franklin lifted the argument of Letter 15 into his brother's Boston newspaper in 1722. His Autobiography and The Way to Wealth are in this collection.

These are newspaper columns, written weekly and on deadline, and they read like it. Each is addressed to the printer of the paper, opens with Sir, and closes with the initial. The tone is an angry columnist's: long sentences, heavy irony, Roman examples, and a flat refusal to be polite about anyone in office.

This volume is a selection of eighteen of the hundred and thirty-eight, chosen as the ones the revolutionary generation actually read and quoted: the letters on free speech and arbitrary power, the run from 59 to 63 on natural rights and how a free government might be built to last, two on property, the letter on colonies, the letter on the encroaching nature of power, and Cato's farewell.
