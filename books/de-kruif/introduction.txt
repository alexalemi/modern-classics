Paul de Kruif (1890–1971) was trained as a bacteriologist at the University of Michigan and worked for a time at the Rockefeller Institute in New York, before he left the laboratory to write about it. His first large piece of work outside it was the science in Sinclair Lewis's novel Arrowsmith, published in 1925, the story of a young doctor who wants to do research. Microbe Hunters came out the following year. It was a best seller, and it has been in print ever since.

It tells the story of the discovery of microbes and of the diseases they cause, one discoverer to a chapter. It begins with Antony Leeuwenhoek, a Dutch dry-goods merchant who ground his own lenses and in the 1670s saw what he called little animals swimming in a drop of rain water. It goes on through Spallanzani, who showed that they do not arise from nothing, and Pasteur and Koch, who proved that particular microbes cause particular diseases. Then come Roux and Behring and their antitoxin, Metchnikoff and the cells that eat microbes, and the men who traced Texas fever to ticks, sleeping sickness to the tsetse fly, malaria to the mosquito and yellow fever to another mosquito. It ends with Paul Ehrlich searching through hundreds of compounds for a "magic bullet," a chemical that would kill a microbe and spare the patient.

De Kruif writes in short bursts, with exclamation marks, and with a reporter's eye for a quarrel. He calls his scientists by their surnames like old friends, and he gives them dialogue and private thoughts that no record preserves. It reads like a set of adventure stories, and it is on the side of its heroes. The rivals they fought and the doubters they proved wrong come off badly, and some of those quarrels look different to historians now.

A few things belong to 1926. The one footnote reports a spiral-shaped microbe as the possible cause of yellow fever; that claim did not survive, and the cause turned out to be a virus. Some of the words he uses for peoples and nations were ordinary then and are not now. These are part of the book and are left as he wrote them.

This is a restored edition, and de Kruif's words are unchanged. The text is the Project Gutenberg transcription of the first edition of 1926, compared word by word with two scanned copies of that printing. It keeps his epigraph and dedication and all of the book's plates: eight full-page plates, seven of them portraits, and the small drawings of microbes and glassware in the text. Each plate now has a description, which also serves as its alt text. The index, whose page numbers mean nothing in a reflowable book, is omitted. The edition also adds modern typesetting, an ebook, and this introduction.
