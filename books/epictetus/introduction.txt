Epictetus taught in a rented room in Nicopolis, a provincial town on the west coast of Greece, sometime around the year 108. He had been born a slave in Phrygia, owned in Rome by a freedman of Nero's, and was lame in one leg. After he was freed he taught in Rome until Domitian threw the philosophers out of the city, and he never went back. His students were the sons of Roman gentlemen, sent to acquire a little philosophy before going into public life, and much of the book is him telling them that they are the ones in chains.

He wrote none of this down. A pupil named Arrian did, and says in the covering letter that he wrote out whatever he heard as nearly as he could in the man's own words, that the notes were for himself, and that he did not publish them. Four books survive of an original eight. What that shorthand preserves is a classroom rather than a treatise: he answers hecklers, loses patience, breaks off to ask a student whether he came here to be flattered or to be improved, and tells the room that a philosopher's school is a surgery and that they should be leaving it in pain rather than in pleasure. It is where the drills of the Enchiridion come from, with the arguments left in, and it is a good deal funnier than the handbook.

The translation this edition works from is George Long's, and it is worth saying why it needed redoing, because nothing in Long sounds old. The trouble is that he renders Greek technical terms with ordinary English words that look transparent and are not. Phantasia becomes appearance, prohairesis becomes will, dogma becomes opinion. A reader who meets the phrase the right use of appearances slides past it having understood nothing, and it is one of the two or three things the whole course is about. An appearance is whatever presents itself to your mind before you have agreed to it — the thought that this is a disaster, arriving unbidden — and the work is in the pause before you assent. The will, in Long's sense, is the faculty that does the agreeing, and it is the only thing Epictetus says nobody can take from you.

His subject is freedom, argued by a man who had been somebody's property. Say what you like about a consul, he says: a man who flatters to keep his position is more a slave than one who has been sold three times. His recommendation that a life can be ended when it is no longer worth living, the open door, recurs throughout and is given here as the argument he made, with no modern note attached. So is Book Three chapter one, a long attack on a young man for plucking his body hair. Marcus Aurelius read these lectures and says so; his notebook is in this collection as the Meditations, alongside Seneca's letters and the Enchiridion.
