Chapter 18: How to Fight Against Impressions

Every habit and every capacity is kept up and increased by the acts that correspond to it: the habit of walking by walking, the habit of running by running. If you want to be a good reader, read; if a writer, write. But when you have not read for thirty days running and have done something else instead, you will see the result. In the same way, lie down for ten days and then get up and try to take a long walk, and you will see how weak your legs have got.

So, in general, if you want to make anything a habit, do it; and if you do not want to make it a habit, do not do it — get into the way of doing something else instead.

It is the same with the states of the soul. When you have been angry, you must understand that not only has this evil happened to you, but that you have also strengthened the habit, and thrown fuel on the fire, so to speak. When you have been beaten by the desire to sleep with somebody, do not count it as a single defeat: count that you have also fed and increased your own lack of self-control. It is impossible for habits and capacities not to be produced by the corresponding acts where they did not exist before, or not to be increased and strengthened by them where they did.

This is certainly how diseases of the mind grow up, as the philosophers say. Once you have desired money, if reason is applied so as to bring you to see the evil, the desire is stopped and the governing part of the mind is restored to its original authority. But if you apply no cure, it does not go back to the same state; roused again by the corresponding impression, it takes fire into desire quicker than before. And when this happens over and over, the mind grows callous, and the disease of the mind fixes the love of money in place.

A man who has had a fever and been relieved of it is not in the state he was in before, unless he has been completely cured. Something of the kind happens with diseases of the soul as well. Certain traces and blisters are left behind, and unless a man rubs them out completely, the next time the lash falls on the same spot it will raise not blisters but open sores.

So if you do not want to be a bad-tempered man, do not feed the habit. Throw nothing on it that will make it grow. At first keep quiet, and count the days on which you have not been angry. I used to lose my temper every day; then every other day; then every third, then every fourth. And if you have gone thirty days without, sacrifice to God. For the habit is weakened at first, and then destroyed altogether.

"I was not vexed today, nor the day after, nor on any day since, for two or three months — but I was careful when provoking things happened." Be sure you are on a good road.

Today, when I saw a handsome woman, I did not say to myself, I wish I could have her, and happy is her husband — because a man who says that is saying, happy is her lover too. Nor do I picture the rest of it: the woman there, undressing, lying down beside me. I stroke my own head and say: well done, Epictetus, you have solved a fine little puzzle, much finer than the one they call the Master Argument. And if the woman is willing as well, and makes signs and sends messages, and if she even touches me and comes close, and I hold off and win — that would be a puzzle solved beyond the Liar and the Quiescent. A man may fairly be proud of a victory like that, and not of setting the Master Argument.

How is it to be done, then? Be willing at last to have your own approval. Be willing to look beautiful to God. Set your heart on being pure, in company with your own pure self and with God.

Then, when any such impression visits you, Plato says: turn to rites of purification, go as a suppliant to the temples of the gods who avert evil. And it is even enough to go into the company of noble and just men and compare yourself with them, whether you can find one living or dead.

Go to Socrates, and see him lying beside Alcibiades and making fun of the young man's beauty. Think what a victory he found in the end that he had won over himself — what an Olympic victory; what his place was in the list running back to Hercules — so that a man might justly greet him, by the gods: hail, extraordinary man; you have beaten not these poor boxers and wrestlers, nor men like them, the gladiators.

Put these things on the other side of the scale and you will beat the impression; you will not be dragged off by it. But first of all, do not be swept away by the speed of it. Say: impression, wait for me a little. Let me see who you are and what you are about. Let me put you to the test. And then do not let the impression lead you on and paint vivid pictures of what will follow, or it will carry you off wherever it likes. Bring in some other impression instead, a beautiful and noble one, and throw this low one out.

If you get used to training in this way, you will see what shoulders you have, what sinews, what strength. As it is, you have nothing but trifling words.

This is the true athlete — the man who trains himself against impressions like these. Stay, wretch; do not be carried off. The fight is great, the work divine: it is for kingship, for freedom, for happiness, for peace of mind. Remember God. Call on him as your helper and protector, as men at sea call on the Dioscuri in a storm.

And what greater storm is there than the one that comes from violent impressions that drive reason out? What is the storm itself but an impression? Take away the fear of death and bring on as much thunder and lightning as you please, and you will find what calm and clear weather there is in the governing part.

But if you are once beaten and say you will win next time, and then say the same thing again, be sure that you will end in such a wretched and feeble condition that you will not even notice afterwards that you are doing wrong; you will start making excuses for it. And then you will prove the truth of Hesiod's line:

	The man who puts things off wrestles with troubles all his life.
