Chapter 22: On the Cynic Life

(Part 1 of 2)

One of his pupils, who seemed inclined toward the Cynic life, asked Epictetus what sort of man a Cynic ought to be, and what the idea of the thing was.

We shall look into that at leisure, said Epictetus. But I have this much to say to you: a man who takes on so great a matter without God is hateful to God, and has no other purpose than to disgrace himself in public.

In any well-run household no man comes forward and says to himself, I ought to be the steward here. If he does, the master turns round, sees him giving orders insolently, drags him out and flogs him. So it is in this great city, the world; here too there is a master of the house who arranges everything.

"You are the sun. By going round you can make the year and the seasons, make the fruits grow and feed them, stir the winds and let them drop, and warm men's bodies properly. Go, travel round, and administer things from the greatest to the least. You are a calf: when a lion appears, do your proper business — run. If you do not, you will suffer for it. You are a bull: come forward and fight, for that is your business and it suits you and you can do it. You can lead the army against Troy: be Agamemnon. You can fight Hector in single combat: be Achilles."

But if Thersites had come forward and claimed the command, either he would not have got it, or if he had, he would have disgraced himself in front of a great many witnesses.

So think the matter over carefully yourself. It is not what it looks like to you. You say: "I wear a cloak now and I shall wear one then. I sleep hard now and I shall sleep hard then. I shall add a little bag and a staff, and go about begging and abusing the men I meet; and if I see a man plucking the hair off his body I shall rebuke him, or if he has had his hair dressed, or if he goes about in purple."

If you imagine the thing is like that, keep well away from it. Do not go near it; it is not for you at all. But if you imagine it as it really is, and do not think yourself unfit for it, consider what a great thing you are taking on.

In the first place, in the things that concern yourself you must be nothing like what you are now. You must blame neither God nor man. You must do away with desire altogether. You must move your avoidance to the things within the power of the will alone. You must feel no anger, no resentment, no envy, no pity. No girl must look handsome to you; you must not love a little reputation; you must not be pleased by a boy or a cake.

For you should understand that other men put walls around themselves, and houses, and darkness, when they do any such thing, and they have plenty of means of concealment. A man shuts the door and posts somebody outside the room: "If anyone comes, say he is out, he is busy." But the Cynic must use self-respect for all these — and if he does not, he will be indecent in his nakedness and under the open sky. This is his house, his door, the slave outside his bedroom, his darkness.

He must not want to hide anything he does; and if he does, he is finished. He has lost the character of a Cynic, of a man who lives under the open sky, of a free man. He has begun to fear something external; he has begun to need concealment, and he cannot have concealment when he wants it. Where would he hide, and how? And if this public teacher, this tutor of mankind, does happen to be caught, what will he be forced to suffer?

So when a man is afraid of these things, is it possible for him to be bold with his whole soul and take charge of other men? It is not. It is impossible.

First, then, you must make your governing part clean, and this way of life clean too. "Now the material I work on is my understanding, as wood is the carpenter's and hides the shoemaker's, and my business is the right use of impressions. The body is nothing to me; its parts are nothing to me. Death? Let it come when it likes, death of the whole or of a part. Go into exile, you say. And where to? Can any man throw me out of the world? He cannot. Wherever I go, there is the sun, there is the moon, there are the stars, dreams, omens, and conversation with the gods."

And then, if he is prepared like that, the true Cynic cannot be satisfied with it. He must know that he has been sent as a messenger from Zeus to men, about good things and bad, to show them that they have gone astray and are looking for the substance of good and evil where it is not, and never think of it where it is. He is a spy, as Diogenes was taken to Philip after the battle of Chaeronea as a spy. In fact a Cynic is a spy on what is good for men and what is bad, and his duty is to examine carefully and to come back and report truly — not to be struck with terror and point out as enemies men who are not enemies, nor to be thrown or confused by impressions in any other way.

So it is his duty to be able, if the occasion arises, to come forward on the tragic stage and say in a loud voice, as Socrates did:

Men, where are you rushing to? What are you doing, wretches? You are wandering up and down like blind people. You are on another road and have left the true one. You look for prosperity and happiness where they are not, and if somebody else shows you where they are, you do not believe him.

Why do you look for it outside? In the body? It is not there. If you doubt me, look at Myro, look at Ophellius. In possessions? It is not there. If you do not believe me, look at Croesus; look at the rich men of today, and what lamentation their lives are full of. In power? It is not there. If it were, men who have been consul twice and three times would have to be happy — and they are not.

Whom shall we believe about this? You, who see their affairs from outside and are dazzled by an appearance, or the men themselves? What do they say? Listen to them when they groan, when they grieve, when they think themselves more wretched and in greater danger precisely because of these consulships and this glory and splendour.

Is it in royal power? It is not; if it were, Nero would have been happy, and Sardanapalus. But not even Agamemnon was happy, and he was a better man than Sardanapalus and Nero. While the others are snoring, what is he doing?

	Much of his rooted hair he tore from his head.
	— Iliad, x 15

And what does he say himself?

	"I am at a loss," he says, "and
	I am shaken," and "my heart is leaping
	out of my breast."
	— Iliad x 91

Wretch, which of your affairs is going badly? Your possessions? No. Your body? No. You are rich in gold and bronze. What is the matter with you, then? That part of you, whatever it is, has been neglected by you and has gone rotten — the part we desire with, and avoid with, and move toward things and away from things with.

Neglected how? He does not know the nature of the good he was made by nature for, nor the nature of evil; nor what is his own and what belongs to another. And when something belonging to others goes badly he says, "Alas for me, the Greeks are in danger." Wretched is his governing part, and it alone is neglected and uncared for.

"The Greeks are going to be destroyed by the Trojans and die." And if the Trojans do not kill them, will they not die? "Yes — but not all at once." And what difference does that make? If death is an evil, it is equally an evil whether men die all together or one by one. Is anything else going to happen but the parting of soul and body? Nothing. And if the Greeks perish, is the door shut? Is it not in your power to die? It is.

Then why do you lament, and say, "You, a king, holding the sceptre of Zeus"? An unhappy king does not exist, any more than an unhappy god. What are you, then? A shepherd, in truth — because you weep as shepherds do when a wolf has carried off one of their sheep. And these men you govern are sheep.

And why did you come here? Was your desire in any danger? Your avoidance? Your pursuits? Your refusals? "No," he says, "but my brother's wife was carried off." Then was it not a great gain to be rid of an adulterous wife? "Are we to be despised by the Trojans, then?" What sort of people are the Trojans, wise or foolish? If they are wise, why are you fighting them? If they are fools, why do you care what they think?

Where is the good, then, since it is not in these things? Tell us, you who are lord and messenger and spy.

It is where you do not think it is, and do not choose to look for it. If you chose to look, you would have found it inside yourselves; and you would not be wandering off the road, or seeking what belongs to others as though it were your own.

Turn your thoughts inward. Notice the preconceptions you have. What sort of thing do you imagine the good to be? "Something that runs smoothly, something happy, something unblocked." Come — do you not naturally imagine it to be great? Do you not imagine it to be valuable? Do you not imagine it to be beyond harm?

In what material, then, ought you to look for what runs smoothly and is unblocked — in what serves, or in what is free? "In what is free." Then do you possess the body free, or in a condition of slavery? "We do not know." Do you not know that it is the slave of fever, of gout, of eye disease, of dysentery, of a tyrant, of fire, of iron, of everything stronger than itself? "Yes, it is a slave."

Then how is it possible for anything belonging to the body to be unblocked? And how is a thing great or valuable that is by nature dead — earth, mud?

Well then, do you possess nothing that is free? "Perhaps nothing." And who is able to force you to assent to what appears false? "No man." And who can force you not to assent to what appears true? "No man." Then by this you see that there is something in you naturally free.

And to desire, or to be averse, or to move toward an object or away from it, or to prepare yourself, or to plan to do something — which of you can do this without having received an impression of what is profitable or of what is a duty? "No man." So in these things too you have something unblocked and free.

Wretched men, work at this. Take care of this. Look for the good here.

"And how is it possible for a man who has nothing, who is naked, homeless, without a hearth, dirty, with no slave and no city, to lead a life that runs smoothly?"

Look — God has sent you a man to show you that it is possible. Look at me: no city, no house, no possessions, no slave. I sleep on the ground. I have no wife, no children, no official residence — only earth and sky and one poor cloak. And what do I lack? Am I not free from sorrow? Am I not free from fear? Am I not free?

When did any of you ever see me fail to get what I desired, or fall into what I would avoid? Did I ever blame God or man? Did I ever accuse anybody? Did any of you ever see me with a sorrowful face? And how do I meet the men you are afraid of and admire? Do I not treat them like slaves? Who, seeing me, does not think he is looking at his king and his master?

This is the language of the Cynics, this is their character, this is their purpose. You say: "No — what marks them is the little bag and the staff and the big jaws; bolting everything you give them, or hoarding it; abusing everybody they meet at the wrong moment; and showing off a fine shoulder."

Do you see how you are going about taking on so great a business? First take a mirror. Look at your shoulders. Look at your loins and your thighs. You are going, my man, to be enrolled as a competitor in the Olympic games — no feeble, wretched contest. At the Olympic games a man is not allowed to be beaten and simply take himself off: first he must be disgraced in the sight of the whole world, not only of Athenians or Spartans or Nicopolitans; and next he must be whipped as well, if he entered the contests rashly; and before being whipped he must endure thirst and heat and swallow a great deal of dust.

Think it over more carefully. Know yourself. Consult the divinity. Attempt nothing without God. If he does advise you to do it, be sure that he intends you either to become great or to take a great many blows.

For this very entertaining quality goes along with being a Cynic: he must be flogged like a donkey, and while he is being flogged he must love the men who flog him, as though he were the father of them all and the brother of them all.

You say: "No — if a man flogs you, stand in the public square and call out, Caesar, what am I suffering under your protection in this time of peace? Let us take the offender before the proconsul."

But what is Caesar to a Cynic, or a proconsul, or anybody except the one who sent him down here and whom he serves — Zeus? Does he call on anybody but Zeus? Is he not convinced that whatever he suffers, it is Zeus who is training him?

Hercules, when Eurystheus was training him, did not think himself wretched; he set about carrying out everything he was given without hesitation. And is the man who is being trained and exercised by Zeus going to cry out and be annoyed — a man fit to carry the staff of Diogenes?

Listen to what Diogenes says to the passers-by when he is in a fever. "Miserable wretches, will you not stop? You go all the way to Olympia to see athletes destroyed or fighting, and you will not choose to watch a fight between a fever and a man?"

Would such a man accuse the God who sent him down, as though God were treating him unworthily — a man who gloried in his circumstances and claimed to be an example to the people going past? What would he accuse him of? Of keeping his behaviour decent? Of showing his virtue more plainly?

And what does he say about poverty, about death, about pain? How did he compare his own happiness with that of the great king of Persia? Or rather, he thought there was no comparison to be made. Where there are disturbances and griefs and fears and unsatisfied desires and avoidances of what cannot be avoided, and envies and jealousies — how is there any road to happiness there? And where the principles are rotten, all these things must necessarily be.
