Chapter 9: What Follows from Our Kinship with God

If what the philosophers say about the kinship of God and man is true, what is left for men to do but what Socrates did — never, when asked what country he belonged to, answer "Athenian" or "Corinthian", but "a citizen of the world"?

Why do you say you are an Athenian, instead of saying you belong only to the little corner your poor body happened to be dropped in at birth? Plainly you name yourself Athenian or Corinthian from the wider thing that has the greater authority — the thing that takes in not only that corner and your whole household, but the entire country your ancestors came down to you from.

So a man who has watched the administration of the world intelligently, and has learned that the greatest and highest and most inclusive community is the one made up of men and God; that the seeds have come down from God not only to my father and grandfather but to everything that is begotten and born on the earth, and above all to rational beings, since these alone are formed by nature to share with God, being joined to Him by reason — why should such a man not call himself a citizen of the world? Why not a son of God? And why should he be afraid of anything that happens among men? If kinship with Caesar, or with any other of the great men at Rome, is enough to let us live in safety, above contempt and with nothing to fear at all — then to have God as your maker and father and guardian, will that not release us from griefs and fears?

"But where am I to get bread, when I have nothing?"

And how do slaves manage when they run away — what do they rely on when they leave their masters? On their estates? Their own slaves? Their silver plate? They rely on nothing but themselves, and food does not fail them. And is one of us, a philosopher, to travel abroad and depend on other people and lean on them, and take no care of himself? Is he to be worse than the animals, and more of a coward — every one of which is self-sufficient, and never fails to find its proper food, or a way of living that suits it and is in line with nature?

For my part, I think the old man sitting here ought not to be occupied with keeping you from low thoughts and low, ignoble talk about yourselves. He ought to be making sure there are no young men among us of this other mind: who, having recognised their kinship with God, and that we are shackled by these bonds — the body, I mean, and its possessions, and whatever else we need on account of them for the ordinary running and commerce of life — resolve to throw the whole lot off as an unbearable, painful burden, and go off to their relations.

This is the work your teacher and instructor ought to be at, if he really were what he ought to be. You should come to him and say: "Epictetus, we can no longer bear being tied to this poor body — feeding it, giving it drink and rest, washing it, and for its sake making up to this man and that. Are these things not indifferent, and nothing to us? Is death not no evil? Are we not in a sense relations of God, and did we not come from Him? Let us go back where we came from. Let us be released at last from these bonds that hold us down and weigh on us. Here there are robbers and thieves and law courts, and men called tyrants who think they have some power over us through the body and its belongings. Let us show them they have no power over anybody."

And I, for my part, would answer: "Friends, wait for God. When He gives the signal and releases you from this service, then go to Him. For the present, put up with living in the place where He has stationed you. The time you have to live here is short, and easy enough to bear for men in your frame of mind. What tyrant, what thief, what law court is frightening to men who have come to count the body and the body's possessions as worth nothing? Wait, then. Do not leave without a reason."

Something like that is what a teacher ought to say to well-born young men. But what actually happens? The teacher is a lifeless body and so are you. You eat your fill today, and then sit down and moan about tomorrow and where the next meal is coming from. Wretch, if you have it, you will have it; and if you do not, you will leave life. The door is open. Why grieve? What room is left for tears? Where is the occasion for flattery? Why should one man envy another? Why should anyone admire the rich or the powerful, even if they are very strong and violent-tempered? What are they going to do to us? What they can do, we shall not care about; and what we do care about, they cannot touch.

How did Socrates behave over such matters? How else than as a man who was convinced he was a relation of the gods? "If you say to me now," Socrates told his judges, "we will acquit you on condition that you stop arguing in the way you have argued up to now, and stop troubling our young men and our old ones — then I shall answer that you are making yourselves ridiculous. You think that if one of our commanders posted me somewhere it would be my duty to hold that post and keep it, and to resolve to die a thousand times rather than desert it; but that if God has put us in a certain place and a certain way of life, we ought to desert that." Socrates talks like a man who really is a relation of the gods.

But we think of ourselves as stomachs and guts and private parts. We are afraid, we want things, we flatter the people who can help us with these matters — and are afraid of them too.

A man once asked me to write to Rome on his behalf. Most people thought he had been unlucky: he had been a man of rank and rich, and had been stripped of everything, and was living here. I wrote for him in a humble tone. He read the letter, handed it back to me and said: "I wanted your help, not your pity. Nothing bad has happened to me."

In the same way Musonius Rufus used to test me by saying: this and that will be done to you by your master. And when I answered that these were the sort of things that happen in the ordinary course of human affairs — then why, he said, should I ask him for anything when I can get it from you? For in fact what a man has from himself it is pointless and foolish to take from someone else. Am I, who can get greatness of spirit and a generous temper from myself, to take land and money or public office from you? I hope not. I will not be so ignorant of what I already own.

But when a man is cowardly and small, what else is there to do for him but write letters about him as you would about a corpse? Please make us a present of the body of so-and-so, and a pint of thin blood. For such a person really is a carcass and a pint of blood, and nothing more. If he were anything more, he would know that no man is made miserable by another.
