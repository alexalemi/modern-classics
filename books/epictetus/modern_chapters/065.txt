Chapter 8: How to Train Yourself Against Impressions

As we train ourselves against trick questions, so we ought to train ourselves daily against impressions; because impressions put questions to us too.

So-and-so's son has died. Answer: that is not within the power of the will; it is not an evil. A father has disinherited his son. What do you think of it? It is outside the power of the will; not an evil. Caesar has condemned somebody. Outside the power of the will; not an evil. The man is crushed by it. Being crushed depends on the will: that is an evil. He has borne the sentence bravely. That is within the power of the will: that is a good.

If we train ourselves this way we shall make progress, because we shall never assent to anything of which we have no impression we can properly take in.

Your son is dead. What has happened? Your son is dead. Nothing more? Nothing. Your ship is lost. What has happened? Your ship is lost. A man has been taken off to prison. What has happened? He has been taken off to prison. That he has come off badly in it — every man adds that out of his own opinion.

"But Zeus does not do right in these matters." Why? Because he has made you able to endure? Because he has made you great in spirit? Because he has taken from what happens to you the power of being an evil? Because it is in your power to be happy while you suffer what you suffer? Because he has opened the door for you when things do not please you? Man, go out, and stop complaining.

If you would like to know how the Romans feel about philosophers, listen to this. Italicus, who had the highest reputation among them, was once annoyed with his own friends while I was there, and said, as though he were suffering something unbearable: "I cannot stand it — you are killing me. You will turn me into that man there." And he pointed at me.
