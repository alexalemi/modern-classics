Chapter 11: On Cleanliness

Some people raise the question whether the social feeling is contained in man's nature. And yet I think these same people would have no doubt that a love of cleanliness certainly is; and that if man is distinguished from the other animals by anything, he is distinguished by this.

When we see some other animal cleaning itself, we usually remark on it with surprise and add that the animal is behaving like a man. And on the other hand, if a man blames an animal for being dirty, we say at once, as though making an excuse for it, that of course the animal is not a human creature. So we suppose there is something superior in man, and that we first receive it from the gods.

Since the gods are by nature pure and free of corruption, then so far as men come near them by reason, so far do they hold to cleanliness and to the habit of it. But since it is impossible for a man's nature to be altogether pure, being mixed out of such materials, reason is applied as far as it can be, and reason tries to make human nature love cleanliness.

The first and highest cleanliness, then, is the cleanliness in the soul; and we say the same about dirt. Now you could not detect dirt in the soul as you could on the body; but in the soul, what else could you find but what makes her filthy with regard to the acts that are her own? And the acts of the soul are movement toward an object and away from it, desire, avoidance, preparation, purpose, assent.

So what is it in these acts that makes the soul filthy and unclean? Nothing but her own bad judgments. Therefore the dirt of the soul is her bad opinions, and the cleaning of the soul is the planting of proper opinions in it; and the clean soul is the one that has proper opinions, since the soul alone in her own acts is free of disturbance and pollution.

Now we ought to work at something like this in the body too, so far as we can. It was impossible for the nose not to run, when man has such a mixture in his body; and so nature made hands, and made the nostrils themselves as channels for carrying the fluids off. So if a man sniffs them back up, I say he is not doing a man's act.

It was impossible for a man's feet not to get muddy and dirty when he goes through dirty places; and so nature made water, and hands. It was impossible for no dirt to be left in the teeth after eating; and so, she says, clean the teeth. Why? So that you may be a man and not a wild beast or a hog.

It was impossible that from sweat and the pressure of clothes some dirt should not be left on the body needing to be cleaned off; and so water, oil, hands, towels, scrapers, soda and sometimes every other kind of means are necessary for cleaning it.

You do not behave so. But the smith will take the rust off the iron and will have tools ready for the purpose; and you wash the dish yourself when you are going to eat, unless you are altogether filthy and dirty. And will you not wash your body and make it clean?

"Why?" he answers.

I will tell you again. In the first place, so that you may do a man's acts; and then, so that you may not be disagreeable to the people you spend your time with.

You are doing something of the kind even in this, and you do not notice it: you think you deserve to stink. Let it be so — deserve to stink. But do you think that the men who sit beside you, who recline at table with you, who kiss you, deserve it too? Either go off into a desert, where you deserve to go, or live by yourself and smell yourself; because it is only just that you should have the enjoyment of your own filth to yourself. But when you are in a city, to behave so thoughtlessly and foolishly — what sort of character do you think that belongs to?

If nature had entrusted a horse to you, would you have overlooked him and neglected him? Consider now that you have been entrusted with your own body as with a horse. Wash it, wipe it, take care that nobody turns away from it and nobody gets out of its way. And who does not get out of the way of a dirty man, a stinking man, a man whose skin is foul — more than he gets out of the way of a man smeared with dung? That smell is from outside and put on him; the other comes from want of care, from inside, and from a body in a manner rotting.

"But Socrates washed himself seldom."

Yes — but his body was clean and handsome, and so agreeable and pleasant that the most beautiful and the most noble young men loved him and wanted to sit beside him rather than beside men with the handsomest bodies. He could have gone without the bath and without washing altogether if he had chosen; and even so his rare use of water had its effect. If you will not wash in warm water, wash in cold.

But Aristophanes says:

	The pale ones, the unshod — those are the men I mean.
	— The Clouds, line 102.

Aristophanes also says of Socrates that he walked on air and stole clothes from the wrestling school. But everybody who has written about Socrates gives exactly the contrary evidence in his favour: they say he was pleasant not only to hear but to look at. And they write the same about Diogenes.

For we ought not to put the crowd off philosophy even by the look of the body. As in everything else, a philosopher should show himself cheerful and untroubled; and so he should in the things that concern the body. See, gentlemen, that I have nothing and want nothing; see how I am without a house, without a city, an exile if it happens so, and without a hearth — and I live with less trouble and more happily than all the well-born and the rich. And look at my poor body too, and see that it is not damaged by my hard way of living.

But if a man says this to me who has the look and the face of a condemned criminal — what god will persuade me to come near philosophy, if it makes men like that? Far from it. I would not choose to, even if it were going to make me a wise man.

For my part I would rather a young man taking his first steps toward philosophy came to me with his hair carefully trimmed than dirty and rough; because there is a certain notion of beauty in him, and a desire for what is becoming, and where he thinks it is, there he tries to have it. It is only necessary to show him what it is, and to say: young man, you are looking for beauty, and you do well. Then you should know that it grows in that part of you where you have the rational faculty. Look for it where you have your movements toward things and away from them, where you have your desires and your avoidances; that is what you have in you of a superior kind. The poor body is by nature only earth. Why do you labour over it for nothing? If you learn nothing else, in time you will learn that the body is nothing.

But if a man comes to me smeared with filth, dirty, with a moustache down to his knees, what can I say to him? By what likeness am I to lead him on? What has he busied himself with that resembles beauty, so that I could change him and say: beauty is not in this, but in that? Would you have me tell him that beauty does not consist in being smeared with dung but lies in the rational part? Has he any desire for beauty? Has he any picture of it in his mind? Go and talk to a hog and tell him not to roll in the mud.

This is why the words of Xenocrates touched Polemo — because he was a lover of beauty. He came into the room with certain sparks in him of the love of beauty, but he was looking for it in the wrong place.

Nature has not even made the animals dirty that live with man. Does a horse ever wallow in mud, or a well-bred dog? The hog does, and the filthy geese, and worms and spiders, which are kept furthest from human company. Then will you, being a man, choose not to be like the animals that live with men, but rather like a worm or a spider?

Will you not wash yourself somewhere, sometime, in whatever way you choose? Will you not wash the dirt off your body? Will you not come clean, so that the people who keep company with you may take pleasure in being with you? And do you come with us into the temples in this state, where it is not permitted to spit or blow the nose — being a heap of spit and snot yourself?

What then? Does anybody require you to adorn yourself? Far from it — except to adorn what we really are by nature: the rational faculty, the judgments, the actions. As for the body, only so far as cleanliness goes, only so far as not to give offence.

But if you are told that you ought not to wear clothes dyed with purple, off you go and smear your cloak with dung, or tear it.

"But how am I to have a neat cloak?"

Man, you have water. Wash it.

Here is a young man worth loving; here is an old man worth loving and being loved by in return; a fit person to be entrusted with a son's education, to whom daughters and young men shall come as the occasion arises — so that the teacher may deliver his lessons to them on a dunghill.

Let it not be so. Every deviation comes from something that is in man's nature; but this deviation comes near to being something that is not in man's nature at all.
