Chapter 4: On Making Progress

The man who is making progress has learned from the philosophers that desire means desiring good things and avoidance means avoiding bad ones. He has learned, too, that a man gets no happiness and no peace of mind except by never failing to get what he desires and never falling into what he wants to avoid. So he strips desire away from himself altogether and puts it off for the time being, and he directs avoidance only at things that depend on his will. Because if he tries to avoid anything outside his will, he knows he will sooner or later run into the very thing he wanted to dodge, and be miserable.

Now if virtue promises good fortune and peace of mind and happiness, then progress toward virtue is certainly progress toward each of those as well. Whatever the finished thing brings us to, progress is an approach to that point.

How is it, then, that we agree virtue is this, and yet go looking for progress somewhere else, and put that on display? What does virtue produce? Peace of mind. So who is making progress? The man who has read a great many books of Chrysippus? Does virtue consist in having understood Chrysippus? Because if it does, then progress is plainly nothing but knowing a great deal of Chrysippus. Yet here we are, admitting that virtue produces one thing while declaring that getting near it is something else entirely.

"So-and-so can already read Chrysippus on his own," somebody says.

Excellent, sir, you are making great progress. Progress of what kind? Why are you mocking the man? Why are you pulling him away from noticing his own troubles? Will you not show him what virtue actually produces, so that he learns where to look for improvement? Look for it where your work is, you wretch. And where is your work? In desire and avoidance — so that you are not disappointed of what you want and do not fall into what you would rather not meet. In pursuit and refusal — so that you make no mistake. In assent and the withholding of assent — that is, in saying yes to an impression or holding back — so that you are not taken in. These come first and they matter most. If you are still trembling and moaning as you try not to fall into what you avoid, tell me: where exactly is the progress?

So show me your progress in these things. If I were talking to an athlete, I would say: show me your shoulders.

"Here are my weights."

You and your weights can look to that. I want to see what the weights have done.

"Take my treatise on impulse, and see how thoroughly I have worked through it."

Slave, that is not what I am asking. I am asking how you exercise pursuit and refusal, desire and avoidance; how you plan and intend and prepare yourself; whether all this is in line with nature or not. If it is, give me the evidence and I will say you are making progress. If it is not — off with you, and not only expound your books but write some of your own. And what will you have gained? Do you not know the whole book costs five denarii? Is the man who expounds it worth more than five denarii, then? So never look for the thing itself in one place and progress toward it in another.

Where is progress, then? If one of you turns away from externals and toward his own will — to work on it and improve it by hard labour, until it is in line with nature, lifted up, free, unrestricted, unblocked, trustworthy and self-respecting; if he has learned that a man who desires or avoids things not in his power can be neither trustworthy nor free, but must inevitably shift as they shift and be knocked about by them as in a storm, and must inevitably make himself subject to anyone with the power to give him or withhold from him what he wants; and if, finally, when he gets up in the morning he watches for these rules and keeps them, bathes like a man who can be trusted, eats like a man with self-respect — if in whatever comes up he works at his governing principles the way a runner works at running and a voice trainer at the voice — that is the man who is truly making progress, and that is the man who has not travelled here for nothing.

But if he has strained every nerve at reading books, and works at nothing else, and travelled here for that, then I tell him to go home again at once and not neglect his business there — because the thing he came for is nothing. The other thing is something: to study how a man may get the moaning and groaning out of his life, and the "poor me, wretch that I am"; how to be rid of misfortune and disappointment; to learn what death is, and exile, and prison, and hemlock, so that he can say when he is in chains, "Dear Crito, if it is the will of the gods that it be so, let it be so" — and not, "Wretched old man that I am, is this what I kept my grey hairs for?"

Who talks like that? Do you suppose I mean some nobody, some man of no standing? Does Priam not say it? Does Oedipus not say it? Every king in the plays says it. For what is tragedy but the sufferings of men who set store by externals, put into that kind of verse? If a man has to be taught by a made-up story that nothing outside his will has anything to do with him, then for my part I am glad of the story, if it lets me live happily and undisturbed. What you want is for you to decide.

So what does Chrysippus give us? The answer is: knowing that these things are not lies — the things that happiness comes from and peace of mind grows out of. Take my books and you will learn how true, how much in line with nature, are the things that leave me undisturbed.

What great good fortune! What a great benefactor, to show us the road! Men have raised temples and altars everywhere to Triptolemus, because he gave us cultivated food. But to the man who found the truth and brought it to light and passed it on to everybody — not the truth about how to stay alive, but about how to live well — which of you has built an altar for that, or a temple, or dedicated a statue, or worships God on account of it? We sacrifice to the gods because they gave us the vine and the wheat. And because they brought forth in the human mind that fruit by which they meant to show us the truth about happiness — for that we will not thank God?
