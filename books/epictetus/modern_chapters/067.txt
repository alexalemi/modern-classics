Chapter 10: How to Bear Being Ill

When the need for each judgment arises, we ought to have it ready: at breakfast, the judgments that have to do with breakfast; in the bath, those that concern the bath; in bed, those that concern bed.

	Let sleep not fall upon your weary eyes
	before you have gone over the day's acts:
	what was done wrong, what done, what left undone.
	Examine all of it from first to last;
	then blame what was amiss, and take joy in what was right.

And we ought to keep these lines in a way that lets us use them, not in a way that lets us recite them aloud, as men cry out "Hail, Apollo".

Again, in a fever we should have ready the judgments that concern fever; and we ought not, the moment the fever starts, to let go of everything and forget. A man with a fever may say: "If I do any more philosophy, let me be hanged. Wherever I go I have to look after this poor body, so that a fever may not come."

But what is doing philosophy? Is it not preparing yourself against what may happen? Do you not see that you are saying something of this kind: "If I go on preparing myself to bear what happens with patience, let me be hanged"? It is exactly as if a man gave up the all-in fight after taking a few blows. In that fight it is in our power to stop and take no more blows. But in this business, if we give up philosophy, what do we gain?

So what should a man say when something painful happens? This is what I trained for. This is what I disciplined myself for.

God says to you: give me proof that you have trained properly as an athlete, that you have eaten what you should have, that you have exercised, that you have obeyed your trainer. And then do you go weak when the moment for action comes?

Now is the time for the fever. Bear it well. Now is the time for thirst. Bear it well. Now is the time for hunger. Bear it well. Is it not in your power? Who is going to stop you? The doctor will stop you drinking, but he cannot stop you bearing thirst well; and he will stop you eating, but he cannot stop you bearing hunger well.

"But I cannot get on with my philosophical studies."

And what do you pursue them for? Slave, is it not so that you may be happy, so that you may be steady, so that you may be in a state that is in line with nature and live that way? What stops you, when you have a fever, from having your governing part in line with nature? Here is the proof of the thing; here is the test of the philosopher. This too is a part of life, like walking, like sailing, like travelling overland — and so is fever.

Do you read while you are walking? No. Nor do you while you have a fever. But if you walk well, you have everything that belongs to a man walking. And if you bear a fever well, you have everything that belongs to a man in a fever.

What is it to bear a fever well? Not to blame God or man; not to be crushed by what is happening; to expect death well and nobly; to do what has to be done. Not to be frightened by what the doctor says when he comes in — and not to be overjoyed if he says you are doing well. What good has he told you? And when you were healthy, what good was that to you? And even if he says you are in a bad way, do not lose heart.

For what is being ill? Is it that you are near the parting of soul and body? What harm is there in that? If you are not near it now, will you not be near it later? Is the world going to be turned upside down when you die?

Why do you flatter the doctor, then? Why do you say, "If you please, master, I shall get well"? Why do you give him the chance to raise his eyebrows at you? Do you not value a doctor as you value a shoemaker measuring your foot, or a carpenter building your house? Treat the doctor as concerned with a body that is not yours, and is by nature dead already.

A man with a fever has the chance to do this; and if he does it, he has what belongs to him. It is not a philosopher's business to look after externals — not his wine, nor his oil, nor his poor body — but his own governing power. And how must he treat externals? So far as not to be careless about them.

Where is there reason for fear, then? Where is there any longer reason for anger, or for fear about what belongs to others, about things of no value?

We ought to have these two principles ready. First: that nothing is good or bad except the will. Second: that we ought not to lead events but to follow them. "My brother ought not to have treated me like that." No — but he will answer for that; and however he behaves, I will conduct myself toward him as I ought. That is my own business; the other is somebody else's. Nobody can stop me in mine; the other thing can be blocked.
