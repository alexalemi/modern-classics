Chapter 4: To Those Who Want a Quiet Life

Remember that it is not only the desire for power and riches that makes us mean and subject to others, but the desire for peace and leisure and travel and learning as well. To put it plainly, whatever the external thing may be, the value we set on it puts us in subjection to somebody.

So what difference is there between wanting to be a senator and not wanting to be one? What difference between wanting power and being content with private life? What difference between saying "I am wretched, I have nothing to do, I am tied to my books like a corpse" and saying "I am wretched, I have no leisure for reading"? Salutations and power are external and outside the will — and so is a book.

What do you choose to read for? Tell me. If you are aiming only at being entertained or learning something, you are a silly fellow who cannot bear hard work. But if you refer reading to its proper end, what else is that end but a life that runs smoothly and happily?

And if reading does not secure you a happy and untroubled life, what use is it? "But it does secure it," the man says, "and that is why I am annoyed at being deprived of it."

And what is this untroubled and happy life, that any man can obstruct — I do not say Caesar or Caesar's friend, but a crow, a piper, a fever, and thirty thousand other things? A happy and untroubled life contains nothing so certain as continuity and freedom from obstruction.

Now I am called to do something. I will go, then, with the purpose of observing the rules I must keep: acting with self-respect and steadiness, without desire and without avoidance where externals are concerned; and then, of attending to men, to what they say and how they are moved — and this not with any bad disposition, and not so as to have something to blame or laugh at, but turning to myself and asking whether I commit the same faults. And how am I to stop committing them? I used to do wrong once, and now I do not: thanks be to God.

Come: when you have done these things and attended to them, have you done a worse act than when you have read a thousand lines or written as many? When you eat, are you grieved because you are not reading? Are you not satisfied to eat according to what you learned by reading — and the same with bathing and with exercise?

Why do you not behave consistently in everything, then, both when you come before Caesar and when you come before anybody? If you keep yourself undisturbed, unalarmed and steady; if you look at what is being done and what happens rather than being looked at yourself; if you do not envy the men preferred before you; if the circumstances around you do not strike you with fear or admiration — what do you lack? Books? How, or what for? Is reading not a preparation for life? And is life itself not made up of certain other things than this?

This is as if an athlete wept on entering the stadium because he is not exercising outside it. This was what you used to do your exercises for; this was what the weights were for, and the dust, and the young men as opponents — and now, when it is time for action, do you go looking for those?

It is as if, in the matter of assent, when impressions present themselves, some of which can be grasped and some of which cannot, we should refuse to distinguish them and choose instead to read what has been written about grasping.

What is the reason for this? The reason is that we never read for this purpose and never wrote for this purpose — so that in our actions we might use the impressions before us in a way that is in line with nature. We stop short at learning what is said, and at being able to expound it to somebody else, and at resolving a syllogism, and handling a hypothetical one. And so it is exactly where our study lies that we find the obstruction.

Would you have, by all means, the things not in your power? Then be prevented, be blocked, fail of your purpose.

But if we read what is written about action, not to see what is said about action but so as to act well; if we read what is said about desire and avoidance so as neither to fail in our desires nor fall into what we try to avoid; if we read what is said about duty so that, remembering how things stand to one another, we may do nothing unreasonably and nothing contrary to those relations — then we should not be annoyed at being obstructed in our reading. We should be satisfied with doing the acts that fit; and we should be reckoning up, not what we have been used to reckon — "Today I read so many lines, I wrote so many" — but this: today I employed my impulse as the philosophers teach; I did not employ desire; I used avoidance only with respect to things within the power of my will; I was not afraid of such and such a man; I was not won over by another's entreaties; I exercised my patience, my abstinence, my cooperation with others. And so we should thank God for what we ought to thank him for.

But as it is, we do not know that in another way we too are like the crowd. One man is afraid that he will not get power; you are afraid that you will. Do not do it, my man. As you laugh at the man who is afraid he will not get power, laugh at yourself as well. It makes no difference whether you are thirsty like a man with a fever or dread water like a man who is mad.

Or how will you still be able to say, as Socrates did, "If it so pleases God, so let it be"? Do you suppose that if Socrates had been eager to spend his leisure in the Lyceum or the Academy and talk with the young men every day, he would have served so readily on so many campaigns? Would he not have lamented and groaned: "Wretch that I am, I have to be miserable here when I might be sunning myself in the Lyceum"?

Why — was that your business, sunning yourself? Is your business not to be happy, unblocked, unobstructed? And could he still have been Socrates if he had lamented like that? How would he still have been able to write hymns in his prison?

In short, remember this: whatever you prize that lies beyond your will, to that extent you have destroyed your will. And these things lie outside the will: not only power but a private station; not only business but leisure.

"Must I live in this tumult, then?"

Why do you call it tumult?

"I mean among a great many men."

Well, and what is the hardship? Suppose you are at Olympia; imagine it a great public assembly, where one man is shouting one thing and another doing another and a third shoving somebody. There is a crowd at the baths. And which of us is not pleased with that assembly, and leaves it unwillingly?

Do not be hard to please, and do not be fussy about what happens. "Vinegar is disagreeable, it is sharp; honey is disagreeable, it upsets me; I do not like vegetables." So too: "I do not like leisure, it is a desert; I do not like a crowd, it is confusion."

If circumstances make it necessary for you to live alone or with a few people, call it quiet and use the thing as you ought: talk with yourself, work on your impressions, work up your preconceptions. If you fall into a crowd, call it games, an assembly, a festival; try to enjoy the festival along with everybody else.

For what is a pleasanter sight to a man who loves mankind than a great many men? We watch herds of horses and oxen with pleasure; we are delighted when we see many ships. Who is pained at seeing many men?

"But they deafen me with their shouting."

Then your hearing is obstructed. What is that to you? Is the power of using impressions obstructed? And who prevents you from using inclination and disinclination, and impulse toward and away from a thing, according to nature? What tumult can do that?

Only keep the general rules in mind: what is mine? What is not mine? What is permitted me? What does God want me to do now, and what does he not want? A little while ago he wanted you to be at leisure, to talk with yourself, to write about these things, to read, to listen, to prepare yourself; and you had enough time for it. Now he says to you: come to the contest; show us what you have learned, how you have practised the athlete's art. How long will you exercise alone? Now is your chance to learn whether you are an athlete worth the victory or one of those who go round the world getting beaten.

Why are you annoyed, then? No contest is without confusion. There must be many men exercising for it, many shouting at the men exercising, many trainers, many spectators.

"But my wish is to live quietly."

Then lament and groan, as you deserve to. What greater punishment is there for the untaught man, and for the man who disobeys the divine commands, than to grieve, to lament, to envy — in a word, to be disappointed and unhappy?

Would you not release yourself from these things?

"And how am I to release myself?"

Have you not often heard that you must take desire away entirely, and apply avoidance only to what is within your power; that you must give up everything — body, property, fame, books, tumult, power, private station? Because whichever way you turn, you are a slave, you are subject, you are blocked, you are compelled, you are wholly in other men's power.

Keep the words of Cleanthes ready instead:

	Lead me, Zeus, and you too, Destiny,
	wherever you have fixed my post.

Is it your will that I go to Rome? I will go to Rome. To Gyara? I will go to Gyara. To Athens? To Athens. To prison? To prison.

If you once say, "When can a man get to Athens?" you are finished. It necessarily follows that this desire, if it is not fulfilled, must make you unhappy; and if it is fulfilled, must make you vain, since you are lifted up by things you ought not to be lifted up by; and on the other side, if you are obstructed, it must make you wretched, because you fall into what you did not want to fall into.

So give all these things up. "Athens is a good place." But happiness is a much better one — and being free of the passions, free of disturbance, having your affairs depend on nobody. "There is tumult at Rome, and calls to pay." But happiness is worth all the troublesome things put together. So if the time comes for them, why do you not take away the wish to avoid them? What need is there to carry a load like a donkey and be beaten with a stick? And if you do not do this, understand that you must always be the slave of whoever has the power to release you and the power to block you, and must serve him as an evil spirit.

There is only one road to happiness, and let this rule be ready morning and noon and night: not to look toward things outside the power of the will; to think nothing your own; to give everything up to the Divinity, to Fortune, and to make them the superintendents of these things, as Zeus has made them; to observe only what is your own, the thing that cannot be blocked; and when we read, to refer our reading to this alone, and our writing and our listening too.

This is why I cannot call a man industrious if all I hear is that he reads and writes; and even if somebody adds that he reads all night, I cannot say it, if he does not know what he should refer his reading to. You do not call a man industrious if he stays awake for a girl; nor do I. If he does it for reputation, I say he loves reputation; if he does it for money, I say he loves money, not labour; and if he does it out of love of learning, I say he loves learning. But if he refers his labour to his own governing part, so as to keep it in a condition in line with nature and to live his life in that condition, then and only then do I call him industrious.

So never praise a man on account of things that are common to everybody; praise him for his judgments, because those are what belong to each man, and they are what make his acts good or bad.

Remembering these rules, take joy in what is present and be content with the things that come in season. If you see something you have learned and enquired about turning up in the course of your life, be delighted at it. If you have laid aside or reduced a bad disposition and the habit of abusing people; if you have done the same with rash temper, foul language, hastiness, sluggishness; if you are not moved by what used to move you, or not in the same way — then you can hold a festival every day: today because you behaved well in one act, tomorrow because you behaved well in another.

How much greater a reason is this for sacrificing than a consulship or the governorship of a province? These things come to you from yourself and from the gods. Remember this: who gives these things, and to whom, and for what purpose.

If you nourish yourself on these thoughts, do you still think it makes any difference where you are happy, where you please God? Are the gods not equally distant from every place? Do they not see what is going on equally from every place?
