Chapter 8: Against Those Who Rush to Put On the Philosopher's Cloak

Never praise or blame a man on account of things that are common to everybody, and do not credit him with any skill or lack of skill on that account; and so you will be free of rashness and of spite.

This man bathes very quickly. Does he do wrong, then? Certainly not. What does he do? He bathes very quickly. Is everything done well, then? By no means: acts that come from right judgments are done well, and acts that come from bad judgments are done badly. But until you know the judgment a man does each thing from, neither praise nor blame the act.

And a judgment is not easily discovered from external acts. This man is a carpenter. Why? Because he uses an axe. And what has that to do with it? This man is a musician, because he sings. And what does that prove? This man is a philosopher — because he wears a cloak and long hair. And what does a street conjurer wear?

So when a man sees a philosopher behaving indecently, he says at once, "Look what the philosopher is doing!" — when because of the indecent behaviour he ought rather to say that the man is not a philosopher.

If the preconception of a philosopher, and what he professes, were to wear a cloak and long hair, men would be right. But if what he professes is rather to keep himself free of faults, why do we not take the name of philosopher away from him, since he does not make his professions good?

That is what we do in every other art. When a man sees another handling an axe badly, he does not say, "What is the use of carpentry? See how badly carpenters do their work." He says the opposite: this man is not a carpenter, because he uses an axe badly. In the same way, if a man hears somebody singing badly, he does not say, "See how musicians sing," but rather, "This man is no musician."

It is only in the matter of philosophy that people do otherwise. When they see a man acting against the profession of a philosopher, they do not take his title away; they assume he is a philosopher, and then, from his acts and the fact that he is behaving indecently, they conclude that philosophy is of no use.

What is the reason for that? Because we set a value on the notion of a carpenter, and of a musician, and of the other craftsmen likewise, and not on the notion of a philosopher; and we judge from externals only that it is a confused and ill-defined thing.

And what other kind of art takes its name from dress and hair, and has no theorems and no material and no end? What is the material of the philosopher? Is it a cloak? No — reason. What is his end? To wear a cloak? No — to have his reason in a right state. What sort of theorems are his? Are they about how the beard is to be grown big or the hair long? No: rather what Zeno says — to know the elements of reason, what each of them is like, and how they are fitted to one another, and what follows from them.

Will you not see first whether he does what he professes, when he behaves unbecomingly, before you blame his pursuit? As it is, when you are behaving soberly yourself, you say, because of what he seems to you to be doing wrong, "Look at the philosopher" — as though it were proper to call a man who does these things a philosopher; and further, "This is how a philosopher behaves."

But you do not say "Look at the carpenter" when you know a carpenter is an adulterer, or when you see him a glutton; and you do not say "See the musician." So up to a point even you understand what a philosopher professes — and then you fall away from the notion and get confused through carelessness.

But even the men called philosophers themselves go after the thing by starting from what is common to them and everybody else. As soon as they have put on a cloak and grown a beard, they say, "I am a philosopher." No man will say "I am a musician" because he has bought a plectrum and a lute, or "I am a smith" because he has put on a cap and an apron. The dress fits the art, and men take their name from the art and not from the dress.

This is why Euphrates used to put it so well. "For a long time," he said, "I tried to be a philosopher without anybody knowing it, and it was useful to me. In the first place, I knew that when I did anything well I did it for my own sake and not for the spectators. I ate well for my own sake; I kept my expression and my walk composed — all of it for myself and for God. And then, as I struggled alone, so I was alone in the danger. If I did anything base or unbecoming, philosophy was not endangered through me; and I did not injure the many by doing anything wrong as a philosopher. So the men who did not know my purpose used to wonder how it was that, though I talked and lived entirely among philosophers, I was not a philosopher myself. And what was the harm in being known for a philosopher by my acts instead of by outward marks?"

See how I eat, how I drink, how I sleep, how I bear and forbear, how I work with others, how I employ desire and how avoidance, how I keep the relations that are natural and the ones I have taken on, how free of confusion, how free of blocking. Judge me from that, if you can. And if you are so deaf and blind that you cannot conceive even Hephaestus to be a good smith unless you see the cap on his head, what is the harm in going unrecognised by so foolish a judge?

Socrates too was not known to most people to be a philosopher; men used to come to him and ask to be introduced to philosophers. Was he annoyed, as we are, and did he say, "And do you not think I am a philosopher?" No: he would take them and introduce them, satisfied with one thing, with being a philosopher — and pleased, too, at not being thought one, and not annoyed, because he was thinking about his own occupation.

What is the work of an honourable and good man? To have many pupils? By no means; the men who are keen on that will see to it. Was it his business to examine difficult theorems carefully? Others will see to that as well. In what, then, was he, and who was he, and whom did he want to be? He was engaged in the thing where harm and advantage lie.

If any man can damage me, he says, I am doing nothing. If I am waiting for another man to do me good, I am nothing. If I want something and it does not happen, I am unlucky. To such a contest he invited every man, and I do not think he would have refused the contest with anybody.

What do you suppose — was it by proclaiming, "I am such a man"? Far from it: by being such a man. Because this is exactly the mark of a fool and a boaster, to say, "I am free of the passions and of disturbance. Do not be unaware, my friends, that while you are uneasy and disturbed about things of no value, I alone am free of all disturbance."

So is it not enough for you to feel no pain, unless you make this announcement: "Gather round, all of you with gout, headaches, fever; you who are lame and blind — and observe that I am sound and free of every ailment"? That is empty and unpleasant to hear, unless like Asclepius you are able to show at once what treatment will free them from their disease too, and unless you show your own health as an example.

For such is the Cynic whom Zeus honours with the staff and the crown, and who says: so that you may see, gentlemen, that you are looking for happiness and peace of mind not where they are but where they are not — look, I have been sent to you by God as an example. I have no property, no house, no wife, no children, not even a bed, or a coat, or a piece of household furniture; and see how healthy I am. Test me; and if you see that I am undisturbed, hear the remedies and how I was cured.

That is both humane and noble. But see whose work it is: the work of Zeus, or of the man Zeus may judge worthy of the service, so that he shall never show the many anything by which he would cancel out his own testimony — the testimony he gives for virtue and against externals:

	His handsome face does not grow pale, nor does he
	wipe a tear from his cheeks.
	— Odyssey, xi 524

And not only that: he neither desires nor looks for anything — not a man, not a place, not an amusement, as children look forward to the vintage or the holidays. He is always fortified by self-respect, as other men are fortified by walls and doors and doorkeepers.

But as it is, these men are merely stirred toward philosophy the way men with a bad stomach are stirred toward certain foods they will soon be sick of — and they rush straight at the staff and the royal power. They grow their hair, they put on the cloak, they bare one shoulder, they quarrel with the men they meet; and if they see a man in a thick winter coat, they quarrel with him.

Man, first train yourself in winter weather. Look at your own inclinations, and see that they are not those of a man with a bad stomach, or of a woman with a craving. First make sure that nobody knows what you are; be a philosopher to yourself for a while.

This is how fruit grows: the seed has to be buried for a time, hidden, growing slowly, so that it may come to perfection. If it produces the ear before the stem has jointed, it is imperfect — the produce of an Adonis garden. You are that sort of poor plant: you have flowered too soon, and the cold will scorch you.

See what farmers say about seed when the warm weather comes too early. They are afraid the seed will grow too lush, and then a single frost will catch it and show how forward it was.

Consider it yourself, my man. You have shot up too soon; you have rushed toward a little fame before the proper season; you think you are something, a fool among fools. The frost will catch you — or rather you have already been frostbitten at the root, while your upper parts still blossom a little, and so you think you are still alive and flourishing.

Let us ripen in the natural way. Why do you strip us bare? Why do you force us? We cannot bear the open air yet. Let the root grow; then let it put out the first joint, then the second, then the third. And then the fruit will force its own way out naturally, even if I do not choose it.

For who that is pregnant and full of such great principles does not also feel his own powers and move toward the acts that answer to them? A bull is not ignorant of his own nature and his own powers when a wild beast appears, and does not wait for somebody to urge him on; nor does a dog when he sees a wild animal.

And if I have the powers of a good man, shall I wait for you to prepare me for my own proper acts? At present I have not got them — believe me. So why do you want me withered before my time, as you have been withered?
