Chapter 5: Against the Quarrelsome and the Aggressive

The wise and good man neither quarrels with anybody himself nor, so far as he can prevent it, allows anybody else to. An example of this, as of everything else, is set before us in the life of Socrates, who not only avoided quarrels himself on every occasion but would not let others quarrel either.

See in Xenophon's Symposium how many quarrels he settled; how he put up with Thrasymachus and Polus and Callicles; how he put up with his wife, and with his son when he tried to refute him and pick holes in him. For he remembered well that no man has another man's governing part in his power. So he wanted nothing except what was his own.

And what is that? Not that this man or that should act according to nature — that belongs to somebody else — but that while other men are doing their own acts as they choose, he should nevertheless be in a condition in line with nature and live in it, doing only what is his own, so that others too may come into a state in line with nature. This is the object the wise and good man always sets before himself.

Is it to be a general? No; but if it is permitted him, his object in that matter is to keep his own governing part. Is it to marry? No; but if marriage is allowed him, his object in that matter is to keep himself in a condition in line with nature. And if he wanted his son not to do wrong, or his wife, he would be wanting what belongs to another not to belong to another. And being taught is precisely this: learning what things are one's own and what belong to somebody else.

So where is any room left for quarrelling, in a man who holds the opinion he ought to hold? Is he surprised at anything that happens? Does anything look new to him? Does he not expect what comes from bad men to be worse and harder than what actually falls on him? Does he not reckon as pure gain whatever they do that falls short of the extreme of wickedness?

Somebody has abused you. Great thanks to him for not striking you. "But he struck me as well." Great thanks that he did not wound you. "But he wounded me as well." Great thanks that he did not kill you.

For when did he ever learn, and in what school, that man is a tame creature, that men love one another, that an act of injustice is a great harm to the man who does it? Since he has not learned it and is not convinced of it, why should he not follow what looks like his own interest?

Your neighbour has thrown stones. Have you done anything wrong, then? "But the things in the house are broken." Are you a piece of household furniture? No: you are a free power of will.

What are you given to do in answer, then? If you are like a wolf, you must bite back and throw more stones. But if you consider what is proper for a man, examine your storehouse; see what capacities you came into the world with. Have you the disposition of a wild beast? Have you the disposition of revenge for an injury?

When is a horse wretched? When he is deprived of his natural capacities — not when he cannot crow like a cock, but when he cannot run. When is a dog wretched? Not when he cannot fly, but when he cannot follow a scent. And is a man unhappy in the same way — not because he cannot strangle lions or throw his arms round statues, since he did not come into the world with powers from nature for that, but because he has lost his honesty and his good faith?

People ought to gather and lament over such a man for the misfortunes he has fallen into — not lamenting because a man has been born or has died, but because in his lifetime it has happened to him to lose what was his own. Not what he received from his father, not his land and his house and his inn and his slaves, for not one of those is a man's own; they all belong to others, they are servile, they are subject to account, and they are given to different men at different times by those who have them in their power. I mean the things that belong to him as a man: the marks stamped on his mind that he came into the world with — the marks we look for on coins too, and if we find them we approve the coin, and if we do not find them we reject it.

What is the stamp on this coin? Trajan's. Hand it over. It is Nero's. Throw it away; it cannot be accepted; it is counterfeit.

So here too. What is the stamp on this man's judgments? Gentleness, a sociable disposition, a tolerant temper, a readiness for mutual affection. Produce those. I accept them: I count this man a citizen, I accept him as a neighbour and a fellow passenger. Only see that he has not Nero's stamp on him. Is he quick-tempered, full of resentment, a fault-finder? Does he break the heads of the men in his way when the whim takes him? Then why did you call him a man?

Is everything judged by the bare shape? If that is so, then say that the shape of an apple in wax has the smell and the taste of an apple. The external figure is not enough; the nose and the eyes are not enough to make the man; he must have the judgments of a man too.

Here is a man who does not listen to reason, who does not know when he is refuted: he is a donkey. In another the sense of shame has gone dead: he is good for nothing, he is anything rather than a man. This one is looking for somebody to meet so that he can kick him or bite him — so he is not even a sheep or a donkey, but a kind of wild animal.

"What then? Would you have me despised?"

By whom? By men who know you? And how will men who know you despise a man who is gentle and modest? By men who do not know you, perhaps? What is that to you? No other craftsman cares about the opinion of men who know nothing about his craft.

"But they will be more hostile to me for it."

Why do you say "me"? Can any man injure your will, or stop you using the impressions before you in a natural way? In no way at all. Then why are you still disturbed, and why do you choose to show yourself afraid? Why do you not come out and announce that you are at peace with all men whatever they do, and laugh most of all at the ones who think they can harm you? These slaves, you can say, know neither who I am nor where my good and my evil lie, because they have no access to what is mine.

This is how men who hold a strong city mock the besiegers: what trouble these men are taking for nothing. Our wall is secure, we have food for a very long time, and every other resource. These are the things that make a city strong and impossible to take. And nothing but his judgments makes a man's soul impossible to take.

For what wall is so strong, what body so hard, what possession so safe, what rank so free from assault? All other things everywhere are perishable and easily stormed; and any man attached to them in any way must be disturbed, must expect what is bad, must fear, lament, find his desires disappointed and fall into what he would avoid.

Then do we not choose to make secure the one means of safety that is offered to us? Do we not choose to withdraw from what is perishable and servile and to work at what is imperishable and free by nature? Do we not remember that no man either harms another or does good to another, but that a man's opinion about each thing is what harms him and what overturns him — that this is fighting, this is civil discord, this is war?

What made Eteocles and Polynices enemies was nothing but this: their opinion about royal power and their opinion about exile, that the one is the extreme of evils and the other the greatest good.

Now it is every man's nature to seek the good and avoid the bad, and to count whoever deprives him of the one and involves him in the other an enemy and a traitor, even if he is a brother, a son, a father. Nothing is closer kin to us than the good. So if externals are good and evil, then a father is no friend to his sons, nor a brother to a brother, and the whole world everywhere is full of enemies and traitors and informers.

But if the will, being as it ought to be, is the only good, and the will, being as it ought not to be, is the only evil — then where is there any strife, where is there any abuse? About what? About things that do not concern us? And strife with whom? With the ignorant, the unhappy, men who have been deceived about the chief things?

Remembering this, Socrates ran his own household and put up with a very bad-tempered wife and a foolish son. And how did she show her bad temper? By pouring water over his head as much as she liked, and by trampling on the cake somebody had sent him. And what is that to me, if I think these things are nothing to me?

But this is my business: no tyrant shall check my will, and no master; the many shall not check me, who am only one; the stronger shall not check me, who am the weaker. God gives this power of being unchecked to every man.

These judgments make love in a household, agreement in a state, peace among nations, and gratitude to God. They make a man confident about externals in everything, as about things belonging to others, as about things of no value.

We are certainly able to write and read these things, and to praise them when they are read, but we do not come anywhere near being convinced of them. So what is said about the Spartans — lions at home, but foxes in Ephesus — will fit us too: lions in the school, but foxes outside it.
