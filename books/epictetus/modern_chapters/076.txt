Chapter 19: The Difference Between an Ordinary Person and a Philosopher

The first difference between an ordinary man and a philosopher is this. The ordinary man says, "Alas for me, on account of my little child, my brother, my father." The philosopher, if he is ever forced to say "Alas for me", stops and adds: "on my own account."

For nothing outside the will can block or damage the will; only the will can block or damage itself. So if we lean this way ourselves — so that when we come off badly we blame ourselves, and remember that nothing else is the cause of being thrown or of losing our peace of mind except our own opinion — then I swear to you by all the gods that we have made progress.

But as things stand we have gone another way from the very beginning. While we were still children, if we ever stumbled through carelessness, the nurse did not scold us: she beat the stone. And what had the stone done? Ought the stone to have moved out of the way on account of your child's carelessness?

Again, if we came out of the bath and found nothing to eat, the tutor never checked our appetite: he flogged the cook. Man, did we make you tutor to the cook and not to the child? Correct the child. Improve him.

And so, even when we are grown, we are like children. A man with no music is a child in music; a man with no letters is a child in learning; a man with no teaching is a child in life.
