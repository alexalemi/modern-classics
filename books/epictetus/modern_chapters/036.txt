Chapter 5: How Greatness of Spirit Goes with Taking Care

The materials themselves are indifferent; the use of them is not. So how is a man to keep his firmness and his peace of mind, and at the same time be careful, and neither reckless nor slack? By copying men who play at dice. The counters are indifferent, the dice are indifferent. How do I know what the throw will be? But to use the throw carefully and skilfully — that is my business.

So in life too the chief business is this: distinguish things and separate them out, and say: externals are not in my power; the will is in my power. Where am I to look for good and bad? Inside, in what is my own. And in what does not belong to you, never use the words good or bad, or profit or loss, or anything of the kind.

What follows? Should we handle such things carelessly? Not at all: that is bad for the will, and so against nature. We should act carefully, because the use is not indifferent; and we should also act firmly and undisturbed, because the material is indifferent. Where the material is not indifferent, nobody can block me or compel me. Where I can be blocked and compelled, getting the thing is not in my power, and is neither good nor bad; but the use is good or bad, and the use is in my power.

It is difficult to mix these two things together — the carefulness of a man who is engaged with the material, and the steadiness of a man who does not care about it. Difficult, but not impossible; and if it is impossible, then happiness is impossible.

We should do as we do on a voyage. What can I do? I can choose the master of the ship, the crew, the day, the moment. Then a storm comes. What more is there for me to care about? My part is done. The business belongs to somebody else — the master.

But the ship is going down. What am I to do then? The only thing I can: not drown full of fear, not screaming, not blaming God, but knowing that what has come into being must also perish. I am not immortal. I am a man, a part of the whole, as an hour is part of a day. I must arrive like the hour and pass like the hour. So what does it matter to me how I pass — whether by suffocation or by fever? I have to go by some such means.

You will see the same thing in men who are good at ball. Nobody cares about the ball as being good or bad; they care about throwing and catching. That is where the skill lies, and the art, the speed, the judgment — so that I may not be able to catch it even if I spread out my coat, while another man catches it when I throw. But if we take the ball and throw it in fear and agitation, what sort of game is that? How is a man to keep steady, and how is he to see the pattern of the play? One will say "throw" and another "do not throw", and a third "you have already thrown once". That is quarrelling, not play.

Socrates knew how to play ball. How? By joking in the court where he was tried. "Tell me, Anytus," he says, "how do you say I do not believe in God? Who do you think the spirits are? Are they not sons of gods, or a mixture of gods and men?" And when Anytus admitted it, Socrates said, "Then who do you think can believe that there are mules and no donkeys?" He said it as though he were playing ball.

And what was the ball in that game? Life, chains, exile, a draught of poison, being taken from his wife, leaving his children orphans. Those were the things he was playing with — and he still played, and threw the ball skilfully.

So we should behave. We must bring all the care of the players and show the same indifference about the ball. We ought by all means to apply our art to some external material — not valuing the material, but showing our art in whatever it happens to be. The weaver does not make the wool; he practises his art on the wool he is given. Somebody else gives you food and property, and is able to take them away again, and your poor body with them. So when you have been given the material, work on it.

Then if you come out of the trial having suffered nothing, everyone who meets you will congratulate you on your escape. But the man who knows how to look at such things, if he sees you have behaved properly, will praise you and be glad; and if he finds you owe your escape to behaving badly, he will do the opposite. Where there is reason to rejoice, there is reason to congratulate.

How is it said, then, that some external things are according to nature and others contrary to it? It is said as it would be said if we were cut off from society. To a foot I shall say that it is according to nature for it to be clean; but if you take it as a foot, and not as something detached, it will be fitting for it to step into mud and tread on thorns, and sometimes to be cut off for the good of the whole body — or else it is not a foot at all.

We ought to think about ourselves in some such way. What are you? A man. If you consider yourself as detached from other men, it is according to nature to live to old age, to be rich, to be healthy. But if you consider yourself as a man and a part of a certain whole, then for the sake of that whole it is fitting that at one time you should be ill, at another take a voyage and run into danger, at another be in want, and in some cases die before your time.

Why are you troubled, then? Do you not know that as a foot is no longer a foot once it is detached from the body, so you are no longer a man once you are separated from other men? For what is a man? A part of a state — first of the state that is made up of gods and men, and then of the one we call next to it, which is a small copy of the universal one.

"What — must I be brought to trial? Must this man have a fever, and that man go to sea, and another die, and another be condemned?"

Yes. In such a body, in such a universe of things, among so many living together, it is impossible that such things should not happen, some to one and some to another. Your duty, since you have come here, is to say what you ought and to arrange these matters as is fitting.

Then somebody says: "I shall charge you with doing me wrong." Much good may it do you. I have done my part; whether you have done yours, you must see to. And there is some danger there too — that it may escape your notice.
