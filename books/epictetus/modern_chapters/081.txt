Chapter 23: To Those Who Read and Lecture to Show Off

First tell yourself who you want to be, and then do what you are doing accordingly. We see this in nearly everything else. Men who take up athletics first settle what they want to be, and then do what follows. If a man is a long-distance runner there is a certain diet, a certain kind of walking, rubbing and exercise; if he is a sprinter, all of it is different; if he is a pentathlete, more different still. You will find the same in the crafts. If you are a carpenter you will have such and such things; if a metalworker, such and such.

Whatever we do, if we refer it to no end we shall be doing it for nothing; and if we refer it to the wrong end we shall miss the mark.

There is a general end and a particular one. First of all we must act as a man. What does that include? Not being like a sheep, gentle though it is; and not being like a wild beast, out to do harm. The particular end has to do with each person's way of life and his choice: the lute player acts as a lute player, the carpenter as a carpenter, the philosopher as a philosopher, the public speaker as a public speaker.

So when you say, "Come and hear me read," take care first that you are not doing it without a purpose; and then, if you find you are doing it for a purpose, consider whether it is the right one. Do you want to do good, or to be praised?

At once you hear him saying, "What is the praise of the crowd worth to me?" And he says it well — it is worth nothing to a musician as a musician, nor to a geometer.

So do you want to be useful? Useful in what? Tell us, so that we can come running to your lecture room. And can a man do anything useful for others who has not received something useful himself? No — any more than a man can do anything useful in carpentry unless he is a carpenter, or in shoemaking unless he is a shoemaker.

Do you want to know whether you have got any benefit, then? Produce your judgments, philosopher. What does desire promise? Not to fail of its object. What does avoidance promise? Not to fall into what it flees. Well — do we keep their promise? Tell me the truth; and if you lie, I shall tell you.

The other day, when your audience came in rather cold and did not applaud, you went away humiliated. And the other day, when you had been praised, you went round asking everybody, "What did you think of me?" — Marvellous, master, I swear by everything I hold dear. — "But how did I handle that particular passage?" — Which? — "The place where I described Pan and the nymphs." — Superb.

And you tell me that in desire and avoidance you are acting according to nature? Be off; try that on somebody else.

Did you not praise a certain man against your own opinion? Did you not flatter a certain senator's son? Would you want your own children to be like them? I hope not. Then why did you praise and flatter him? "He is a well-born young man and listens well to discourses." How so? "He admires me." There is your proof.

And what do you think? Do these very people not secretly despise you? When a man who knows perfectly well that he has never done any good and never thinks about it finds a philosopher saying, "You have great natural talent, and a frank and good disposition" — what else do you suppose he says to himself but "This man wants something from me"?

Or tell me: what act has he shown that indicates a great mind? Look — he has been in your company a long time, he has listened to your talks, he has heard you read. Has he become more modest? Has he been turned to reflect on himself? Has he seen what a bad state he is in? Has he thrown off his conceit? Is he looking for somebody to teach him?

He is. A man to teach him how to live? No, fool — how to talk. That is what he admires you for as well. Listen to what he says: "This man writes with perfect art, far better than Dion." That is a different thing altogether. Does he say, "This man is modest, trustworthy, undisturbed"? And even if he did say it, I would ask him: since this man is trustworthy, tell me what a trustworthy man is. And if he could not tell me, I would add: first understand what you are saying, and then say it.

So you, in your wretched state, gaping after applause and counting your audience — do you mean to be useful to other people? "Many more attended my lecture today." Yes, many; let us say five hundred. That is nothing; suppose there were a thousand. "Dion never had so many hearers." How could he? "And they understand what is said beautifully." What is fine, master, can move even a stone.

There — those are the words of a philosopher. That is the disposition of a man who is going to do others good. Here is a man who has listened to discourses, who has read what was written about Socrates as Socratic writing and not as compositions by Lysias and Isocrates. "'I have often wondered by what arguments...' No: by what argument. That is more exact than the other."

Have you read the words in any way different from the way you read little songs? If you read them as you ought, you would not have been attending to such matters. You would have been looking at these words instead: "Anytus and Meletus can kill me, but they cannot harm me." And: "I have always been the sort of man to pay attention to nothing of my own except the reasoning that on examination seems to me best."

So who ever heard Socrates say, "I know something and I teach it"? He used to send different men to different teachers. And so they used to come to him and ask him to introduce them to philosophers, and he would take them and recommend them.

No — instead, as he walked along with them, he would say: "Hear me lecture today at the house of Quadratus."

Why should I hear you? Do you want to show me that you put words together cleverly? You do put them together, man — and what good will it do you?

"Only praise me."

What do you mean by praising? "Say to me: admirable, wonderful." Very well, I say it. But if that is praise, then whatever it is philosophers mean by the name of good — what have I to praise in you? If speaking well is a good, teach me and I will praise you.

"What then — ought a man to listen to such things without pleasure?" I hope not. For my part I do not listen even to a lute player without pleasure. Must I stand and play the lute for that reason?

Hear what Socrates says: "Nor would it be seemly for a man of my age to come before you like a young man putting together speeches." Like a young man, he says. And in truth this little art is an elegant thing: to pick out words and put them together and come forward and read them or speak them gracefully, and while reading to say, "There are not many who can do this" — I swear by everything you hold dear.

Does a philosopher invite people to hear him? As the sun draws men to it, or as food does, does the philosopher not draw those who will get some benefit? What doctor invites a man to be treated by him? Though I do hear that these days even the doctors in Rome invite patients; when I lived there, doctors were invited.

"I invite you to come and hear that things are in a bad way with you; that you take care of everything except what you ought to take care of; that you do not know good from bad and are unlucky and unhappy." A fine sort of invitation. And yet if the philosopher's words do not have that effect on you, he is dead, and so is the speaker.

Rufus used to say: if you have leisure to praise me, I am talking to no purpose. And in fact he used to talk in such a way that every one of us sitting there supposed somebody had denounced him to Rufus — so exactly did he touch on what we were doing, so completely did he put every man's faults before his eyes.

Gentlemen, a philosopher's school is a surgery. You ought not to leave it feeling pleasure, but feeling pain. You are not in good health when you come in: one man has a dislocated shoulder, another an abscess, a third a fistula, a fourth a headache.

And am I to sit and utter neat little thoughts and exclamations so that you may praise me and go away — one with his shoulder in the same state it came in, another with his head still aching, a third with his fistula or his abscess exactly as they were? Is this what young men are to leave home for, and leave their parents and friends and family and property, so that they can say "Wonderful!" while you are making your exclamations? Was this what Socrates did, or Zeno, or Cleanthes?

"What then — is there no such thing as the style of exhortation?" Who denies it? As there is the style of refutation and the style of instruction. But who ever counted a fourth style beside them, the style of display?

What is the style of exhortation? Being able to show a man, or many men, the struggle they are actually in, and that they are thinking about anything rather than what they really want. They want the things that lead to happiness and they look for them in the wrong place.

And in order to do that, a thousand seats have to be set out and men invited to listen, and you have to go up into the pulpit in a fine robe and describe the death of Achilles. Stop, I beg you by the gods, spoiling good words and good acts as far as you possibly can.

Nothing has more power in exhortation than a speaker showing his hearers that he needs them. But tell me: who, hearing you read or lecture, is anxious about himself, or turns to reflect on himself? Who, going out, says, "The philosopher hit me there; I must stop doing these things"? Does he not rather say to somebody — even if you have a great reputation — "He spoke finely about Xerxes"? And another says, "No, about the battle of Thermopylae." Is this what listening to a philosopher means?
