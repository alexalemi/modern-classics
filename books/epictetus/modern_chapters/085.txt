Chapter 26: To Those Who Are Afraid of Being Poor

Are you not ashamed to be more cowardly and meaner than runaway slaves? How do they leave their masters when they run? What estates do they count on, what servants do they rely on? Do they not steal a little, enough for the first few days, and after that make their way over land or sea, contriving one method after another of keeping themselves alive? And what runaway slave ever died of hunger?

But you are afraid that necessities may fail you, and you lie awake at night over it. Wretch, are you so blind that you cannot see the road that the want of necessities leads to?

"Well, where does it lead?"

To the same place a fever leads, or a stone falling on you: to death. Have you not said this yourself to your companions, often? Have you not read a great deal of this kind, and written a great deal? And how often have you boasted that you were easy about death?

"Yes — but my wife and children will go hungry too."

Well then, does their hunger lead anywhere else? Is there not the same descent to the same place for them? Is there not the same condition down there for them? Will you not choose to look toward that place, full of boldness against every want and shortage — the place the richest men and the men who have held the highest offices, and kings themselves, and tyrants, all have to go down to? You will go down hungry, if it happens that way; they will go down burst with indigestion and drink.

How rarely have you seen a beggar who was not an old man, and of extreme old age at that? Chilled with cold night and day, lying on the ground, eating only what is absolutely necessary — and they come close to being unable to die at all.

Can you not write? Can you not look after children? Can you not be a doorkeeper at somebody else's house?

"But it is shameful to come to such a necessity."

Then learn first what things are shameful, and after that tell us you are a philosopher. For the present, do not allow it even if somebody else calls you one.

Is a thing shameful to you when it is not your own act, when you are not the cause of it, when it has come to you by accident, like a headache or a fever? If your parents were poor and left their property to others, and if while they live they do not help you at all, is that shameful to you? Is this what you learned from the philosophers?

Did you never hear that what is shameful is what deserves blame, and that what deserves blame is worth blaming? Whom do you blame for an act that is not his, that he did not do himself? Did you make your father what he is? Is it in your power to improve him? Is that power given to you? Well then — ought you to want things that are not given to you, or to be ashamed if you do not get them?

And in studying philosophy, have you got into the habit of looking to others and hoping for nothing from yourself? Then lament and groan, and eat in fear that you may have no food tomorrow. Tremble about your wretched slaves, in case they steal, in case they run away, in case they die.

Live that way and go on living that way, you who have come near philosophy in name only, and have disgraced its theorems as far as you can by showing them to be useless and unprofitable to the men who take them up. You have never looked for steadiness, or freedom from being thrown about, or freedom from the passions. You have never sought out anybody for that purpose, though you have sought out plenty for syllogisms. You have never thoroughly examined any of these impressions on your own: am I able to bear this, or am I not? What is left for me to do?

Instead, as though all your affairs were secure and in order, you have settled on the third topic, the one about things staying unchanged — so that you may possess unchanged what? Cowardice, meanness of spirit, admiration of the rich, desire that reaches nothing, and avoidance that fails in the attempt. These are the things you have been anxious to make secure.

Ought you not first to have gained something from reason, and then made that secure? Whom did you ever see building a parapet all round and not putting a wall under it? What doorkeeper is stationed to guard where there is no door?

But you practise so as to be able to prove — what? You practise so that you may not be tossed about as at sea by sophisms. Tossed about away from what? Show me first what you are holding, what you are measuring, what you are weighing; and show me the scales, or the measure. Or how long will you go on measuring dust?

Ought you not to be demonstrating the things that make men happy, that make things go for them as they wish, and that show us why we should blame nobody, accuse nobody, and accept the administration of the universe? Show me those.

"See, I show them: I will resolve syllogisms for you."

That is the measure, slave — it is not the thing measured.

So now you are paying the penalty for what you neglected, philosophy. You tremble, you lie awake, you take advice from everybody; and if your deliberations are not likely to please everybody, you think you have deliberated badly.

Then you fear hunger, as you suppose. But it is not hunger you fear. You are afraid that you will not have a cook, that you will not have a second man to buy provisions for the table, a third to take off your shoes, a fourth to dress you, others to rub you down and follow you about — so that in the bath, when you have taken your clothes off and stretched yourself out like a man being crucified, you may be rubbed on this side and on that, and the attendant may say to the slave, "Move him; give me his side; take his head; show me the shoulder." And then, when you have left the bath and gone home, you may call out, "Is nobody bringing anything to eat?" and then, "Take the tables away; wipe them down."

This is what you are afraid of: that you may not be able to lead the life of a sick man.

Learn instead the life of men who are in health: how slaves live, how labourers live, how genuine philosophers live; how Socrates lived, who had a wife and children; how Diogenes lived; how Cleanthes lived, who ran the school and drew water. If you choose to have these things, you will have them everywhere, and you will live in complete confidence.

Confidence in what? In the only thing a man can be confident in: what is secure, what cannot be blocked, what cannot be taken away — that is, your own will.

And why have you made yourself so useless and good for nothing that nobody will choose to take you into his house or take care of you? If a whole and useful pot were thrown out, any man who found it would pick it up and count it a gain. Nobody will pick you up; every man will count you a loss. So can you not do the work even of a dog, or of a cock? Then why do you choose to go on living, being what you are?

Does any good man fear he will run out of food? It does not run out for the blind, or for the lame: will it run out for a good man? A good soldier does not lack somebody to pay him, nor does a labourer, nor a shoemaker: and will the good man lack such a person?

Does God so neglect the things he established — his servants, his witnesses, the only men he uses as examples to the untaught, to show that he exists and administers the whole well and does not neglect human affairs, and that for a good man there is no evil, living or dead?

And what about when he does not supply him with food? What else is he doing but giving me, like a good general, the signal to retreat? I obey. I follow, agreeing with the commander's words and praising his acts. I came when it pleased him and I shall go when it pleases him; and while I lived, my duty was to praise God, both by myself and to each man and to many.

He does not supply me with much, or with plenty; he does not want me to live in luxury. He did not supply Hercules either, who was his own son: somebody else was king of Argos and Mycenae, and Hercules obeyed orders and laboured and was trained. And Eurystheus was what he was, king neither of Argos nor of Mycenae, since he was not even king of himself; while Hercules was ruler and leader of the whole earth and sea, who cleared away lawlessness and brought in justice and holiness — and he did it naked and alone.

And when Odysseus was cast ashore from the shipwreck, did want humiliate him? Did it break his spirit? How did he go off to the girls to ask for necessities — begging, which is thought the most shameful thing of all?

	Like a lion bred in the mountains, trusting in his strength.
	— Odyssey vi 130.

Trusting in what? Not in reputation, not in wealth, not in the power of an office, but in his own strength — that is, in his judgments about what is in our power and what is not. These are the only things that make men free, that let them escape being blocked, that lift the heads of men who are cast down, that let them look at rich men and tyrants with a steady eye.

And this was the gift given to the philosopher. But you will not come out bold; you will come out trembling for your paltry clothes and your silver plate. Unhappy man — have you wasted your time until now like this?

"And what if I fall ill?"

You will be ill in the way you ought to be.

"Who will look after me?"

God. Your friends.

"I shall lie on a hard bed."

But you will lie on it like a man.

"I shall not have a comfortable room."

Then you will be ill in an uncomfortable one.

"Who will provide the food I need?"

The people who provide it for others. You will be ill as Manes is ill.

And what will the end of the illness be? Anything but death?

Then will you not consider that this — not death, but the fear of death — is the chief of all evils for a man, and the chief mark of a mean spirit and of cowardice? Train yourself against that fear, then; that is my advice. Let all your reasoning tend that way, and your exercises, and your reading. And you will know that this, and only this, is how men are made free.
