Chapter 3: To Those Who Send People to Philosophers with a Recommendation

Diogenes answered a man who asked him for a letter of recommendation very well. "That you are a man," he said, "he will know as soon as he sees you. Whether you are good or bad he will know if he has the experience to tell the good from the bad; and if he has not, he will never know it, though I write to him ten thousand times."

It is exactly as if a silver coin asked to be recommended to somebody for testing. If the man is skilled at testing silver, he will know what you are — you will recommend yourself.

So in life too we ought to have a skill of the same sort as with coin, so that a man can say, as the assayer of silver says: bring me any coin you like and I will test it. With syllogisms I could say: bring me any man you please and I will pick out for you the man who knows how to resolve syllogisms from the man who does not. Why? Because I know how to resolve syllogisms myself. I have the very power a man must have if he is to identify the men who have it.

But in life, how do I behave? At one moment I call a thing good, and at the next I call it bad. What is the reason? The opposite of what holds with syllogisms: ignorance and inexperience.
