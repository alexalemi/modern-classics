Chapter 10: How to Work Out Your Duties from the Names You Bear

Consider who you are. In the first place, a man — that is, one who has nothing superior to his own will, and everything else subject to it, while the will itself he holds unenslaved and free.

So consider what reason has separated you from. It has separated you from wild beasts; it has separated you from the flocks. Beyond that, you are a citizen of the world and a part of it — not one of the parts that serve, but one of the parts that rule, since you are capable of following the divine administration and of thinking about how things are connected.

And what does the character of a citizen promise? To hold nothing as profitable to himself alone; to deliberate about nothing as though he were cut off from the community, but to act as the hand or the foot would act if they had reason and understood how nature is constituted — never setting themselves in motion or wanting anything except with reference to the whole.

So the philosophers say rightly that if the good man knew in advance what was going to happen, he would work along with his own sickness and death and maiming, knowing that these things are allotted him by the arrangement of the whole, and that the whole is superior to the part and the state to the citizen. But as it is, since we do not know the future, it is our duty to hold to the things that are by their nature more suitable to choose — because that too is one of the things we were made for.

After this, remember that you are a son. And what does that character promise? To count everything the son has as belonging to the father; to obey him in everything; never to blame him to anybody else; never to say or do anything that harms him; to give way to him in everything and yield, working with him as far as you can.

After this, know that you are a brother too, and that this character calls for making concessions, for being easily persuaded, for speaking well of your brother, for never claiming against him any of the things outside the will but readily giving them up, so as to have the larger share in what is inside it. See what a thing it is to gain a good disposition in exchange for a lettuce, if it comes to that, or a seat. What an advantage.

Next, if you are a councillor of some city, remember that you are a councillor; if a young man, that you are young; if an old man, that you are old. Each of these names, when it is examined, marks out its proper duties.

But if you go and blame your brother, I say to you: you have forgotten who you are and what your name is. If you were a smith and used the hammer wrongly, you would have forgotten the smith. And if you have forgotten the brother, and instead of a brother have become an enemy, will you seem to have exchanged nothing for nothing? And if instead of a man — a tame and social creature — you have become a mischievous wild animal, treacherous and given to biting, have you lost nothing?

Must you lose a little money, then, in order to have suffered damage? Does the loss of nothing else damage a man? If you lost your grasp of grammar or of music, would you count the loss a damage? And if you lose self-respect, restraint and gentleness, do you think that is no loss at all?

And yet the first things are lost through some external cause outside the will, while the second are lost by our own fault. Neither having the first nor losing them is shameful; but not having the second, and losing them, is shameful, and a reproach, and a misfortune.

What does a man lose who lets himself be used for another man's pleasure? He loses being a man. And what does the man lose who uses him so? A great many things — and he loses being a man no less than the other does. What does the adulterer lose? He loses the self-respecting man, the moderate man, the decent man, the citizen, the neighbour. What does the angry man lose? Something else. What does the coward lose? Something else.

No man is bad without suffering some loss and damage. So if you look for the damage only in lost money, then none of these men suffers any harm or damage at all — it may even be that they profit and gain, if they pick up a little money by any of these acts.

But consider: if you refer everything to a small coin, then not even the man who loses his nose is damaged, in your view. "Yes he is," you say, "he is mutilated in his body." Well — and does the man who has only lost his sense of smell lose nothing? Is there no energy of the soul, then, that is an advantage to the man who has it and a damage to the man who has lost it?

"Tell me what sort of energy you mean."

Have we not a natural sense of shame? "We have." And does the man who loses that suffer no damage? Is he deprived of nothing? Does he part with nothing that belonged to him? Have we not natural good faith, natural affection, a natural readiness to help others, a natural readiness to bear with them? Can the man who lets himself be damaged in these matters be unharmed and uninjured?

"What of it? Am I not to hurt the man who has hurt me?"

First consider what hurt is, and remember what you have heard from the philosophers. If the good lies in the will and the bad in the will too, then see whether what you are saying does not come to this: since that man has hurt himself by doing me an injustice, am I not to hurt myself by doing him one?

Why do we not put it to ourselves in some such way? Where there is any injury to the body or to our property, there we find harm; but where the same thing happens to the will, there — you suppose — there is no harm. The man who has been deceived, or who has done an unjust act, suffers nothing in his head, or his eye, or his hip, and he does not lose his estate; and we want nothing except that these things should be safe.

But whether we shall have a will that is self-respecting and trustworthy, or shameless and faithless, we do not care in the least — except in the school, and there only as far as a few words go. And that is why our progress is limited to those few words, and beyond them does not exist in the slightest degree.
