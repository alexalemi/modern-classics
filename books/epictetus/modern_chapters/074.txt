Chapter 17: On Providence and Why the Wicked Prosper

When you bring a charge against Providence, consider it, and you will find the thing has happened according to reason.

"Yes, but the unjust man has the advantage."

In what? "In money." Yes — because he is better than you at this: he flatters, he has no shame, he is wide awake. What is surprising about it? But see whether he has the advantage of you in good faith and in self-respect. You will not find that he has; you will find that you have the advantage wherever you are the better man.

I once said to a man who was upset because Philostorgus was doing well: would you be willing to go to bed with Sura?

"May that day never come," he answered.

Then why are you upset if the man gets something in return for what he sells? And how can you count him happy, when he acquires those things by means you find disgusting? And what wrong does Providence do, if it gives the better things to the better men? Is it not better to be self-respecting than to be rich?

He agreed that it was.

Then why are you upset, man, when you have the better thing?

So remember always, and keep it ready, that this is a law of nature: the superior has the advantage of the inferior in the thing he is superior in. And you will never be upset.

"But my wife treats me badly." Well: if anybody asks you what this is, say, my wife treats me badly. Is there nothing more? Nothing. "My father gives me nothing." What is this? My father gives me nothing. Is there nothing else? Nothing.

But to say that this is an evil is something that has to be added on from outside, and added falsely. This is why we must get rid, not of poverty, but of our opinion about poverty — and then we shall be happy.
