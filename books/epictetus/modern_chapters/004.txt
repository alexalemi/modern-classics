Chapter 3: What Follows from God Being the Father of All

If a man could really assent to this doctrine as he ought — that we are all descended from God in a special way, and that God is the father of men and of gods alike — I do not think he would ever have a low or shabby thought about himself again. If Caesar adopted you, nobody could stand your arrogance. And if you know that you are the son of Zeus, will you not be puffed up?

Yet we are not. Two things are mixed together in the making of a man: a body, which we share with the animals, and reason and intelligence, which we share with the gods. And most of us lean toward the wretched, mortal kinship, and only a few toward the divine and happy one.

Now, since every man necessarily deals with everything according to the judgment he holds about it, the few — the ones who think they were made for loyalty and self-respect and the sound use of impressions — never have a low or shabby thought about themselves. With the crowd it is the exact reverse. "What am I?" they say. "A poor miserable creature, and this wretched bit of flesh of mine."

Wretched it is. But you have something better than a bit of flesh. Why neglect the better thing, and cling to this?

Because of that kinship with the flesh, some of us who lean toward it turn into wolves — treacherous, faithless, out to do harm. Some turn into lions, savage and wild and untameable. But most of us turn into foxes, or something worse. What else is a slanderer, a man with malice in him, but a fox — or some meaner and more miserable animal still?

So look to it. Take care you do not become one of these wretched things.
