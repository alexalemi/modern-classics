Chapter 5: Against Those Who Go Home Because They Are Ill

"I am ill here," said one of the pupils, "and I want to go home."

At home, I suppose, you were never ill? Do you not consider whether you are doing anything here that might be useful to the training of your will, so that it may be put right? Because if you are doing nothing toward that end, you came for nothing. Go away. Look after your business at home.

If your governing part cannot be kept in a condition that is in line with nature, your land can. You will be able to increase your money, look after your father in his old age, spend time in the marketplace, hold public office — and being a bad man, you will do badly at whatever else you have to do.

But if you understand yourself, and know that you are throwing away certain bad opinions and taking on others in their place; if you have shifted your way of life from the things outside your will to the things inside it; and if, when you ever say "Alas", you say it not on account of your father or your brother but on account of yourself — do you still plead your illness?

Do you not know that both illness and death must catch us doing something? The farmer while he is tilling, the sailor while he is at sea. What would you like to be doing when death catches you — since it must catch you doing something? If you can be doing anything better than this when it comes, do that.

For my part, I want to be caught by illness or death while I am looking after nothing except my own will: undisturbed, unblocked, uncompelled, and free. I want to be found practising these things, so that I can say to God:

Have I broken your commands in any way? Have I misused the powers you gave me? Have I misused my senses, or my preconceptions? Have I ever blamed you? Have I ever found fault with the way you run things? I have been ill, because it was your will, and so have others — but I was content to be ill. I have been poor, because it was your will — but I was content with that too. I have held no public office, because it was not your pleasure that I should; and I never wanted it. Did you ever see me look sour on that account? Have I not always come before you with a cheerful face, ready to do your commands and obey your signals? Is it your will now that I should leave this gathering of men? I go. I thank you with all my heart that you allowed me to join in this gathering of yours, and to see your works, and to follow the way you run things.

May death catch me thinking these thoughts, writing this, reading this.

"But my mother will not be there to hold my head when I am ill."

Then go to your mother — you are exactly the sort of person who needs his head held when he is ill.

"But at home I used to lie in a lovely bed."

Then go away to your bed. Indeed you are fit to lie in such a bed even when you are well. So do not miss out on what you can do there.

And what does Socrates say? As one man is pleased at improving his land and another at improving his horse, so I am pleased every day at noticing that I am getting better.

"Better at what — at using nice little phrases?" Man, do not say that. "At little points of theory?" What are you talking about? "And in fact I do not see what else philosophers spend their time on."

Does it seem nothing to you never to have found fault with anybody, God or man? To have blamed nobody? To carry the same face always, going out and coming in? That is what Socrates knew — and yet he never said he knew anything or taught anything. If any man asked him for nice little phrases or little theories, he would take him off to Protagoras or Hippias; and if a man came asking for greens, he would take him to the gardener.

So which of you has that purpose? If you really had it, you would be content in illness, and in hunger, and in death. And if any of you has ever been in love with a charming girl, he knows I am telling the truth.
