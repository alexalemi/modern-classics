Chapter 26: What Is Really Going On in a Mistake

Every error contains a contradiction. Since the man who goes wrong does not want to go wrong but to be right, plainly he is not doing what he wants.

What does the thief want to do? What is in his own interest. So if the theft is not in his interest, he is not doing what he wants.

Now every rational soul is by nature offended by contradiction; and so long as it does not see the contradiction, nothing stops it doing contradictory things. But once it does see the contradiction, it must necessarily back away from it, exactly as a man must dissent from a falsehood once he sees the thing is false — while as long as the falsehood does not appear to him, he assents to it as true.

So the man who is strong in argument, and has the power of urging others on and refuting them, is the man who can show each person the contradiction he is going wrong through, and prove clearly that he is not doing what he wants and is doing what he does not want. Show a man that, and he will withdraw from what he is doing of his own accord; and as long as you do not show it, do not be surprised if he keeps on. He does what he does because it looks to him like the right thing.

That is why Socrates, trusting to this power, used to say: I am not in the habit of calling any other witness to what I say. I am always satisfied with the man I am arguing with; I ask him for his opinion and call him as my witness, and though he is only one man, he does instead of all of them.

For Socrates knew what moves a rational soul: it is like a pair of scales, and then it must tip, whether it chooses to or not. Show the rational governing part a contradiction and it will back away from it. And if you do not show it, blame yourself rather than the man who is not convinced.
