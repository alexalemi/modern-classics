Chapter 7: On the Use of Trick Arguments and Hypotheticals

Handling trick arguments and hypothetical ones, and those that draw their conclusions out of questioning — in a word, handling arguments of every such kind — has to do with the duties of life, though most people do not know it. In every matter we are asking how the wise and good man is to find the right road and the right method of dealing with it. So let people either say that a serious man will not go down into the contest of question and answer, or else that if he does go down into it, he will take no care to avoid conducting himself rashly and carelessly in the questioning and the answering. And if they will allow neither of those, they must admit that some inquiry ought to be made into the very subjects on which questioning and answering are chiefly employed.

For what is the aim of reasoning? To establish true propositions, to get rid of false ones, and to withhold assent where a thing is not plain. Is it enough, then, to have learned only that much?

"It is enough," a man may say.

Is it enough, then, for a man who wants to make no mistake in handling coin, to have heard the rule that he should take the genuine drachmas and refuse the counterfeit? It is not. What has to be added to the rule? What else but the capacity that tests genuine drachmas and counterfeit ones and tells them apart? So in reasoning too, what has been said is not enough: a man must acquire the capacity to examine the true and the false and the not-plain, and to tell them apart. He must.

And besides that, what does reasoning set out to do? That you should accept what follows from what you have properly granted. Well, is it enough here just to know that? It is not. A man has to learn how one thing is a consequence of others, and when one thing follows from one thing, and when it follows from several things taken together. Consider, then, whether this power too must not be acquired by anyone who intends to conduct himself skilfully in reasoning: the power of demonstrating for himself the various things he has put forward, the power of following other men's demonstrations, and of not being taken in by sophists who are only pretending to demonstrate. That is why the practice and exercise of valid arguments and figures has grown up among us, and it has been shown to be necessary.

But it does in fact happen sometimes that we have properly granted the premises, and something results from them which is not true, and results all the same. What am I to do then? Am I to admit the falsehood? How can I? Well then, am I to say that I did not properly grant what we agreed on? I am not allowed to do that either. Am I to say that the conclusion does not follow from what was conceded? That is not allowed either.

So what is to be done? Consider whether it is not this. Having borrowed money is not enough to make a man still a debtor: there has to be added the fact that he goes on owing it and has not paid it back. In the same way, having granted the premises is not enough to force you to admit the inference — you must also stand by what you granted. If the premises stay to the end just as they were when they were granted, then we are absolutely bound to stand by what we granted and to accept the consequences. But if the premises do not stay as they were when granted, then we are just as absolutely bound to withdraw from what we granted, and to refuse a conclusion that does not follow from the words our concession was made in. For the inference is no longer ours, and no longer results with our assent, since we have withdrawn from the premises we granted.

So we ought to examine premises of this kind, and the changes and shifts of meaning in them by which — in the course of questioning or answering, or in drawing the syllogistic conclusion, or in some other such way — the premises come to vary and give foolish men occasion to be thrown into confusion, if they do not see what the consequences are. And why ought we to examine them? So that in this matter we may not be employed improperly or in a muddle.

The same goes for hypotheses and hypothetical arguments. Sometimes it is necessary to ask that a hypothesis be granted, as a kind of bridge to the argument that follows. Must we then allow every hypothesis put to us, or not every one? And if not every one, which ones? And when a man has allowed a hypothesis, must he in every case stand by having allowed it? Or must he sometimes withdraw from it, and accept the consequences but not accept contradictions?

Yes — but suppose a man says: if you admit the hypothesis of something possible, I will drag you to something impossible. Is a man of sense to refuse to take that on, and dodge discussion and conversation with him? But who else besides the man of sense can use argument, and be skilful at questioning and answering, and impossible to cheat and deceive with false reasoning? And is he to go into the contest and then not take care to argue neither rashly nor carelessly? And if he takes no care, how is he the man we take him to be? Yet without some such training and preparation, can he keep up a continuous and consistent argument? Let them show that he can, and all these speculations become unnecessary — absurd, and out of keeping with our idea of a good and serious man.

Why are we still lazy and slack and sluggish? Why do we look for excuses to avoid working and keeping watch over our own reason?

"So if I go wrong in these matters, is it as though I have killed my father?"

Slave, where was there a father here for you to kill? What you have done is the only fault it was possible to commit here.

That is exactly the remark I made to Rufus when he found fault with me for failing to spot the one thing left out of a certain syllogism. I suppose, I said, that I have burnt down the Capitol.

Slave, he replied, was the thing left out here the Capitol? Or are these the only crimes there are — burning the Capitol and killing your father? To use the impressions put before you rashly and foolishly and carelessly; to fail to follow an argument, or a demonstration, or a piece of sophistry; in a word, not to see in questioning and answering what is consistent with what we have granted and what is not — is there no error in that?
