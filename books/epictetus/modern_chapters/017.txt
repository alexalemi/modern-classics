Chapter 16: On Providence in the Smallest Things

Do not be surprised that for the other animals everything the body needs is provided — not only food and drink but bedding too — and that they need no shoes, no bedclothes, no clothing, while we need all these extras. They were not made for themselves but for service, and it would not have been fitting to make them so that they needed other things. Just think what it would be for us to have to look after cattle and donkeys as well as ourselves — how they should be clothed and shod, and how they should eat and drink.

Soldiers stand ready for their commander already shod, clothed and armed; it would be a hard business for the tribune to go round shoeing and clothing his thousand men. In the same way nature has made the animals that are meant for service ready and equipped, needing no further care. So one small boy with a stick drives the cattle along.

But as it is, instead of being grateful that we do not have to take the same care over animals as over ourselves, we complain to God about our own case. And yet, in the name of Zeus and the gods, any single one of the things that exist would be enough to make a man see the providence of God — at least a man with modesty and gratitude in him. Never mind the great things: take only this, that milk is produced from grass, and cheese from milk, and wool from hides. Who made these things, or thought them up? Nobody, you say. What amazing shamelessness and stupidity.

Well, let us leave nature's main works aside and look at her side-work. Is there anything less useful than the hair on a man's chin? And has nature not put even this hair to the best possible use? Has she not marked off male from female by it? Does not each man's nature call out from a distance, "I am a man: approach me as such, speak to me as such, look for nothing else — see the signs"? And in women, just as she mixed something softer into the voice, so she left the chin bare.

Not so, you say: the human animal ought to have been left without any marks of distinction, and each of us should have had to announce, "I am a man." But is the sign not beautiful, and fitting, and worthy of respect? How much more beautiful than a cock's comb, how much more becoming than a lion's mane! And so we ought to keep the signs God has given us. We ought not to throw them away, nor to blur the distinctions between the sexes as far as we can manage it.

Are these the only works of providence in us? What words could be enough to praise them and set them out as they deserve? If we had any sense, should we be doing anything else, in company and alone, but singing hymns to the deity and blessing him and telling over his benefits? Should we not sing this hymn to God as we dig and plough and eat? "Great is God, who has given us these tools to work the ground with. Great is God, who has given us hands, and the power of swallowing, and a stomach, and growth we never feel happening, and breathing while we are asleep." This is what we ought to sing on every occasion — and to sing the greatest and most divine hymn of all for his giving us the power to understand these things and to use them in the proper way.

Well then: since most of you have gone blind, ought there not to be somebody to fill this office and sing the hymn to God on everyone's behalf? What else can I do, a lame old man, but sing hymns to God? If I were a nightingale, I would do a nightingale's part; if I were a swan, a swan's. But as it is I am a rational creature, and I ought to praise God. This is my work. I do it, and I will not desert this post as long as I am allowed to keep it — and I urge you to join in the same song.
