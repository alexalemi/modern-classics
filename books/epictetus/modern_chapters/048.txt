Chapter 17: How to Fit Preconceptions to Particular Cases

What is the first business of the man who takes up philosophy? To throw away conceit. It is impossible for a man to begin learning what he thinks he already knows.

As to what ought to be done and ought not to be done, and good and bad, and beautiful and ugly, we all talk about them at random and then go to the philosophers. On these matters we praise, we censure, we accuse, we blame, we pass judgment about what is honourable and dishonourable. And why do we go to the philosophers? Because we want to learn what we do not think we know. And what is that? Theorems. We want to learn what philosophers say, as something elegant and sharp; and some want to learn so as to profit by it.

It is ridiculous to suppose that a man wants to learn one thing and will learn another, or that he will make progress in what he does not learn.

But most men are taken in by what took in Theopompus the rhetorician, when he blames even Plato for wanting everything defined. What does he say? "Did none of us use the words good or just before you? Or did we utter the sounds meaninglessly and emptily, without understanding what each of them signified?"

Now who tells you, Theopompus, that we had no natural notions of these things, no preconceptions? But it is not possible to fit preconceptions to the objects that answer to them unless we have analysed them and enquired what object has to be put under each preconception.

You could bring the same charge against doctors. Which of us did not use the words healthy and unhealthy before Hippocrates lived? Did we utter them as empty sounds? We have a certain preconception of health too — but we are not able to fit it. That is why one man says "go without food", another says "give food", another says "bleed him", and another "use cupping". What is the reason? What but that a man cannot properly fit the preconception of health to particular cases?

So it is here too, in the matters that concern living. Which of us does not talk about good and bad, useful and useless? Which of us has not a preconception of each? And is it a clear and complete preconception? Show me. How shall I show it? By fitting the preconception properly to particular things.

Plato, for instance, puts definitions under the preconception of the useful, and you put them under the preconception of the useless. Is it possible that you are both right? How could it be? Does one man not fit the preconception of good to wealth, and another not to wealth but to pleasure, or to health? In general, if all of us who use these words know each of them well enough and need take no trouble over making the notions distinct, why do we differ? Why do we quarrel? Why do we blame one another?

And why am I bringing up this quarrelling with each other at all? If you fit your own preconceptions properly, why are you unhappy? Why are you blocked?

Let us leave aside for the moment the second topic, about impulses and the study of the duties belonging to them. Let us leave aside the third as well, about assent. I make you a present of those two. Let us stay with the first, which offers an almost obvious proof that we do not fit our preconceptions properly.

Do you now desire what is possible, and possible for you? Then why are you blocked? Why are you unhappy? Do you not now try to avoid the unavoidable? Then why do you fall into anything you would avoid? Why are you unlucky? Why, when you desire a thing, does it not happen; and when you do not desire it, does it happen? This is the greatest proof of unhappiness and misery: I wish for something and it does not happen. What is more wretched than I am?

It was because she could not bear this that Medea came to murder her children — an act of a great spirit, at least in this respect: she had a correct view of what it is for a thing one wishes for not to come off. And then she says: so I shall be avenged on the man who wronged and insulted me. And what do I gain if he is punished so? How is it to be done? I will kill my children — but I shall be punishing myself as well. And what do I care?

This is the wandering of a soul with great energy in it. She did not know where the power of doing what we wish actually lies — that you cannot get it from outside, nor by rearranging things and refitting them. Do not desire the man, and nothing you desire will fail to happen. Do not obstinately desire that he live with you. Do not desire to stay in Corinth. In a word, desire nothing but what God wills. And who will block you then? Who will compel you? No man will compel you, any more than he will compel Zeus.

When you have such a guide, and your wishes and desires are the same as his, why do you still fear disappointment? Hand your desire over to wealth and your avoidance to poverty, and you will be disappointed of the one and fall into the other. Hand them over to health, and you will come off badly. Hand them over to public office, honours, country, friends, children — in a word to anything not in a man's power — and you will come off badly.

But hand them over to Zeus and the rest of the gods. Surrender them to the gods; let the gods govern; let your desire and your avoidance be ranged on the gods' side — and where will you be unhappy any longer?

But if, you lazy wretch, you envy and complain and are jealous and afraid, and never let a single day pass without complaining both of yourself and of the gods, why do you still talk of being educated? What kind of education, man? Do you mean that you have busied yourself with shifting syllogisms? Will you not, if it is possible, unlearn all that and begin at the beginning — and see at the same time that so far you have not even touched the matter? And then, starting from that foundation, will you not build up everything that comes after, so that nothing happens that you do not choose, and nothing fails to happen that you do?

Give me one young man who has come to the school with this intention, who has become a champion for this cause and says: I give up everything else, and it is enough for me if it ever comes into my power to pass my life unblocked and untroubled, to hold out my neck to everything like a free man, and to look up to heaven as a friend of God and fear nothing that can happen.

Let any of you point such a man out to me, so that I may say: come, young man, into possession of what is your own. It is your destiny to be an ornament to philosophy. Yours are these possessions, yours these books, yours these discourses.

And then, when he has laboured enough and trained himself in that part of the matter, let him come to me again and say: I want to be free of passion and free of disturbance; and I want, as a devout man and a philosopher and a careful one, to know what my duty is to the gods, to my parents, to my brothers, to my country, to strangers. Come to the second matter as well, I shall say; this too is yours.

"But I have studied the second part enough now, and I would gladly be secure and unshaken — not only awake, but asleep too, and when I am full of wine, and when I am low."

Man, you are a god. Your designs are great.

No: "what I want is to understand what Chrysippus says in his treatise on the Liar." Will you not go and hang yourself, wretch, with an intention like that? And what good will it do you? You will read the whole thing in sorrow, and speak about it to others trembling.

And this is just what you do. "Would you like me to read to you, brother, and you to me?" You write excellently, my man. And you, excellently, in the style of Xenophon; and you in Plato's style; and you in the style of Antisthenes. And then, having told each other your dreams, you go back to exactly the same things: the same desires, the same avoidances, the same pursuits, the same plans and purposes; you want the same things and work for the same things.

And after that you do not even look for anybody to give you advice — you are annoyed if you hear the sort of thing I am saying. Then you say: "A bad-tempered old fellow. When I was leaving he did not weep, and he did not say, 'What danger you are going into! If you come through safe, my child, I will light lamps for it.' That is what a kindly man would do."

It will be a great thing indeed if you do come back safe, and well worth lighting lamps over — for such a person as you. You ought to be immortal, of course, and exempt from disease.

So, as I say, throwing away this conceit of thinking we know something useful, we must come to philosophy as we come to geometry and to music. Otherwise we shall not so much as approach making progress, though we read every collection and commentary of Chrysippus, and Antipater's and Archedemus' as well.
