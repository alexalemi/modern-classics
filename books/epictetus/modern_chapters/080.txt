Chapter 22: On the Cynic Life

(Part 2 of 2)

The young man asked: if a Cynic falls ill and a friend asks him to come to his house and be looked after, should the Cynic accept the invitation?

And where, I ask you, are you going to find a friend of a Cynic? The man who invites him would have to be another such man himself, if he is to be worth counting as a Cynic's friend. He would have to share the Cynic's staff and his kingship, and be a worthy servant of it, if he means to be thought worthy of a Cynic's friendship — as Diogenes was the friend of Antisthenes, and Crates of Diogenes.

Do you think that a man who comes up to a Cynic and greets him is the Cynic's friend, and that the Cynic will judge him fit to have a Cynic in his house? So think about this too, if you please: look round instead for some convenient dunghill where you can have your fever, sheltered from the north wind so that you do not get chilled. But it looks to me as though you want to get into somebody's house and be well fed there for a while. Why are you thinking of attempting so great a thing at all?

"But," said the young man, "should a Cynic take on marriage and the raising of children as a chief duty?"

Give me a community of wise men, Epictetus replied, and perhaps no man will readily take up the Cynic practice at all. On whose account would he take up that way of life? But suppose he does; then nothing will stop him marrying and having children, for his wife will be another like himself, and his father-in-law another like himself, and his children will be brought up like him.

But in the present state of things, which is like an army drawn up in battle order, is it not right that the Cynic should be free of distraction and engaged only in the service of God — able to go about among men, not tied down to the ordinary duties of mankind, not entangled in the ordinary relations of life? Because if he neglects those relations he will not keep the character of an honourable and good man, and if he observes them he will lose the character of the messenger and spy and herald of God.

Consider: he has a duty to his father-in-law, and something to the rest of his wife's family, and something to his wife too, if he has one. And being a Cynic shuts him out from nursing his own family in sickness and from providing for their keep. To say nothing of the rest, he must have a vessel for heating water for the baby so that he can wash it in the bath, and wool for his wife when she has given birth, and oil, a bed, a cup — and so the furniture of the house grows. I say nothing of his other occupations and his distractions.

Where is that king now, the man who gives himself to the public interest,

	the guardian of the people, weighed down with cares,
	— Homer, Iliad ii 25

whose duty it is to look after other men — the married ones and the ones with children; to see who treats his wife well and who badly; who quarrels; which household is well run and which is not — going about as a doctor does and feeling pulses? He says to one, you have a fever; to another, you have a headache, or gout. He tells one to go without food, another to eat, another to keep out of the baths; and another that he needs the knife or the cautery.

How is a man tied to the duties of ordinary life to have time for this? Is it not his duty to supply his children with clothing, and send them to the schoolmaster with writing tablets and pens? And must he not supply them with beds as well? They cannot be full-grown Cynics the moment they are born. If he is not going to do these things, it would be better to expose the children as soon as they are born than to kill them this way.

See what we are bringing the Cynic down to, and how we are taking his kingship away from him. "Yes, but Crates took a wife." You are talking about a case that arose out of love, and about a woman who was another Crates. We are enquiring about ordinary marriages, free of complications; and making that enquiry, we do not find marriage in this state of the world a thing especially suited to the Cynic.

How is society to keep going, then? In God's name, which men do society more good — the ones who bring two or three squalling children into the world to take their own places, or the ones who watch over the whole of mankind as far as they can, and see what men do, how they live, what they attend to and what they neglect against their duty?

Did the Thebans who left small children behind do them more good than Epaminondas, who died childless? Did Priam, who fathered fifty worthless sons, or Danaus, or Aeolus, contribute more to the community than Homer?

Well then: shall a general's duty, or a writer's work, excuse a man from marriage and from having children, and shall such a man not be judged to have taken on childlessness for nothing — and shall the kingship of a Cynic not be counted as worth the want of children? Do we not see his grandeur? Do we not rightly contemplate the character of Diogenes? And do we instead turn our eyes to the Cynics of today, dogs waiting at tables, who imitate the old Cynics in nothing at all except perhaps in breaking wind?

Otherwise such matters would not have troubled us in the least, and we should not have wondered at a Cynic not marrying and not having children. Man, the Cynic is father of all men. The men are his sons and the women his daughters; that is how carefully he visits them all, that is how well he cares for them all. Do you suppose it is out of idle impertinence that he rebukes the people he meets? He does it as a father, as a brother, and as the servant of the father of all — the servant of Zeus.

Ask me too, if you like, whether a Cynic shall take part in the government of the state. Fool, do you look for a greater form of government than the one he is already engaged in? Do you ask whether he shall appear before the Athenians and say something about revenue and supply — a man who has to talk to all men alike, to Athenians, to Corinthians, to Romans, and not about supply or revenue or peace or war, but about happiness and unhappiness, good fortune and bad, slavery and freedom? When a man has taken on the government of such a state as that, do you ask me whether he shall go into government?

Ask me too whether he shall hold office. Again I shall say to you: fool, what greater office shall he hold than the one he holds now?

Such a man also needs a certain bodily condition. If he looks consumptive, thin and pale, his testimony does not carry the same weight. He must not only prove to ordinary people, by showing the qualities of his soul, that it is possible to be a good man independently of the things they admire; he must also show by his body that a simple, frugal life in the open air does not damage even the body. "Look," he says, "I am the proof of it, and so is my body."

That is what Diogenes did: he used to go about looking fresh, and he caught the attention of the crowd by his very appearance. But if a Cynic is an object of pity he looks like a beggar; everybody turns away from him, everybody is offended by him. He ought not to look dirty either, or he will drive men off that way too; his very roughness ought to be clean and attractive.

A Cynic ought also to have a great deal of natural grace and quickness — and if he does not, he is a stupid fellow and nothing more. He needs these qualities so as to be able to meet any circumstance that arises readily and aptly.

So Diogenes replied to a man who said, "Are you the Diogenes who does not believe there are gods?" And how can that be, said Diogenes, when I think that you are hateful to them?

And on another occasion, when Alexander stood over him while he was asleep and quoted Homer's line,

	A man who is a councillor should not sleep all night,

he answered, still half asleep,

	the guardian of the people, weighed down with cares.

But before everything, the Cynic's governing part must be purer than the sun; and if it is not, he must be a cunning rogue and a man of no principle, since he will be reproving others while he is tangled up in some vice himself.

See how the matter stands. Kings and tyrants have their guards and their weapons, and these give them the power of reproving certain men and even of punishing wrongdoers, bad as they are themselves. But for a Cynic, what gives that power, instead of weapons and guards, is his conscience.

When he knows that he has kept watch and laboured for mankind; that he has slept pure and that sleep has left him purer still; that whatever he thought, he thought as a friend of the gods, as their servant, as a sharer in the power of Zeus; and that on every occasion he is ready to say

	Lead me, Zeus, and you too, Destiny,

and also, if it so pleases the gods, so let it be — then why should he not have the confidence to speak freely to his own brothers, to his children, in a word to his own family?

That is why a man in this state of mind is neither over-inquisitive nor a busybody. He is not meddling with other men's affairs when he is watching over human affairs; he is looking after his own. Otherwise you might as well say that a general is a busybody when he inspects his soldiers and examines them and watches them and punishes the disorderly.

But if you rebuke other people while you have a cake under your arm, I shall say to you: will you not rather go off into a corner and eat what you have stolen? What have you to do with other men's affairs? Who are you? Are you the bull of the herd, or the queen of the bees? Show me the marks of your supremacy, such as they have from nature. And if you are a drone claiming sovereignty over the bees, do you not suppose your fellow citizens will put you down as the bees put down the drones?

The Cynic must also have such power of endurance that ordinary people take him for a stone with no feeling in it. Nobody insults him, nobody strikes him, nobody offends him: he gives his body over for any man who likes to do what he likes with it. For he keeps in mind that the inferior must be overpowered by the superior in whatever it is inferior in, and the body is inferior to the many, the weaker to the stronger. So he never goes down into a contest where he can be overpowered. He gives up at once what belongs to others; he lays no claim to what is servile.

But where will and the use of impressions are concerned, there you will see how many eyes he has — so many that you would call Argus blind beside him. Is his assent ever hasty? Is his impulse ever rash? Does his desire ever fail of its object? Does what he would avoid ever come upon him? Is his purpose ever unfulfilled? Does he ever find fault, is he ever humiliated, is he ever envious? To these he gives all his attention and energy; and as to everything else, he lies on his back and snores. All is peace. There is no robber who can take away his will, and no tyrant.

But what about his body? There is. And his property? There is. And offices and honours? What does he care about them? So when anybody tries to frighten him with these, he says: be off, look for children — masks are frightening to them; I know they are made of shell and there is nothing inside.

This is the sort of matter you are deliberating about. So, if you please, I urge you in God's name: put the thing off, and first consider whether you are prepared for it. Look at what Hector says to Andromache: go back into the house, he says, and weave —

	war is the work of men,
	of all men, but of me above all.
	— Iliad vi 490.

So he was conscious of what he was fit for, and knew what she was not.
