Chapter 14: To Naso

A certain Roman came in with his son and listened to one reading. Epictetus said: This is the method of instruction — and stopped. When the Roman asked him to go on, he said:

Every art, while it is being taught, is laborious to a man who does not know it and has no skill in it. The things that come out of the arts show their use at once, in the purpose they were made for, and most of them have something attractive and pleasing about them. It is no pleasure to stand and watch a shoemaker learning his trade; but the shoe is useful, and not unpleasant to look at either. Watching a smith at his training is very disagreeable to a bystander who knows nothing of the art; but the work shows what the art is for. And you will see this much more clearly with music: if you are present while somebody is learning, the drill will seem thoroughly disagreeable, and yet the results of music are pleasant and delightful even to people who know nothing about it.

Here too we conceive the work of a philosopher to be something of this kind: he must fit his wishes to what happens, so that nothing that does happen happens against our wish, and nothing that fails to happen fails when we wish it would. And the result, for men who have arranged the work of philosophy in this way, is not to fail in their desires, and not to fall into what they would avoid; to pass through life themselves without unease, without fear, without being thrown about — and along with their companions to keep up every relation, natural and acquired: son, father, brother, citizen, man, wife, neighbour, fellow traveller, ruler, subject.

This is roughly what we conceive the work of a philosopher to be. It remains to enquire how it is to be accomplished.

Now we see that a carpenter becomes a carpenter by learning certain things, and a pilot becomes a pilot by learning certain things. Is it not likely, then, that in philosophy too it is not enough simply to wish to be wise and good, and that there are certain things one must learn? So we enquire what those things are.

The philosophers say we ought to learn first that God exists and provides for everything, and that it is not possible to hide our acts from him — nor even our intentions and thoughts. The next thing is to learn what the nature of the gods is; because whatever they are found to be, a man who wants to please them and obey them must try with all his power to be like them. If the divine is trustworthy, a man must be trustworthy. If it is free, a man must be free; if generous, generous; if great in spirit, great in spirit. So as an imitator of God he must do and say everything consistently with that.

Where must we begin, then? If you will go into the discussion, I will tell you: you must first understand words.

"So you are saying I do not understand words now."

You do not understand them.

"Then how do I use them?"

The way the illiterate use writing, the way cattle use impressions. Using and understanding are two different things. But if you think you understand them, produce any word you please and let us test whether we understand it.

"It is a disagreeable thing for a man to be refuted when he is old, and has perhaps served his three campaigns."

I know it. You have come to me as a man in want of nothing — and what could you even imagine yourself to be short of? You are rich; you have children, and perhaps a wife, and many slaves; Caesar knows you; you have many friends at Rome; you pay everybody their dues; you know how to repay a man who does you a favour and to pay back in kind a man who does you a wrong. What are you lacking?

Now suppose I show you that you lack the most necessary things of all, the chief things for happiness; that so far you have attended to everything except what you ought; and, to crown it, that you know neither what God is nor what man is, nor what good is nor what bad. If I say you are ignorant of the other matters, that might perhaps be borne. But if I say that you know nothing about yourself — how could you possibly bear with me and stand the proof and stay here? It is not possible; you go off at once in a bad temper.

And yet what harm have I done you? Unless the mirror harms an ugly man too, by showing him himself as he is. Unless the doctor insults the sick man when he tells him: man, do you think there is nothing wrong with you? You have a fever. Go without food today and drink water. Nobody says, "What an insult!" But say to a man, "Your desires are inflamed, your avoidances are low, your intentions are inconsistent, your impulses are not in line with nature, your judgments are rash and false" — and off he goes at once saying, "He insulted me."

Our way of doing things is like a crowded fair. Cattle and oxen are brought to be sold, and most of the men come to buy and sell; and there are a few who come to look at the market and enquire how it is run, and why, and who fixed the day of the meeting and for what purpose.

So it is in this assembly of ours too. Some, like cattle, trouble themselves about nothing but their fodder. To all of you who are busy about property and land and slaves and public office, these things are nothing but fodder.

But there are a few who attend the fair as men who love to look on and consider: what is the world? Who governs it? Has it no governor? How is it possible that a city or a household cannot last even the shortest time without an administrator and a guardian, and that a system so great and so beautiful should be run in such good order and yet without purpose, by chance? So there is an administrator. What kind, and how does he govern? And who are we, whom he produced, and what for? Have we some connection with him and some relation to him, or none?

That is how these few are affected; and then they apply themselves to this one thing only, to examine the meeting, and then to go away.

And what of it? They are laughed at by the crowd, as the sightseers at a fair are laughed at by the traders. And if the cattle had any understanding, they would laugh at anybody who admired anything but fodder.
