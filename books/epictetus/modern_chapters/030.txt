Chapter 29: On Steadiness

The nature of the good is a certain kind of will; the nature of the bad is a certain kind of will. What are externals, then? Materials for the will, which the will works on and by which it gets its own good or evil. How does it get the good? By not overvaluing the materials. Judgments about the materials, if they are right, make the will good; twisted and distorted judgments make it bad.

God has laid down this law, and says: if you want anything good, get it from yourself. You say: no, I will have it from somebody else. Do not. Get it from yourself.

So when the tyrant threatens me and summons me, I say: whom are you threatening? If he says, "I will put you in chains," I say, you are threatening my hands and my feet. If he says, "I will cut off your head," I say, you are threatening my head. If he says, "I will throw you into prison," I say, you are threatening the whole of this poor body. And if he threatens exile, I say the same.

Is he not threatening you at all, then? If I feel that none of these things concerns me, he is not threatening me at all. If I fear any of them, then it is me he is threatening. Whom do I fear, then? The master of what? The master of things in my own power? There is no such master. Do I fear the master of things not in my power? And what are they to me?

"So do you philosophers teach us to despise kings?"

I hope not. Which of us teaches you to claim against them the power over things they actually possess? Take my poor body, take my property, take my reputation, take the people around me. If I advise anybody to claim these things, let him accuse me and welcome.

"Yes, but I intend to command your judgments as well."

And who gave you that power? How are you going to conquer another man's judgment? "By putting terror on it," he says. "That will conquer it." Do you not know that a judgment is conquered by itself and not by anything else? Nothing can conquer the will except the will itself.

This is why the law of God is the most powerful and the most just: let the stronger always prevail over the weaker. Ten are stronger than one. For what? For chaining, for killing, for dragging a man where they please, for taking away what he has. So the ten conquer the one in the thing they are stronger in. And where are the ten weaker? Where the one has right judgments and they have not. Can the ten conquer in that? How could they? If we were put in the scales, must not the heavier pull its own scale down?

"How strange, then, that Socrates should have been treated so by the Athenians."

Slave, why do you say Socrates? Say the thing as it is: how strange that the poor body of Socrates should have been seized and dragged off to prison by stronger men; that somebody should have given hemlock to the poor body of Socrates, and that it should breathe its life out. Do these things seem strange? Do they seem unjust? Do you blame God for them? Had Socrates nothing to set against them? Where did the nature of good lie, for him? Whom shall we listen to, you or him? And what does Socrates say? Anytus and Meletus can kill me, but they cannot harm me. And further: if it so pleases God, so let it be.

Show me the man with the inferior principles overpowering the man whose principles are superior. You will never show it, or come anywhere near showing it. This is the law of nature and of God: the superior always overpowers the inferior. In what? In the thing it is superior in. One body is stronger than another. Many are stronger than one. A thief is stronger than a man who is not a thief. That is why I lost my lamp too: the thief was better than me at staying awake. But he bought the lamp at a price — for a lamp he became a thief, a man who cannot be trusted, a wild animal. It looked like a good bargain to him. Very well.

But suppose a man has taken hold of my cloak and is dragging me to the marketplace, and the others start shouting: "Philosopher, what good have your judgments done you? Look, you are being dragged to prison, you are going to have your head cut off."

And what introduction to philosophy could I have put together so that if a stronger man took hold of my cloak I should not be dragged off, and if ten men laid hold of me and threw me into prison I should not be thrown in? Have I learned nothing else, then? I have learned to see that everything which happens, if it is outside my will, is nothing to me. Have you not gained by that? Then why do you look for advantage anywhere but where you have learned advantage lies?

And then, sitting in prison, I say: the man who shouts like that neither hears what words mean nor understands what is said, and does not care in the least to know what philosophers say or what they do. Leave him alone.

But now he says to the prisoner: come out of your prison. If you have no further need of me in prison, I come out. If you should need me there again, I will go back in.

"How long will you go on like this?"

As long as reason requires me to stay with the body. When reason does not require it, take the body away, and goodbye to you. Only we must not do it thoughtlessly, or weakly, or on some slight excuse — because on the other side, God does not want it done, and He has need of a world like this and inhabitants like these in it. But if He sounds the retreat, as He did to Socrates, we must obey the one who gives the signal, as we would a general.

Well then — ought we to say such things to the crowd? Why should we? Is it not enough for a man to be convinced himself? When children come clapping their hands and shouting, "Today is the good Saturnalia," do we say to them, "The Saturnalia are not good"? Not at all: we clap along with them. So you too, when you cannot make a man change his mind, be sure that he is a child, and clap along with him; and if you would rather not do that, keep quiet.

A man must keep this in mind, and when he is called to any such difficulty he should know that the moment has come to show whether he has been taught anything. A man who has come into a difficulty is like a young man fresh from school who has practised resolving syllogisms: if somebody sets him an easy one he says, set me a properly complicated syllogism instead, so that I can exercise myself. Even athletes are dissatisfied with slight young opponents — "he cannot lift me." There is a young man of noble disposition.

But you do not behave so. When the moment of trial comes, one of you has to weep and say, "I wish I had learned more." More of what? If you did not learn these things in order to show them in practice, why did you learn them?

I think there is somebody among you sitting here who is in labour like a woman, saying to himself: I only wish a difficulty would come to me like the one that has come to this man. Am I to waste my life in a corner when I might be crowned at Olympia? When will anyone announce such a contest for me? That is how every one of you ought to be disposed.

Even among Caesar's gladiators there are men who complain bitterly that they are not brought forward and matched, and who pray to God and go to their trainers begging to be allowed to fight. And will none of you show himself such a man? I would gladly take a voyage to Rome to see what my athlete is doing, and how he is studying his subject.

"I do not want a subject of this kind," he says.

Why — is it in your power to take whatever subject you choose? You have been given the body you have, the parents you have, the brothers, the country, the position in your country. And then you come to me and say, change my subject. Have you not the abilities to manage the subject you were given? What you ought to say is: it is your business to set the subject, and mine to work at it well. But you do not say that. You say: do not set me that mode of argument, set me this one; do not press that objection on me, press this one.

There will come a time, perhaps, when tragic actors think that they are nothing but masks and high boots and a long robe. Man, these things are your material and your subject. Say something, so that we can tell whether you are a tragic actor or a clown — because everything else you both have in common. If somebody takes away the tragic actor's boots and his mask and brings him on stage as a mere shadow of himself, is the tragic actor lost, or is he still there? If he has a voice, he is still there.

Here is another example. "Take the governorship of a province." I take it, and having taken it, I show how a man who has been taught behaves. "Now put off the senator's stripe, put on rags, and come forward in that character." And what of it? Have I lost the power of showing a good voice?

How do you appear now, then? As a witness summoned by God. "Come forward, you, and give evidence for me — you are worthy to be brought forward by me as a witness. Is anything outside the will good or bad? Do I harm any man? Have I made each man's interest depend on anybody but himself? What evidence do you give for God?"

I am in a wretched state, Lord, and I am unlucky. Nobody cares about me, nobody gives me anything, everybody blames me, everybody speaks ill of me.

Is this the evidence you mean to give? Will you disgrace the summons of the one who did you such honour, and thought you worthy to be called to give such testimony?

But suppose the man with the power has pronounced: "I judge you to be irreverent and unholy." What has happened to you? I have been judged irreverent and unholy. Nothing else? Nothing else.

Now if the same man had passed judgment on a hypothetical syllogism and declared, "the conclusion that if it is day it is light — I pronounce that false," what has happened to the syllogism? Who is being judged here? Who has been condemned — the syllogism, or the man who was deceived about it?

Does the man who has power to make a declaration about you know what is holy and unholy? Has he studied it? Has he learned it? Where? From whom? A musician pays no attention to a man who declares that the lowest string of the lyre is the highest, and neither does a geometer if the man declares that the lines from the centre of a circle to the circumference are unequal. And is a man who has really been taught to pay attention to an untaught man passing judgment on what is holy and unholy, just and unjust? What a signal wrong the educated do. Was this what they learned here?

Will you not leave these little arguments about such matters to other people — to lazy fellows, so that they can sit in a corner and collect their miserable fees, or grumble that nobody gives them anything — and come forward and use what you have learned? It is not little arguments that are wanted now: the writings of the Stoics are full of them. What is wanted, then? A man who will apply them; a man who by his acts will bear witness to his words. Take on that character, I beg you, so that we need no longer use the ancients as our examples in the schools, but may have an example of our own.

And whose business is it to think about these matters? The man who has leisure — for man is a creature that loves contemplation. But it is shameful to contemplate them the way runaway slaves do. We should sit as we sit in a theatre, undistracted, listening now to the tragic actor and now to the lute player, and not behave as slaves do. The slave takes his place and praises the actor, and at the same time looks over his shoulder; and then if somebody calls out his master's name he is frightened and thrown at once. It is shameful for philosophers to contemplate the works of nature like that.

For what is a master? A man is not another man's master. Death is, and life, and pleasure and pain. Bring Caesar to me without these things and you will see how steady I am. But when he comes with them, thundering and lightening, and I am afraid of them — what am I doing then but recognising my master, like the runaway slave? So long as I have any respite from these terrors I stand as the runaway slave stands in the theatre: I bathe, I drink, I sing — and I do all of it in terror and unease. But if I once free myself from my masters — that is, from the things that make masters formidable — what trouble have I left? What master have I now?

Well then: ought we to announce all this to everybody? No. We ought to accommodate ourselves to the untaught and say: this man is recommending to me what he thinks good for himself. I excuse him.

Socrates excused the jailer too, the man who had charge of him in prison and wept when Socrates was about to drink the poison. How generously he grieves for us, he said. Does he say to the jailer that this is why we sent the women away? No: he says that to his friends, who were able to take it in. The jailer he treats as a child.
