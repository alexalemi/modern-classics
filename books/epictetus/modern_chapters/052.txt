Chapter 21: On Inconsistency

There are some things men admit readily and others they do not. Nobody will admit that he is a fool or that he lacks understanding; quite the contrary — you will hear every man saying, "I wish my luck were equal to my understanding."

But men readily admit that they are timid. "I am rather timid, I confess; but in other respects you will not find me a fool." A man will not readily admit that he lacks self-control, and that he is unjust he will not admit at all. He will certainly not admit that he is envious or a busybody. Most men will admit that they are soft-hearted.

What is the reason? The chief thing is inconsistency and confusion about good and evil. But different men have different reasons, and in general they will not admit at all what they take to be shameful. They suppose timidity is a mark of a good disposition, and so is pity; but stupidity they take to be the absolute mark of a slave. And they will not admit at all the faults that are offences against society.

With most errors, the reason they are brought to admit them is that they imagine there is something involuntary in them, as with timidity and pity. And if a man does admit to a lack of self-control, he pleads love as his excuse, as though it were involuntary. But men do not imagine injustice to be involuntary at all. In jealousy too, they suppose, there is something involuntary, and so they admit to jealousy as well.

Living among men as confused as this, then — so ignorant of what they are saying, and of what evils they have and have not, and where they came from and how they are to be rid of them — I think it worth a man's trouble to keep watch on himself constantly and ask: am I one of them too? What do I imagine about myself? How am I conducting myself? Am I conducting myself as a sensible man? As a self-controlled man? Do I ever say this — that I have been taught to be ready for whatever may happen?

Have I the consciousness a man who knows nothing ought to have, that I know nothing? Do I go to my teacher as men go to an oracle, prepared to obey? Or do I go to school like a snivelling boy, to learn some history and understand books I did not understand before, and, if it turns out that way, to explain them to other people too?

Man, you have had a fight at home with a wretched slave, you have turned the household upside down, you have frightened the neighbours — and then you come to me as though you were a wise man, and take your seat and pass judgment on how I explained some word, and on how I babbled whatever came into my head.

You come full of envy and humbled, because you bring nothing from home; and you sit through the discussion thinking about nothing but how your father and your brother feel about you. "What are they saying about me back there? They think I am doing well now; they are saying he will come back knowing everything. I wish I could learn it all before I go back — but that takes a lot of work, and nobody sends me anything, and the baths at Nicopolis are filthy. Everything is bad at home and bad here."

And then they say nobody gets any profit from the school. Why — who comes to the school? Who comes for the purpose of being improved? Who comes to hand over his judgments to be cleaned? Who comes to learn what he lacks?

Why be surprised, then, if you carry away from the school exactly what you brought into it? You do not come to lay your principles aside, or correct them, or take on others in their place. Nothing of the kind. What you look to instead is whether you already have what you came for.

You want to prattle about theorems? Well then — do you not become a greater trifler? Do your little theorems not give you some opportunity for showing off? You solve trick syllogisms. Do you not examine the premises of the syllogism called the Liar? Do you not examine hypothetical syllogisms? Then why are you still annoyed, if you get the things you came to the school for?

"Yes — but if my child dies, or my brother, or if I have to die or be racked, what good will these things do me?"

Well, is that what you came for? Is that what you sit beside me for? Did you ever light your lamp or stay awake for that? Or, when you went out to the walking-place, did you ever put up an impression you had been struck by instead of a syllogism, and go through it with your friends? Where? When?

And then you say theorems are useless. Useless to whom? To men who make bad use of them. Eye ointment is not useless to men who use it as they should and when they should. Hot compresses are not useless. Dumbbells are not useless — but they are useless to some men and useful to others.

If you ask me now whether syllogisms are useful, I shall say they are, and prove it if you like.

"And how are they going to be useful to me?"

Man, did you ask whether they are useful to you, or whether they are useful in general? Let a man with dysentery ask me whether vinegar is useful; I shall say it is. "Will it be useful to me, then?" I shall say: no. First get the discharge stopped and the ulcers closed.

And you, gentlemen — first cure your ulcers and stop your discharge. Get your mind quiet, bring it into the school free of distraction, and then you will know what power reason has.
