Chapter 21: Against Those Who Rush to Set Up as Teachers

Men who have taken in bare theorems want to bring them straight up again, as people with a bad stomach do with food. Digest the thing first, and do not vomit it up like that. If you do not digest it, the thing really does become an emetic — raw food, unfit to eat.

But after you have digested it, show us some change in your governing part, as athletes show in their shoulders what they have trained at and what they have eaten, and as men who have taken up a trade show what they have learned. The carpenter does not come along and say, "Hear me talk about carpentry." He takes on the building of a house, builds it, and proves that he knows the art.

You ought to do something of the same kind. Eat like a man, drink like a man, dress, marry, have children, do a citizen's duty, put up with being abused, bear with an unreasonable brother, bear with your father, your son, your neighbour, your companion. Show us these things, so that we can see you have really learned something from the philosophers.

"No — come and hear me read my commentaries."

Go away and find somebody else to be sick over.

"But I will expound the writings of Chrysippus to you as no other man can. I will make his text perfectly clear. I will add the force of Antipater and Archedemus too, if I can."

Is it for this that young men are to leave their country and their parents — to come here and hear you explain words? Ought they not to go home able to endure, active in dealing with other people, free of passions, free of being thrown about, with such provisions for the journey of life that they will be able to bear well whatever happens and take honour from it? And how are you to give them any of that, when you do not have it yourself?

Have you done anything at all, from the beginning, except busy yourself with resolving syllogisms and shifting arguments and the ones that work by questioning?

"But so-and-so has a school. Why should I not have one too?"

These things are not done carelessly, man, or just as it happens. There has to be a suitable age, and a life, and God as a guide. You say no. But no man sails out of harbour without having sacrificed to the gods and asked their help; nobody sows without having called on Demeter. And is a man who has taken on so great a work going to undertake it safely without the gods? Are men who take this work on going to come to it successfully?

What are you doing, man, but giving away the mysteries? You say: "There is a temple at Eleusis, and there is one here too. There is a hierophant at Eleusis, and I will make a hierophant. There is a herald, and I will appoint a herald. There is a torchbearer at Eleusis, and I will appoint a torchbearer. There are torches at Eleusis, and I will have torches here. The words are the same. How do the things done here differ from the things done there?"

Most irreverent of men, is there no difference? Those things are done in the right place and at the right time, accompanied by sacrifice and prayers, when a man has first been purified and has brought his mind to the thought that he is about to approach sacred rites, and ancient ones. That is how the mysteries are useful; that is how we come to the notion that all these things were established by the ancients for the instruction and correction of life.

But you publish them and give them away at the wrong time, in the wrong place, without sacrifices, without purity. You do not have the robes the hierophant ought to have, nor the hair, nor the headdress, nor the voice, nor the age; and you have not purified yourself as he has. You have only learned the words by heart, and you say: the words are sacred in themselves.

You ought to come at these matters another way. The thing is great, it is a mystery, it is not a common thing and it is not given to every man. Not even wisdom is perhaps enough to let a man take charge of young men: he needs a certain readiness and fitness for it as well, and a certain bodily quality — and above all he needs God to advise him to take the office, as God advised Socrates to take the place of a man who refutes error, and Diogenes the office of kingship and reproof, and another man the office of teaching precepts.

But you open a doctor's shop with nothing in it but drugs, and you do not know where or how they are to be applied, and you have taken no trouble to find out. "Look," the man says, "I have ointments for the eyes too." And have you the power of using them? Do you know when and how they will do good, and whom they will do good to?

Why do you take chances in matters of the greatest importance, then? Why are you careless? Why do you take on a thing that in no way suits you? Leave it to men who can do it, and do it well. Do not bring disgrace on philosophy by your own acts, and do not be one of the men who give it a bad name.

If theorems please you, sit still and turn them over on your own — but never say you are a philosopher, and do not let anybody else say it. Say instead: he is mistaken; my desires are no different from what they were before, my activity is not directed at other objects, I do not assent to other things, and in the use of impressions I have not changed at all from what I was.

This is what you must think and say about yourself, if you want to think as you ought. And if not, take your chances and go on doing what you are doing — it suits you.
