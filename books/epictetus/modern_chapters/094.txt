Chapter 7: On Being Free from Fear

What makes a tyrant formidable? The guards, you say, and their swords, and the men of the bedchamber, and the men who keep out those who want to come in.

Then why is a child not afraid when you bring him to the tyrant while the guards are there? Is it because the child does not understand these things? And if a man does understand what guards are, and that they have swords, and comes to the tyrant precisely because he wants to die on account of some circumstance and is looking for an easy death at another man's hand — is he afraid of the guards? No: because he wants the very thing that makes the guards formidable.

And if a man comes to the tyrant neither wanting to die nor wanting to live by all means, but only as it may be allowed him, what stops him coming without fear? Nothing.

And if a man has the same opinion about his property as the man I have described has about his body — and about his children too, and his wife; if he is in a word so affected by some madness or despair that he does not care whether he has them or not; if, as children playing with shells care about the game but not about the shells, he too has set no value on the materials but values the pleasure he gets from them and the occupation — then what tyrant is formidable to him, or what guards, or what swords?

So it is possible for a man to be disposed toward these things through madness, and for the Galileans to be so through habit; and is it not possible for anybody to learn from reason and demonstration that God made everything in the universe, and the universe itself, completely unblocked and complete, and made the parts of it for the use of the whole?

All the other animals are incapable of taking in how it is administered; but the rational animal, man, has the capacities for considering all this, and for understanding that he is a part, and what kind of part, and that it is right for the parts to be subordinate to the whole.

And besides this, being naturally noble and great-spirited and free, man sees that of the things around him some are unblocked and in his power and others are subject to blocking and in the power of others; that the unblocked ones are within the power of the will, and the ones subject to blocking are not.

And for this reason, if he thinks his good and his interest lie only in the things that are unblocked and in his own power, he will be free, prosperous, happy, unharmed, great in spirit, reverent, thankful to God for everything, never finding fault with any of the things that were not put in his power and never blaming them.

But if he thinks his good and his interest lie in externals and in things not within the power of his will, then he must necessarily be blocked and obstructed and a slave to whoever has power over the things he admires and fears; he must necessarily be irreverent, because he thinks God harms him; and he must be unjust, because he is always claiming more than belongs to him; and he must necessarily be abject and mean.

What stops a man who has clearly separated these things from living light-hearted and holding the reins easily, quietly expecting whatever can happen and enduring what has already happened?

Would you have me bear poverty? Come and see what poverty is when it has found somebody who can play the part of a poor man well. Would you have me hold power? Let me have power — and the trouble of it too. Exile? Wherever I go, it will be well with me there; because here, where I am, it was not the place that made it well with me but my judgments, and I shall carry those away with me. No man can take them from me; my judgments alone are mine and cannot be taken away, and I am satisfied as long as I have them, wherever I am and whatever I am doing.

"But now it is time to die."

Why do you say to die? Do not make a tragedy of the thing; say it as it is. It is now time for the matter to be resolved into the things it was put together from. And what is formidable in that? What is going to perish, of the things in the universe? What new or marvellous thing is going to happen?

Is this what makes a tyrant formidable? Is this why the guards seem to have swords that are large and sharp? Tell that to other people. I have thought all of this over; no man has power over me. I have been made free; I know his commands; nobody can lead me away as a slave now. I have a proper man to assert my freedom, and proper judges.

Are you not master of my body? What is that to me? Are you not master of my property? What is that to me? Are you not master of my exile and my chains? Well — from all of these, and from the poor body itself, I depart at your bidding, whenever you please. Try your power, and you will find out how far it reaches.

Whom can I still fear, then? The men in charge of the bedchamber? In case they do — what? Shut me out? If they find that I want to go in, let them shut me out.

Why do you go to the doors, then? Because I think it becomes me to join in the game while it lasts. Then how are you not shut out? Because unless somebody lets me in, I do not choose to go in; I am always content with what happens. I think what God chooses is better than what I choose. I will attach myself to him as a servant and a follower; I have the same impulses as he has, the same desires; in a word, I want the same things. There is no shutting out for me — only for the men who try to force their way in.

Why do I not force my way in, then? Because I know that nothing good is handed out inside to the men who go in. But when I hear a man called fortunate because Caesar has honoured him, I say: what does he happen to get? A province. Does he also get the judgment he ought to have? A prefecture. Does he also get the power of using the office well? Why should I still strive to get in?

A man scatters dried figs and nuts: the children snatch at them and fight one another over them; men do not, because they think it a small matter. But if a man throws shells about, not even the children snatch at them.

Provinces are handed out: let children see to that. Money is handed out: let children see to that. Praetorships and consulships are handed out: let children scramble for them, let them be shut out and beaten, let them kiss the hands of the giver and of his slaves. To me they are dried figs and nuts.

What then? If you fail to get one while Caesar is scattering them about, do not be troubled. If a dried fig falls into your lap, take it and eat it; a fig may be worth that much. But if I have to stoop down and turn another man over, or be turned over myself, and flatter the men who have got into the chamber, then a dried fig is not worth the trouble — nor is anything else among the things that are not good, which the philosophers have convinced me not to think good.

Show me the guards' swords. See how big they are, and how sharp. And what do these big sharp swords do? They kill. And what does a fever do? Nothing else. And a falling roof tile? Nothing else.

Would you have me marvel at these things and worship them and go about a slave to all of them? I hope that will not happen. Once I have learned that everything which has come into existence must also go out of it, so that the universe may not stand still or be obstructed, I no longer consider it makes any difference whether a fever does it, or a tile, or a soldier. And if a man must make a comparison between them, I know that the soldier will do it with less trouble to me, and quicker.

So when I neither fear anything a tyrant can do to me nor want anything he can give, why do I still look on at him in wonder? Why am I still confused? Why do I fear the guards? Why am I pleased if he speaks to me in a friendly way and receives me, and why do I go and tell other people how he spoke to me? Is he a Socrates, is he a Diogenes, that his praise should be proof of what I am? Have I been eager to imitate his character?

No — I keep up the game and go to him and serve him, so long as he does not order me to do anything foolish or unreasonable. But if he says to me, "Go and bring Leon of Salamis," I say: find somebody else, I am not playing any more.

"Take him away to prison." I follow; that is part of the game. "But your head will come off." Does the tyrant's head always stay where it is? And the heads of you who obey him? "But you will be thrown out unburied." If the corpse is me, then I shall be thrown out; but if I am something other than the corpse, then speak more accurately, as the fact is, and do not think of frightening me.

These things are formidable to children and to fools. But if a man has once entered a philosopher's school and does not know what he is, then he deserves to be full of fear and to flatter the men he used to flatter afterwards — if he has not yet learned that he is not flesh, or bones, or sinews, but the thing that uses these parts of the body and governs them and follows the impressions of things.

"Yes, but this talk makes us despise the laws."

And what sort of talk makes men who use it more obedient to the laws? The things in a fool's power are not law. And yet see how this talk disposes us as we ought to be even toward such men, since it teaches us to claim against them none of the things they are able to beat us at.

This talk teaches us to give up the body; to give up property as well; to withdraw from children, parents and brothers, and give up everything. It makes an exception only of our judgments, which Zeus himself has willed to be every man's own private property. What breaking of the laws is there in that? What folly?

Where you are better and stronger, I gave way to you. And on the other hand, where I am better, you give way to me — because I have taken care about this and you have not. Your study is to live in houses with floors of many-coloured stone, and how your slaves and dependents shall wait on you, and how you shall wear fine clothes and keep a lot of huntsmen and lute players and tragic actors. Do I lay claim to any of it?

Have you made any study of judgments, or of your own rational capacity? Do you know what parts it is made of, how they are put together, how they are connected, what powers it has and of what kind? Then why are you annoyed if another man, who has made it his study, has the advantage of you in it?

"But these things are the greatest things."

And who stops you being occupied with them and looking after them? And who has a better stock of books, of leisure, of people to help him?

Only turn your mind to these things at last; attend, if only for a short time, to your own governing part. Consider what this thing is that you possess, and where it came from — the thing that uses all the others and tests them and picks and rejects.

But so long as you busy yourself with externals, you will possess those as no other man does — and you will have this thing exactly as you choose to have it: dirty and neglected.
