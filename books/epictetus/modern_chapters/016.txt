Chapter 15: What Philosophy Promises

A man was consulting him about how to persuade his brother to stop being angry with him. Epictetus replied:

Philosophy does not undertake to secure any external thing for a man. If it did, it would be taking on something outside its own province. The carpenter's material is wood; the sculptor's is bronze; and the material of the art of living is each man's own life.

"What about my brother's life, then?"

That belongs to his art. As far as yours goes, it is one of the externals — like a piece of land, like health, like reputation. And philosophy promises none of those. In every circumstance, she says, I will keep the governing part — the mind's command centre — in line with nature. Whose governing part? The one belonging to the man I am in, she says.

"So how is my brother to stop being angry with me?"

Bring him to me and I will tell him. To you I have nothing to say about his anger.

The man consulting him said: "What I want to know is this. Even if my brother is not reconciled with me, how am I to keep myself in a state that is in line with nature?"

Nothing great, said Epictetus, is produced all at once — not even a grape or a fig. If you tell me now that you want a fig, I shall answer that it takes time. Let it flower first, then set fruit, then ripen. So the fruit of a fig tree is not brought to perfection suddenly, in a single hour, and you expect to have the fruit of a man's mind in so short a time and so easily? Do not expect it, even if I tell you that you can.
