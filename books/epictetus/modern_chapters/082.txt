Chapter 24: Why We Should Not Be Moved by Longing for What Is Not Up to Us

(Part 1 of 2)

Do not let what is against nature in another man be an evil to you. You were not made by nature to be depressed along with others, or unhappy along with them, but to be happy along with them.

If a man is unhappy, remember that his unhappiness is his own fault. God made all men to be happy and undisturbed, and gave them the means: certain things belonging to each as his own, and other things not as his own. The things subject to blocking and compulsion and loss are not a man's own; the things not subject to blocking are. And the nature of good and evil — as was fitting for one who takes care of us and protects us like a father — he made our own.

"But I have parted from somebody, and he is grieving."

Why did he count as his own what belongs to another? Why, when he looked at you and was glad, did he not also reckon that you are mortal, and that it is natural for you to leave him for another country? So he is suffering the consequences of his own folly.

And why do you bewail yourself, and what for? Because you also gave no thought to these things? Like worthless women, you enjoyed everything you took pleasure in as if you would enjoy it forever — places, and men, and conversation — and now you sit weeping because you do not see the same people and do not live in the same places.

Indeed you deserve it: to be more wretched than crows and ravens, which can fly where they please and change their nests and cross the seas without lamenting or pining for their old condition.

"Yes, but that is because they are creatures without reason."

Was reason given us by the gods, then, for the purpose of unhappiness and misery, so that we might spend our lives in wretchedness and lamentation? Must everybody be immortal, and nobody go abroad, and must we ourselves never travel but stay rooted like plants? And if one of our friends goes abroad, must we sit and weep, and when he comes back must we dance and clap our hands like children?

Shall we not wean ourselves at last and remember what we heard from the philosophers — if we did not listen to them as though they were street performers? They tell us that this world is one city, and that the substance it was formed out of is one; that there must be a certain cycle; that some things must give way to others, some be dissolved and others come in their place, some stay where they are and others be moved; and that everything is full of friendship — first of the gods, and then of men, who are made by nature to be one family. Some must be together and others must be separated, rejoicing in those who are with them and not grieving for those who have been taken away.

And a man, besides being naturally of a noble temper and having contempt for everything outside the power of his will, also has this property: not to be rooted, not to be naturally fixed to the earth, but to go at different times to different places — sometimes because some occasion presses, and sometimes simply for the sake of seeing.

That was how it was with Odysseus, who saw

	the cities of many men, and learned their ways.

And earlier still it fell to Hercules to visit the whole inhabited world,

	seeing men's lawless deeds and their good government,

throwing out the lawlessness and clearing it away and putting good government in its place. And how many friends do you suppose he had in Thebes, how many in Argos, how many in Athens? How many do you think he made as he went about? And he married too, when it seemed to him the right occasion, and had children, and left them without lamenting or pining or feeling he was leaving them orphans — because he knew that no man is an orphan; it is the father who takes care of all men, always and without a break.

It was not as mere hearsay that he had heard Zeus is the father of men: he thought Zeus was his own father, and called him so, and looked to him in everything he did. And so he was able to live happily wherever he was.

It is never possible for happiness and a longing for what is absent to come together. What is happy must have everything it desires; it must be like a man who has eaten his fill, with neither thirst nor hunger.

"But Odysseus felt a longing for his wife and wept as he sat on a rock."

Do you take Homer and his stories as authority in everything? And if Odysseus really wept, what was he but an unhappy man? And what good man is unhappy? In truth the whole is badly administered if Zeus does not take care of his own citizens so that they may be happy as he is. But it is neither lawful nor right to think such things. And if Odysseus did weep and lament, he was not a good man.

For who is good if he does not know who he is? And who knows what he is, if he forgets that the things that have been made are perishable, and that it is not possible for one human being to be with another always? To desire the impossible, then, is the mark of a slavish character, and it is foolish: it is the mark of a stranger, a man fighting against God in the only way he can, by his opinions.

"But my mother laments when she does not see me."

Why has she not learned these principles? And I do not say we should not take care that she does not lament; I say we ought not to want what is not our own, by every means.

Another man's grief is his own; my grief is my own. So I will stop my own grief by every means, because it is in my power; and another man's grief I will try to stop as far as I can, but I will not attempt it by every means. If I do, I shall be fighting against God, opposing Zeus, setting myself against him in the administration of the universe. And the penalty for this fighting against God and this disobedience will be paid not only by my children's children but by me — startled by dreams by night and by day, thrown about, trembling at every piece of news, with my peace of mind depending on other men's letters.

"Somebody has arrived from Rome. I only hope nothing has gone wrong." But what harm can come to you where you are not? "Somebody has come from Greece; I hope nothing has gone wrong." In this way every place on earth can be a source of misfortune to you. Is it not enough to be unfortunate where you are, without being unfortunate across the sea as well, and by letter? Is this what having your affairs in a state of security looks like?

"Well then, suppose my friends have died in places far from me."

What have they suffered but the condition of mortals? Or how can you want at the same time to live to old age and never see the death of anybody you love? Do you not know that over a long stretch of time many different things must happen — that a fever will carry off one man, a robber another, a tyrant a third? Such is the condition of things around us, and such are the people who live in the world with us. Cold and heat, unsuitable diets, journeys by land and voyages by sea, winds, all the various circumstances round us — these destroy one man, exile another, throw one into an embassy and another into an army.

So sit there in a flutter over all of it, lamenting, unhappy, unlucky, dependent on somebody else — and not on one or two, but on tens of thousands.

Is this what you heard when you were with the philosophers? Is this what you learned? Do you not know that human life is a campaign; that one man must stand watch, another go out as a scout, and a third fight? It is not possible for everybody to be in one place, and it would not be better if they were.

But you neglect to carry out the general's orders and complain when something harder than usual is put on you — and you do not notice what you are turning the army into, as far as it is in your power: if everybody copied you, nobody would dig a trench, nobody would throw up a rampart, nobody would keep watch or expose himself to danger, and everyone would turn out useless for the purposes of an army.

Again: if you go to sea as a sailor, keep to one spot and stick to it. If you are told to climb the mast, refuse; if to run to the bow, refuse. What ship's master will put up with you? Will he not throw you overboard as a useless thing, an obstruction and a bad example to the other sailors?

So it is here too. Every man's life is a kind of campaign, and it is a long one and full of variety. You must do a soldier's duty and do everything at the general's nod — guessing his wishes if possible, since there is no resemblance between that general and this one, either in strength or in quality of character.

You are placed in a great command, not in a mean position; you are always a senator. Do you not know that such a man must give little time to his household affairs and be often away from home — as a governor or as one governed, or discharging some office, or serving in war, or acting as a judge? And then do you tell me you want to be fixed to the same places and rooted, like a plant?

"Yes, because it is pleasant." Who says it is not? But soup is pleasant, and a handsome woman is pleasant. What else do the men who make pleasure their end say? Do you not see whose language you have been using — that it is the language of Epicureans and of men who have lost all shame about sex? And then, while you are doing what they do and holding their opinions, do you speak the words of Zeno and Socrates to us? Will you not throw away, as far as you can, the things belonging to other men that you decorate yourself with, when they do not fit you at all?

What else do such men want than to sleep without being blocked or compelled; and, having got up, to yawn at their leisure, and wash their faces, and then write and read what they like, and then talk about some trifle and be praised by their friends whatever they say; then to go out for a walk, and having walked a little to take a bath, and then eat, and then sleep — the kind of sleep such men have? Why need I say how? It is easy enough to guess.

Come — tell me your own way of passing the time, the one you want, you who are an admirer of truth and of Socrates and Diogenes. What do you want to do in Athens? The same as everybody else, or something different? Then why do you call yourself a Stoic?

Well: men who falsely claim to be Roman citizens are severely punished, and are men who falsely claim so great and venerable a thing and name to get off unpunished? Or is that not possible, and is this the divine and strong and unavoidable law — the one that exacts the heaviest punishments from those who commit the greatest crimes?

And what does this law say? Let the man who lays claim to things that do not belong to him be a boaster and a vain man. Let the man who disobeys the divine administration be base and a slave. Let him grieve, let him be envious, let him pity; and in a word, let him be unhappy and let him lament.

"Well then — do you want me to pay court to a certain man? To go to his door?"

If reason requires it for the sake of your country, for your family, for mankind, why should you not go? You are not ashamed to go to a shoemaker's door when you need shoes, or a gardener's when you want lettuces; and are you ashamed to go to a rich man's door when you want something?

"Yes, because I stand in no awe of a shoemaker." Then stand in no awe of the rich. "Nor will I flatter the gardener." Then do not flatter the rich either.

"How am I to get what I want, then?"

Do I tell you to go as though you were certain to get it? Do I not tell you only that you may do what becomes you?

"Then why should I go at all?"

So that you shall have gone; so that you shall have discharged the duty of a citizen, of a brother, of a friend. And remember besides that you have gone to a shoemaker, to a seller of vegetables, men who have no power over anything great or noble, however dear they sell.

You go to buy lettuces: they cost a penny, not a fortune. So it is here as well. The matter is worth going to the rich man's door for — very well, I will go. It is worth talking about — very well, I will talk with him. But you must kiss his hand as well and flatter him with praise. Away with that: that costs a fortune. It is no profit to me, nor to the state, nor to my friends, to have done the thing that ruins a good citizen and a good friend.

"But you will look as though you were not keen about the business, if you do not succeed."

Have you forgotten again why you went? Do you not know that a good man does nothing for the sake of appearances, but for the sake of doing right?

"And what advantage is it to him to have done right?"

And what advantage is it to a man writing the name Dion to write it as he ought? The advantage is to have written it.

"Is there no reward, then?"

Do you look for a greater reward for a good man than doing what is good and just? At Olympia you want nothing more; it seems enough to you to be crowned at the games. Does being good and happy seem to you so small and worthless a thing?

The gods have brought you into this city for these purposes, and it is now your duty to take up a man's work — and do you still want nurses and a mother? Do foolish women move you with their weeping and make you soft? Will you never stop being a foolish child? Do you not know that a man who behaves like a child is all the more ridiculous the older he gets?

In Athens did you see nobody by going to his house? "I visited any man I pleased." Here too be ready to see people, and you will see whom you please — only let it be without meanness, and without desire and without avoidance, and your affairs will go well. But that result does not depend on going, or on standing at doors: it depends on what is inside, on your judgments.

When you have learned not to value things that are external and not dependent on the will, and to consider that not one of them is your own — that these things only are your own, to judge well, to form opinions, to move toward an object, to desire, to turn away — where is there room left for flattery, and where for meanness?

Why do you still long for the quiet there, and for the places you are used to? Wait a little and you will find these places familiar too. And then, if you have so ignoble a nature, when you leave these as well, weep and lament again.

"How am I to become an affectionate man, then?"

By being of a noble disposition, and happy. It is not reasonable to be mean-spirited, or to bewail yourself, or to depend on somebody else, or ever to blame God or man. Become an affectionate man that way, I beg you, by keeping these rules. But if through this affection, as you call it, you are going to be a slave and wretched, then there is no profit in being affectionate.

And what stops you loving another person as one who is subject to death, as one who may go away from you? Did Socrates not love his own children? He did — but as a free man, as one who remembered that he must first be a friend to the gods. That is why he violated nothing that becomes a good man, neither in making his defence nor in proposing his own penalty, nor earlier in his life when he was a councillor or a soldier.

But we are fully supplied with excuses for a mean temper — one man for a child's sake, another for a mother's, another for his brothers'. It is not fitting for us to be unhappy on anybody's account; we should be happy on everybody's account, and chiefly on God's, who made us for this end.

Well then — did Diogenes love nobody, who was so kind and so much a lover of mankind that he willingly took on so much labour and bodily suffering for mankind in general? He did love mankind — but how? As a servant of God should: caring for men and at the same time being subject to God. That is why the whole earth was his country and no particular place; and when he was taken prisoner he did not pine for Athens or his friends and companions there, but even became familiar with the pirates and tried to improve them. And when he was sold afterwards he lived in Corinth as he had lived in Athens; and he would have behaved just the same if he had gone to the country of the Perrhaebi.

This is how freedom is got. That is why he used to say: ever since Antisthenes set me free, I have not been a slave.

How did Antisthenes set him free? Hear what he says. Antisthenes taught me what is my own and what is not. Possessions are not my own, nor family, nor servants, nor friends, nor reputation, nor familiar places, nor my way of life; all of these belong to others.

What is your own, then? The use of impressions. He showed me that I have this free of blocking and free of compulsion; nobody can put an obstacle in my way, nobody can force me to use impressions otherwise than I wish. Who has any power over me, then? Philip, or Alexander, or Perdiccas, or the great king? How should they have it?

For if a man is going to be overpowered by a man, he must long before have been overpowered by things. So if pleasure cannot subdue a man, nor pain, nor fame, nor wealth, and if he is able whenever he chooses to spit out his whole poor body in a man's face and leave life — whose slave can he still be?

But if he had lived in Athens with pleasure and been overpowered by that way of living, his affairs would have been at everybody's command; the stronger man would have had the power to grieve him. How do you suppose Diogenes would have flattered the pirates to make them sell him to some Athenian, so that he might one day see that beautiful Piraeus, and the Long Walls, and the Acropolis?

In what condition would you see them? As a captive, a slave, a mean creature — and what use would that be to you?

"Not so: I should see them as a free man."

Show me how you would be free. Look: somebody has caught you and is leading you away from the place you are used to living in, and says, "You are my slave, because it is in my power to stop you living as you please. It is in my power to treat you gently or to humble you, whenever I choose." And on the other side you are cheerful and go off to Athens elated.

What do you say to the man who treats you as a slave? What means have you of finding somebody to rescue you from slavery? Or can you not even look him in the face, but without another word beg to be let go?

Man, you ought to go to prison gladly, hurrying, going ahead of the men who are taking you there. And then, I ask you, are you unwilling to live in Rome, and do you long to live in Greece? And when you have to die, will you fill our ears with your lamentations then too, because you will not see Athens and will not walk about in the Lyceum?

Is this what you went abroad for? Was this why you looked for somebody you might get some benefit from? What benefit? So that you could resolve syllogisms more readily, or handle hypothetical arguments? And was it for this that you left brother, country, friends, family — to come back having learned these things?

So you did not go abroad to get steadiness of mind, or freedom from being thrown about; not so that, being safe from harm, you might never complain of anybody, or accuse anybody, and nobody could wrong you, and you might keep up your relations to other men unblocked?

This is a fine line of trade you have gone abroad for — syllogisms and trick arguments and hypotheticals. If you like, take a place in the marketplace and cry them for sale like a drug seller. Will you not deny even everything you have learned, so as not to give your theorems a bad name as useless?

What harm has philosophy done you? How has Chrysippus injured you, that you should prove by your acts that his labours were useless? Were the evils you had at home not enough — the ones that were the cause of your pain and lamentation even if you had never gone abroad? Have you added more to the list? And if you make other acquaintances and friends, you will have more causes for lamentation; and the same if you grow fond of another country.

Why do you live to heap sorrow upon sorrow round yourself, and be unhappy through them? And then, I ask you, do you call this affection? What affection, man! If it is a good thing, it is the cause of no evil; and if it is a bad thing, I want nothing to do with it. I am made by nature for my own good; I am not made for my own harm.
