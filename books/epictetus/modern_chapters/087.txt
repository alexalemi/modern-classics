Chapter 1: On Freedom

(Part 2 of 3)

Have you nothing at all, then, that is in your own power, that depends on you alone and cannot be taken from you? Or have you something of the kind?

"I do not know."

Then look at the thing this way and examine it. Can any man make you assent to what is false? "No man." Then in the matter of assent you are unblocked and unobstructed. "Granted."

Well — and can a man force you to have an impulse toward something you do not choose? "He can: when he threatens me with death or with chains, he forces me to have the impulse."

If you despise death and chains, then, do you pay him any more attention? "No." Then is despising death an act of your own or not? "It is my own act." Then having an impulse toward a thing is also your own act, or is it not? "It is my own act." And having an impulse away from a thing — whose act is that? That too is yours.

"And what if I try to walk and somebody stops me?"

What part of you does he stop? Does he stop your assent? "No — my poor body." Yes, as he would stop a stone. "Granted; but I no longer walk."

And who told you that walking is your own act and unblocked? I said that this alone is unblocked: having the impulse. Where the body and its cooperation are needed, you heard long ago that nothing is your own. "Granted that as well."

And who can force you to desire what you do not want? "No man." And to intend or plan, or in short to use the impressions that come to you — can any man force you? "He cannot do that; but he will block me from getting what I desire, when I desire."

If you desire something of your own, one of the things that cannot be blocked, how will he block you? "He cannot in any way." Then who tells you that a man who desires what belongs to others is unblocked?

"Must I not desire health, then?"

By no means, nor anything else belonging to others. What it is not in your power to get or to keep when you please belongs to another. Keep away from it, then — not only your hands, but more than that, your desires as well. If you do not, you have handed yourself over as a slave; you have put your neck under the yoke, if you admire anything not your own, of everything that depends on other men's power and is perishable, everything you have taken a liking to.

"Is my hand not my own?"

It is a part of your own body — but by nature it is earth, subject to blocking, to compulsion, and slave to everything stronger than itself. And why do I say your hand? You ought to possess your whole body as you possess a loaded donkey, as long as it is possible, as long as you are allowed to. But if there is a requisition and a soldier takes hold of it, let it go, do not resist and do not complain; if you do, you will get a beating and lose the donkey anyway.

And when this is how you should feel about the body, consider what is left to do about everything else that is provided for the body's sake. When the body is a donkey, all the other things are the donkey's tackle: the pack-saddles, the shoes, the barley, the fodder. Let those go too; get rid of them faster and more readily than of the donkey.

When you have made this preparation, and have trained at this discipline — telling what belongs to another from what is your own, and the things subject to blocking from the things that are not; counting the unblocked things as your concern and the others as no concern of yours; keeping your desire steadily fixed on what does concern you and turned away from what does not — do you still fear any man? No one.

What will you be afraid about? About the things that are your own, in which the nature of good and evil consists? And who has power over these? Who can take them away? Who can obstruct them? Nobody can, any more than they can obstruct God.

But will you be afraid about your body and your possessions — about things that are not yours, that in no way concern you? And what else have you been studying from the beginning but how to tell your own from not your own, what is in your power from what is not, what is subject to blocking from what is not? And why did you come to the philosophers? So that you might be unlucky and unhappy just the same?

So in this way you will be without fear and without disturbance. And what is grief to you? Fear comes from what you expect, grief from what is present. And what more will you desire? Of the things within the power of the will, being good and present, you have a proper and regulated desire; and of the things not within the power of the will you desire none, and so you give no room at all to what is irrational and impatient and hasty beyond measure.

When you are in this state toward things, what man can still be formidable to you? What has one man that is formidable to another, whether you see him or speak to him or live with him? No more than one horse has to another, or one dog to another, or one bee to another bee.

Things are formidable to every man; and when a man is able to give those things to another or take them away, then he too becomes formidable.

So how is a citadel demolished? Not by the sword and not by fire, but by opinion. If we abolish the citadel that stands in the city, have we abolished the citadel of fever as well, and the citadel of beautiful women? Can we, in a word, abolish the citadel inside us and throw out the tyrants inside us — the ones we have standing over us daily, sometimes the same tyrants and sometimes different ones?

This is where we have to begin; this is how we demolish the citadel and throw out the tyrants: by giving up the body, its parts, its capacities, its possessions, reputation, offices, honours, children, brothers, friends, and counting all of them as belonging to others.

And if the tyrants have been thrown out of me, why should I still blockade the citadel with a wall — on my own account, at any rate? If it is still standing, what does it do to me? Why should I still throw out the tyrant's guards? Where do I feel them? They have their rods and their spears and their swords for other men. But I have never been blocked in my will, and never compelled when I did not will it.

And how is that possible? I have put my impulse to act under God's orders. Is it his will that I have a fever? It is my will too. Is it his will that I move toward something? It is my will too. Is it his will that I get something? It is my wish too. Does he not will it? Then I do not wish it. Is it his will that I die, is it his will that I be racked? Then it is my will to die, my will to be racked.

Who can still block me against my own judgment, or compel me? No more than they can block or compel Zeus.

This is how the more cautious travellers behave. A traveller has heard the road is full of robbers; he does not risk it alone, but waits for the company of an ambassador, or a quaestor, or a proconsul, and having attached himself to such men he goes along the road safely.

So the wise man behaves in the world. There are many bands of robbers, tyrants, storms, difficulties, losses of what is dearest to him. Where is there a refuge? How is he to pass along without being attacked by robbers? What company shall he wait for so as to pass safely? Whom shall he attach himself to? To whom in general — to the rich man, to the man of consular rank? And what use is that to me? Such a man is stripped himself; he groans and laments. And what if my fellow traveller turns on me and becomes my robber, what shall I do then?

I will be a friend of Caesar; when I am Caesar's companion nobody will wrong me. In the first place, what must I endure and suffer in order to become distinguished? How often, and by how many men, must I be robbed? And then, if I do become Caesar's friend, he is mortal too. And if Caesar becomes my enemy for some reason, where is it best for me to retire to? A desert? Well — does fever not come there?

What is to be done, then? Is it not possible to find a safe fellow traveller, a faithful one, strong, proof against every surprise? So he considers, and sees that if he attaches himself to God he will make his journey safely.

How do you understand attaching yourself to God? In this sense: that whatever God wills, a man shall will too; and what God does not will, a man shall not will either.

And how is that to be done? In what other way than by examining God's movements and his administration? What has he given me as my own and in my own power? What has he kept for himself? He has given me the things within the power of the will; he has put them in my power, free of obstruction and blocking. How could he make the earthy body unblocked? He could not, and so he made possessions, household goods, house, children and wife subject to the revolution of the whole.

Why do I fight against God, then? Why do I will what does not depend on the will? Why do I will to have absolutely what is not granted to me?

But how ought I to will to have things? In the way they are given, and for as long as they are given. And the one who gave them takes them away. Why do I resist, then? I do not say that I shall be a fool if I use force against a stronger — I shall first of all be unjust.

For where did I get anything when I came into the world? My father gave it to me. And who gave it to him? And who made the sun? And who made the fruits of the earth? Who made the seasons? Who made the connection of men with one another, and their fellowship?

And then, having received everything from another, and yourself as well, are you angry and do you blame the giver if he takes something from you?

Who are you, and what did you come into the world for? Did he not bring you here? Did he not show you the light? Did he not give you fellow workers, and senses, and reason? And as what did he bring you here? Did he not bring you as a mortal, to live on the earth with a little flesh, and to watch his administration, and to join him in the spectacle and the festival for a short time?

Then will you not, as long as you are allowed, having seen the spectacle and the solemnity, go out when he leads you out, worshipping him and giving thanks for what you have heard and seen?

"No — I would like to enjoy the feast longer."

So would the initiated like to be longer at their initiation; so, perhaps, would the crowds at Olympia like to see more athletes. But the solemnity is over. Go away like a grateful and modest man; make room for others. Others must be born, as you were; and being born, they must have a place, and houses, and necessities. And if the first men do not withdraw, what is left?

Why are you insatiable? Why are you not content? Why do you crowd the world?

"Yes, but I want my little children with me, and my wife."

What — are they yours? Do they not belong to the giver, to the one who made you? Then will you not give up what belongs to others? Will you not give way to your superior?

"Why did he bring me into the world on these conditions, then?"

And if the conditions do not suit you, leave. He has no need of a spectator who is not satisfied. He wants men who join in the festival, who take part in the chorus, so that they may applaud and admire and sing the solemnity's praises. Those who cannot bear any trouble, and the cowards, he will not be sorry to see absent from the great assembly; because when they were there they did not behave as men should at a festival, and did not fill their place properly, but lamented and found fault with the deity, with fortune, with their companions — not seeing what they had, and not seeing their own powers, which they were given for the opposite purposes: the powers of greatness of spirit, of a generous mind, of courage, and the one we are enquiring about now, freedom.

"What did I receive these things for, then?"

To use them.

"For how long?"

For as long as the one who lent them chooses.

"And what if they are necessary to me?"

Do not attach yourself to them and they will not be necessary. Do not tell yourself they are necessary, and then they are not.

This is the study you should practise from morning till night, beginning with the smallest things and the ones most easily damaged — with an earthenware pot, with a cup. Then go on in the same way to a tunic, to a little dog, to a horse, to a small piece of land; then to yourself, to your body, to the parts of your body, to your children, to your wife, to your brothers.

Look all round and throw these things away from you. Clean out your judgments, so that nothing that is not your own clings to you or grows onto you, and nothing gives you pain when it is torn away. And say, while you do your daily exercises there in the school, not that you are doing philosophy — that is an arrogant word — but that you are presenting a man who claims his freedom. Because this is really freedom.

It was to this freedom that Antisthenes called Diogenes, and Diogenes said that from then on nobody could enslave him. That is why, when he was taken prisoner, he behaved to the pirates as he did. Did he call any of them master? I do not mean the word — I am not afraid of the word — but the state of mind the word comes out of. How he reproached them for feeding their captives badly! And how was he sold? Did he look for a master? No: for a slave.

And once he was sold, how did he behave to his master? He argued with him at once, and told him he ought not to be dressed as he was, or shaved that way; and about the children, he told them how he ought to bring them up.

And what was strange in that? If his master had bought a trainer, would he have used him in the wrestling school as a servant or as a master? And the same if he had bought a doctor or an architect. And so in every matter: the man with the skill must necessarily be the superior of the man without it.

So whoever has the science of life in general — what else must he be but master? Who is master on a ship? The man at the helm. Why? Because whoever will not obey him suffers for it.

"But a master can have me flogged."

Can he do it without suffering for it, though? So I used to think too. But because he cannot do it without suffering for it, it is not in his power; and no man can do an injustice without suffering for it.

And what is the penalty for the man who chains his own slave? What do you suppose it is? The fact of having chained him. And you will admit this too, if you will hold to the truth that man is not a wild beast but a tame creature.

When is a vine doing badly? When it is in a condition contrary to its nature. When is a cock? Just the same. So a man too. And what is a man's nature? To bite, to kick, to throw men into prison and cut off their heads? No: to do good, to work together with others, to wish them well. So at that moment he is in a bad condition, whether you choose to admit it or not — when he is behaving like a fool.
