Chapter 18: Why We Should Not Be Angry at Other People's Mistakes

If what the philosophers say is true — that in every man there is one governing principle: in assent, the conviction that a thing is so; in dissent, the conviction that it is not; in suspending judgment, the conviction that it is uncertain; and likewise, in any movement toward a thing, the conviction that it is to the man's advantage; and if it is impossible to think one thing advantageous and want another, or to judge one thing proper and move toward another — then why are we angry with the crowd?

"They are thieves and robbers," you may say.

What do you mean, thieves and robbers? They have gone wrong about good and evil. Ought we to be angry with them, then, or to pity them? Show them their error and you will see how they give it up. If they do not see the error, they have nothing better than the opinion they hold now.

"Ought this robber and this adulterer not to be put to death, then?"

Do not put it that way. Put it like this instead: this man who has gone wrong and been deceived about the most important things of all, and been blinded — not in the power of sight that tells white from black, but in the power that tells good from bad — ought we not to destroy him? Put it that way and you will see how inhuman a thing you are saying, and that it is exactly as if you said, "Ought we not to destroy this blind and deaf man?"

If the greatest harm is being deprived of the greatest things, and the greatest thing in any man is a will that is as it ought to be, and a man is deprived of that — why are you angry with him on top of it? Man, you ought not to be affected against nature by another man's bad things. Pity him instead. Drop this readiness to take offence and to hate, and these phrases the crowd throws about: "these damned, disgusting people." How did you get so wise, all of a sudden? And how did you get so bad-tempered?

Why are we angry, then? Is it because we set such store by the things these men rob us of? Stop admiring your clothes and you will not be angry with the thief. Stop admiring your wife's beauty and you will not be angry with the adulterer. Learn that a thief and an adulterer have no foothold in what is yours, only in what belongs to others and is not in your power. Give these things up and count them as nothing, and who is left for you to be angry with? But so long as you set store by them, be angry with yourself rather than with the thief and the adulterer.

Look at it this way. You have fine clothes; your neighbour has not. You have a window; you want to air them. The thief does not know what a man's good consists in — he thinks it consists in having fine clothes, exactly what you think. Is he not bound to come and take them? When you show a cake to greedy men and then eat it all yourself, do you expect them not to snatch it? Do not provoke them. Do not have a window. Do not air your clothes.

I had an iron lamp myself the other day, standing by my household gods. I heard a noise at the door, ran down, and found the lamp gone. I reflected that the man who took it had done nothing surprising. And so? Tomorrow, I said, you will find an earthenware one — because a man only loses what he has.

I have lost my coat. Yes: the reason is that you had a coat. I have a pain in my head. Have you a pain in your horns? Then why are you upset? We lose only the things we have, and have pains only about the things we possess.

But the tyrant will chain — what? A leg. He will take away — what? A neck. What will he never chain and never take away? The will. This is why the ancients taught us the maxim: know yourself.

So we ought to train ourselves on small things, and go on from them to bigger ones. I have a pain in my head. Do not say "alas!" I have a pain in my ear. Do not say "alas!" I am not saying you are forbidden to groan — only do not groan on the inside. And if your slave is slow bringing the bandage, do not shout and put yourself in a state and say, "Everybody hates me." Who would not hate a man like that?

From now on, trusting these judgments, walk about upright and free — not relying on the size of your body, like an athlete. A man ought not to be unbeatable in the way a donkey is.

Who is the unbeatable man, then? The one whom nothing outside his will can disturb.

Then I go over his circumstances one after another, as I would with an athlete. He has come off victorious in the first bout — well then, what about the second? And what if it is very hot? And what if it is at Olympia? Just so here. Throw money in his way: he will despise it. Well, put a young girl in his way — what then? And what if it is in the dark? What if it is a little reputation? Or abuse? Or praise? Or death? He can beat all of them.

And what if it is in blazing heat? What if it is in the rain? What if he is in a black mood? What if he is asleep? He will win even then. This is my unbeatable athlete.
