Chapter 23: On the Power of Speaking

Every man reads a book with more pleasure, or at least more easily, if it is written in a finer hand. So every man will also listen more readily to what is spoken, if it is put in fitting and becoming words. We must not say, then, that there is no such thing as a capacity for expression. To say so is the mark of an irreverent man, and of a cowardly one as well.

Of an irreverent man, because he undervalues the gifts that come from God — as if he were doing away with the usefulness of sight or hearing. Has God given you eyes for nothing? Has he put into them for nothing a spirit so strong and so cunningly contrived that it reaches a long way off and shapes the forms of what is seen? What messenger is so swift and so watchful? And has he made the air in between so efficient and so elastic for nothing, that vision passes through it when it is set moving? And has he made light for nothing, without which nothing else would be of any use?

Man, be neither ungrateful for these gifts nor forgetful of the things that are better than they are. Give thanks to God for sight and hearing, and indeed for life itself and the things that support it — for dried fruits, for wine and oil. But remember that he has given you something better than all of them: the power of using them, testing them, and estimating what each is worth.

For what is it that gives you information about each of these powers, and what each is worth? Is it the capacity itself? Did you ever hear the capacity of sight say anything about itself? Or hearing? Or wheat, or barley, or a horse, or a dog? No: they are appointed as servants and slaves to serve the capacity that has the power of using impressions.

And if you enquire what each thing is worth, whom do you enquire of? Who answers you? How can any other capacity be more powerful than this one, which uses the others as its servants and tests each of them itself and pronounces on them? Which of them knows what it is itself, and what it is worth? Which of them knows when it ought to employ itself and when not?

What is the capacity that opens and shuts the eyes, and turns them away from things they ought not to be applied to, and applies them to others? Is it the capacity of sight? No — it is the will. What capacity closes and opens the ears? What makes them curious and enquiring, or on the other hand unmoved by what is said? Is it hearing? It is nothing but the will.

Here is a capacity standing among all the others, which are blind and dumb and unable to see anything except the very acts they are appointed to in order to serve it — and this one alone sees sharply and sees what each of the rest is worth. Will this capacity tell us that something else is best, and not itself?

And what else does the eye do, when it is opened, but see? But whether we ought to look at a certain man's wife, and in what manner — who tells us that? The will. And whether we ought to believe what is said or not; and if we do believe it, whether we ought to be moved by it — who tells us? Is it not the will?

And this capacity of speaking and of ornamenting words, if there really is any such special capacity — what else does it do, when there happens to be talk about something, than ornament the words and arrange them, as hairdressers arrange hair? But whether it is better to speak or keep silent, and better to speak this way or that, and whether this is becoming or not, and the right moment for each and the use of it — what tells us that but the will? Would you have it come forward and condemn itself?

What then? the will says: if that is how things stand, can the servant be superior to the one it serves? Can the horse be superior to the rider, or the dog to the huntsman, or the instrument to the musician, or the servants to the king?

What is it that makes use of the rest? The will. What takes care of everything? The will. What destroys the whole man — sometimes by starvation, sometimes by hanging, sometimes by a cliff? The will. Is anything in men stronger than this, then? And how can things that are subject to restraint be stronger than what is not?

What things are naturally formed to block sight? Both the will and things outside the will. It is the same with hearing, and the same with speaking. But what has a natural power of blocking the will? Nothing outside the will — only the will itself, when it is twisted. And that is why the will alone is vice, or alone is virtue.

Then, being so great a capacity and set over all the rest, let it come forward and tell us that the most excellent of all things is the flesh. Not even if the flesh itself declared it was the most excellent would anybody put up with hearing it say so.

But what is it, Epicurus, that pronounces this? What wrote about the End of our being, and about the Nature of Things, and about the Standard of truth? What made you grow a beard? What wrote, as it was dying, that it was spending its last day, and a happy one? Was that the flesh, or the will? Then do you admit that you possess something superior to it — and are you not mad? Are you really so blind and so deaf?

What of it, then? Does anybody despise the other capacities? I should hope not. Does anybody say there is no use or excellence in the capacity for speaking? I should hope not. That would be foolish, irreverent, ungrateful toward God.

A man gives each thing its due value. There is some use in a donkey, but not as much as in an ox. There is use in a dog, but not as much as in a slave. There is use in a slave, but not as much as in citizens; and use in citizens, but not as much as in magistrates. And because some things are superior we must not undervalue the use the others have.

There is a certain value in the power of speaking, but not as great as in the power of the will. So when I talk like this, let nobody think I am asking you to neglect the power of speaking — any more than I ask you to neglect your eyes, or your ears, or your hands or feet, or your clothes and shoes.

But if you ask me what is the most excellent thing of all, what must I say? I cannot say the power of speaking. I must say the power of the will, when it is straight. For it is this that uses the other, and all the rest, great and small. When the will is set straight, a man who was not good becomes good; and when it fails, a man becomes bad. It is through this that we are unlucky and that we are lucky, that we blame one another and are pleased with one another. In a word, it is this which, neglected, makes unhappiness, and which, carefully looked after, makes happiness.

But to do away with the capacity of speaking and say that there is no such thing in reality is the act not only of a man ungrateful to those who gave it, but of a cowardly man. Such a person seems to me to be afraid that if there is any such capacity we shall not be able to despise it.

It is the same with men who say there is no difference between beauty and ugliness. Then a man would be affected in the same way by the sight of Thersites and the sight of Achilles; in the same way by Helen and by any other woman. These are stupid, boorish notions, the notions of men who do not know the nature of each thing but are afraid that if a man sees the difference he will be seized and carried off, beaten, on the spot.

But this is the great thing: to leave each thing the capacity it has, and, leaving it that, to see what the capacity is worth; and to learn what is the most excellent thing of all, and to pursue that always, and be diligent about it, counting everything else of secondary value in comparison — while still, as far as we can, not neglecting those other things. We must take care of the eyes too, not as being the most excellent thing, but for the sake of the most excellent thing, since that will not be in its true natural condition unless it uses the other capacities rightly and prefers some things to others.

And what usually happens? Men behave like a traveller on his way home who comes to a good inn and, finding it pleasant, stays there. Man, you have forgotten your purpose. You were not travelling to this inn; you were passing through it. "But it is a pleasant inn." And how many other inns are pleasant? How many meadows are pleasant? Yet only to pass through. Your purpose is to get back to your own country, to relieve your family's anxiety, to do a citizen's duties, to marry, to have children, to hold the usual offices. You did not come to pick out the pleasanter places, but to live in the place where you were born and were made a citizen.

Something of the kind happens in the matter we are considering. Since it is by speech and the sort of instruction you get here that you must advance toward perfection, and clean your will, and correct the capacity that uses impressions; and since the teaching of theorems must be done in a certain style, with a certain variety and sharpness — some men are captivated by these very things and stay among them. One is captivated by the expression, another by syllogisms, another by sophisms, another by some other inn of the kind; and there they stay and waste away, as though among the Sirens.

Man, your business was to make yourself capable of using in line with nature the impressions put before you; not to be frustrated in your desires; not to fall into what you would avoid in your avoidances; never to be out of luck, as one might say, and never to have bad luck; to be free, unblocked, uncompelled, fitting yourself to the administration of Zeus and obeying it, well satisfied with it, blaming nobody, charging nobody with fault, able to say these lines with your whole soul:

	Lead me, Zeus, and you too, Destiny,

And with that purpose in front of you, if some little turn of phrase pleases you, or some theorems please you, do you settle down among them and choose to live there, forgetting what is at home? And do you say, "These things are fine"? Who says they are not fine — but only as a road home, as inns are.

What stops you being an unlucky man even if you speak like Demosthenes? What stops you, if you can resolve syllogisms like Chrysippus, from being wretched, from grieving, from envying — in a word, from being disturbed and unhappy? Nothing.

So you see that these were inns, worth nothing, and that the purpose set before you was something else. When I talk like this to some people they think I am rejecting care about speaking, or care about theorems. I am not rejecting the care. I am rejecting the settling down among these things forever and putting our hopes in them. If a man does harm to his hearers by this teaching, count me too among the men who do that harm; for when I see one thing that is most excellent and supreme, I am not able to say that something else is, in order to please you.
