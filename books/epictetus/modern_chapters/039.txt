Chapter 8: What the Nature of the Good Is

God is beneficial. And the good is beneficial too. So it follows that where the nature of God is, there the nature of the good is also.

What is the nature of God, then? Flesh? "Certainly not." An estate in land? "By no means." Fame? "No." Is it intelligence, knowledge, right reason? "Yes."

Then look for the nature of the good simply here. I take it you do not look for it in a plant. "No." Do you look for it in an animal without reason? "No." Then if you look for it in a rational creature, why do you go on looking anywhere except in what makes rational creatures superior to irrational ones?

Plants have not even the power of using impressions, and that is why you do not call them good. So the good requires the use of impressions. Does it require only that? If you say it requires only that, then say that good, and happiness and unhappiness, exist in animals too. But you do not say so, and you are right. Even if they have the use of impressions in the highest degree, they do not have the power of understanding that use — and with good reason, since they exist to serve others and exercise no superiority.

The donkey, I take it, does not exist for any superiority over others. No: we needed a back that could carry something, and in truth we needed him to be able to walk as well, and for that he was given the power of using impressions, since without it he could not have walked. And there the matter stopped. If he had also been given the power of understanding that use, then plainly, and reasonably, he would not have been subject to us and would not have done us these services; he would have been our equal and our like.

Will you not look for the nature of good in the rational creature, then? If it is not there, you will not want to say it exists in anything else.

"What of it? Are plants and animals not God's works too?"

They are; but they are not superior things, and they are not parts of the gods. You are a superior thing. You are a portion broken off from the divine. You have in yourself a certain portion of him.

Why, then, are you ignorant of your own high descent? Why do you not know where you came from? Will you not remember, when you are eating, who you are who eat, and whom you are feeding? When you lie with a woman, will you not remember who you are who do it? When you are in company, when you are exercising, when you are arguing, do you not know that you are feeding a god, that you are exercising a god? Wretch, you are carrying a god about with you and you do not know it.

Do you suppose I mean some god of silver or gold, outside you? You carry him inside yourself, and you do not notice that you are defiling him with unclean thoughts and dirty acts. If an image of God were standing here you would not dare to do any of the things you do; but when God himself is present inside you, seeing everything and hearing everything, you are not ashamed to think such things and do such things — ignorant of your own nature, and lying under the anger of God.

Then why are we afraid, when we send a young man out of the school into active life, that he may do something improper, eat improperly, sleep with women improperly? Afraid that the rags he is wrapped in may debase him, or that fine clothes may make him proud? A young man who behaves so does not know his own God; he does not know who set out with him.

And can we bear it when he says, "I wish I had God with me"? Have you not got God with you? And do you look for another when you have him? Or would God tell you anything different from this?

If you were a statue by Phidias — an Athena or a Zeus — you would think both of yourself and of the artist; and if you had any power of perception you would try to do nothing unworthy of the man who made you, or of yourself, and try not to appear in an unfitting posture to the people looking at you. And now, because Zeus made you, is that a reason for not caring how you will appear?

And is the one artist anything like the other? Is the one work anything like the other? What work of any artist has in itself the powers the artist shows in making it? Is it not marble, or bronze, or gold, or ivory? Phidias' Athena, once she has stretched out her hand and taken the figure of Victory into it, stands in that attitude forever. But the works of God have the power of movement, they breathe, they can use impressions and examine them.

Being the work of such an artist, will you dishonour him? And what shall I say — not only that he made you, but that he entrusted you to yourself and made you a deposit in your own keeping? Will you not think of that either? Will you dishonour your guardianship too?

If God had put an orphan into your care, would you neglect him like this? He has handed you over to your own care, and says: I had nobody fitter to entrust him to than yourself. Keep him for me as he is by nature — self-respecting, trustworthy, upright, unterrified, free from passion and free from being thrown about.

And then you do not keep him so.

But some will say: where has this fellow got his arrogance and these superior looks? I have not yet the gravity that suits a philosopher; I have not yet any confidence in what I have learned and assented to; I am still afraid of my own weakness. Let me get the confidence, and then you shall see the face I ought to have and the bearing I ought to have. Then I will show you the statue, when it is finished and polished.

What do you expect — a superior look? Does the Zeus at Olympia raise his eyebrow? No: his look is fixed, as befits one who is ready to say

	Irrevocable is my word, and it shall not fail.
	— Iliad, i 526.

That is how I will show myself to you: trustworthy, self-respecting, noble, undisturbed.

What, and immortal too, and exempt from old age and sickness? No — dying as a god dies, falling sick as a god falls sick. This power I have; this I can do. The rest I do not have, and cannot do.

I will show you the sinews of a philosopher. What sinews are those? A desire that is never disappointed. An avoidance that never lands on the thing it would avoid. An impulse that is proper, a purpose that is careful, an assent that is not rash. These you shall see.
