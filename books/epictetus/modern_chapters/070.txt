Chapter 13: What Loneliness Is, and What Kind of Person Is Truly Alone

Loneliness is a certain condition of a helpless man. A man is not lonely just because he is by himself, any more than a man among crowds is not lonely.

So when we have lost a brother, or a son, or a friend we used to lean on, we say we have been left lonely — though we are often in Rome, though such a crowd goes by us, though so many people live in the same place, and though we sometimes have a great number of slaves. The man who counts as lonely is understood to be a helpless one, exposed to anybody who wants to harm him.

That is why, when we travel, we say we are lonely above all when we fall among robbers. It is not the sight of a human creature that takes us out of loneliness, but the sight of one who is trustworthy and decent and helpful to us.

If being by oneself were enough to make loneliness, you might say that Zeus himself is lonely at the great conflagration, and bewails himself, saying: unhappy that I am — I have no Hera, no Athena, no Apollo, no brother, no son, no descendant, no kinsman. Some people say this is what he does when he is alone at the conflagration. But they do not understand how a man passes his time when he is alone, because they start from a certain natural principle — from the natural desire for community and mutual affection, and from the pleasure of human conversation.

All the same, a man ought to be prepared for this too: to be able to be sufficient to himself and to be his own companion. As Zeus lives with himself and is at peace by himself and thinks about his own administration and its nature, and busies himself with thoughts that suit him, so we too ought to be able to talk with ourselves and not need other people; not to be at a loss for ways of passing the time; to observe the divine administration and our own relation to everything else; to consider how we used to be affected by what happens and how we are now; what things still give us pain, and how those too can be cured and taken away; and if anything needs improving, to improve it by reason.

You see that Caesar appears to give us a great peace: there are no more enemies, or battles, or great bands of robbers and pirates, and we can travel at any hour and sail from east to west. But can Caesar give us security against fever as well? Against shipwreck? Against fire, or earthquake, or lightning? Can he — I will go further — give us security against love? He cannot. Against grief? He cannot. Against envy? He cannot. In a word he cannot protect us against any of these things.

But the doctrine of the philosophers promises to give us security even against these. And what does it say? Men, if you will attend to me, then wherever you are and whatever you are doing you will feel no grief, no anger, no compulsion, no blocking; you will pass your time undisturbed and free of everything.

When a man has this peace — proclaimed not by Caesar (how could he proclaim it?) but by God through reason — is he not content when he is alone? When he looks and reflects: no evil can happen to me now; for me there is no robber, no earthquake; everything is full of peace, full of quiet. Every road, every city, every gathering, every neighbour, every companion is harmless. One whose business it is supplies me with food; another with clothing; another with senses and preconceptions. And if he does not supply what is needed, he gives the signal for retreat, opens the door, and says to you: go.

Go where? To nothing terrible — to the place you came from, to your friends and kinsmen, to the elements. What was fire in you goes to fire, what was earth to earth, what was breath to air, what was water to water. No Hades, no Acheron, no Cocytus, no Pyriphlegethon: everything is full of gods and spirits.

When a man has such things to think about, and sees the sun and the moon and the stars, and enjoys the earth and the sea, he is not lonely, and not even helpless.

"Well then — suppose somebody comes on me when I am alone and murders me?"

Fool, he does not murder you. He murders your poor body.

What sort of loneliness is left, then? What lack? Why do we make ourselves worse than children? What do children do when they are left alone? They pick up shells and ashes and build something, then knock it down and build something else, and so they are never at a loss for a way to pass the time. Am I to sit down and weep, then, if you sail away, because I have been left alone and lonely? Am I to have no shells and no ashes? Children do what they do out of a want of understanding; and we are unhappy out of understanding.

Every great capacity is dangerous to beginners. So you must bear such things as you are able, and in line with nature; but not — [the text is defective here.]

Practise sometimes living like a man in poor health, so that at some point you may live like a man in health. Go without food, drink water, hold off from desire altogether sometimes, so that at some point you may desire in accordance with reason; and if in accordance with reason, then when you have something good in you, you will desire well.

Not at all — we want to live like wise men straight away and be useful to mankind. Useful how? What are you doing? Have you been useful to yourself? But I suppose you want to urge them on. You, urge them on! You want to be useful to them. Then show them by your own example what sort of men philosophy makes, and stop trifling.

When you are eating, do good to the men eating with you; when you are drinking, to the men drinking with you. By giving way to everybody, yielding, putting up with them — do them good that way, and do not spit your bad temper over them.
