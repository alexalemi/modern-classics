Chapter 24: Why We Should Not Be Moved by Longing for What Is Not Up to Us

(Part 2 of 2)

What is the training for this, then? The first and chief one, standing as it were in the doorway, is this: when you are delighted by anything, be delighted with it as with a thing that can be taken away — as with an earthenware pot, or a glass cup, so that when it is broken you may remember what it was and not be upset.

So here too. When you kiss your own child, or your brother, or your friend, never give the impression free rein and never let your pleasure go as far as it likes. Check it and curb it, as the men who stand behind generals in their triumphs do, reminding them that they are mortal. Remind yourself in the same way that the one you love is mortal, and that what you love is nothing of your own. It has been given to you for the present, not so that it cannot be taken away, and not for all time, but as a fig is given, or a bunch of grapes, at its appointed season of the year. If you want them in winter you are a fool. So if you want your son or your friend when it is not allowed to you, you must know you are wanting a fig in winter.

For what winter is to a fig, every event that comes from the universe is to the things it takes away in the course of nature.

And further: at the times when you are delighted with a thing, put the contrary impressions in front of yourself. What harm is there, while you are kissing your child, in saying under your breath, "Tomorrow you will die"? And to a friend, "Tomorrow you will go away, or I shall, and we shall never see each other again"?

"But these are words of ill omen."

Some charms are of ill omen too; and because they are useful, I do not care about that — only let them be useful. Do you call anything a bad omen except what signifies some evil? Cowardice is a word of ill omen, and meanness of spirit, and sorrow, and grief, and shamelessness. Those are words of ill omen; and even so we ought not to hesitate to say them, in order to protect ourselves against the things.

Do you tell me that a word signifying something natural is of evil omen? Then say that the reaping of the ears of corn is of evil omen, since it signifies the destruction of the ears — though not of the world. Say that the falling of the leaves is of ill omen, and the dried fig taking the place of the green one, and grapes being turned into raisins. All these are changes from a former state into other states: not destruction, but a certain fixed ordering and administration.

So is going abroad: a small change. So is death: a greater change, not from what is now to what is not, but to what is not now.

"Shall I no longer exist, then?"

You will not exist, but you will be something else, of which the world now has need. For you came into existence not when you chose, but when the world had need of you.

And so the wise and good man, remembering who he is and where he came from and by whom he was made, attends only to this: how he may fill his place in good order and obediently to God.

Do you still want me to exist? Then I will go on existing as a free man, as a man noble by nature, as you wanted me to exist — for you made me unblocked in what is my own. But have you no further need of me? I thank you. I have stayed until now for your sake and for nobody else's, and now in obedience to you I depart.

How do you depart? Again, as you have pleased: as a free man, as your servant, as one who has known your commands and your prohibitions.

And as long as I stay in your service, what do you want me to be — a prince or a private man, a senator or an ordinary person, a soldier or a general, a teacher or the head of a household? Whatever place and position you assign me, I will die ten thousand times, as Socrates says, rather than desert it.

And where do you want me to be? In Rome, or Athens, or Thebes, or Gyara? Only remember me there, where I am. If you send me to a place where there are no means for a man to live according to nature, I shall not leave life in disobedience to you, but as though you had given me the signal to retreat. I do not leave you — far be that from my intention — but I see that you have no further need of me. And if the means of living according to nature are allowed me, I will look for no other place than the one I am in, and no other men than the ones I am among.

Let these thoughts be ready to hand night and day. These you should write; these you should read; about these you should talk to yourself and to others. Ask a man, "Can you help me at all toward this?" And then go to another, and another.

Then if something happens against your wish, this reflection will relieve you at once: that it was not unexpected. It is a great thing in every case to be able to say, "I knew that the son I fathered was mortal." So you will also say: I knew that I am mortal; I knew that I may leave my home; I knew that I may be thrown out of it; I knew that I may be taken off to prison.

And then, if you turn round and look at yourself and look for the quarter the thing came from, you will remember at once that it comes from the region of things outside the power of the will, things that are not my own. So what is it to me?

Then you will ask — and this is the chief thing — and who was it that sent it? The commander, the general, the state, the law of the state. Give it to me, then; I must always obey the law in everything.

And when the impression gives you pain — for it is not in your power to prevent that — fight against it with the help of reason. Beat it. Do not let it gain strength, and do not let it lead you on to the consequences by raising whatever images it pleases and however it pleases.

If you are on Gyara, do not picture the way of living at Rome, and how many pleasures there were for a man living there and how many there would be if he went back. Fix your mind on this instead: how a man who lives on Gyara ought to live on Gyara, like a man of courage. And if you are in Rome, do not picture life in Athens; think only about life in Rome.

Then, in place of every other delight, put this one: being conscious that you are obeying God; that not in word but in fact you are performing the acts of a wise and good man.

What a thing it is for a man to be able to say to himself: whatever the rest of them may be saying solemnly in the schools, and may be thought to be saying in a strange way, this I am doing. They are sitting and discoursing about my virtues, and enquiring about me, and praising me — and Zeus has willed that I should get the proof of it from myself, and should know for myself whether he has the soldier he ought to have, the citizen he ought to have; and whether he has chosen to produce me before the rest of mankind as a witness of the things that lie outside the will.

See that you fear without reason. See that you foolishly desire what you desire. Do not look for the good in externals; look for it in yourselves — and if you do not, you will not find it.

For this purpose he leads me here at one time and sends me there at another; shows me to men as poor, without authority, and sick; sends me to Gyara, takes me into prison — not because he hates me, far be such a thought from him, for who hates the best of his servants? nor because he does not care about me, for he neglects not even the smallest things — but in order to train me and to use me as a witness to others.

Appointed to such a service, do I still care what place I am in, or whom I am with, or what men say about me? Do I not turn my thoughts entirely to God and to his instructions and commands?

Keep these things always to hand, and exercise them on your own, and hold them in readiness, and you will never be in want of somebody to comfort and strengthen you. For it is not shameful to have nothing to eat; it is shameful not to have reason enough to keep off fear and sorrow.

And if you once win exemption from sorrow and fear, will there be any tyrant for you any longer, or any tyrant's guard, or any of Caesar's attendants? Will any appointment to office at court cause you pain? Will the men who sacrifice on the Capitol on being named to certain functions cause any pain to you, who have received so great an authority from Zeus?

Only do not make a proud display of it, and do not boast of it; show it by your acts. And if nobody notices, be satisfied that you yourself are in a healthy state, and happy.
