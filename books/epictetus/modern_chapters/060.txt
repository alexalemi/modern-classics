Chapter 3: What a Good Person Should Work On, and What We Should Practice Most

The material of the wise and good man is his own governing part, as the body is the material of the doctor and of the trainer who rubs men with oil, and the land is the material of the farmer. And the business of the wise and good man is to use impressions in line with nature.

Now just as it is the nature of every soul to assent to the true, to dissent from the false, and to hold back over what is uncertain, so it is its nature to be moved toward desiring the good and avoiding the bad, and to feel neither way about what is neither good nor bad.

A banker is not allowed to refuse Caesar's coin, nor is a greengrocer; show the coin and, willing or not, he must hand over what it buys. It is the same with the soul. When the good appears it draws the soul to itself at once; the bad repels it. The soul will never refuse a plain impression of the good, any more than men refuse Caesar's coin. On this principle every movement depends, of man and of God alike.

This is why the good is preferred to every family tie. There is no family tie between me and my father, but there is between me and the good.

"Are you so hard-hearted?"

Yes, because that is my nature, and this is the coin God has given me. If the good is something other than the beautiful and the just, then father goes, and brother, and country, and everything.

But am I to overlook my own good so that you may have it? Am I to give it up to you? Why? "I am your father." But you are not my good. "I am your brother." But you are not my good.

And if we put the good in the right direction of the will, then the very keeping up of the relations of life is a good, and a man who gives up any external thing gets what is good.

Your father takes your property. But he does not injure you. Your brother will have the larger part of the estate. Let him have as much as he likes. Will he have a larger share of self-respect, of good faith, of brotherly affection? Who is going to evict you from that property? Not even Zeus — because he did not choose to; he put it in my power and gave it to me exactly as he holds it himself, free from blocking, compulsion and obstruction.

Now when another man's currency is a different coin, then if you present that coin you get what it buys. Suppose a thieving proconsul comes into the province: what currency does he use? Silver. Show it to him and carry off what you like. Suppose an adulterer comes: what currency does he use? Young girls. Take the coin, a man says, and sell me the little thing. Hand it over, says the seller, and buy. Another is keen on boys. Give him the coin and take what you want. Another is fond of hunting: give him a fine horse or a dog. He may groan and complain, but he will sell you what you want for it. Somebody else compels him, from inside — the man who fixed that currency.

This above all is what a man should train himself against. As soon as you go out in the morning, examine every man you see and every man you hear, and answer as if to a question. What have you seen? A handsome man or woman. Apply the rule. Is this outside the will or inside it? Outside. Then take it away.

What have you seen? A man grieving over the death of a child. Apply the rule. Death is outside the will. Take it away.

Did the proconsul meet you? Apply the rule. What kind of thing is a proconsulship — outside the will or inside? Outside. Take that away too. It does not stand examination. Throw it away; it is nothing to you.

If we practised this and drilled ourselves in it every day from morning to night, something would actually get done. As it is, we are caught half asleep by every impression, and only in the school, if ever, are we roused a little. Then we go out, and if we see a man grieving we say, "He is finished." If we see a consul we say, "He is a happy man." If we see an exile we say, "Poor wretch." If we see a poor man we say, "Miserable creature — he has nothing to eat."

These are the bad opinions we ought to root out, and this is where all our effort should go. For what is weeping and lamenting? Opinion. What is bad luck? Opinion. What is civil strife, disagreement, blame, accusation, irreverence, empty talk? All of these are opinions and nothing else — opinions about things outside the will, as though those things were good and bad.

Let a man shift these opinions over to the things inside the will, and I will guarantee that he will be steady and constant, whatever the state of things around him.

The soul is like a dish of water, and impressions are like the ray of light falling on the water. When the water is disturbed the ray seems to be disturbed too, though it is not. So when a man is seized with dizziness, it is not the arts and the virtues that are thrown into confusion but the spirit they are impressed on; and if the spirit is restored to its settled state, they are restored too.
