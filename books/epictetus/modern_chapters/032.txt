Chapter 1: Why Confidence and Caution Are Not Opposites

The philosophers' view may strike some people as a paradox, but let us examine as best we can whether it is true that everything can be done both cautiously and confidently. Caution looks in a way like the opposite of confidence, and opposites are never consistent with each other.

What seems to many a paradox in this matter is, I think, of this kind. If we asserted that we ought to use caution and confidence about the same things, men could fairly accuse us of joining what cannot be joined. But as it is, where is the difficulty in what has been said? If it is true — as has often been said and often proved — that the nature of good lies in the use of impressions, and the nature of evil likewise, and that things outside our will admit of neither, what paradox are the philosophers asserting when they say that where things do not depend on the will you should use confidence, and where they do depend on the will you should use caution?

If the bad consists in a bad exercise of the will, then caution ought to be used only where things depend on the will. And if things outside the will and not in our power are nothing to us, then toward those we must use confidence. So we shall be both cautious and confident — indeed confident because of our caution. By being cautious about things that are really bad, we shall end up confident about the things that are not.

We behave like deer bolting in fright from the hunters' feathers. Where do they turn, and where do they look for safety? They run into the nets — and so they die, having confused what they ought to fear with what they ought not.

We do exactly the same. What do we fear? Things outside the will. What do we treat with confidence, as though there were no danger in it? Things that depend on the will. To be deceived, to act rashly or shamelessly, to chase after something out of low desire — none of that concerns us at all, so long as we hit the mark in things outside our will. But where there is death, or exile, or pain, or disgrace, there we try to run, there we are struck with terror.

So, as one would expect of men who go wrong in the greatest matters, we turn our natural confidence into recklessness, desperation, rashness, shamelessness; and we turn our natural caution and self-respect into cowardice and meanness, full of fear and confusion. If a man moved his caution over to the will and the acts of the will, then simply by willing to be careful he would also have the power of avoiding what he chose. But if he moves it to things not in his power and not in his will, and tries to avoid what is in other people's power, then he must fear, he must be unsteady, he must be thrown. For death and pain are not the terrible things; the fear of death and pain is.

This is why we approve the poet who said:

	Death is not the evil, but a shameful death.

Confidence, then, ought to be used against death, and caution against the fear of death. As it is, we do the reverse: against death we use flight, and toward our judgment about it we use carelessness, rashness and indifference.

Socrates rightly used to call these things tragic masks. Masks look frightening and terrible to children because they have no experience of them, and we are affected by events for exactly the same reason. What is a child? Ignorance. What is a child? Want of knowledge. Once a child knows these things, he is no worse off than we are.

What is death? A tragic mask. Turn it round and look at it. See — it does not bite. The poor body has to be separated from the spirit, now or later, as it was separated before. So why are you upset if it happens now? If it does not happen now, it will happen afterwards. Why? So that the cycle of the universe may be completed, since it needs the present, and the future, and the past.

What is pain? A mask. Turn it round and look. The poor flesh is moved roughly, and then smoothly again. If that does not satisfy you, the door is open; if it does, put up with it. The door ought to be open on every occasion, and then we have no trouble.

What is the fruit of these judgments, then? Exactly what ought to be the finest and most fitting fruit for men who are really educated: release from being thrown about, release from fear, freedom.

On these matters we must not believe the crowd, who say only free men ought to be educated; we should rather believe the philosophers, who say only the educated are free.

"How is that?"

Like this. Is freedom anything but the power of living as we choose? "Nothing else." Then tell me, men: do you want to live in error? "We do not." So nobody who lives in error is free. Do you want to live in fear? In grief? Thrown about? "By no means." Then nobody who is in fear or grief or turmoil is free; and whoever is delivered from griefs and fears and turmoil is at the same moment delivered from slavery.

So how can we go on believing you, most excellent lawgivers, when you say, "We allow only free men to be educated"? For the philosophers say: we allow nobody to be free except the educated — that is, God does not allow it.

"So when a man has turned his own slave round in front of the praetor, has he done nothing?"

He has done something.

"What?"

He has turned his own slave round in front of the praetor.

"Nothing more?"

Yes — he is also liable to pay the five per cent tax on him.

"Well then, has the man who went through that ceremony not become free?"

No more than he has become free of being thrown about. You, who are able to turn other men round and free them — have you no master? Is money not your master, or a girl, or a boy, or some tyrant, or some friend of a tyrant? Why do you tremble, then, when you go off to face a trial of that kind?

This is why I say so often: study and keep ready the principles by which you may settle what things you ought to be confident about and what things you ought to be cautious about — brave about what does not depend on your will, careful about what does.

"But have I not read to you? Do you not know what I have been doing?"

In what? In your little essays. Show me how you stand with regard to desire and avoidance. Show me that you do not fail to get what you want and do not fall into what you would avoid. As for those long, laboured sentences, you will take them and rub them out.

"What then — did Socrates not write? And who wrote so much?"

But how? Since he could not always have somebody at hand to argue against his principles, or to be argued against in turn, he used to argue with himself and examine himself, and he was always working through some subject in a practical way. These are the things a philosopher writes. Little essays and the method I am talking about he leaves to others — to the stupid, or to those happy men who are free from disturbance and have time on their hands, or to men too foolish to reckon consequences.

And are you now, when the opportunity invites, going to go off and display what you have, and recite it, and make an idle show, and say, "See what dialogues I can make"? Do not do it, man. Say instead: see how I am not disappointed of what I want. See how I do not fall into what I would avoid. Set death in front of me and you will see. Set pain in front of me, and prison, and disgrace, and a sentence.

That is the proper display for a young man who has come out of the schools. Leave the rest to others; let nobody ever hear a word from you about it; and if anyone praises you for it, do not accept it — think yourself nobody and think you know nothing. Show only that you know this one thing: how never to be disappointed of your desire and never to fall into what you would avoid.

Let other men labour at court cases and problems and syllogisms. You labour at thinking about death, chains, the rack, exile — and do it all with confidence, relying on the one who called you to these sufferings, who judged you worthy of the post where, being stationed, you will show what the rational governing power can do when it takes its stand against forces outside our will.

And then this paradox will no longer look impossible or paradoxical: that a man ought to be cautious and courageous at once — courageous toward things that do not depend on the will, and cautious in the things that are within its power.
