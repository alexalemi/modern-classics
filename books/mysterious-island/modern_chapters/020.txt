Chapter 21: A Few Degrees Below Zero

_A few degrees below zero — Exploring the marshy southeast — The culpeos — A view of the sea — A conversation about the future of the Pacific Ocean — The ceaseless work of the infusoria — What will become of the globe — The hunt — Shelduck Marsh_

From that moment on, not a single day went by without Pencroff paying a visit to what he called, in all seriousness, his "wheat field." And woe to any insect that ventured into it! It could expect no mercy.

Toward the end of June, after endless rains, the weather turned decidedly cold. On the 29th a Fahrenheit thermometer would certainly have read no more than twenty degrees.

The next day, June 30, which corresponds to December 31 in the northern year, was a Friday. Nab remarked that the year was ending on an unlucky day. But Pencroff answered that, naturally, the next one would begin on a lucky one, which was better.

In any case, it began with a very sharp cold. Chunks of ice piled up at the mouth of the Mercy, and before long the lake froze over from end to end.

The supply of fuel had to be renewed several times. Pencroff had not waited for the river to freeze before bringing enormous timber rafts down to where they were needed. The current was a tireless engine, and it was put to work carrying driftwood until the cold came and chained it up. To the fuel the forest provided so generously, the colonists also added several cartloads of coal, which had to be fetched from the foot of the spurs of Mount Franklin. The powerful heat of the coal was warmly appreciated when the temperature fell, on July 4, to eight degrees Fahrenheit. A second fireplace had been built in the dining room, and there they all worked together.

During this cold spell Cyrus Smith had every reason to congratulate himself on having brought a small stream of Lake Grant's water down to Granite House. The water was drawn from below the frozen surface and led through the old spillway, so it stayed liquid. It arrived in an inside reservoir, which had been dug out at the corner of the back storeroom, and the overflow ran away down the well to the sea.

About this time, the weather being extremely dry, the colonists, dressed as warmly as they could manage, decided to spend a day exploring the part of the island that lay to the southeast, between the Mercy and Claw Cape. It was a vast stretch of marshland, and there might be good hunting there, since water birds were bound to swarm in it.

They reckoned on eight or nine miles out and as many back, so the day would be well used. And since it also meant exploring an unknown part of the island, the whole colony had to take part. So on July 5, at six in the morning, with dawn barely breaking, Cyrus Smith, Gédéon Spilett, Harbert, Nab and Pencroff left Granite House, armed with spears, snares, bows and arrows and carrying enough provisions, with Top frisking ahead of them.

They took the shortest way, and the shortest way was to cross the Mercy on the ice that was then choking it.

"But," the reporter rightly observed, "this is no substitute for a real bridge!"

And so the building of a "real" bridge was added to the list of jobs to be done.

It was the first time the colonists had set foot on the right bank of the Mercy and ventured among those tall, magnificent conifers, now covered in snow.

But they had not gone half a mile when a whole family of four-footed animals burst out of a thick clump of brush where they had made their home. Top's barking had put them to flight.

"Oh! They look like foxes!" cried Harbert, as he watched the whole band clear out as fast as they could go.

They were foxes, as it happened, but very large ones, and they made a sort of barking sound that seemed to astonish Top himself. He stopped short in his chase and gave the swift animals time to disappear.

The dog had a right to be surprised, since he knew no natural history. But by their barking these foxes, reddish gray in coat, with black tails ending in a white tuft, had given away who they were. Harbert gave them their true name without a moment's hesitation: "culpeos." Culpeos are often found in Chile, in the Falklands, and all along the American coasts that lie between the thirtieth and fortieth parallels. Harbert was very sorry that Top had not managed to catch one of these carnivores.

"Can you eat them?" asked Pencroff, who never looked at the island's wildlife from more than one particular point of view.

"No," answered Harbert, "but zoologists still haven't settled whether the pupil of these foxes' eyes is made for day or for night, or whether they ought to be classed with the dogs proper."

Cyrus Smith could not help smiling at the boy's remark, which showed a serious mind. As for the sailor, once these foxes could not be classed in the edible genus, he did not care what they were. All the same, he pointed out that when a poultry yard was set up at Granite House, it would be wise to take a few precautions against probable visits from these four-legged robbers. Nobody disagreed.

After rounding Jetsam Point, the colonists came upon a long beach washed by the open sea. It was then eight o'clock in the morning. The sky was perfectly clear, as it often is in long spells of hard cold. But Cyrus Smith and his companions, warmed by their walk, did not feel the sting of the air too sharply. Besides, there was no wind, and that makes a deep drop in temperature far easier to bear. A brilliant sun, which gave no warmth at all, was just rising out of the ocean, and its enormous disk hung swaying on the horizon. The sea lay smooth and blue, like a Mediterranean bay under a clear sky. Claw Cape, curved like a scimitar, tapered to a sharp point some four miles off to the southeast. To the left, the edge of the marsh ended abruptly at a little point of land that the sun's rays were now outlining in a line of fire. Certainly, in this part of Union Bay, with nothing to shelter it from the open sea, not even a sandbank, ships beaten by the east winds would have found no refuge at all. From the calm of the sea, which no shoals disturbed, from its even color, unspotted by any yellowish tinge, and above all from the absence of any reef, you could tell that this coast dropped off steeply and that the ocean covered deep abysses here. Behind them, to the west, the first lines of trees of the Far West forests rose, but four miles away. You might almost have thought you were on the desolate coast of some island in the Antarctic, overrun by ice. The colonists halted there for lunch. They lit a fire of brushwood and dried wrack, and Nab prepared a lunch of cold meat, adding a few cups of Oswego tea.

As they ate, they looked around them. This part of Lincoln Island really was barren, a sharp contrast to the whole western region. That led the reporter to remark that if chance had first thrown the castaways onto this beach, they would have formed a dismal idea of their future domain.

"I don't think we could even have reached it," the engineer answered, "because the sea here is deep, and it didn't offer us a single rock to take refuge on. In front of Granite House, at least, there were sandbanks and an islet, and they multiplied our chances of being saved. Here there's nothing but the abyss!"

"It's rather odd," observed Gédéon Spilett, "that an island this small should have such varied ground. Logically, that kind of diversity belongs only to continents of a certain size. You'd really think that the western part of Lincoln Island, so rich and fertile, was washed by the warm waters of the Gulf of Mexico, while its northern and southeastern shores stretched along some kind of Arctic sea."

"You're right, my dear Spilett," Cyrus Smith replied. "I've noticed it too. I find this island strange, in its shape as much as in its nature. It's like a summary of every aspect a continent can show, and I wouldn't be surprised if it had once been a continent."

"What! A continent in the middle of the Pacific?" cried Pencroff.

"Why not?" answered Cyrus Smith. "Why shouldn't Australia, New Ireland, everything the English geographers call Australasia, together with the archipelagos of the Pacific, once have formed a sixth part of the world, as important as Europe or Asia, as Africa or the two Americas? My mind has no trouble accepting that all the islands rising out of this vast ocean are nothing but the peaks of a continent now sunk beneath it, a continent that stood above the waters in prehistoric times."

"Like Atlantis long ago," said Harbert.

"Yes, my boy... if it ever existed, that is."

"And Lincoln Island would have been part of that continent?" asked Pencroff.

"Probably," answered Cyrus Smith, "and that would go a long way toward explaining the variety of things we see growing on it."

"And the large number of animals that still live here," Harbert added.

"Yes, my boy," the engineer replied, "and you've just handed me a new argument in support of my theory. From what we've seen, it's certain that there are a great many animals on the island, and, what's stranger, that the species are extremely varied. There's a reason for that, and in my view it's that Lincoln Island may once have been part of some vast continent that has slowly sunk beneath the Pacific."

"Then one fine day," Pencroff retorted, not seeming entirely convinced, "what's left of that old continent may disappear in its turn, and there'll be nothing at all between America and Asia?"

"Not so," answered Cyrus Smith. "There'll be the new continents that billions upon billions of tiny animals are working to build at this very moment."

"And who are these bricklayers?" asked Pencroff.

"The coral infusoria," answered Cyrus Smith. "Those microscopic creatures, by their unending labor, built Clermont-Tonnerre Island, the atolls, and the many other coral islands of the Pacific Ocean. It takes forty-seven million of them to weigh a single grain, and yet, out of the sea salts they absorb and the solid elements in the water that they take in, these tiny animals produce limestone. And that limestone forms enormous undersea foundations as hard and solid as granite. Long ago, in the first ages of creation, nature used fire, and produced the land by thrusting it up from below. But now she has handed the job over to microscopic animals, because the power of that fire inside the globe has plainly grown weaker, as the great number of volcanoes now extinct on the earth's surface shows. And I really do believe that, as century follows century and infusoria follow infusoria, this Pacific may one day turn into a vast continent, which new generations will settle and civilize in their turn."

"That'll take a while!" said Pencroff.

"Nature has time on her side," the engineer replied.

"But what's the use of new continents?" asked Harbert. "It seems to me that the habitable lands we have now are enough for humanity. And nature never does anything useless."

"Nothing useless, it's true," the engineer went on. "But here's one way the need for new continents in the future might be explained, and in exactly this tropical zone where the coral islands lie. At least, the explanation seems plausible to me."

"We're listening, Mr. Cyrus," Harbert answered.

"Here's what I think. Scientists generally agree that one day our globe will come to an end, or rather that animal and plant life will no longer be possible on it, because of the intense cooling it will undergo. Where they disagree is on the cause of that cooling. Some think it will come from the drop in the sun's temperature after millions of years. Others think it will come from the gradual dying out of the fires inside our globe, which have more influence on it than people generally suppose. I hold with the second theory myself, and I base it on this fact: the moon is truly a cooled-off body, which can no longer support life, even though the sun goes on pouring the same amount of heat onto its surface. So if the moon has cooled, it's because the inner fires to which it owed its origin, like every other body in the starry universe, have gone out completely. In any case, whatever the cause, our globe will cool one day, but the cooling will happen only little by little. And what will follow? The temperate zones, at some more or less distant time, will be no more habitable than the polar regions are now. So the populations of men, like the herds and flocks of animals, will flow back toward the latitudes that get the sun's most direct heat. An immense migration will take place. Europe, Central Asia, North America will gradually be abandoned, just like Australasia and the lower parts of South America. The plant life will follow the human migration. The flora will retreat toward the equator at the same time as the fauna. The central parts of South America and Africa will become the inhabited continents above all others. The Sami and the Samoyeds will find the climate of the polar sea on the shores of the Mediterranean. Who can tell us that, by then, the equatorial regions won't be too small to hold and feed the whole human race? So why shouldn't far-seeing nature, to give a refuge to that whole migration of plants and animals, be laying the foundations of a new continent under the equator even now, and why shouldn't she have given the infusoria the job of building it? I've often thought about all these things, my friends, and I seriously believe that the face of our globe will one day be completely transformed. As new continents rise, the seas will cover the old ones, and in centuries to come, new Columbuses will set out to discover the islands of Chimborazo, of the Himalayas or of Mont Blanc, all that is left of a drowned America, Asia and Europe. And then, finally, these new continents will in their turn become uninhabitable themselves. The heat will die out, like the heat of a body the soul has just left, and life will disappear from the globe, if not for good, then at least for a time. Perhaps then our sphere will rest, will renew itself in death, to rise again one day in higher conditions! But all that, my friends, is the secret of the Maker of all things, and, starting from the work of the infusoria, I've let myself be carried a little too far, perhaps, in prying into the secrets of the future."

"My dear Cyrus," Gédéon Spilett replied, "to me these theories are prophecies, and one day they'll come true."

"That's God's secret," said the engineer.

"That's all very well," said Pencroff, who had been listening with both ears, "but will you tell me, Mr. Cyrus, whether Lincoln Island was built by your infusoria?"

"No," answered Cyrus Smith. "It's purely volcanic in origin."

"Then it'll disappear one day?"

"Probably."

"I certainly hope we won't be on it then."

"No, don't worry, Pencroff. We won't be on it, because we have no wish to die here, and we may manage to get ourselves off it in the end."

"Meanwhile," answered Gédéon Spilett, "let's settle in as if it were for eternity. Never do anything by halves."

That ended the conversation. Lunch was over. The exploration began again, and the colonists reached the edge of the marshy region.

It was a true marsh, and it stretched all the way to the rounded coast at the southeastern end of the island, covering perhaps twenty square miles. The ground was a silt of clay and silica, mixed with a great deal of rotting plant matter. Pond algae, rushes, sedges and bulrushes covered it, with here and there a few patches of grass as thick as a heavy carpet. Frozen pools sparkled in many places in the sunlight. Neither the rains nor any river swollen by a sudden flood could have formed these reserves of water. The natural conclusion was that the marsh was fed by water seeping up through the ground, and so it was. There was even reason to fear that in the hot season the air here would fill with the foul vapors that breed marsh fevers.

Above the water plants, over the surface of the still water, fluttered a whole world of birds. A marsh hunter, or a professional shooting from a blind, could not have wasted a single shot here. Wild ducks, pintails, teal and snipe lived there in flocks, and these birds, not at all shy, let people come close to them easily.

A single charge of birdshot would certainly have brought down a few dozen of them, so tightly packed were their ranks. The colonists had to make do with arrows. The result was smaller, but the silent arrow had the advantage of not frightening the birds, which the bang of a gun would have scattered to every corner of the marsh. So this time the hunters settled for a dozen ducks, white-bodied with a cinnamon band around them, green heads, black, white and russet wings and flattened bills, which Harbert recognized as "shelducks." Top helped skillfully in catching them, and their name was given to this marshy part of the island: Shelduck Marsh. So the colonists had a plentiful reserve of water game there. When the time came, it would only be a matter of using it properly, and it was likely that several species of these birds could be, if not tamed, at least settled in the neighborhood of the lake, which would put them more directly within reach of the people who meant to eat them.

At about five in the evening Cyrus Smith and his companions started back for home. They crossed Shelduck Marsh and recrossed the Mercy on the bridge of ice.

By eight o'clock that evening they were all back at Granite House.
