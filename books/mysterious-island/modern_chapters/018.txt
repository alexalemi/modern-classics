Chapter 19: Granite House

_Cyrus Smith's plan — The front of Granite House — The rope ladder — Pencroff's dreams — The aromatic herbs — A natural warren — Bringing water to the new home — The view from the windows of Granite House_

The next day, May 22, work began on fitting out the new home. The colonists were impatient, in fact, to trade their cramped shelter in the Chimneys for this vast, healthy retreat, hollowed out of the solid rock and safe from both the sea and the sky. The Chimneys were not to be abandoned entirely, though. The engineer meant to turn them into a workshop for the heavy jobs.

Cyrus Smith's first concern was to find out exactly where on the cliff the front of Granite House lay. He went down to the beach at the foot of the enormous wall. The pick that had slipped from the reporter's hands must have fallen straight down, so all they had to do was find the pick, and they would know the spot where the hole had been broken through the granite.

The pick was easily found. And sure enough, a hole opened in a straight vertical line above the place where it had stuck in the sand, about eighty feet above the beach. A few rock pigeons were already flying in and out through that narrow opening. It really seemed as if Granite House had been discovered for their sake!

The engineer intended to divide the right-hand portion of the cavern into several rooms, opening off an entrance corridor, and to light it with five windows and a door cut through the face of the cliff. Pencroff was fine with the five windows, but he could not see the use of the door, since the old spillway gave them a natural staircase by which they could always get into Granite House easily.

"My friend," Cyrus Smith answered him, "if it's easy for us to reach our home through the spillway, it will be just as easy for others. On the contrary, I plan to block the spillway at its mouth and seal it up tight. If necessary, I'll even hide the entrance completely by building a dam and raising the level of the lake."

"Then how will we get in?" asked the sailor.

"By a ladder on the outside," replied Cyrus Smith. "A rope ladder. Once it's pulled up, no one will be able to reach our home."

"But why all these precautions?" said Pencroff. "So far the animals haven't seemed all that dangerous. And as for natives, there aren't any on our island!"

"Are you quite sure of that, Pencroff?" asked the engineer, looking straight at the sailor.

"Well, obviously we won't be sure until we've explored every part of it," Pencroff answered.

"Exactly," said Cyrus Smith, "because so far we know only a small part of it. But in any case, even if we have no enemies inside the island, they may come from outside it. These waters of the Pacific are bad waters. So let's take our precautions against anything that might happen."

Cyrus Smith was talking sense, and Pencroff, without raising any further objection, got ready to carry out his orders.

So the front of Granite House was to be lit by five windows and a door, serving what made up the "apartment," properly speaking, and also by one wide bay and a row of small round windows that would let light pour into that marvelous nave, which was to serve as the great hall. This front, eighty feet above the ground, faced east, and the rising sun greeted it with its first rays. It stretched along the section of the cliff wall that lay between the projecting corner over the mouth of the Mercy and a line drawn straight up from the heap of rocks that formed the Chimneys. That meant the bad winds — the ones from the northeast — struck it only at a slant, because the very angle of that projecting corner sheltered it. Besides, until the window frames could be made, the engineer planned to close the openings with thick shutters that would let through neither wind nor rain, and that he could disguise if he needed to.

The first job, then, was to hollow out these openings. Working that hard rock with a pick would have been too slow, and Cyrus Smith, as we know, was a man for big methods. He still had a certain amount of nitroglycerin on hand, and he put it to good use. The explosive's effect was properly confined, and under its force the granite broke open at the very places the engineer had chosen. Then pick and mattock finished shaping the pointed Gothic arches of the five windows, the big bay, the round windows, and the door. They trimmed and squared the frames, whose outlines came out rather fanciful, and a few days after the work began, Granite House was flooded with the light of the eastern sky, which reached into its most hidden depths.

Under the plan Cyrus Smith had settled on, the apartment was to be divided into five compartments, all looking out over the sea. On the right came an entrance hall served by a door, where the ladder would end. Next came a first room, thirty feet wide, which would be the kitchen; then a dining room forty feet wide; then a bedroom just as wide; and finally a "guest room," which Pencroff had insisted on, and which lay next to the great hall.

These rooms — or rather this row of rooms that made up the apartment of Granite House — were not meant to fill the whole depth of the cavern. A corridor was to run behind them, between the rooms and a long storeroom, where the tools, the provisions, and the reserves would all find plenty of space. Everything gathered on the island, plant or animal, would keep there in excellent condition, completely protected from damp. There was no shortage of room, and every item could be stored in proper order. On top of that, the colonists still had the small cave above the great cavern, which would serve as the attic of their new house.

With the plan settled, all that remained was to carry it out. So the miners turned brickmakers once again, and the bricks were brought over and stacked at the foot of Granite House.

Until now Cyrus Smith and his companions had been able to get into the cavern only by way of the old spillway. To do that, they first had to climb to Prospect Plateau by a roundabout path along the riverbank, then go down two hundred feet through the tunnel, and climb back up just as far whenever they wanted to return to the plateau. All that cost a lot of time and a great deal of effort. So Cyrus Smith decided to get on at once with making a strong rope ladder, which, once it was pulled up, would make the entrance to Granite House absolutely impossible to reach.

This ladder was made with enormous care. Its two side ropes were braided from the fibers of the kurrajong, twisted together with a hand-turned winch, and they were as strong as a heavy cable. As for the rungs, they came from a kind of red cedar whose branches were light but tough. Pencroff did the work, and he did it like a master.

Other ropes were made from plant fibers too, and a rough block and tackle was rigged at the door. That way the bricks could easily be hoisted up to the level of Granite House. Moving the materials became much simpler, and the work on the inside began right away. There was plenty of lime, and several thousand bricks lay ready for use. They had no trouble putting up the framework for the partitions — a very simple one, as it happened — and in a very short time the apartment was divided into rooms and a storeroom, just as planned.

All this work went quickly under the direction of the engineer, who handled the hammer and the trowel himself. No kind of manual labor was foreign to Cyrus Smith, and in this way he set an example for companions who were intelligent and eager. They worked with confidence, even cheerfully. Pencroff always had a joke ready — carpenter one moment, ropemaker the next, mason after that — and he spread his good humor through their whole little world. His faith in the engineer was absolute. Nothing could have shaken it. He believed Smith could take on anything and succeed at everything. The question of clothes and shoes — a serious question, certainly — the question of light through the winter nights, the farming of the fertile parts of the island, the transformation of this wild plant life into cultivated crops: all of it looked easy to him, with Cyrus Smith's help, and all of it would be done in its own good time. He dreamed of rivers turned into canals to carry the wealth of the soil, of quarries and mines to be opened, of machines for every kind of industry, of railroads — yes, railroads! — whose network would surely cover Lincoln Island one day.

The engineer let Pencroff talk. He did not trim back a single one of that good-hearted man's exaggerations. He knew how catching confidence is. He even smiled to hear him, and he said nothing of the worries the future sometimes gave him. For in this part of the Pacific, far from the shipping lanes, he had reason to fear that help might never come. The colonists would have to rely on themselves, on themselves alone, because Lincoln Island lay so far from any other land that to venture out in a boat — and any boat they built would be a makeshift one — would be a serious and dangerous business.

"But," as the sailor said, "they stood a hundred cubits above the Robinsons of the old days, who had to work a miracle for every single thing."

And in fact, they "knew." And the man who "knows" succeeds where others would merely scrape by, and would inevitably die.

During all this work Harbert distinguished himself. He was intelligent and active, quick to understand and good at carrying things out, and Cyrus Smith grew more and more attached to the boy. Harbert, for his part, felt a warm and respectful friendship for the engineer. Pencroff could see perfectly well the close bond forming between the two of them, but he was not the least bit jealous.

Nab was Nab. He was what he would always be: courage, eagerness, devotion, and self-forgetfulness in person. He had the same faith in his master as Pencroff did, but he showed it less noisily. Whenever the sailor got carried away with enthusiasm, Nab always looked as though he were answering, "But what could be more natural?" He and Pencroff were very fond of each other, and they had soon dropped all formality between them.

As for Gédéon Spilett, he did his share of the common work, and he was not the clumsiest of them, either — which never stopped surprising the sailor a little. A "journalist" who was clever not just at understanding everything, but at doing everything!

The ladder was finally put in place on May 28. It had no fewer than a hundred rungs over the eighty feet of sheer height it covered. Luckily Cyrus Smith had been able to divide it into two sections by taking advantage of an overhang in the wall that jutted out about forty feet above the ground. This ledge, carefully leveled with the pick, became a kind of landing. The first ladder was fastened to it, which cut the ladder's swing in half, and a rope made it possible to haul that lower ladder up to the level of Granite House. As for the second ladder, it was made fast both at its lower end, which rested on the ledge, and at its upper end, which was tied to the door itself. That made the climb a good deal easier. Besides, Cyrus Smith meant to install a hydraulic elevator later on, which would spare the inhabitants of Granite House all that effort and lost time.

The colonists quickly got used to the ladder. They were nimble and skillful, and Pencroff, as a sailor used to running up the ratlines of a ship's rigging, was able to give them lessons. But he had to give lessons to Top as well. The poor dog, with his four paws, was not built for this kind of exercise. Pencroff, however, was such a devoted teacher that Top ended up making his climbs quite respectably, and he was soon going up the ladder the way his fellow dogs do every day in the circus. How proud the sailor was of his pupil, words cannot say. Still, more than once, Pencroff carried him up on his back, and Top never complained about that.

It should be pointed out here that during all this work — and it was pushed hard, since the bad season was coming — the food question had not been neglected. Every day the reporter and Harbert, who had now definitely become the colony's suppliers, spent a few hours hunting. So far they had worked only Jacamar Wood, on the left bank of the river, because without a bridge or a boat no one had crossed the Mercy yet. All those immense forests that had been given the name of the Far West forests were still unexplored. That important expedition was being saved for the first fine days of the coming spring. But Jacamar Wood had game enough. Kangaroos and wild boars were plentiful there, and the hunters' iron-tipped spears, bow, and arrows did wonders. What was more, near the southwest corner of the lagoon, Harbert discovered a natural warren — a sort of slightly damp meadow covered with willows and aromatic herbs that perfumed the air: thyme, wild thyme, basil, savory, all the fragrant plants of the mint family that rabbits are so fond of.

The reporter remarked that, since the table was so clearly laid for rabbits, it would be surprising if the rabbits failed to show up, and the two hunters explored this warren carefully. In any case, it grew useful plants in abundance, and a naturalist would have found plenty of specimens of the plant kingdom to study there. Harbert gathered a good quantity of shoots of basil, rosemary, lemon balm, betony, and others, which have various medicinal properties: some good for the chest, some astringent, some for bringing down fevers, others for easing cramps or rheumatism. And when Pencroff later asked what all this harvest of herbs was for:

"To take care of us," the boy answered, "to treat us when we're sick."

"Why would we get sick, when there aren't any doctors on the island?" Pencroff replied, perfectly seriously.

There was no answer to that. But the boy gathered his harvest all the same, and it was very well received at Granite House — all the more because, along with these medicinal plants, he was able to bring in a good amount of bee balm, Monarda didyma, the plant known in North America as "Oswego tea," which makes an excellent drink.

At last, that day, by searching hard, the two hunters came upon the actual site of the warren. The ground there was riddled with holes like a colander.

"Burrows!" cried Harbert.

"Yes," the reporter replied, "I can see them."

"But is anybody living in them?"

"That's the question."

The question was soon answered. Almost at once, hundreds of little animals that looked like rabbits went scattering in every direction, so fast that even Top could not have outrun them. Hunters and dog ran their hardest, but the rodents got away from them with ease. The reporter, however, was determined not to leave the spot before he had caught at least half a dozen of these animals. He wanted to stock the larder with them first, and later on they could tame any they caught. With a few snares set at the mouths of the burrows, the operation could not fail. But at that moment they had no snares, and nothing to make them with. So they had to resign themselves to visiting every hole, poking around in it with a stick, and doing by sheer patience what they could not do any other way.

At last, after an hour of digging around, four of the rodents were caught in their holes. They were rabbits quite like their European cousins, commonly known as "American rabbits."

So the day's catch was carried back to Granite House, and it appeared at supper that evening. The residents of that warren were not to be sneezed at, for they were delicious. Here was a precious resource for the colony, and one that looked as if it would never run out.

On May 31 the partitions were finished. All that was left was to furnish the rooms, which would be the work of the long winter days. A fireplace was built in the first room, which served as the kitchen. The pipe that was to carry the smoke outside gave the makeshift stove-fitters some trouble. Cyrus Smith thought it simplest to make it out of brick clay. Since there could be no question of running it out through the plateau above, they drilled a hole in the granite over the kitchen window, and the pipe, slanting upward, ended at that hole, like the pipe of a sheet-iron stove. Perhaps — very likely, in fact — the chimney would smoke whenever the strong east winds blew straight against the front of the cliff. But those winds were rare, and anyway Master Nab, the cook, was not so particular.

When this work inside was done, the engineer turned to blocking the mouth of the old spillway where it opened onto the lake, so as to shut off any way in by that route. Blocks of rock were rolled into the opening and firmly cemented. Cyrus Smith did not yet carry out the plan he had made to drown that opening under the waters of the lake by raising them back to their old level with a dam. For now he was content to hide the blockage with grasses, shrubs, and brush, planted in the cracks between the rocks, which the coming spring would make grow thick and wild.

He did, however, use the spillway to bring a thin stream of the lake's fresh water all the way down to their new home. A small channel cut below the surface of the lake did the job, and this branch of a pure, never-failing source delivered twenty-five to thirty gallons a day. So Granite House would never be short of water.

At last everything was finished — and none too soon, for the bad season was coming on. Thick shutters could close the windows in the front of the cliff until the engineer had time to make window glass.

Gédéon Spilett had very artistically arranged plants of all kinds in the projections of the rock around the windows, along with long, trailing grasses, so that the openings were framed in picturesque greenery that looked charming.

The inhabitants of this solid, healthy, and safe home could only be delighted with their work. From the windows their gaze could roam over a boundless horizon, closed off to the north by the two Mandible Capes and to the south by Claw Cape. All of Union Bay spread out magnificently before them. Yes, these good colonists had every reason to be satisfied, and Pencroff was not stingy in his praise of what he jokingly called "his apartment on the fifth floor above the mezzanine!"
