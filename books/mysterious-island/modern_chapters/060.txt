Chapter 19: Fire Against Water

_Cyrus Smith's account of his exploration — The shipbuilding is pushed on — A last visit to the corral — The battle between fire and water — What is left on the surface of the island — They decide to launch the ship — The night of March 8 to 9_

The next morning, January 8, after a day and a night spent at the corral, with everything there put in order, Cyrus Smith and Ayrton came back to Granite House.

At once the engineer gathered his companions together and told them that Lincoln Island was in terrible danger, a danger no human power could avert.

"My friends," he said — and his voice betrayed a deep emotion — "Lincoln Island is not one of those islands that will last as long as the globe itself. It is doomed to be destroyed, sooner or later. The cause lies inside it, and nothing can save it!"

The colonists looked at one another, and then at the engineer. They could not understand him.

"Explain yourself, Cyrus!" said Gédéon Spilett.

"I will," answered Cyrus Smith. "Or rather, I'll simply pass on to you the explanation Captain Nemo gave me during the few minutes we spoke in private."

"Captain Nemo!" cried the colonists.

"Yes. It was the last service he wanted to do us before he died!"

"The last service!" cried Pencroff. "The last service! You'll see — dead as he is, he'll do us more!"

"But what did Captain Nemo tell you?" asked the reporter.

"Here it is, then, my friends," answered the engineer. "Lincoln Island is not built like the other islands of the Pacific. Captain Nemo told me of a particular feature of its structure, and sooner or later that feature must break apart the island's framework under the sea."

"Break apart! Lincoln Island! Come on, now!" cried Pencroff, and for all the respect he had for Cyrus Smith, he could not help shrugging his shoulders.

"Listen to me, Pencroff," the engineer went on. "This is what Captain Nemo had observed, and what I observed for myself yesterday, when I explored the Dakkar Crypt. That cave runs on under the island as far as the volcano, and the only thing separating it from the volcano's central chimney is the wall that closes off its far end. Now, that wall is scored with cracks and fissures, and they are already letting through the sulfurous gases that build up inside the volcano."

"Well?" asked Pencroff, his forehead deeply furrowed.

"Well, I found that those cracks were widening under the pressure from inside. The basalt wall is splitting little by little, and before very long it will give way and let in the seawater that fills the cave."

"Good!" replied Pencroff, trying to joke one more time. "The sea will put out the volcano, and that'll be the end of it!"

"Yes, that will be the end of it!" answered Cyrus Smith. "The day the sea bursts through that wall and pours down the central chimney into the bowels of the island, where the molten matter is boiling — on that day, Pencroff, Lincoln Island will blow up, just as Sicily would blow up if the Mediterranean rushed down into Etna!"

The colonists made no answer to so positive a statement. They understood what danger hung over them.

And it must be said that Cyrus Smith was not exaggerating in the least. Plenty of people have had the idea that volcanoes — nearly all of which rise on the shores of seas or lakes — might perhaps be put out by opening a channel to let the water in. What they did not know was that this would risk blowing up a piece of the globe, the way a boiler bursts when its steam is suddenly driven up by a blast of heat. Water rushing into a closed space whose temperature may run to thousands of degrees would turn to steam with such sudden force that no casing in the world could hold it.

There was no doubt, then, that the island, threatened with a frightful upheaval that could not be far off, would last only as long as the wall of the Dakkar Crypt lasted. It was not even a question of months, or of weeks. It was a question of days — perhaps of hours!

The colonists' first feeling was deep grief! They did not think of the danger to themselves. They thought of the destruction of this ground that had given them shelter — this island they had made fruitful, this island they loved, which they had hoped to see flourishing one day! So much toil spent for nothing, so much work lost!

Pencroff could not hold back a big tear that slid down his cheek, and he made no attempt to hide it.

The conversation went on for some time longer. They talked over whatever chances the colonists could still cling to. But in the end they agreed that there was not an hour to lose: the building and fitting out of the ship must be pushed on with tremendous energy, and that, now, was the one chance of rescue for the inhabitants of Lincoln Island!

So every pair of hands was called to the work. What good would it do now to reap, to harvest, to hunt, to add to the stores of Granite House? What was still in the storeroom and the pantries would be enough, and more than enough, to provision the ship for a voyage, however long it might be! What mattered was that the ship should be ready for the colonists before the inevitable catastrophe came.

The work was taken up again with feverish zeal. By about January 23 the ship was half planked. Until then, nothing had changed at the summit of the volcano. There were still the same vapors and smoke, mixed with flames and shot through with red-hot stones, pouring out of the crater. But during the night of January 23 to 24, under the thrust of the lava, which had risen to the level of the volcano's first plateau, the volcano had its top knocked off: the cone that sat on it like a hat. There was a frightful crash. At first the colonists thought the island was breaking apart. They rushed out of Granite House.

It was about two o'clock in the morning.

The sky was on fire. The upper cone — a mass a thousand feet high, weighing billions of pounds — had been hurled down onto the island, and the ground shook. Luckily, that cone had been leaning to the north, and it fell on the plain of sand and tuff that stretched between the volcano and the sea. The crater, gaping wide now, threw up such an intense light toward the sky that, simply by its glare reflecting off the clouds, the whole atmosphere seemed to be glowing red-hot. At the same time a torrent of lava swelled up at the new summit and spilled over in long cascades, like water overflowing a basin that is too full, and a thousand serpents of fire went crawling down the volcano's slopes.

"The corral! The corral!" cried Ayrton.

It was indeed toward the corral that the lava was heading, because of the way the new crater faced. And so it was the fertile parts of the island — the sources of Red Creek, Jacamar Wood — that were threatened with immediate destruction.

At Ayrton's cry the colonists had dashed to the onaggas' stable. The cart was harnessed. They all had a single thought: to race to the corral and set free the animals penned up inside it.

Before three o'clock in the morning they had reached the corral. Frightful bellowing told them plainly enough how terrified the mouflons and the goats were. Already a torrent of red-hot matter, of liquefied minerals, was pouring down from the mountain's shoulder onto the meadow and eating away that side of the palisade. Ayrton flung the gate open, and the maddened animals bolted in every direction.

An hour later the boiling lava filled the corral. It turned the water of the little stream that ran through it to steam, set the house on fire so that it blazed up like a heap of straw, and devoured the palisade down to the very last post. Of the corral, nothing was left!

The colonists had wanted to fight this invasion. They had tried — but madly and uselessly, for man is helpless in the face of such great cataclysms.

Day had come — January 24. Before going back to Granite House, Cyrus Smith and his companions wanted to see what direction this flood of lava would finally take. The ground sloped down overall from Mount Franklin toward the east coast, and there was reason to fear that, in spite of the dense cover of Jacamar Wood, the torrent might spread as far as Prospect Plateau.

"The lake will protect us," said Gédéon Spilett.

"I hope so!" answered Cyrus Smith, and that was all he answered.

The colonists would have liked to go as far as the plain where the upper cone of Mount Franklin had come crashing down, but by then the lava barred their way. It was flowing down the valley of Red Creek on one side, and down the valley of Fall River on the other, boiling both streams away as it went. There was no possibility of crossing that torrent. On the contrary, they had to fall back before it. The volcano, with its crown knocked off, was unrecognizable. It now ended in a sort of flat table that had replaced the old crater. Two gaps, gouged out of its rim on the south and the east, poured out lava without a pause, so that it ran down in two separate streams. Above the new crater a cloud of smoke and ash blended with the vapors massed in the sky over the island. Great claps of thunder burst out and mingled with the rumbling of the mountain. From its mouth flew blazing rocks, hurled more than a thousand feet up, which burst in the clouds and scattered like grapeshot. The sky answered the eruption with flash after flash of lightning.

By about seven o'clock in the morning the colonists, who had taken refuge at the edge of Jacamar Wood, could no longer hold their position. Not only were the flying rocks starting to rain down around them, but the lava, overflowing the bed of Red Creek, threatened to cut the Corral Road. The first rows of trees caught fire, and their sap, suddenly turned to steam, made them burst like fireworks, while others, not so full of moisture, stood untouched in the middle of the flood.

The colonists had started back along the Corral Road. They walked slowly — backward, so to speak. But because of the slope of the ground the torrent was gaining fast toward the east, and as soon as the lower layers of lava hardened, fresh boiling sheets of it immediately flowed over them.

Meanwhile the main stream, the one in the valley of Red Creek, grew more and more threatening. That whole part of the forest was ablaze, and enormous rolls of smoke billowed above the trees, whose roots were already crackling in the lava.

The colonists stopped near the lake, half a mile from the mouth of Red Creek. A question of life or death was about to be decided for them.

Cyrus Smith, who was used to reckoning up a serious situation, and who knew he was speaking to men able to hear the truth, whatever it might be, now said:

"Either the lake will stop this stream, and part of the island will be saved from total devastation, or the stream will overrun the Far West forests, and not one tree, not one plant will be left on the face of the ground. Then all we'll have to look forward to on these bare rocks is death — and the explosion of the island won't keep us waiting long for it!"

"Then," cried Pencroff, crossing his arms and stamping his foot on the ground, "there's no use working on the boat, is there?"

"Pencroff," answered Cyrus Smith, "a man must do his duty to the very end!"

At that moment the river of lava, having cut its way through the fine trees it was devouring, reached the edge of the lake. At that spot the ground rose somewhat — a ridge that might perhaps have been enough to hold back the torrent, if only it had been higher.

"To work!" cried Cyrus Smith.

They understood the engineer's idea at once.

They had to dam up the torrent, so to speak, and so force it to spill over into the lake.

The colonists ran to the shipyard. They brought back shovels, picks and axes, and there, by heaping up earth and felling trees, they managed in a few hours to raise a dike three feet high and a few hundred paces long. When they had finished, it seemed to them that they had been working for only a few minutes!

It was just in time. Almost at once the molten matter reached the foot of the ridge. The stream swelled like a river in full flood trying to burst its banks, and it threatened to top the one obstacle that could keep it from overrunning the whole Far West... But the dike held it back, and after a minute's hesitation — a terrible minute — it plunged into Lake Grant over a fall twenty feet high.

Breathless, without making a move, without saying a word, the colonists stood and watched that struggle between the two elements.

What a sight it was, that battle between water and fire! What pen could describe that scene of marvelous horror, and what brush could paint it? The water hissed as it boiled away at the touch of the seething lava. The steam, shot into the air, whirled up to a height beyond all measuring, as if the safety valves of some immense boiler had suddenly been thrown open. But however vast the mass of water in the lake, it was bound to be used up in the end, since nothing was replacing it, while the torrent, fed from a source that could never run dry, kept rolling in fresh waves of red-hot matter.

The first lava to fall into the lake hardened instantly and piled up, so that it soon rose above the surface. Over it slid more lava, which turned to stone in its turn, but a little farther out toward the middle. In this way a jetty formed and threatened to fill the lake in completely. The lake could not overflow, because whatever water it had to spare was being spent as steam. Hissing and sizzling tore the air with a deafening noise, and the clouds of vapor, carried off by the wind, fell back as rain over the sea. The jetty grew longer and longer, and blocks of hardened lava piled up one on top of another. Where calm water had once spread out, there now rose an enormous heap of smoking rocks, as if an upheaval of the ground had thrown up thousands of reefs. Imagine those waters churned up by a hurricane and then suddenly frozen solid by a cold of four degrees below zero, and you will have some idea of how the lake looked three hours after the unstoppable torrent burst into it.

This time water was bound to be beaten by fire.

All the same, it was a stroke of luck for the colonists that the flow of lava had been turned toward Lake Grant. It gave them a few days' breathing space. Prospect Plateau, Granite House and the shipyard were spared for the moment. And those few days had to be used to finish planking the ship and caulking her carefully. Then they would launch her and take refuge aboard, and rig her once she was afloat in her own element. With the threat of an explosion that might destroy the island, there was no longer any safety in staying on land. Granite House, that retreat so secure until now, might close its granite walls on them at any minute!

For the next six days, from January 25 to 30, the colonists worked on the ship as hard as twenty men could have done. They barely took any rest, and the glare of the flames leaping out of the crater let them keep going night and day. The volcano was still pouring out lava, though perhaps less of it. That was fortunate, because Lake Grant was almost completely filled in, and if fresh lava had slid over the surface of the old, it would certainly have spread over Prospect Plateau, and from there down onto the beach.

But if the island was partly protected on that side, the same was not true of its western part.

For the second stream of lava, the one that had followed the valley of Fall River, could not meet any obstacle there: it was a wide valley, and the ground sloped away on either side of the stream. So the red-hot liquid had spread out through the Far West forest. At that time of year, when the trees were dried out by the scorching heat, the forest caught fire instantly, so that the blaze spread both along the bases of the trunks and through the high branches, whose interlacing helped the fire along. It even seemed that the stream of flame was racing faster through the treetops than the stream of lava along the ground.

And so the animals, maddened with terror, wild beasts and others alike — jaguars, wild boars, capybaras, koulas, game with fur and game with feathers — fled toward the Mercy and into Shelduck Marsh, beyond the road to Balloon Harbor. But the colonists were too busy with their work to pay any attention even to the most dangerous of these animals. Besides, they had left Granite House. They had not even wanted to take shelter in the Chimneys, and they were camping in a tent near the mouth of the Mercy.

Every day Cyrus Smith and Gédéon Spilett climbed up to Prospect Plateau. Sometimes Harbert went with them — never Pencroff, who could not bear to see the island in its new shape, laid waste so utterly!

It was a heartbreaking sight indeed. The whole wooded part of the island was now stripped bare. A single clump of green trees still stood at the tip of Serpentine Peninsula. Here and there a few blackened stumps, their branches gone, twisted up like grimaces. The ground where the forests had been was more barren than Shelduck Marsh. The invasion of the lava had been complete. Where all that wonderful greenery had once grown, the ground was now nothing but a wild jumble of volcanic tuff. The valleys of Fall River and of the Mercy no longer sent a single drop of water to the sea, and the colonists would have had no way to quench their thirst if Lake Grant had dried up completely. But luckily its southern tip had been spared and made a sort of pond, holding all that was left of the drinkable water on the island. To the northwest the spurs of the volcano stood out in harsh, sharp ridges, like a gigantic claw pressed down on the ground. What a painful sight, what a dreadful view, and what grief for these colonists! They had had a fertile estate, covered with forests, watered by streams, rich with crops, and in an instant they found themselves set down on a devastated rock where, without their stores, they would not even have found enough to live on!

"It breaks your heart!" said Gédéon Spilett one day.

"Yes, Spilett," answered the engineer. "May Heaven give us time to finish that ship, our only refuge now!"

"Don't you think, Cyrus, that the volcano seems to be calming down? It's still throwing out lava, but less of it, if I'm not mistaken!"

"That makes no difference," answered Cyrus Smith. "The fire is still burning hot in the bowels of the mountain, and the sea may rush in at any moment. We're like passengers on a ship that's being eaten up by a fire they can't put out, who know that sooner or later it will reach the powder magazine! Come, Spilett, come, and let's not lose an hour!"

For eight more days — that is, until February 7 — the lava went on spreading, but the eruption kept within the limits already described. What Cyrus Smith feared above all was that the molten matter might pour out onto the beach, and in that case the shipyard would not have been spared. About that time, however, the colonists felt tremors in the framework of the island, and these alarmed them in the highest degree.

It was now February 20. It would take another month before the ship was fit to go to sea. Would the island hold out till then? Pencroff and Cyrus Smith meant to launch the ship as soon as her hull was watertight enough. The deck, the upperworks, the fitting out of the inside and the rigging could all be done afterward. What mattered was that the colonists should have a sure refuge off the island. It might even be wise to take the ship round to Balloon Harbor — that is, as far as possible from the center of the eruption — because at the mouth of the Mercy, between the islet and the granite wall, she ran the risk of being crushed if the island broke apart. So all the workers' efforts went toward finishing the hull.

In this way they reached March 3, and they could count on the launch taking place in about ten days.

Hope came back into the hearts of these colonists, who had been so sorely tried during this fourth year of their stay on Lincoln Island! Even Pencroff seemed to come a little way out of the dark, silent gloom into which the ruin and devastation of his domain had plunged him. It is true that by then he could think of nothing but the ship, on which all his hopes were fixed.

"We'll finish her," he said to the engineer. "We'll finish her, Mr. Cyrus, and it's high time, because the season's getting on, and we'll soon be right in the middle of the equinox. Well, if we have to, we'll put in at Tabor Island and spend the winter there! But Tabor Island, after Lincoln Island! Ah, the misery of it! Who'd ever have thought I'd live to see such a thing!"

"Let's hurry!" the engineer would always answer.

And they worked without losing a moment.

"My master," Nab asked a few days later, "if Captain Nemo were still alive, do you think all this would have happened?"

"Yes, Nab," answered Cyrus Smith.

"Well, I don't believe it!" Pencroff murmured in Nab's ear.

"Neither do I!" Nab answered seriously.

During the first week of March, Mount Franklin became menacing again. Thousands of threads of glass, spun from the fluid lava, fell like rain on the ground. The crater filled with lava once more, and it spilled over down every side of the volcano. The torrent ran over the surface of the hardened tuff and finished off the thin skeletons of trees that had survived the first eruption. This time the stream followed the southwest shore of Lake Grant, ran on past Glycerin Creek and overran Prospect Plateau. This last blow to the colonists' work was a terrible one. Of the mill, the poultry-yard buildings and the stables, nothing was left. The terrified birds scattered in every direction. Top and Jup showed signs of the greatest fear, and their instinct warned them that a catastrophe was near. A good many of the island's animals had died in the first eruption. Those that survived found no other refuge than Shelduck Marsh, apart from a few that Prospect Plateau had sheltered. But that last retreat was finally closed to them too, and the river of lava, spilling over the edge of the granite wall, began to pour its cataracts of fire down onto the beach. The sublime horror of that sight is beyond all description. At night it looked like a Niagara of molten iron, with glowing vapors above it and boiling masses below!

The colonists were driven into their last stronghold, and although the upper seams of the ship were not yet caulked, they resolved to launch her!

So Pencroff and Ayrton made the preparations for the launch, which was to take place the next day, the morning of March 9.

But during that night of March 8 to 9, an enormous column of steam burst out of the crater and rose, amid frightful detonations, more than three thousand feet into the air. The wall of the Dakkar Crypt had obviously given way under the pressure of the gases, and the sea, rushing down the central chimney into that fiery abyss, had turned to steam all at once. But the crater could not give that steam a wide enough outlet. An explosion that could have been heard a hundred miles away shook the layers of the air. Pieces of mountains fell back into the Pacific, and within a few minutes the ocean covered the place where Lincoln Island had been.
