Chapter 17: The Last Hours of Captain Nemo

_The last hours of Captain Nemo — The wishes of a dying man — A keepsake for his friends of a day — Captain Nemo's coffin — Some advice to the colonists — The supreme moment — At the bottom of the sea_

Day had come. Not a ray of it reached that deep crypt. The sea, at high tide just then, blocked its opening. But the artificial light that streamed out in long beams through the sides of the Nautilus had not weakened, and the sheet of water still glowed all around the floating vessel.

By now an extreme weariness had overwhelmed Captain Nemo, who had sunk back onto the divan. There could be no thought of carrying him to Granite House. He had made it plain that he wished to stay among these marvels of the Nautilus, which millions could not have bought, and there to wait for a death that could not be long in coming.

Through a fairly long collapse that left him almost unconscious, Cyrus Smith and Gédéon Spilett watched the sick man's condition closely. It was plain that the captain was fading little by little. Strength was about to fail that body, once so powerful and now the frail shell of a soul about to escape. All the life left in him was gathered in his heart and his head.

The engineer and the reporter had consulted in low voices. Was there any care they could give this dying man? If they could not save him, could they at least prolong his life for a few days? He himself had said that there was no remedy, and he was waiting calmly for death, which he did not fear.

"There's nothing we can do," said Gédéon Spilett.

"But what's he dying of?" asked Pencroff.

"He's burning out," answered the reporter.

"Still," the sailor went on, "if we carried him out into the open air, into the full sun, maybe he'd revive?"

"No, Pencroff," answered the engineer, "there's nothing to try! Besides, Captain Nemo would never agree to leave his ship. He's lived aboard the Nautilus for thirty years, and it's aboard the Nautilus that he means to die."

Captain Nemo must have heard Cyrus Smith's answer, for he raised himself a little, and in a weaker voice, but one still perfectly clear, he said:

"You are right, sir. I must die here, and I wish to. And so I have a request to make of you."

Cyrus Smith and his companions had come closer to the divan, and they arranged its cushions so that the dying man was better supported.

Then they could see his gaze come to rest on all the marvels of that salon, lit by electric rays softened through the arabesques of a luminous ceiling. One after another he looked at the paintings that hung on the splendid tapestries of the walls, those masterpieces of the Italian, Flemish, French and Spanish masters; at the marble and bronze statuettes standing on their pedestals; at the magnificent organ set against the after bulkhead; then at the glass cases arranged around a central basin, in which the most wonderful products of the sea were on display — marine plants, zoophytes, strings of pearls beyond price. And at last his eyes stopped on the motto inscribed over the doorway of this museum, the motto of the Nautilus:

    Mobilis in Mobili.

It seemed as though he wanted to caress with his eyes, one last time, these masterpieces of art and nature to which he had limited his horizon through so many years spent in the depths of the seas!

Cyrus Smith had respected the silence Captain Nemo was keeping. He waited for the dying man to speak again.

After a few minutes, during which, no doubt, he saw his whole life pass before him once more, Captain Nemo turned toward the colonists and said to them:

"You believe, gentlemen, that you owe me some gratitude?..."

"Captain, we would give our lives to prolong yours!"

"Good," Captain Nemo went on, "good!... Promise me that you will carry out my last wishes, and I shall be repaid for everything I have done for you."

"We promise," answered Cyrus Smith.

And with that promise he bound his companions and himself alike.

"Gentlemen," the captain resumed, "tomorrow I shall be dead."

With a sign he stopped Harbert, who wanted to protest.

"Tomorrow I shall be dead, and I wish to have no other tomb than the Nautilus. She is my coffin — mine! All my friends rest at the bottom of the sea, and I wish to rest there too."

A deep silence met these words of Captain Nemo's.

"Listen to me carefully, gentlemen," he went on. "The Nautilus is imprisoned in this cave, whose entrance has been raised up. But if she cannot leave her prison, she can at least sink into the abyss beneath it and keep my mortal remains there."

The colonists listened to the dying man's words with reverence.

"Tomorrow, after my death, Mr. Smith," the captain went on, "you and your companions will leave the Nautilus, for all the riches she contains must disappear with me. Only one keepsake will remain to you of Prince Dakkar, whose story you now know. This casket... there... holds several millions' worth of diamonds — most of them mementos of the time when, as a father and a husband, I almost believed in happiness — and a collection of pearls gathered by my friends and me at the bottom of the sea. With this treasure you will be able, when the day comes, to do good things. In hands like yours and your companions', Mr. Smith, money cannot be a danger. So from up there I shall be a partner in your works, and I have no fear of them!"

After a few moments of rest, made necessary by his extreme weakness, Captain Nemo went on in these words:

"Tomorrow you will take this casket and leave this salon, closing its door behind you. Then you will climb back up onto the platform of the Nautilus, and you will close the hatch and fasten it down with its bolts."

"We will, Captain," answered Cyrus Smith.

"Good. You will then go aboard the boat that brought you here. But before you leave the Nautilus, go to the stern, and there open two large valves that are set at the waterline. The water will flow into the tanks, and the Nautilus will sink little by little beneath the surface, to come to rest at the bottom of the abyss."

And at a gesture from Cyrus Smith, the captain added:

"Have no fear! You will be burying only a dead man!"

Neither Cyrus Smith nor any of his companions would have thought it right to raise any objection with Captain Nemo. These were his last wishes he was handing on to them, and all they had to do was carry them out.

"I have your promise, gentlemen?" Captain Nemo added.

"You have it, Captain," answered the engineer.

The captain made a sign of thanks and asked the colonists to leave him alone for a few hours. Gédéon Spilett insisted on staying near him in case a crisis came on, but the dying man refused, saying:

"I shall live until tomorrow, sir!"

They all left the salon, passed through the library and the dining room, and came forward to the engine room, where the electrical apparatus was installed that supplied the Nautilus with mechanical power as well as heat and light.

The Nautilus was a masterpiece that held masterpieces, and the engineer was filled with wonder.

The colonists climbed up onto the platform, which rose seven or eight feet above the water. There they stretched out beside a thick lens-shaped pane of glass that closed off a kind of great eye, from which a sheaf of light was pouring. Behind this eye lay a hollowed-out cabin that held the wheels of the rudder, where the helmsman stood when he steered the Nautilus through the layers of the deep — layers that the electric rays must have lit up over a considerable distance.

At first Cyrus Smith and his companions stayed silent, for they were deeply moved by what they had just seen and what they had just heard. Their hearts ached when they thought that the man whose arm had come to their rescue so many times, this protector they would have known for barely a few hours, was on the eve of death!

Whatever judgment posterity might pass on the deeds of that existence — an existence, so to speak, beyond the human — Prince Dakkar would always remain one of those strange figures whose memory can never fade.

"There's a man for you!" said Pencroff. "Can you believe he lived like that, at the bottom of the ocean? And when I think that maybe he didn't find any more peace down there than anywhere else!"

"The Nautilus," Ayrton observed then, "might have let us leave Lincoln Island and reach some inhabited land."

"A thousand devils!" cried Pencroff. "I'm not the man who'd ever risk steering a boat like that. Sailing on the sea — fine! But under the sea — no!"

"I'd think," the reporter answered, "that handling an underwater machine like this Nautilus must be very easy, Pencroff, and that we'd soon get used to it. No storms, no collisions to fear. A few feet below the surface, the waters of the sea are as calm as a lake's."

"Could be!" the sailor shot back. "But I'd rather have a good stiff blow aboard a well-rigged ship. A boat is made to go on the water, not under it."

"My friends," the engineer answered, "there's no point — at least where the Nautilus is concerned — in arguing over this question of underwater ships. The Nautilus isn't ours, and we have no right to dispose of her. Nor could she be of any use to us in any case. Besides the fact that she can no longer get out of this cavern, whose entrance is now closed by the uplift of the basalt rocks, Captain Nemo wants her to go down with him after his death. His wish is explicit, and we will carry it out."

After a conversation that went on a while longer, Cyrus Smith and his companions went back down inside the Nautilus. There they took some food and then returned to the salon.

Captain Nemo had come out of the collapse that had overwhelmed him, and his eyes had regained their brilliance. Something like a smile could be seen forming on his lips.

The colonists went up to him.

"Gentlemen," the captain said to them, "you are brave men, honest and good. You have all given yourselves without reserve to the common work. I have often watched you. I have loved you — I love you!... Your hand, Mr. Smith!"

Cyrus Smith held out his hand to the captain, who pressed it warmly.

"That is good!" he murmured.

Then, going on:

"But that is enough talk of me! I have to talk to you of yourselves, and of Lincoln Island, where you have found refuge... You are thinking of leaving it?"

"To come back to it, Captain!" Pencroff answered quickly.

"Come back to it?... Indeed, Pencroff," the captain answered with a smile, "I know how much you love this island. It has been transformed by your efforts, and it is truly yours!"

"Our plan, Captain," Cyrus Smith said then, "would be to make a gift of it to the United States, and to found on it a port of call for our navy — one that would be very well placed in this part of the Pacific."

"You are thinking of your country, gentlemen," the captain answered. "You are working for its prosperity, for its glory. You are right. One's country!... That is where one must return! That is where one ought to die!... And I — I am dying far from everything I have loved!"

"Do you have any last wish to pass on?" the engineer asked quickly. "Any keepsake to give to the friends you may have left behind in those mountains of India?"

"No, Mr. Smith. I have no friends left! I am the last of my race... and I have been dead a long time to everyone I ever knew... But let us come back to you. Solitude, isolation, are sad things, beyond human strength... I am dying of having believed that a man could live alone!... So you must try everything to leave Lincoln Island and to see again the land where you were born. I know that those wretches destroyed the boat you had built..."

"We're building a ship," said Gédéon Spilett, "a ship big enough to carry us to the nearest lands. But if we do manage to leave, sooner or later we'll come back to Lincoln Island. Too many memories bind us to it for us ever to forget it!"

"It is here that we came to know Captain Nemo," said Cyrus Smith.

"It's only here that we'll find the whole memory of you!" Harbert added.

"And it is here that I shall rest in the eternal sleep, if..." the captain answered.

He hesitated, and instead of finishing his sentence he simply said:

"Mr. Smith, I would like to speak with you... with you alone!"

The engineer's companions, respecting the dying man's wish, withdrew.

Cyrus Smith stayed shut in with Captain Nemo for only a few minutes, and soon he called his friends back. But he told them nothing of the secret things the dying man had wished to confide to him.

Gédéon Spilett then watched the sick man with the closest attention. It was obvious that the captain was now held up by nothing but strength of spirit, which would soon be unable to fight back against his physical decline.

The day ended without any change appearing. The colonists did not leave the Nautilus for a moment. Night had come, though there was no way to tell it in that crypt.

Captain Nemo was not in pain, but he was sinking. His noble face, paled by the approach of death, was calm. From his lips there sometimes escaped words that could barely be made out, touching on various incidents of his strange existence. They could feel the life withdrawing little by little from that body, whose hands and feet were already cold.

Once or twice more he spoke to the colonists gathered beside him, and he smiled at them with that last smile that lasts on even into death.

At last, a little after midnight, Captain Nemo made one supreme movement, and he managed to cross his arms over his chest, as though he wished to die in that attitude.

By about one o'clock in the morning, all the life left in him had taken refuge in his eyes alone. A last fire shone in those eyes, from which so many flames had once flashed. Then, murmuring the words "God and country!", he gently breathed his last.

Cyrus Smith bent down and closed the eyes of the man who had been Prince Dakkar and who was now no longer even Captain Nemo.

Harbert and Pencroff were weeping. Ayrton wiped away a furtive tear. Nab was on his knees beside the reporter, turned to stone.

Cyrus Smith raised his hand above the dead man's head.

"May God have his soul!" he said, and, turning to his friends, he added:

"Let us pray for the one we have lost!"

A few hours later the colonists were keeping the promise they had made to the captain. They were carrying out the dead man's last wishes.

Cyrus Smith and his companions left the Nautilus, taking with them the one keepsake their benefactor had left them, that casket which held a hundred fortunes.

The marvelous salon, still flooded with light, had been carefully closed. Then the sheet-iron door of the hatch was bolted down, so that not a drop of water could get into the rooms of the Nautilus.

Then the colonists went down into the boat, which was moored alongside the submarine vessel.

The boat was brought round to the stern. There, at the waterline, were the two large valves that connected with the tanks used to make the vessel dive.

The valves were opened, the tanks filled, and the Nautilus, sinking little by little, disappeared beneath the surface.

But the colonists could still follow her down through the deep layers of water. Her powerful light lit up the clear water, while the crypt went dark again. Then that vast outpouring of electric radiance faded at last, and soon the Nautilus, now Captain Nemo's coffin, lay at rest at the bottom of the sea.
