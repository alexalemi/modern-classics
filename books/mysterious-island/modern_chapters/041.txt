Chapter 20: Shark Gulf

_The night at sea — Shark Gulf — Confidences — Preparations for winter — The bad season comes early — Great cold — Work indoors — After six months — A photographic plate — An unexpected incident_

Things turned out just as Pencroff had foreseen, for his hunches could not be wrong. The wind freshened, and from a good breeze it rose to a gale — that is, it reached a speed of forty to forty-five miles an hour, the kind of wind in which a ship at sea would have been down to close-reefed topsails, with her topgallant masts struck. Now, it was about six o'clock when the Bonadventure came abreast of the gulf, and at that moment the ebb was running, so it was impossible to get in. They had no choice but to stand off from the land, for even if he had wanted to, Pencroff could not have reached the mouth of the Mercy either. So he rigged his jib on the mast as a storm sail, and he waited, keeping her head toward the shore.

Very fortunately, though the wind was very strong, the sea, sheltered by the coast, did not build up much. So there was no need to dread the heavy seas that break aboard, which are a great danger to small boats. The Bonadventure would no doubt not have capsized, for she was well ballasted; but huge masses of water crashing onto her deck could have put her in danger if the hatches had given way. Pencroff, skilled sailor that he was, was ready for anything. Certainly he had enormous confidence in his boat, but he waited for daylight with a certain anxiety all the same.

During that night Cyrus Smith and Gédéon Spilett had no chance to talk together, and yet the sentence the engineer had spoken into the reporter's ear was well worth one more discussion of that mysterious influence which seemed to rule over Lincoln Island. Gédéon Spilett could not stop thinking about this new and inexplicable incident, about this fire that had appeared on the coast of the island. He had really seen that fire! His companions, Harbert and Pencroff, had seen it just as he had! That fire had shown them where the island lay on that dark night, and they had never doubted that it was the engineer's hand that had lit it. And now here was Cyrus Smith declaring flatly that he had done nothing of the kind!

Gédéon Spilett promised himself that he would come back to this incident as soon as the Bonadventure was home again, and that he would urge Cyrus Smith to tell their companions about these strange events. Perhaps then they would decide to make, all together, a complete search of every part of Lincoln Island.

Be that as it may, no fire was lit that evening on those still-unknown shores that formed the entrance of the gulf, and the little boat went on standing off the land all night long.

When the first gleams of dawn began to show on the eastern horizon, the wind, which had eased a little, shifted two points and let Pencroff run more easily into the narrow entrance of the gulf. At about seven o'clock in the morning, the Bonadventure, having borne away toward North Mandible Cape, carefully entered the passage and ventured out onto those waters, which were shut in by the strangest frame of lava.

"Now there," said Pencroff, "is a stretch of sea that would make a wonderful anchorage, where whole fleets could maneuver at their ease!"

"What's especially curious," Cyrus Smith observed, "is that this gulf was formed by two flows of lava, thrown out by the volcano and piled up by one eruption after another. The result is that the gulf is completely sheltered on every side, and it's likely that even in the worst winds the sea in here is as calm as a lake."

"No doubt," the sailor went on, "since the only way the wind can get in is through that narrow gap between the two capes, and even then the north cape covers the south one, so it's very hard for a squall to get in. Honestly, our Bonadventure could stay in here from one end of the year to the other without even straining at her anchors!"

"It's a bit big for her!" the reporter pointed out.

"Ah, Mr. Spilett," replied the sailor, "I grant you it's too big for the Bonadventure. But if the fleets of the Union ever need a safe shelter in the Pacific, I don't believe they'll ever find a better anchorage than this one!"

"We're in the Shark's jaws," Nab remarked then, alluding to the shape of the gulf.

"Right inside its jaws, my good Nab!" replied Harbert. "But you're not afraid they'll snap shut on us, are you?"

"No, Mr. Harbert," replied Nab, "and yet I don't much like this gulf! It has a wicked look about it!"

"Well, now!" cried Pencroff. "Here's Nab running down my gulf, just when I'm thinking of making a present of it to America!"

"But are the waters deep, at least?" asked the engineer. "Because what's enough for the Bonadventure's keel wouldn't be enough for our ironclads."

"Easy to check," replied Pencroff.

And the sailor let down a long rope that served him as a sounding line, with a block of iron tied to the end of it. The line measured about fifty fathoms, and it ran out to the very end without touching bottom.

"There we are," said Pencroff. "Our ships can come in here! They won't run aground!"

"Indeed," said Cyrus Smith, "this gulf is a real abyss. But considering the volcanic origin of the island, it's not surprising that the sea floor should have hollows like this."

"It looks, too," Harbert observed, "as though these walls had been cut straight down. I really believe that at their foot, even with a line five or six times as long, Pencroff wouldn't find bottom."

"That's all very well," the reporter said then, "but I'll point out to Pencroff that his anchorage is missing one important thing!"

"And what's that, Mr. Spilett?"

"A gap, a cutting of some kind, that gives a way into the interior of the island. I can't see a single spot where anyone could set foot!"

And in fact the high lava walls, very steep, offered not one place along the whole edge of the gulf where a landing could be made. It was an impassable rampart, like the fjords of Norway, only more barren still. The Bonadventure, skimming so close along those high walls that she almost touched them, did not find even a ledge where her passengers could step ashore.

Pencroff consoled himself by saying that with the help of blasting they could easily break open that wall when it became necessary. And since there was plainly nothing to be done in this gulf, he steered his boat toward the narrow entrance and came out of it at about two o'clock in the afternoon.

"Phew!" said Nab, with a sigh of satisfaction.

You really would have said that good Nab did not feel at ease in those enormous jaws!

From Mandible Cape to the mouth of the Mercy it was hardly more than about eight miles. So they set a course for Granite House, and the Bonadventure, with the wind free in her sails, ran along the coast a mile out. The enormous lava rocks soon gave way to those fantastic dunes among which the engineer had been found in such a strange way, and which the seabirds haunted by the hundreds.

At about four o'clock, leaving the point of the islet to port, Pencroff entered the channel that separated it from the coast, and at five o'clock the Bonadventure's anchor bit into the sandy bottom at the mouth of the Mercy.

The colonists had left their home three days before. Ayrton was waiting for them on the beach, and Master Jup came joyfully to meet them, giving out hearty grunts of satisfaction.

So the whole of the island's coastline had now been explored, and no suspicious trace had been seen. If some mysterious being lived there, it could only be under cover of the impenetrable woods of Serpentine Peninsula, where the colonists had not yet carried their search.

Gédéon Spilett talked these things over with the engineer, and they agreed that they would draw their companions' attention to the strange character of certain incidents that had taken place on the island, the latest of which was one of the most inexplicable.

So Cyrus Smith, coming back to the business of a fire lit on the shore by an unknown hand, could not help asking the reporter for the twentieth time:

"But are you sure you really saw it? Wasn't it a partial eruption of the volcano, a meteor of some kind?"

"No, Cyrus," replied the reporter. "It was certainly a fire lit by a human hand. Besides, ask Pencroff and Harbert. They saw it just as I saw it myself, and they'll confirm what I say."

And so it came about that a few days later, on the evening of April 25, when all the colonists were gathered on Prospect Plateau, Cyrus Smith spoke up and said:

"My friends, I think I ought to call your attention to certain things that have happened on the island, and I'd very much like to have your opinion about them. These things are, so to speak, supernatural..."

"Supernatural!" cried the sailor, puffing out a cloud of tobacco smoke. "Could our island be supernatural?"

"No, Pencroff, but mysterious, certainly," replied the engineer, "unless you can explain to us what Spilett and I have not been able to understand so far."

"Go on, Mr. Cyrus," replied the sailor.

"Well, then," said the engineer, "have you understood how it could have happened that, after I fell into the sea, I was found a quarter of a mile inland, without being aware of having been moved?"

"Unless, while you were unconscious..." said Pencroff.

"That isn't possible," replied the engineer. "But let's go on. Have you understood how Top was able to find your shelter, five miles from the cave where I was lying?"

"The dog's instinct..." replied Harbert.

"A strange instinct!" the reporter pointed out. "Because in spite of the rain and wind that were raging that night, Top arrived at the Chimneys dry and without a speck of mud on him!"

"Let's go on," the engineer went on. "Have you understood how our dog was so strangely thrown up out of the water of the lake, after his fight with the dugong?"

"No! Not really, I admit," replied Pencroff. "And the wound the dugong had in its side, a wound that looked as if it had been made by a cutting blade, makes no more sense either."

"Let's go on again," Cyrus Smith resumed. "Have you understood, my friends, how that lead pellet came to be in the body of the young peccary? How that chest came ashore so luckily, without any trace of a shipwreck? How that bottle with the document in it turned up just when it did, on our first trip out to sea? How our canoe broke its mooring and came down the current of the Mercy to join us exactly when we needed it? How, after the invasion of the monkeys, the ladder was so conveniently sent back down from the heights of Granite House? And how, last of all, the document that Ayrton claims he never wrote fell into our hands?"

Cyrus Smith had just listed, without forgetting a single one, the strange events that had taken place on the island. Harbert, Pencroff and Nab looked at one another, not knowing what to answer, for the string of incidents, grouped together like this for the first time, could not fail to astonish them in the highest degree.

"Upon my word," Pencroff said at last, "you're right, Mr. Cyrus, and it's hard to explain those things!"

"Well, my friends," the engineer went on, "one last event has been added to those, and it's every bit as incomprehensible as the others!"

"What is it, Mr. Cyrus?" Harbert asked quickly.

"When you came back from Tabor Island, Pencroff," the engineer went on, "you say a fire appeared to you on Lincoln Island?"

"Certainly," replied the sailor.

"And you're quite sure you saw that fire?"

"As sure as I see you."

"You too, Harbert?"

"Oh, Mr. Cyrus!" cried Harbert. "That fire was shining like a star of the first magnitude!"

"But wasn't it a star?" the engineer insisted.

"No," replied Pencroff, "because the sky was covered with heavy clouds, and anyway a star wouldn't have been so low on the horizon. But Mr. Spilett saw it as well as we did, and he can confirm what we say!"

"I'll add," said the reporter, "that the fire was very bright, and that it threw out a sort of sheet of electric light."

"Yes! Yes! Exactly..." replied Harbert. "And it was certainly up on the heights of Granite House."

"Well, my friends," replied Cyrus Smith, "on that night of October 19 to 20, neither Nab nor I lit a fire on the coast."

"You didn't...?" cried Pencroff, so utterly astonished that he could not even finish his sentence.

"We never left Granite House," replied Cyrus Smith, "and if a fire appeared on the coast, it was some other hand than ours that lit it!"

Pencroff, Harbert and Nab were dumbfounded. There could have been no illusion: a fire really had struck their eyes during that night of October 19 to 20!

Yes! They had to admit it: a mystery existed! An inexplicable influence — clearly well disposed toward the colonists, but highly provoking to their curiosity — was making itself felt on Lincoln Island, and always, it seemed, at exactly the right moment. Was there, then, some being hidden in its deepest retreats? That was what they would have to find out, at any cost!

Cyrus Smith also reminded his companions of the odd behavior of Top and Jup when they used to prowl around the mouth of the well that connected Granite House with the sea, and he told them that he had explored that well without discovering anything suspicious. In the end, the conclusion of this conversation was a decision, taken by every member of the colony, to search the whole island from end to end as soon as the fine season returned.

But from that day on, Pencroff seemed troubled. This island, which he had made into his own personal property, no longer seemed to belong to him entirely. It seemed to him that he shared it with another master, to whom, like it or not, he felt himself subject. He and Nab often talked about these inexplicable things, and both of them, being much inclined by their very natures to believe in marvels, were not far from thinking that Lincoln Island was under the rule of some supernatural power.

Meanwhile the bad days had come with the month of May — the November of the northern latitudes. The winter looked likely to be hard and to come early. So the work of preparing for winter was taken in hand without delay.

Besides, the colonists were well prepared to meet this winter, however hard it might be. They had no shortage of felt clothing, and the mouflons, numerous by then, had supplied plenty of the wool needed to make that warm cloth.

It goes without saying that Ayrton had been given these comfortable clothes too. Cyrus Smith offered to let him come and spend the bad season at Granite House, where he would be better housed than at the corral, and Ayrton promised to do so as soon as the last jobs at the corral were finished. This he did around the middle of April. From then on Ayrton shared the common life and made himself useful at every opportunity; but always humble and sad, he never took part in his companions' pleasures!

For most of this third winter the colonists spent on Lincoln Island, they stayed shut up in Granite House. There were very great storms and terrible squalls that seemed to shake the rocks to their very foundations. Immense tidal surges threatened to sweep right over the island, and any ship anchored off its shores would certainly have been lost with all hands. Twice during one of these storms the Mercy rose so high that they feared the bridge and the little bridges would be carried away, and they even had to strengthen the ones on the beach, which vanished under the water whenever the sea beat against the shore.

You can well imagine that such gales, as violent as waterspouts and full of mingled rain and snow, did damage on Prospect Plateau. The mill and the poultry yard suffered particularly. The colonists often had to make urgent repairs there, or the lives of the birds would have been seriously threatened.

In these spells of really bad weather, a few pairs of jaguars and bands of monkeys would venture right up to the edge of the plateau, and there was always the fear that the nimblest and boldest of them, driven by hunger, would manage to get across the creek — which, besides, gave them an easy crossing when it was frozen. Crops and domestic animals would then have been destroyed without fail if not for a constant watch, and they often had to open fire to keep these dangerous visitors at a respectful distance. So the winterers never ran short of work, for besides the jobs outdoors there were always a thousand things to fit out and improve in Granite House.

There were also some fine hunts, made in the hard frosts on the vast Shelduck Marsh. Gédéon Spilett and Harbert, helped by Jup and Top, never wasted a shot among those myriads of ducks, snipe, teal, pintails and lapwings. Getting to that rich hunting ground was easy, too, either by going along the Balloon Harbor road after crossing the bridge over the Mercy, or by going around the rocks of Jetsam Point, and the hunters never went more than two or three miles from Granite House.

So the four months of winter passed — and they were truly harsh: June, July, August and September. But all in all, Granite House did not suffer too much from the bad weather. The same was true of the corral, which, being less exposed than the plateau and largely sheltered by Mount Franklin, caught only the tail ends of the gales, already broken by the forests and the high rocks of the coast. So the damage there was slight, and Ayrton's busy, skillful hands were enough to repair it quickly when, in the second half of October, he went back to spend a few days at the corral.

During this winter no new inexplicable incident occurred. Nothing strange happened, although Pencroff and Nab were on the lookout for the smallest events they might have been able to link to a mysterious cause. Even Top and Jup no longer prowled around the well, and gave no sign of uneasiness. So it seemed that the series of supernatural incidents had been interrupted, although they often talked about them during the evenings at Granite House, and it remained firmly agreed that the island would be searched down to its hardest-to-explore corners. But an event of the gravest kind, whose consequences might be disastrous, came along to turn Cyrus Smith and his companions away from their plans for a while.

It was October. The fine season was coming back fast. Nature was renewing herself under the rays of the sun, and amid the evergreen foliage of the conifers that lined the edge of the woods, the new leaves of the nettle trees, banksias and deodars were already appearing.

You will remember that Gédéon Spilett and Harbert had several times taken photographic views of Lincoln Island.

Now, on October 17, at about three o'clock in the afternoon, Harbert, tempted by the clearness of the sky, had the idea of photographing the whole of Union Bay, which lay facing Prospect Plateau, from Mandible Cape to Claw Cape.

The horizon stood out beautifully sharp, and the sea, gently rippling under a soft breeze, lay as still in the distance as the waters of a lake, flecked here and there with sparkles of light.

The camera had been set up at one of the windows of the great hall of Granite House, and so it looked down over the beach and the bay. Harbert went about it as he usually did, and once he had his negative on its glass plate, he went to fix it with the chemicals that were kept in a dark little closet of Granite House.

When he came back out into full daylight and examined it carefully, Harbert noticed on the plate a little, almost invisible dot that marked the sea horizon. He tried to make it disappear by washing it again and again, but he could not manage it.

"It's a flaw in the glass," he thought.

And then he was curious enough to examine this flaw with a strong lens, which he unscrewed from one of the telescopes.

But he had hardly looked when he let out a cry, and the plate nearly slipped from his hands.

Running at once to the room where Cyrus Smith was, he held out the plate and the lens to the engineer and pointed to the little spot.

Cyrus Smith examined the dot. Then, seizing his spyglass, he rushed to the window.

The spyglass swept slowly along the horizon and finally stopped on the suspicious point, and Cyrus Smith, lowering it, said only one word:

"A ship!"

And indeed, a ship was in sight of Lincoln Island!
