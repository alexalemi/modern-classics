Chapter 6: Why the Wire Went Dead

_Plans for an expedition — Ayrton at the corral — A visit to Balloon Harbor — Pencroff's remarks aboard the Bonadventure — A telegram sent to the corral — No answer from Ayrton — Setting out the next day — Why the wire no longer works — A gunshot_

Meanwhile, the colonists' great concern was to carry out the complete exploration of the island that they had decided on. That exploration would now have two aims: first, to find the mysterious being whose existence could no longer be disputed; and at the same time, to find out what had become of the pirates — what hideout they had chosen, what sort of life they were leading, and what the colonists might have to fear from them.

Cyrus Smith wanted to set out without delay. But since the expedition would last several days, it had seemed sensible to load the cart with camping gear and various utensils that would make it easier to set up their stops along the way. Now, just then one of the onaggas had hurt its leg and could not be harnessed. It needed a few days' rest, and they decided they could put off their departure by a week, to November 20, without any harm done. In that latitude, the month of November corresponds to May in the northern regions. So it was the fine season. The sun was reaching the Tropic of Capricorn and giving the longest days of the year. The time would be ideal for the planned expedition — an expedition which, even if it did not achieve its main goal, could prove rich in discoveries, especially in the island's plants and animals, since Cyrus Smith meant to explore the dense Far West forests that stretched all the way to the tip of Serpentine Peninsula.

It was agreed that during the nine days before their departure they would put the finishing touches on the work on Prospect Plateau.

Ayrton, however, needed to go back to the corral, where the domestic animals required his care. So it was decided that he would spend two days there, and that he would not come back to Granite House until he had well stocked the stables.

Just as he was about to leave, Cyrus Smith asked him whether he would like one of them to go with him, pointing out that the island was less safe than it used to be.

Ayrton answered that there was no need: he could handle the work alone, and besides, he was not afraid of anything. If anything happened at the corral or in the neighborhood, he would warn the colonists at once by sending a telegram to Granite House.

So Ayrton left at dawn on the 9th, taking the cart, harnessed to a single onagga, and two hours later the electric bell announced that he had found everything in order at the corral.

During those two days, Cyrus Smith set about carrying out a project that was to put Granite House beyond the reach of any surprise attack for good. The idea was to hide completely the upper mouth of the old spillway, at the southern corner of Lake Grant. It was already walled up with masonry and half hidden under grass and plants. Nothing could have been easier, since all it took was to raise the level of the lake by two or three feet, and the opening would then be completely under water.

And to raise that level, all they had to do was build a dam across each of the two channels that had been cut from the lake — the ones that fed Glycerin Creek and the creek that ran to the great waterfall. The colonists were all called in to help with this work, and the two dams — which, after all, were no more than seven or eight feet wide and three feet high — were quickly put up out of blocks of stone, well cemented together.

When the job was finished, it would have been impossible to suspect that at that end of the lake there was an underground channel through which the lake's overflow had once poured out.

It goes without saying that the little side channel that kept Granite House's tank supplied and worked the elevator had been carefully preserved, so that they would never in any circumstances run short of water. Once the elevator was raised, this safe and comfortable retreat could defy any surprise or sudden attack.

This work had been dispatched quickly, and Pencroff, Gédéon Spilett and Harbert found time to make a quick trip out to Balloon Harbor. The sailor was very anxious to know whether the convicts had paid a visit to the little cove at the far end of which the Bonadventure lay at anchor.

"The thing is," he pointed out, "those gentlemen came ashore on the south coast, and if they followed the shoreline, I'm afraid they may have found the little harbor. And in that case I wouldn't give you half a dollar for our Bonadventure."

Pencroff's fears were not without some foundation, and a visit to Balloon Harbor seemed very much in order.

So the sailor and his companions set out in the afternoon of November 10, well armed. Pencroff, slipping two balls into each barrel of his gun so that everyone could see, kept shaking his head, which boded no good for anyone who came too close to him — "beast or man," as he said. Gédéon Spilett and Harbert took their guns too, and at about three o'clock all three of them left Granite House.

Nab went with them as far as the bend of the Mercy, and when they had crossed, he raised the bridge. It was agreed that a gunshot would announce the colonists' return, and that at that signal Nab would come back and reconnect the two banks of the river.

The little party went straight down the harbor road toward the south coast of the island. It was a distance of only three and a half miles, but Gédéon Spilett and his companions took two hours to cover it. That was because they searched the whole edge of the road, both on the side of the thick forest and on the side of Shelduck Marsh. They found no trace of the fugitives. No doubt, not yet knowing how many colonists there were or what means of defense they had, the convicts had made for the least accessible parts of the island.

When Pencroff reached Balloon Harbor, he was enormously pleased to see the Bonadventure riding quietly at anchor in the narrow inlet. And in fact, Balloon Harbor was so well hidden among those high rocks that no one could spot it from either the sea or the land — not unless they were right on top of it or right inside it.

"Well," said Pencroff, "those scoundrels haven't been here yet. Tall grass suits snakes better, and it's obviously in the Far West that we'll find them again."

"And a very lucky thing, too," Harbert added. "If they'd found the Bonadventure, they'd have seized her to get away, and that would have kept us from going back to Tabor Island any time soon."

"Indeed," the reporter answered. "It'll be important to take a document there telling where Lincoln Island lies and where Ayrton lives now, in case the Scottish yacht comes back to fetch him."

"Well, the Bonadventure's still right here, Mr. Spilett!" the sailor replied. "She and her crew are ready to sail at the first signal!"

"I think, Pencroff, that's something to do as soon as our expedition around the island is finished. After all, it's possible that this stranger — if we manage to find him — knows a great deal about both Lincoln Island and Tabor Island. Let's not forget that he's unquestionably the author of the document, and perhaps he knows what to expect about the yacht's return!"

"A thousand devils!" cried Pencroff. "Who on earth can he be? He knows us, this character, and we don't know him! If he's just a castaway, why is he hiding? We're decent people, I'd say, and nobody minds the company of decent people! Did he come here of his own free will? Can he leave the island if he feels like it? Is he still here? Is he gone?..."

As they talked, Pencroff, Harbert and Gédéon Spilett had gone aboard and were walking around the deck of the Bonadventure. All of a sudden the sailor, who had been examining the bitt that the anchor cable was made fast to, cried out:

"Well, I'll be! Now that's a strong one!"

"What's the matter, Pencroff?" asked the reporter.

"The matter is, I didn't tie that knot!"

And Pencroff pointed to a rope that lashed the cable to the bitt itself, to keep it from slipping.

"What do you mean, you didn't?" asked Gédéon Spilett.

"No! I'd swear to it. That's a reef knot, and I always tie two half hitches — a knot that never works loose."

"You must have made a mistake, Pencroff."

"I did not make a mistake!" the sailor declared. "You get that in your hands without thinking, and the hand doesn't make mistakes!"

"Then the convicts must have come aboard?" asked Harbert.

"I don't know about that," answered Pencroff, "but one thing's certain: somebody hauled up the Bonadventure's anchor and then dropped it again! And look here! Here's another proof. Somebody's paid out more of the anchor cable, and its chafing gear — the old canvas we wrap around the cable so it won't wear through where it runs out the hawsehole — isn't sitting at the hawsehole anymore. I'm telling you again, somebody has used our boat!"

"But if the convicts had used her, they'd either have plundered her or else they'd have escaped in her..."

"Escaped!... Where to?... Tabor Island?..." Pencroff shot back. "Do you really think they'd have risked it in a boat this small?"

"Besides, we'd have to assume they knew about the islet," the reporter answered.

"Be that as it may," said the sailor, "as sure as I'm Bonadventure Pencroff of the Vineyard, our Bonadventure has been sailing without us!"

The sailor was so positive that neither Gédéon Spilett nor Harbert could argue with what he said. It was clear that the boat had been moved, to some extent, since Pencroff had brought her back to Balloon Harbor. As far as the sailor was concerned, there was no doubt whatever that the anchor had been raised and then sent back down to the bottom. And why would anyone do those two things, unless the boat had been used for some expedition?

"But how could we have failed to see the Bonadventure sailing past the island?" the reporter pointed out. He was determined to raise every possible objection.

"Oh, Mr. Spilett," the sailor answered, "all you have to do is leave at night with a good breeze, and in two hours you're out of sight of the island!"

"All right," Gédéon Spilett went on. "I'll ask it again: what reason would the convicts have had to use the Bonadventure, and why, after using her, would they have brought her back to the harbor?"

"Oh, Mr. Spilett," the sailor answered, "let's just put it down among the inexplicable things and not think about it anymore! What mattered was that the Bonadventure should be here, and she is. The trouble is, if the convicts took her a second time, she might not turn up in her place again!"

"Then, Pencroff," said Harbert, "maybe it would be wise to bring the Bonadventure round to Granite House?"

"Yes and no," Pencroff answered. "Or rather, no. The mouth of the Mercy is a bad place for a boat, and the sea is rough there."

"But if we hauled her up on the sand, right to the foot of the Chimneys?..."

"Maybe... yes..." Pencroff answered. "Anyway, since we've got to leave Granite House for a fairly long expedition, I think the Bonadventure will be safer here while we're away, and we'd do well to leave her here until the island's been cleaned out of those rascals."

"I think so too," said the reporter. "At least if the weather turns bad she won't be exposed the way she would be at the mouth of the Mercy."

"But what if the convicts come to visit her again?" said Harbert.

"Well, my boy," Pencroff answered, "if they didn't find her here, they'd soon go looking for her over by Granite House, and while we're gone there'd be nothing to stop them taking her! So I think, like Mr. Spilett, that we ought to leave her at Balloon Harbor. But when we come back, if we haven't rid the island of those scoundrels, it'll be wise to bring our boat round to Granite House until she has no more nasty visits to fear."

"Agreed. Let's go!" said the reporter.

When Pencroff, Harbert and Gédéon Spilett got back to Granite House, they told the engineer what had happened, and he approved their plans for the present and for the future. He even promised the sailor that he would study the part of the channel lying between the islet and the coast, to see whether it might be possible to make an artificial harbor there by building dams. That way the Bonadventure would always be within reach, under the colonists' eyes, and under lock and key if need be.

That same evening they sent Ayrton a telegram asking him to bring back a couple of goats from the corral, which Nab wanted to get used to living in the meadows of the plateau. Oddly enough, Ayrton did not acknowledge the message the way he usually did. This rather surprised the engineer. But it could be that Ayrton was not at the corral just then, or even that he was already on his way back to Granite House. In fact, two days had gone by since he left, and it had been decided that he would be back on the evening of the 10th, or early on the morning of the 11th at the latest.

So the colonists waited for Ayrton to appear on the heights of Prospect Plateau. Nab and Harbert even kept watch near the bridge, so as to lower it as soon as their companion arrived.

But by ten o'clock that night there was still no sign of Ayrton. So they thought it best to send another telegram, asking for an immediate reply.

The bell at Granite House stayed silent.

Then the colonists grew very uneasy. What had happened? Was Ayrton no longer at the corral? Or if he was still there, was he no longer free to move? Should they go to the corral on such a dark night?

They talked it over. Some wanted to go, others to stay.

"But," said Harbert, "maybe there's been some accident to the telegraph, and it isn't working anymore?"

"That could be," said the reporter.

"Let's wait until tomorrow," answered Cyrus Smith. "It's possible, in fact, that Ayrton didn't receive our message, or even that we didn't receive his."

So they waited — not, as you can imagine, without a certain anxiety.

At the first light of day, on November 11, Cyrus Smith sent the electric current through the wire again, and got no answer.

He tried again. Same result.

"We're off to the corral!" he said.

"And well armed!" Pencroff added.

It was decided at once that Granite House would not be left on its own, and that Nab would stay there. After going with his companions as far as Glycerin Creek, he would raise the bridge, and then, hidden behind a tree, he would watch for their return or for Ayrton's.

If the pirates showed up and tried to force the crossing, he would try to stop them with his gun, and in the last resort he would take refuge in Granite House, where, once the elevator was raised, he would be safe.

Cyrus Smith, Gédéon Spilett, Harbert and Pencroff were to go straight to the corral, and if they did not find Ayrton there, they would beat the woods all around it.

At six o'clock in the morning the engineer and his three companions had crossed Glycerin Creek, and Nab took up his post behind a low bank of earth, topped by a few tall dragon trees, on the left bank of the stream.

After leaving Prospect Plateau, the colonists immediately took the Corral Road. They carried their guns over their arms, ready to fire at the slightest sign of hostility. The two carbines and the two guns had been loaded with ball.

On either side of the road the undergrowth was thick and could easily have hidden criminals, who, thanks to their weapons, would have been truly dangerous.

The colonists walked quickly and in silence. Top went ahead of them, sometimes running along the road, sometimes making a little detour into the woods, but always silent and showing no sign of sensing anything unusual. And they could count on it that the faithful dog would not let himself be taken by surprise, and that he would bark at the slightest hint of danger.

As they followed the road, Cyrus Smith and his companions also followed the telegraph wire that linked the corral with Granite House. After walking about two miles, they had still not noticed any break in it. The poles were in good condition, the insulators intact, the wire evenly stretched. From that point on, however, the engineer noticed that the wire seemed less taut than before. And at last, when they reached pole No. 74, Harbert, who was out in front, stopped and shouted:

"The wire's broken!"

His companions hurried forward and reached the spot where the young man had stopped.

There the pole lay overturned across the road. So the break in the wire was confirmed, and it was obvious that messages from Granite House could not have reached the corral, nor messages from the corral Granite House.

"It wasn't the wind that knocked down this pole," Pencroff observed.

"No," answered Gédéon Spilett. "The earth has been dug out around its foot. It was pulled up by human hands."

"What's more, the wire's broken," Harbert added, pointing to the two ends of the iron wire, which had been violently snapped.

"Is the break fresh?" asked Cyrus Smith.

"Yes," answered Harbert. "It certainly hasn't been long since it was broken."

"To the corral! To the corral!" cried the sailor.

The colonists were now halfway between Granite House and the corral. That meant they still had two and a half miles to go. They broke into a run.

There was good reason to fear that something serious had happened at the corral. No doubt Ayrton might have sent a telegram that had never arrived, and that in itself was not what should worry his companions. But — and this was harder to explain — Ayrton, who had promised to come back the evening before, had not reappeared. And finally, it was not for nothing that all communication between the corral and Granite House had been cut. Who but the convicts had any reason to cut it?

So the colonists ran, their hearts tight with dread. They had grown truly attached to their new companion. Were they about to find him struck down by the very men whose leader he had once been?

Soon they came to the place where the road ran alongside the little stream, branching off Red Creek, that watered the corral's meadows. Here they slowed their pace, so as not to be out of breath at the moment when a fight might become necessary. Their guns were no longer at half-cock but fully cocked. Each man kept watch on one side of the forest. Top was letting out a few low growls, which did not bode well.

At last the palisade appeared through the trees. There was no sign of any damage. The gate was shut, as usual. A deep silence hung over the corral. Neither the usual bleating of the mouflons nor Ayrton's voice could be heard.

"Let's go in!" said Cyrus Smith.

And the engineer stepped forward, while his companions kept watch twenty paces behind him, ready to fire.

Cyrus Smith lifted the inside latch of the gate and was about to push one of its leaves open when Top barked furiously. A shot rang out above the palisade, and a cry of pain answered it.

Harbert, struck by a bullet, lay on the ground!
