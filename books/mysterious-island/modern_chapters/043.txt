Chapter 2: Six Against Fifty

_Discussions — Forebodings — A proposal from Ayrton — It is accepted — Ayrton and Pencroff on Grant Islet — The convicts of Norfolk Island — Their plans — Ayrton's heroic attempt — His return — Six against fifty_

There could be no more doubt about the pirates' intentions. They had dropped anchor a short distance from the island, and it was plain that the next day they meant to come ashore in their boats!

Cyrus Smith and his companions were ready to act, but however determined they were, they must not forget to be careful. Perhaps their presence could still be kept hidden, if the pirates were content to land on the shore without going up into the interior of the island. It was quite possible, after all, that they had no other plan than to take on fresh water at the watering place at the mouth of the Mercy, and it was not impossible that the bridge, a mile and a half upstream, and the fittings of the Chimneys might escape their notice.

But why had that flag been run up to the brig's gaff? Why that cannon shot? Pure bravado, no doubt — unless it was a sign that they were taking possession! Cyrus Smith now knew that the ship was formidably armed. And what did the colonists of Lincoln Island have to answer the pirates' cannon? A few guns, nothing more.

"All the same," Cyrus Smith pointed out, "here we're in a position no one can take. The enemy can't find the mouth of the old spillway now that it's hidden under reeds and grass, so there's no way for them to get into Granite House."

"But our plantings, our poultry yard, our corral — everything, everything!" cried Pencroff, stamping his foot. "They can lay it all waste, wreck it all in a few hours!"

"Everything, Pencroff," answered Cyrus Smith, "and we have no way of stopping them."

"Are there many of them? That's the question," said the reporter then. "If there are only a dozen, we'll know how to stop them. But forty, fifty, maybe more!..."

"Mr. Smith," said Ayrton, stepping up to the engineer, "will you give me permission for something?"

"For what, my friend?"

"To go out to that ship and find out how strong her crew is."

"But, Ayrton..." the engineer answered, hesitating, "you'd be risking your life..."

"Why not, sir?"

"That's more than your duty."

"I have more than my duty to do," answered Ayrton.

"You'd take the canoe out to the ship?" asked Gédéon Spilett.

"No, sir. I'll swim. The canoe couldn't get through where a man can slip along just under the surface."

"Do you realize the brig is a mile and a quarter from the coast?" said Harbert.

"I'm a good swimmer, Mr. Harbert."

"It's risking your life, I tell you," the engineer said again.

"That doesn't matter," answered Ayrton. "Mr. Smith, I'm asking this of you as a favor. It may be a way for me to rise again in my own eyes!"

"Go, Ayrton," answered the engineer, who felt that a refusal would have hurt the former convict deeply — the convict who had become an honest man again.

"I'll come with you," said Pencroff.

"You don't trust me!" Ayrton answered sharply.

Then, more humbly:

"Alas!"

"No! No!" Cyrus Smith broke in warmly. "No, Ayrton! Pencroff doesn't distrust you! You've misunderstood him."

"That's right," answered the sailor. "What I'm proposing is to go with Ayrton as far as the islet, and no farther. It's possible — not likely, but possible — that one of those rascals has landed there, and if so, two men won't be too many to keep him from raising the alarm. I'll wait for Ayrton on the islet, and he'll go on to the ship alone, since that's what he offered to do."

With that settled, Ayrton got ready to go. His plan was daring, but it could succeed, thanks to the darkness of the night. Once he reached the ship, Ayrton could hang on to the bobstays under the bowsprit or to the chainplates where the shrouds were fastened along her side, and from there he could count the convicts and perhaps overhear what they meant to do.

Ayrton and Pencroff went down to the shore, followed by their companions. Ayrton undressed and rubbed himself with grease, so as to suffer less from the water, which was still cold. He might, after all, have to stay in it for several hours.

Meanwhile Pencroff and Nab had gone to fetch the canoe, which was moored a few hundred paces farther up, on the bank of the Mercy, and by the time they came back Ayrton was ready to leave.

A blanket was thrown over Ayrton's shoulders, and the colonists came up to shake his hand.

Ayrton got into the canoe with Pencroff.

It was half past ten at night when the two of them disappeared into the darkness. Their companions went back to the Chimneys to wait for them.

They crossed the channel easily, and the canoe came in to land on the far shore of the islet. They did this with some care, in case pirates were prowling about there. But after a good look around, it seemed certain that the islet was deserted. So Ayrton, with Pencroff behind him, crossed it at a quick pace, startling the birds that nested in the holes in the rocks. Then, without a moment's hesitation, he threw himself into the sea and swam noiselessly toward the ship, whose exact position was now marked by a few lights that had been lit not long before.

As for Pencroff, he crouched in a hollow in the rocks along the shore and waited for his companion to come back.

Meanwhile Ayrton swam with powerful strokes, gliding through the water without making even the faintest ripple. His head barely broke the surface, and his eyes were fixed on the dark mass of the brig, whose lights were reflected in the sea. He thought only of the duty he had promised to carry out, and gave no thought at all to the dangers he was running — not only on board the ship, but in these very waters, where sharks often came. The current was carrying him, and he was moving quickly away from the coast.

Half an hour later, without having been seen or heard, Ayrton slipped in under the surface, reached the ship, and caught hold of the bowsprit's bobstays with one hand. Then he got his breath back, and, pulling himself up by the chains, he managed to reach the very tip of the brig's head, the beak that juts out beneath the bowsprit. A few pairs of sailors' trousers were hanging there to dry. He pulled on a pair. Then, having wedged himself in firmly, he listened.

No one was asleep on board the brig. Quite the opposite. They were arguing, singing, laughing. And these were the remarks, mixed with oaths, that struck Ayrton most:

"Fine bargain we got in this brig!"

"She sails well, the Speedy! She lives up to her name!"

"The whole Norfolk navy can come chasing after her! Let 'em try!"

"Hurrah for her commander!"

"Hurrah for Bob Harvey!"

What Ayrton felt when he heard this scrap of conversation will be understood once it is known that in this Bob Harvey he had just recognized one of his old companions from Australia, a bold seaman who had taken up his own criminal schemes where Ayrton had left them off. Bob Harvey had seized this brig in the waters around Norfolk Island. She had been loaded with weapons, ammunition, utensils and tools of every kind, bound for one of the Sandwich Islands — what we now call Hawaii. His whole gang had gone aboard, and, pirates now after being convicts, these wretches were scouring the Pacific, destroying ships and massacring their crews, fiercer even than the Malay pirates!

The convicts were talking loudly, boasting of their exploits as they drank far more than they should, and this is what Ayrton was able to make out:

The Speedy's present crew was made up entirely of English prisoners who had escaped from Norfolk Island.

And here is what Norfolk Island is.

At latitude 29° 2′ south and longitude 165° 42′ east, to the east of Australia, lies a small island some fifteen miles around, overlooked by Mount Pitt, which rises eleven hundred feet above sea level. That is Norfolk Island, which has become the seat of a penal settlement where the most unmanageable convicts from the English prisons are penned up. There are five hundred of them there, held under iron discipline and the threat of terrible punishments, guarded by a hundred and fifty soldiers and a hundred and fifty officials under the orders of a governor. It would be hard to imagine a worse gathering of villains. Now and then — though it is rare — in spite of the extreme watch kept over them, a number of them manage to escape by seizing ships they take by surprise, and then they roam the island groups of Polynesia.

That is what this Bob Harvey and his companions had done. That is what Ayrton himself had once wanted to do. Bob Harvey had seized the brig Speedy while she lay at anchor in sight of Norfolk Island. Her crew had been massacred, and for a year now the ship, turned pirate vessel, had been beating about the seas of the Pacific under the command of Harvey — once a deep-sea merchant captain, now a sea robber, and a man Ayrton knew well!

Most of the convicts were gathered in the poop, the raised cabin at the stern of the ship, but a few were stretched out on the deck, talking loudly.

As the conversation went on amid shouting and drinking, Ayrton learned that it was pure chance that had brought the Speedy within sight of Lincoln Island.

Bob Harvey had never yet set foot on it. But, just as Cyrus Smith had guessed, when he came across this unknown land in his path, whose position no chart showed, he had made up his mind to look it over, and, if it suited him, to make it the brig's home port.

As for the black flag run up to the Speedy's gaff, and the cannon shot fired, the way warships fire a gun when they hoist their colors — that was pure pirates' bravado. It was not a signal, and as yet there was no connection of any kind between the escaped Norfolk convicts and Lincoln Island.

So the colonists' domain was threatened by an immense danger. Clearly the island — with its easy watering place, its little harbor, its resources of every kind that the colonists had put to such good use, and the hidden depths of Granite House — could only suit the convicts down to the ground. In their hands it would become an excellent hiding place, and, precisely because it was unknown, it would guarantee them safety and freedom from punishment, perhaps for a long time. Clearly, too, the colonists' lives would not be spared, and the first thing Bob Harvey and his accomplices would do would be to massacre them without mercy. So Cyrus Smith and his people did not even have the option of running away and hiding somewhere on the island, since the convicts meant to live there, and since, even if the Speedy left on some raid, a few of her crew would probably stay ashore to settle in. So they would have to fight. They would have to destroy these wretches to the last man — wretches unworthy of pity, against whom any means would be fair.

That was what Ayrton thought, and he knew very well that Cyrus Smith would see it the same way.

But was resistance possible — and, in the end, was victory? That depended on how the brig was armed and how many men she carried.

That was what Ayrton made up his mind to find out at any cost. An hour after he arrived, the bellowing had begun to die down, and a good many of the convicts were already sunk in a drunken sleep. So Ayrton did not hesitate to venture onto the Speedy's deck, which was now in deep darkness, since her lanterns had been put out.

He hauled himself up onto the beak, then made his way along the bowsprit to the brig's forecastle. Slipping between the convicts sprawled here and there, he went all the way around the ship, and found that the Speedy was armed with four cannon that must fire balls of eight to ten pounds. He even made sure, by feeling them, that they were breech-loaders. So these were modern guns, easy to handle and terrible in their effect.

As for the men lying on deck, there must have been about ten of them, but it was reasonable to suppose that others, many more, were asleep below. Besides, from listening to them, Ayrton had gathered that there were about fifty on board. That was a great many for the six colonists of Lincoln Island! But at least, thanks to Ayrton's devotion, Cyrus Smith would not be taken by surprise. He would know the strength of his enemies, and he would make his arrangements accordingly.

So all that was left for Ayrton was to go back and report to his companions on the mission he had taken on, and he got ready to make his way back to the bow of the brig so as to slip down into the sea.

But then, to this man who wanted — he had said so — to do more than his duty, there came a heroic thought. It meant sacrificing his life, but he would save the island and the colonists. Cyrus Smith plainly could not hold out against fifty bandits armed to the teeth, who would get the better of the colonists either by breaking into Granite House by force or by starving them out. And then he pictured his rescuers — the people who had made him a man again, and an honest man, the people to whom he owed everything — killed without pity, their work destroyed, their island turned into a pirates' den! He told himself that he, Ayrton, was when all was said and done the root cause of so much disaster, since his old companion Bob Harvey had only carried out Ayrton's own plans — and a feeling of horror seized his whole being. Then he was gripped by an irresistible urge to blow up the brig, and with her everyone she carried. Ayrton would die in the explosion, but he would have done his duty.

Ayrton did not hesitate. Getting to the powder magazine, which is always located at the stern of a ship, would be easy. A ship in that line of work could not be short of powder, and a single spark would be enough to destroy her in an instant.

Ayrton let himself down carefully to the lower deck, which was strewn with sleepers held heavy more by drink than by sleep. A lantern was burning at the foot of the mainmast, and around the mast hung a rack filled with firearms of every kind.

Ayrton took a revolver from the rack and made sure it was loaded and primed. He needed nothing more to carry out the work of destruction. So he crept toward the stern, so as to get beneath the brig's poop, where the magazine had to be.

But on that lower deck, which was almost pitch-dark, it was hard to crawl along without bumping into some convict who was not quite asleep. Then came curses and blows. More than once Ayrton had to stop and wait. But at last he reached the bulkhead that closed off the stern compartment, and he found the door that must open onto the magazine itself.

Ayrton had no choice but to force it, and he set to work. It was a hard job to do without noise, because it meant breaking a padlock. But under Ayrton's powerful hand the padlock snapped, and the door was open...

At that moment a hand came down on Ayrton's shoulder.

"What are you doing there?" a tall man demanded in a harsh voice, rising up out of the shadows and thrusting a lantern sharply into Ayrton's face.

Ayrton threw himself backward. In a quick flash of the lantern he had recognized his old accomplice, Bob Harvey. But Harvey could not recognize him, since he must have believed Ayrton dead long ago.

"What are you doing there?" said Bob Harvey, seizing Ayrton by the waistband of his trousers.

But Ayrton, without answering, shoved the convicts' leader violently away and tried to dash into the magazine. One revolver shot among those kegs of powder, and it would all have been over!...

"To me, boys!" Bob Harvey had shouted.

Two or three pirates, woken by his voice, had got to their feet, and, throwing themselves on Ayrton, they tried to wrestle him to the floor. The powerful Ayrton broke free of their grip. Two shots from his revolver rang out, and two convicts fell; but a knife thrust that he could not ward off slashed into the flesh of his shoulder.

Ayrton understood that he could no longer carry out his plan. Bob Harvey had shut the magazine door again, and there was a stir on the lower deck that meant the whole pirate crew was waking up. Ayrton had to save himself to fight at Cyrus Smith's side. There was nothing left for him but to run!

But was escape still possible? That was doubtful, though Ayrton was determined to try everything to get back to his companions.

He had four shots left. He fired two of them now. One, aimed at Bob Harvey, did not hit him — or at least not seriously — and Ayrton, taking advantage of a moment when his attackers fell back, rushed for the companion ladder that led up to the brig's deck. As he passed the lantern he smashed it with a blow from the butt of his revolver, and a deep darkness fell, which was bound to help his escape.

Two or three pirates, woken by the noise, were coming down the ladder at that very moment. A fifth shot from Ayrton's revolver sent one of them tumbling down the steps, and the others drew back, not understanding what was going on. In two bounds Ayrton was on the brig's deck, and three seconds later, after firing his revolver one last time into the face of a pirate who had just grabbed him by the neck, he swung over the bulwarks and threw himself into the sea.

Ayrton had not swum six strokes before bullets were crackling around him like hail.

What must Pencroff have felt, sheltering under a rock on the islet — and Cyrus Smith, the reporter, Harbert and Nab, huddled in the Chimneys — when they heard those shots go off on board the brig! They had rushed out onto the beach, and, guns at their shoulders, they stood ready to drive back any attack.

For them there could be no doubt at all! Ayrton had been caught by the pirates and murdered, and perhaps those wretches were going to take advantage of the night to make a landing on the island!

Half an hour went by in mortal dread. The shots had stopped, though, and neither Ayrton nor Pencroff came back. Had the islet been invaded, then? Shouldn't they hurry to help Ayrton and Pencroff? But how? The sea, high at that moment, made the channel impossible to cross. The canoe was not there! Imagine the terrible anxiety that took hold of Cyrus Smith and his companions!

At last, toward half past midnight, a canoe carrying two men came in to the beach. It was Ayrton, slightly wounded in the shoulder, and Pencroff, safe and sound, and their friends received them with open arms.

At once they all took shelter in the Chimneys. There Ayrton told them what had happened, and he did not hide the plan he had tried to carry out, to blow up the brig.

Every hand reached out to Ayrton, who did not hide how grave the situation was. The pirates were on their guard now. They knew Lincoln Island was inhabited. They would land only in force and well armed. They would spare nothing. If the colonists fell into their hands, they could expect no mercy!

"Well, then! We'll know how to die!" said the reporter.

"Let's go in and keep watch," answered the engineer.

"Have we got any chance of coming out of this, Mr. Cyrus?" asked the sailor.

"Yes, Pencroff."

"Hm! Six against fifty!"

"Yes! Six!... Not counting..."

"Who, then?" asked Pencroff.

Cyrus did not answer, but he pointed to the sky.
