Chapter 8: Guncotton

_Linen — Sealskin shoes — Making guncotton — Various sowings — Fishing — Turtle eggs — Master Jup's progress — The corral — Hunting mouflons — New riches, plant and animal — Memories of the homeland far away_

The first week of January was given over to making the linen the colony needed. The needles found in the chest went to work between fingers that were strong, if not delicate, and it is safe to say that whatever was sewn stayed sewn.

There was no shortage of thread, thanks to Cyrus Smith's idea of reusing the thread that had already served to stitch together the strips of the balloon. Gédéon Spilett and Harbert unpicked those long strips with admirable patience, because Pencroff had been forced to give up the job, which drove him to distraction. But once it came to sewing, he had no equal. Everybody knows, after all, that sailors have a remarkable gift for the seamstress's trade.

Next, the cloth that had made up the balloon's envelope was scoured with soda and potash, obtained by burning plants, so that the cotton, freed of its varnish, got back its natural softness and stretch. Then it was left out in the open air, which bleached it, until it turned a perfect white.

In this way they made a few dozen shirts and socks — the socks not knitted, of course, but cut and sewn from cloth. What a pleasure it was for the colonists to put on clean white linen at last — very rough linen, no doubt, but they were past worrying about such small things — and to sleep between sheets, which turned the bunks of Granite House into thoroughly respectable beds.

It was also around this time that they made shoes of sealskin, which arrived just in time to replace the shoes and boots they had brought from America. It is safe to say that these new shoes were wide and long, and never pinched the walkers' feet!

As the year 1866 began, the heat held on and on, but the hunting in the woods never let up. Agoutis, peccaries, capybaras, kangaroos, game with fur and game with feathers were positively swarming, and Gédéon Spilett and Harbert were by now too good as shots ever to waste a single shot.

Cyrus Smith kept urging them to go easy on the ammunition, and he took steps to replace the powder and shot found in the chest, which he wanted to keep in reserve for the future. After all, did he know where chance might someday throw him and his companions, if they ever left their domain? They had to be ready for every need the unknown might bring, and so they had to save their ammunition by putting other materials, easy to replace, in its place.

To replace lead, of which Cyrus Smith had found no trace on the island, he used iron shot, which was easy to make, and did so without much loss. Since these pellets were not as heavy as lead pellets, he had to make them bigger, and each charge held fewer of them, but the hunters' skill made up for the difference. As for gunpowder, Cyrus Smith could have made it, since he had saltpeter, sulfur and charcoal on hand. But making it calls for extreme care, and without special equipment it is hard to turn out powder of good quality.

So Cyrus Smith preferred to make pyroxylin — that is, guncotton — a substance in which cotton is not essential, because cotton goes into it only as cellulose. Now, cellulose is nothing other than the basic tissue of plants, and it is found in an almost pure state not only in cotton but in the textile fibers of hemp and flax, in paper, in old linen, in the pith of the elder, and so on. And it so happened that elders grew in abundance on the island, near the mouth of Red Creek. The colonists were already using the berries of these shrubs, which belong to the honeysuckle family, in place of coffee.

So all they had to do was gather this elder pith — the cellulose. As for the other material needed to make guncotton, it was nothing but fuming nitric acid. And Cyrus Smith, who had sulfuric acid at his disposal, had already been able to produce nitric acid easily by treating the saltpeter that nature had provided.

So he decided to make guncotton and use it, though he recognized that it had some fairly serious drawbacks: its effect was very uneven; it was far too flammable, since it catches fire at three hundred and thirty-eight degrees Fahrenheit instead of four hundred and sixty-four; and it burned so instantaneously that it could damage firearms. On the other hand, guncotton had these advantages: dampness did not spoil it, it did not foul the barrels of the guns, and its propelling force was four times that of ordinary gunpowder.

To make guncotton, you need only plunge cellulose into fuming nitric acid for a quarter of an hour, then wash it in plenty of water and let it dry. As you see, nothing could be simpler.

Cyrus Smith had only ordinary nitric acid on hand, not the fuming or monohydrated kind — that is, the acid that gives off whitish vapors when it meets damp air. But by using ordinary nitric acid in its place, mixed in the proportion of three volumes to five volumes of concentrated sulfuric acid, the engineer was bound to get the same result — and he got it. So the island's hunters soon had a perfectly prepared substance at their disposal, which, used with discretion, gave excellent results.

Around this time the colonists cleared three acres of Prospect Plateau, and the rest was kept as meadow to feed the onaggas. Several excursions were made into Jacamar Wood and the Far West forests, and they brought back a real harvest of wild vegetables — spinach, cress, horseradish, turnips — which careful cultivation would soon improve, and which were going to balance the high-protein diet that the colonists of Lincoln Island had lived on until then. They also hauled in sizable loads of wood and coal. Every excursion was at the same time a chance to improve the roads, whose surface was gradually being packed down under the wheels of the cart.

The warren still supplied its share of rabbits to the pantries of Granite House. Since it lay a little outside the point where Glycerin Creek began, its residents could not get onto the protected plateau, and so they could not ravage the new plantings there. As for the oyster bed, laid out among the rocks of the beach and frequently restocked, it gave them excellent shellfish every day. Besides that, the fishing, whether in the lake or in the current of the Mercy, soon paid off, because Pencroff had set out bottom lines armed with iron hooks. On these they often caught fine trout, and certain extremely tasty fish whose silvery sides were sprinkled with little yellowish spots. And so Master Nab, who was in charge of the cooking, could vary the menu of every meal very pleasantly. Only bread was still missing from the colonists' table, and, as has been said, it was a privation they truly felt.

Around this time they also hunted sea turtles, which came to the beaches of Mandible Cape. There the sand was covered with little bumps, each one holding perfectly round eggs with hard white shells, whose albumen has the property of not setting when cooked, as the white of a bird's egg does. The sun took care of hatching them, and there were naturally a great many of them, since each turtle can lay as many as two hundred and fifty a year.

"A regular field of eggs," Gédéon Spilett remarked, "and all we have to do is harvest them."

But they did not settle for the produce; they went after the producers too, and that hunt let them bring back to Granite House a dozen of these chelonians, which are truly excellent eating. Turtle soup, spiced up with aromatic herbs and set off with a few crucifers, often won Master Nab, who made it, the praise he deserved.

One more lucky event should be mentioned here, which let them lay in new stores for the winter. Salmon came in schools and ventured into the Mercy, swimming up it for several miles. It was the season when the females, going in search of suitable places to spawn, went ahead of the males and made a great commotion as they passed through the fresh water. A thousand or so of these fish, some as long as two and a half feet, poured into the river this way, and it was enough to build a few weirs to hold a great many of them back. In this way they caught several hundred, which were salted and stored away for the time when winter would freeze the streams and make all fishing impossible.

It was at this time that the very intelligent Jup was promoted to the post of valet. He had been dressed in a jacket, short breeches of white canvas, and an apron whose pockets were his delight, because he stuffed his hands into them and would not let anyone come rummaging in them. The clever orangutan had been wonderfully trained by Nab, and Nab and the ape seemed to understand each other when they talked together. Jup, besides, had a real fondness for Nab, and Nab returned it. Unless someone needed his services, whether to haul wood or to climb to the top of some tree, Jup spent most of his time in the kitchen and tried to imitate Nab in everything he saw him do. The teacher, for his part, showed great patience and even an extreme zeal in instructing his pupil, and the pupil showed a remarkable intelligence in profiting from the lessons his teacher gave him.

So you can imagine how delighted the diners of Granite House were one day when Master Jup, napkin over his arm, came in without any warning to wait on them at table. Skillful and attentive, he performed his service perfectly, changing the plates, bringing in the dishes, pouring the drinks, all with a gravity that amused the colonists no end and sent Pencroff into raptures.

"Jup, some soup!"

"Jup, a little agouti!"

"Jup, a plate!"

"Jup! Good Jup! Honest Jup!"

That was all you could hear, and Jup, never once flustered, answered every call and kept an eye on everything. And he nodded his intelligent head when Pencroff, repeating his joke from the first day, told him:

"Jup, there's no doubt about it — we'll have to double your wages!"

Needless to say, by now the orangutan was completely at home in Granite House, and he often went with his masters into the forest without ever showing the least wish to run away. You should have seen him then, walking along in the funniest way, with a cane that Pencroff had made him, which he carried on his shoulder like a gun! If they needed a fruit picked from the top of a tree, how fast he was up there! If the wheel of the cart got stuck in the mud, with what strength Jup set it back on its way with a single heave of his shoulder!

"What a fellow!" Pencroff often exclaimed. "If he were as wicked as he is good, there'd be no handling him!"

It was toward the end of January that the colonists undertook some major works in the central part of the island. It had been decided to build a corral near the sources of Red Creek, at the foot of Mount Franklin. It would hold the ruminants, which would have been a nuisance at Granite House — and above all the mouflons, which were to supply wool for making winter clothes.

Every morning the colony — sometimes all of it, more often only Cyrus Smith, Harbert and Pencroff — went out to the sources of the creek. With the onaggas helping, it was now nothing more than a five-mile outing under a dome of greenery, along the newly cut road, which came to be called "the Corral Road."

There a large site had been chosen, on the far side of the mountain's southern shoulder. It was a meadow dotted with clumps of trees, lying at the very foot of a spur that closed it in on one side. A little stream, born on the spur's slopes, ran diagonally across the meadow, watering it, and then lost itself in Red Creek. The grass was fresh, and the trees growing here and there let the air move freely over the ground. So all they had to do was surround the meadow with a palisade running in a curve, with each end resting against the spur, high enough that not even the most agile animals could jump it. This enclosure could hold a hundred or so horned animals, mouflons or wild goats, along with the young that would be born later on.

So the engineer laid out the boundary of the corral, and they had to set about felling the trees needed to build the palisade. But since cutting the road had already cost a good number of trunks, they hauled those over, and these supplied about a hundred stakes, which were planted firmly in the ground.

In the front of the palisade they left a fairly wide opening, closed by a double gate made of stout planks and meant to be strengthened by bars on the outside.

Building this corral took no less than three weeks, because besides the work on the palisade, Cyrus Smith put up large plank sheds where the ruminants could take shelter. Besides, these structures had to be built extremely solidly, because mouflons are powerful animals, and their first fits of violence were something to fear. The stakes, sharpened at the top and hardened there in the fire, had been tied together with bolted crossbars, and at intervals props secured the strength of the whole.

With the corral finished, the next step was a great roundup at the foot of Mount Franklin, in the middle of the pastures where the ruminants grazed. This took place on February 7, on a beautiful summer day, and everyone took part. The two onaggas, already fairly well broken and ridden by Gédéon Spilett and Harbert, were a great help on this occasion.

The whole maneuver consisted of driving the mouflons and goats inward by gradually tightening the circle of beaters around them. So Cyrus Smith, Pencroff, Nab and Jup took up positions at various points in the woods, while the two riders and Top galloped around the corral within a radius of half a mile.

There were plenty of mouflons in this part of the island. These handsome animals, as big as deer, with horns stronger than a ram's and a grayish fleece mixed with long hairs, looked like argalis.

What an exhausting day of hunting it was! So much coming and going, so much running back and forth, so much shouting! Of the hundred or so mouflons that were driven in, more than two-thirds slipped past the beaters. But in the end some thirty of these ruminants and about ten wild goats, pushed little by little toward the corral, whose open gate seemed to offer them a way out, rushed into it and could be shut in.

All in all, the result was satisfying, and the colonists had nothing to complain about. Most of the mouflons were females, some of which would soon give birth. So it was certain that the flock would thrive, and that before long they would have plenty not only of wool but of hides as well.

That evening the hunters came back to Granite House worn out. Even so, they went back to visit the corral the very next day. The prisoners had certainly tried to knock down the palisade, but they had not managed it, and they soon settled down.

Nothing of any importance happened during that month of February. The daily work went on methodically, and while the roads to the corral and to Balloon Harbor were being improved, a third was begun, which ran from the enclosure toward the west coast. The part of Lincoln Island still unknown was the great forest that covered Serpentine Peninsula, where the wild beasts took refuge — and Gédéon Spilett fully intended to rid his domain of them.

Before the cold season came back, the most careful attention was also given to cultivating the wild plants that had been transplanted from the forest to Prospect Plateau. Harbert hardly ever came back from an excursion without bringing some useful plants. One day it was specimens of the chicory tribe, whose very seed could be pressed to yield an excellent oil; another day it was a common sorrel, whose properties against scurvy were not to be scorned; then some of those precious tubers that have been grown in South America since time immemorial — potatoes, of which more than two hundred kinds are known today. The vegetable garden, now well tended, well watered and well protected from the birds, was divided into little square beds where lettuces, purple potatoes, sorrel, turnips, horseradish and other crucifers were growing. The soil of the plateau was wonderfully fertile, and they could hope for abundant harvests.

There was no lack of drinks either, and as long as they did not demand wine, even the fussiest had no reason to complain. To the Oswego tea that the bee balm supplied, and the fermented drink made from the roots of the dragon tree, Cyrus Smith had added real beer. He brewed it from the young shoots of the black spruce, Abies nigra, which, once boiled and fermented, gave that pleasant and particularly healthful drink the Anglo-Americans call spruce beer.

Toward the end of the summer the poultry yard held a fine pair of bustards of the houbara species, known by a sort of cape of feathers; a dozen shovelers, whose upper bill was extended on each side by a flap of skin; and some magnificent roosters, black in the comb, the wattles and the skin, like the roosters of Mozambique, which strutted about on the shore of the lake.

So everything was succeeding, thanks to the energy of these brave and intelligent men. Providence did a great deal for them, no doubt; but true to the great rule that Heaven helps those who help themselves, they helped themselves first, and Heaven came to their aid afterward.

After those hot summer days, in the evening, when the work was done and the sea breeze was rising, they liked to sit at the edge of Prospect Plateau, under a sort of veranda covered with climbing plants that Nab had put up with his own hands. There they talked, they taught one another, they made plans, and the sailor's hearty good humor kept cheering up this little world, in which the most perfect harmony had never stopped reigning.

They talked about home, too, about their dear, great America. How was the Civil War going? It obviously could not have dragged on! Richmond had surely fallen quickly into General Grant's hands! The capture of the Confederate capital must have been the last act of that deadly struggle! By now the North had triumphed for the good cause. Oh, how welcome a newspaper would have been to the exiles of Lincoln Island! For eleven months now, all contact between them and the rest of humankind had been cut off, and soon, on March 24, would come the anniversary of the day the balloon had flung them onto this unknown coast! They had been nothing but castaways then, not even knowing whether they could hold on to their miserable lives against the elements! And now, thanks to their leader's knowledge, thanks to their own intelligence, they were real colonists, equipped with weapons, tools and instruments, who had learned to turn the animals, the plants and the minerals of the island — the three kingdoms of nature — to their own use!

Yes! They often talked about all these things, and they still made many plans for the future!

As for Cyrus Smith, silent most of the time, he listened to his companions more often than he spoke. Now and then he smiled at some remark of Harbert's or some sally of Pencroff's, but always and everywhere he was thinking about those inexplicable events, that strange riddle whose secret still escaped him!
