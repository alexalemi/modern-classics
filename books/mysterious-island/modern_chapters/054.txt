Chapter 13: Ayrton's Story

_Ayrton's story — The plans of his former accomplices — They settle in at the corral — Lincoln Island's dispenser of justice — The Bonadventure — Searching around Mount Franklin — The upper valleys — Rumblings underground — An answer from Pencroff — At the bottom of the crater — Home again_

What had happened? Who had struck the convicts down? Could it have been Ayrton? No, since only a moment before he had been dreading their return!

But Ayrton was by then sunk in a deep sleep, and there was no longer any way to rouse him from it. After the few words he had spoken, a crushing torpor had taken hold of him, and he had fallen back on his bed without moving.

The colonists, a prey to a thousand confused thoughts and in a state of violent excitement, waited all through the night without leaving Ayrton's house and without going back to the place where the bodies of the convicts lay. As for the circumstances in which those men had met their deaths, Ayrton would most likely be unable to tell them anything, since he had not even known that he was in the house at the corral. But at least he would be able to tell them what had happened before that terrible execution.

The next day Ayrton came out of his torpor, and his companions warmly showed him all the joy they felt at seeing him again, more or less safe and sound, after a hundred and four days apart.

Ayrton then told them in a few words what had happened, or at least what he knew.

On the day after he arrived at the corral — November 10 of the year before — at nightfall, he had been taken by surprise by the convicts, who had climbed over the palisade. They tied him up and gagged him, and then he was carried off to a dark cave at the foot of Mount Franklin, where the convicts had taken refuge.

They had decided on his death, and the next day he was about to be killed when one of the convicts recognized him and called him by the name he had gone by in Australia. These wretches had been ready to murder Ayrton. They spared Ben Joyce!

But from that moment on, Ayrton was hounded day and night by his former accomplices. They wanted to win him back to their side, and they were counting on him to help them seize Granite House, to get inside that unreachable dwelling, and to make themselves masters of the island after murdering the colonists!

Ayrton refused. The former convict, repentant and forgiven, would sooner have died than betray his companions.

Bound, gagged, and watched every moment, Ayrton lived in that cave for four months.

The convicts, meanwhile, had found the corral not long after they landed on the island, and since then they had been living off its stores, though they did not live in it. On November 11, two of these bandits, caught off guard by the colonists' unexpected arrival, fired on Harbert, and one of them came back boasting that he had killed one of the island's inhabitants. But he came back alone. His companion, as we know, had fallen under Cyrus Smith's dagger.

Imagine Ayrton's anxiety and despair when he heard that news of Harbert's death! The colonists were only four now, and so, you might say, at the mercy of the convicts!

After that, and for the whole time the colonists stayed at the corral, held there by Harbert's illness, the pirates did not leave their cave. Even after they had plundered Prospect Plateau, they did not think it wise to abandon it.

The ill treatment they gave Ayrton grew worse then. His hands and feet still bore the bloody marks of the ropes that bound him day and night. At every moment he expected a death that it did not seem he could possibly escape.

So it went on until the third week of February. Still watching for a good opportunity, the convicts seldom left their hiding place, and made only a few hunting trips, either into the interior of the island or as far as the south coast. Ayrton had no more news of his friends, and he no longer hoped to see them again!

At last, weakened by ill treatment, the unhappy man fell into a deep prostration in which he could no longer see or hear anything. And so from that point on — that is, for the past two days — he could not even say what had happened.

"But, Mr. Smith," he added, "if I was a prisoner in that cave, how is it that I find myself back at the corral?"

"How is it that the convicts are lying out there dead, in the middle of the enclosure?" answered the engineer.

"Dead!" cried Ayrton, and weak as he was, he half raised himself.

His companions held him up. He wanted to get to his feet. They let him, and all of them went toward the little stream.

It was broad daylight.

There on the bank, in the very positions in which a death that must have been as sudden as a lightning stroke had caught them, lay the five bodies of the convicts!

Ayrton was stunned. Cyrus Smith and his companions watched him without saying a word.

At a sign from the engineer, Nab and Pencroff examined the bodies, which were already stiff with the cold.

They bore no visible trace of any wound.

But after looking them over carefully, Pencroff noticed on the forehead of one, on the chest of another, on the back of this one, on the shoulder of that one, a small red spot — a kind of bruise, barely visible, and impossible to account for.

"That's where they were struck!" said Cyrus Smith.

"But with what weapon?" cried the reporter.

"A weapon that strikes like lightning, and whose secret we don't have!"

"And who struck them down?" asked Pencroff.

"The island's dispenser of justice," answered Cyrus Smith. "The one who carried you here, Ayrton. The one whose influence has just made itself felt once again. The one who does for us everything we can't do for ourselves, and who then, once it's done, slips away from us."

"Then let's find him!" cried Pencroff.

"Yes, let's look for him," answered Cyrus Smith. "But the superior being who works such wonders — we'll find him only if he finally chooses to call us to him!"

This invisible protection, which reduced their own efforts to nothing, both irritated and touched the engineer. The relative inferiority it made plain was the kind that can wound a proud soul. A generosity that arranges things so as to dodge every mark of gratitude suggested a sort of disdain for the people it obliged, and in Cyrus Smith's eyes that spoiled, to some degree, the value of the kindness.

"Let's search," he went on, "and God grant that one day we may be allowed to prove to this haughty protector that he isn't dealing with ungrateful people! What wouldn't I give for us to be able to repay him, by doing him some great service in our turn, even at the cost of our lives!"

From that day on, this search was the one concern of the inhabitants of Lincoln Island. Everything drove them to find the answer to the riddle, and that answer could only be the name of a man gifted with a truly inexplicable and, in a way, superhuman power.

After a few moments the colonists went back into the house at the corral, where their care soon restored Ayrton's strength of mind and body.

Nab and Pencroff carried the convicts' bodies into the forest, some distance from the corral, and buried them deep.

Then Ayrton was told everything that had happened while he was held prisoner. He heard about Harbert's adventures, and about the whole series of trials the colonists had been through. As for them, they had given up hope of ever seeing Ayrton again, and had feared that the convicts had murdered him without mercy.

"And now," said Cyrus Smith, as he finished his account, "we still have a duty to carry out. Half our task is done. But if the convicts are no longer to be feared, it isn't to ourselves that we owe being masters of the island again."

"Well, then," answered Gédéon Spilett, "let's search the whole labyrinth of Mount Franklin's spurs! Let's not leave a single hollow, a single hole unexplored! Ah! If ever a reporter found himself face to face with a thrilling mystery, my friends, it's the one talking to you now!"

"And we won't go back to Granite House," said Harbert, "until we've found our benefactor."

"Yes!" said the engineer. "We'll do everything that's humanly possible... but I'll say it again — we'll find him only if he's willing to let us!"

"Do we stay at the corral?" asked Pencroff.

"Let's stay here," answered Cyrus Smith. "There are plenty of provisions, and we're right at the center of the circle we have to search. Besides, if need be, the cart can get to Granite House quickly."

"Good," answered the sailor. "Only, one remark."

"What's that?"

"The fine season's coming on, and we mustn't forget we have a crossing to make."

"A crossing?" said Gédéon Spilett.

"Yes! To Tabor Island," answered Pencroff. "We have to take a notice over there giving the position of our island, and saying that Ayrton is here now, in case the Scottish yacht comes back to pick him up. Who knows if it isn't already too late?"

"But, Pencroff," asked Ayrton, "how do you plan to make that crossing?"

"In the Bonadventure!"

"The Bonadventure!" cried Ayrton. "She doesn't exist anymore."

"My Bonadventure doesn't exist anymore!" roared Pencroff, leaping to his feet.

"No!" answered Ayrton. "The convicts found her in her little harbor barely a week ago. They put to sea, and..."

"And?" said Pencroff, his heart pounding.

"And with no Bob Harvey to handle her, they ran her onto the rocks, and the boat was smashed to pieces!"

"Oh, the wretches! The bandits! The low-down scoundrels!" cried Pencroff.

"Pencroff," said Harbert, taking the sailor's hand, "we'll build another Bonadventure — a bigger one! We have all the brig's ironwork and all her rigging to use!"

"But do you know," answered Pencroff, "that it takes five or six months at least to build a boat of thirty to forty tons?"

"We'll take our time," answered the reporter, "and we'll give up making the crossing to Tabor Island this year."

"What can you do, Pencroff? We have to resign ourselves," said the engineer. "And I hope this delay won't do us any harm."

"Oh, my Bonadventure! My poor Bonadventure!" cried Pencroff, truly heartbroken at the loss of the boat he had been so proud of.

The destruction of the Bonadventure was obviously a regrettable thing for the colonists, and they agreed that the loss would have to be made good as soon as possible. Once that was settled, their only concern was to carry through the exploration of the most hidden parts of the island.

The search began that very day, February 19, and went on for a whole week. The base of the mountain, between its spurs and their many branches, formed a labyrinth of valleys and side valleys laid out in the most haphazard way. It was obviously there, at the bottom of those narrow gorges, or perhaps even inside the mass of Mount Franklin itself, that the search ought to be pursued. No part of the island would have been better suited to hide a dwelling whose occupant wished to remain unknown. But the spurs were so tangled together that Cyrus Smith had to explore them with strict method.

The colonists first went over the whole valley that opened to the south of the volcano and collected the first waters of Fall River. It was there that Ayrton showed them the cave where the convicts had taken refuge and where he had been held prisoner until he was carried to the corral. The cave was exactly as Ayrton had left it. They found there a certain quantity of ammunition and food that the convicts had carried off, meaning to lay in a store of their own.

The whole valley that led to the cave, shaded by fine trees, mostly conifers, was explored with the greatest care. Then, after rounding the tip of the southwest spur, the colonists made their way into a narrower gorge that began at that very picturesque heap of basalt columns along the shore.

Here the trees were scarcer. Stone took the place of grass. Wild goats and mouflons skipped about among the rocks. This was where the barren part of the island began. They could already see that of the many valleys branching out at the foot of Mount Franklin, only three were wooded and rich in pasture like the valley of the corral, which bordered the valley of Fall River on the west and the valley of Red Creek on the east. Those two streams, which turned into rivers farther down by taking in a few tributaries, were formed from all the waters of the mountain, and so they were what made its southern part fertile. As for the Mercy, it was fed more directly by plentiful springs hidden under the cover of Jacamar Wood, and it was springs of the same kind, spilling out in a thousand little trickles, that watered the soil of Serpentine Peninsula.

Now, of these three valleys where there was no shortage of water, any one could have served as a retreat for some solitary man, who would have found everything there that he needed to live. But the colonists had already explored them, and nowhere had they found any sign of a human presence.

Was it, then, at the bottom of those barren gorges, among the rockfalls, in the rugged ravines of the north, between the lava flows, that the hiding place and its occupant would be found?

At its base, the northern part of Mount Franklin consisted of nothing but two valleys, broad, shallow, without a trace of green, strewn with stray boulders, streaked with long moraines, paved with lava, bulging here and there with great mineral swellings, and sprinkled with obsidian and labradorite. This part called for long and difficult exploration. A thousand hollows had been dug out there — not very comfortable, no doubt, but perfectly hidden and hard to get into. The colonists even went through dark tunnels that dated from the age when fire shaped the earth, still blackened by the fires that had passed through them long ago, and that ran deep into the mass of the mountain. They went along those dark galleries, carrying blazing torches of resin; they searched the smallest hollows and sounded the smallest depths. But everywhere there was silence and darkness. It did not seem that a human being had ever set foot in those ancient passages, or that any arm had ever moved a single one of those blocks. They lay just as the volcano had flung them up when the island rose above the waters.

And yet, though these underground vaults seemed to be entirely deserted, and though the darkness in them was complete, Cyrus Smith was forced to admit that complete silence did not reign there.

On reaching the far end of one of those dark hollows, which ran several hundred feet into the mountain, he was surprised to hear dull rumblings, made louder by the echoing rock.

Gédéon Spilett, who was with him, heard those distant murmurs too, and they pointed to a reawakening of the underground fires. Several times the two of them stopped to listen, and they agreed that some chemical reaction was working itself out in the depths of the earth.

"So the volcano isn't completely extinct?" said the reporter.

"It's possible," answered Cyrus Smith, "that since we explored the crater some change has taken place in the lower layers. Any volcano, even one that's thought to be extinct, can obviously light up again."

"But if Mount Franklin were getting ready to erupt," asked Gédéon Spilett, "wouldn't Lincoln Island be in danger?"

"I don't think so," answered the engineer. "The crater — that is, the safety valve — is there, and the overflow of vapors and lava will escape, as it used to, through its usual outlet."

"Unless the lava cuts itself a new way out toward the fertile parts of the island!"

"Why, my dear Spilett," answered Cyrus Smith, "why wouldn't it follow the road that's naturally laid out for it?"

"Well! Volcanoes are unpredictable!" answered the reporter.

"Notice," the engineer went on, "that the slope of the whole mass of Mount Franklin favors an outflow of material toward the valleys we're exploring right now. It would take an earthquake shifting the mountain's center of gravity for that outflow to change course."

"But an earthquake is always to be feared in conditions like these," Gédéon Spilett pointed out.

"Always," answered the engineer, "especially when the forces underground are beginning to wake, and the insides of the globe may have become blocked up after a long rest. So, my dear Spilett, an eruption would be a serious matter for us, and it would be far better if this volcano had no wish to wake up! But there's nothing we can do about it, is there? In any case, whatever happens, I don't believe our domain on Prospect Plateau can be seriously threatened. Between the plateau and the mountain the ground dips noticeably, and if the lava ever took the road toward the lake, it would be thrown off onto the dunes and the nearby parts of Shark Gulf."

"We haven't yet seen any smoke at the top of the mountain to suggest an eruption is coming," said Gédéon Spilett.

"No," answered Cyrus Smith. "Not a wisp of vapor is coming out of the crater — I was looking at its summit only yesterday. But it's possible that over time rocks, ashes, and hardened lava have piled up in the lower part of the volcanic chimney, and that the safety valve I spoke of is overloaded for the moment. Still, at the first serious push, every obstacle will be swept away, and you can be sure, my dear Spilett, that neither the island, which is the boiler, nor the volcano, which is the smokestack, will burst under the pressure of the gases. All the same, I repeat, it would be better if there were no eruption."

"And yet we aren't mistaken," the reporter went on. "We can definitely hear dull rumblings in the very depths of the volcano!"

"Indeed," answered the engineer, listening again with the closest attention, "there's no mistaking it... some reaction is taking place there, and we can't judge how great it is or how it will end."

When they came out, Cyrus Smith and Gédéon Spilett rejoined their companions and told them how matters stood.

"Fine!" cried Pencroff. "So this volcano wants to get up to its tricks! Well, let it try! It'll find its master!..."

"Who's that?" asked Nab.

"Our genie, Nab, our genie! He'll gag its crater for it if it so much as looks like opening it!"

As you can see, the sailor's faith in his island's own private god was absolute. And certainly the hidden power that had shown itself so far through so many inexplicable acts seemed to have no limits. But it also knew how to escape the colonists' painstaking search, because in spite of all their efforts, in spite of the zeal — more than zeal, the stubbornness — they brought to their exploration, the strange hiding place could not be found.

From February 19 to 25, the circle of the search was widened to take in the whole northern region of Lincoln Island, and its most secret nooks were ransacked. The colonists went so far as to sound every rock wall, the way police officers tap the walls of a suspect house. The engineer even made a very exact survey of the mountain, and pushed his search down to the lowest foundations that supported it. In the same way it was explored up as high as the truncated cone that topped the first tier of rock, and then right up to the upper rim of that enormous hat at the bottom of which the crater opened.

They did more: they went down into the pit. It was still extinct, but rumblings could be distinctly heard in its depths. Yet not a puff of smoke, not a wisp of vapor, not the least warming of the rock walls pointed to an eruption to come. But neither there nor in any other part of Mount Franklin did the colonists find any trace of the one they were looking for.

Next, the search turned to the whole region of the dunes. They went carefully over the high lava walls of Shark Gulf, from bottom to top, though it was extremely hard even to get down to the level of the gulf itself. No one! Nothing!

In the end, those two words summed up so much fatigue spent for nothing, so much stubbornness that came to nothing, and there was a sort of anger in the disappointment of Cyrus Smith and his companions.

So they had to think about going back, since this search could not go on forever. The colonists had every right to believe now that the mysterious being did not live on the surface of the island, and then the wildest theories haunted their overexcited imaginations. Pencroff and Nab especially were no longer content with the strange, and let themselves be carried off into the world of the supernatural.

On February 25 the colonists returned to Granite House. By means of the double rope, which an arrow carried back up to the doorsill, they restored the link between their home and the ground below.

A month later, on the twenty-fifth day of March, they celebrated the third anniversary of their arrival on Lincoln Island!
