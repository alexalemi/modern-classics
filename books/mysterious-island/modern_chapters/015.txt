Chapter 16: A Fight Under the Water

_The question of a home comes up again — Pencroff's flights of fancy — An exploration north of the lake — The northern edge of the plateau — The snakes — The far end of the lake — Top uneasy — Top swimming — A fight under the water_

It was now May 6, which corresponds to November 6 in the lands of the northern hemisphere. For some days the sky had been growing misty, and it was important to make certain arrangements with the winter in mind. The temperature, however, had not yet dropped noticeably, and a thermometer brought to Lincoln Island would still have shown an average of fifty to fifty-four degrees Fahrenheit. That average should not surprise anyone. Lincoln Island, very probably lying somewhere between the thirty-fifth and fortieth parallels, had to be subject, in the southern hemisphere, to the same climate as Sicily or Greece in the northern. But just as Greece and Sicily have spells of fierce cold that bring snow and ice, so Lincoln Island would no doubt suffer, at the very depth of winter, certain drops in temperature that it would be wise to guard against.

In any case, even if the cold was not yet a threat, the rainy season was close. On this isolated island, out in the middle of the Pacific Ocean and exposed to every kind of weather the open sea could throw at it, bad weather was bound to be frequent, and probably terrible.

So the question of a home more comfortable than the Chimneys had to be thought over seriously and settled quickly.

Pencroff, naturally, had a certain fondness for the shelter he had discovered himself. But he understood perfectly well that they had to look for another. The sea had already paid the Chimneys a visit, under circumstances the reader will remember, and they could not risk another accident like that one.

"Besides," added Cyrus Smith, who was talking these things over with his companions that day, "we have some precautions to take."

"Why?" said the reporter. "The island isn't inhabited."

"Probably not," answered the engineer, "although we haven't explored all of it yet. But even if there's no human being here, I'm afraid dangerous animals may be plentiful. So we ought to put ourselves out of reach of a possible attack, and stop making one of us stay up every night to keep a fire going. And then, my friends, we have to plan for everything. We're in a part of the Pacific that's often visited by Malay pirates..."

"What?" said Harbert. "This far from any land?"

"Yes, my boy," answered the engineer. "Those pirates are bold sailors as well as dangerous criminals, and we have to take our measures accordingly."

"Well then," answered Pencroff, "we'll fortify ourselves against wild beasts on two legs and on four. But, Mr. Cyrus, wouldn't it make sense to explore every part of the island before we start on anything?"

"That would be better," added Gédéon Spilett. "Who knows? We might find one of those caves on the opposite coast that we looked for on this one and never found."

"That's true," answered the engineer, "but you're forgetting, my friends, that we ought to settle near running water, and from the top of Mount Franklin we didn't see a single stream or river toward the west. Here, on the other hand, we're right between the Mercy and Lake Grant, and that's a considerable advantage we mustn't throw away. What's more, this coast faces east, so it isn't exposed the way the other one is to the trade winds, which blow from the northwest in this hemisphere."

"Then, Mr. Cyrus," answered the sailor, "let's build a house on the shore of the lake. We aren't short of bricks or tools now. We've been brickmakers, potters, smelters, and blacksmiths. We can certainly manage to be masons too, devil take it!"

"Yes, my friend, but before we decide, we have to look around. A home that nature had built for us at her own expense would save us a great deal of work, and it would probably give us an even safer refuge, because it would be as well defended against enemies from inside the island as against enemies from outside."

"True enough, Cyrus," answered the reporter, "but we've already examined that whole granite mass along the coast, and there isn't a hole in it, not even a crack!"

"No, not one!" added Pencroff. "Ah! If only we could have dug ourselves a home in that wall, high enough up to be out of reach — now that would have suited us! I can see it from here: five or six rooms on the face that looks out to sea..."

"With windows to let the light in!" said Harbert, laughing.

"And a staircase to get up to them!" added Nab.

"You're laughing," cried the sailor, "and why, I'd like to know? What's impossible about what I'm proposing? Don't we have picks and mattocks? Won't Mr. Cyrus know how to make gunpowder for blasting? Isn't that right, Mr. Cyrus — you'll make gunpowder the day we need it?"

Cyrus Smith had listened to the enthusiastic Pencroff as he spun out his somewhat fanciful plans. Attacking that mass of granite, even with blasting charges, would be a Herculean labor, and it really was a pity that nature had not done the hardest part of the job for them. But the engineer's only answer to the sailor was to suggest that they examine the wall more carefully, from the mouth of the river to the corner where it ended in the north.

So out they went, and the exploration was carried out, over a stretch of about two miles, with the greatest care. But nowhere did the smooth, straight wall show a single hollow of any kind. The nests of the rock pigeons that fluttered about its top turned out to be nothing more than holes bored into the very crest, along the jagged upper edge of the granite.

It was an unfortunate state of affairs, and as for attacking the mass with picks or gunpowder to hollow out a big enough space, that was not to be thought of. As chance would have it, on that whole stretch of the coast Pencroff had found the only shelter fit to live in for the time being — that is, the Chimneys, which they now had to give up after all.

When the exploration was finished, the colonists found themselves at the northern corner of the wall, where it ended in long slopes that ran down and died away on the beach. From there all the way to its western limit, the wall was nothing more than a kind of embankment: a thick mass of stones, earth and sand, bound together by plants, shrubs and grass, and sloping at an angle of only forty-five degrees. Here and there the granite still broke through and jutted out in sharp points from this sort of cliff. Clumps of trees rose in tiers up its slopes, and a fairly thick growth of grass carpeted it. But the vegetation went no farther than that, and a long sandy plain, starting at the foot of the embankment, stretched all the way to the shore.

Cyrus Smith thought, not without reason, that it must be on this side that the lake's overflow spilled out in a waterfall. After all, the surplus water that Red Creek poured in had to escape somewhere. Yet the engineer had not yet found that spot on any part of the shore they had already explored — that is, from the mouth of the creek in the west to Prospect Plateau.

So the engineer suggested to his companions that they climb the slope they were looking at and return to the Chimneys by the heights, exploring the northern and eastern shores of the lake on the way.

The suggestion was accepted, and within a few minutes Harbert and Nab had reached the plateau above. Cyrus Smith, Gédéon Spilett and Pencroff followed at a steadier pace.

Two hundred feet away, through the leaves, the lovely sheet of water was glittering in the sunlight. The landscape was charming there. The trees, their leaves turning yellow, were grouped in the most wonderful way, a real feast for the eyes. A few huge old trunks, felled by age, stood out with their blackish bark against the green carpet that covered the ground. A whole world of noisy cockatoos chattered there — living prisms in motion — hopping from one branch to the next. It was as if the light could only reach the ground broken into its colors by that strange canopy.

Instead of heading straight for the north shore of the lake, the colonists went around the edge of the plateau so as to reach the mouth of the creek on its left bank. It was a detour of a mile and a half at most. The walk was easy, because the trees were widely spaced and left a free passage between them. They could tell that the fertile zone stopped at this boundary, and the vegetation here was less vigorous than in all the land between the creek and the Mercy.

Cyrus Smith and his companions walked with a certain caution over this ground that was new to them. Bows, arrows, and sticks tipped with sharp iron points were their only weapons. No wild beast showed itself, however, and it was likely that such animals kept rather to the thick forests of the south. But the colonists had the unpleasant surprise of seeing Top stop short in front of a large snake, fourteen or fifteen feet long. Nab killed it with a blow of his stick. Cyrus Smith examined the reptile and declared that it was not venomous, since it belonged to the species of diamond snakes that the Aboriginal people of New South Wales eat. But there might well be others whose bite was deadly, such as the deaf adders with forked tails that rear up under your foot, or those winged snakes with two little ear-flaps that let them launch themselves with tremendous speed. Once his first surprise was over, Top went hunting the reptiles so furiously that they feared for him, and his master kept calling him back.

They soon reached the mouth of Red Creek, where it emptied into the lake. On the opposite bank the explorers recognized the spot they had already visited on their way down from Mount Franklin. Cyrus Smith noted that the creek carried a fairly large flow of water. Somewhere, therefore, nature had to have provided an outlet for the lake's overflow. That outlet was what they had to find, for it surely formed a waterfall, and it might be possible to put its mechanical power to use.

So the colonists, walking as they pleased but without straying too far from one another, began to make their way around the shore of the lake, which dropped off very steeply. The water seemed to be teeming with fish, and Pencroff promised himself he would make some fishing tackle to take advantage of it.

First they had to round the sharp northeastern point. One might have supposed that the water drained away there, since the end of the lake came almost up to the edge of the plateau. But that was not so, and the colonists went on exploring the shore, which, after a slight curve, ran back down parallel to the coast.

On this side the bank was less wooded, but a few clumps of trees scattered here and there added to the beauty of the landscape. Lake Grant now lay before them in all its extent, and not a breath of wind ruffled its surface. Top, beating through the brush, flushed flocks of various birds, which Gédéon Spilett and Harbert greeted with their arrows. One of them was even neatly hit by the boy and fell among some marsh grass. Top rushed to it and brought back a handsome swimming bird, slate-colored, with a short beak, a large plate on its forehead, toes widened by a scalloped fringe, and wings edged with a thin white border. It was a coot, about the size of a big partridge, one of the group called macrodactyls, the "long-toed" birds that form the link between the order of wading birds and the order of web-footed ones. Poor game, all in all, and the taste was bound to leave something to be desired. But Top would no doubt be less fussy than his masters, and it was agreed that the coot would be his supper.

The colonists were now following the eastern shore of the lake, and they would soon reach the part they had already surveyed. The engineer was very much surprised, because he could see no sign of the overflow draining away anywhere. The reporter and the sailor were talking with him, and he made no secret of his astonishment.

At that moment Top, who had been perfectly calm until then, began to show signs of agitation. The intelligent animal ran back and forth along the bank, stopped suddenly, and stared at the water with one paw raised, as though he were pointing at some invisible game. Then he barked furiously, as if casting about for a scent, and suddenly fell silent.

At first neither Cyrus Smith nor his companions had paid any attention to Top's behavior. But soon the dog's barking became so frequent that the engineer grew concerned.

"What is it, Top?" he asked.

The dog bounded several times toward his master, clearly and genuinely uneasy, and then dashed back to the bank. Then, all at once, he plunged into the lake.

"Here, Top!" shouted Cyrus Smith, who did not want to let his dog venture into those suspicious waters.

"What on earth is going on down there?" asked Pencroff, studying the surface of the lake.

"Top must have smelled some amphibious animal," answered Harbert.

"An alligator, I suppose?" said the reporter.

"I don't think so," answered Cyrus Smith. "Alligators are found only in lower latitudes."

Top, meanwhile, had come back at his master's call and returned to the bank. But he could not settle down. He kept leaping about in the tall grass, and, guided by his instinct, he seemed to be following some invisible creature that was gliding along under the water, skimming close to the shore. Yet the water was calm, and not a ripple disturbed its surface. Several times the colonists stopped on the bank and watched closely. Nothing appeared. There was some mystery here.

The engineer was greatly intrigued.

"Let's finish this exploration," he said.

Half an hour later they had all reached the southeastern corner of the lake and were back on Prospect Plateau itself. At this point the survey of the lake's shores had to be considered complete — and yet the engineer had not been able to discover where or how the water drained away.

"All the same, that outlet exists," he kept repeating, "and since it isn't on the outside, it must be cut through the inside of the granite mass along the coast!"

"But why does it matter so much to you to find out, my dear Cyrus?" asked Gédéon Spilett.

"It matters a good deal," answered the engineer, "because if the water flows out through the rock, there may be some cavity inside it that would be easy to make livable once we had turned the water aside."

"But isn't it possible, Mr. Cyrus," said Harbert, "that the water drains out through the bottom of the lake itself and reaches the sea by an underground channel?"

"That may well be," answered the engineer, "and if it is, we'll have to build our house ourselves, since nature hasn't paid the first costs of construction."

So the colonists were getting ready to cross the plateau and go back to the Chimneys, for it was five o'clock in the evening, when Top showed fresh signs of agitation. He barked furiously, and before his master could stop him, he rushed into the lake a second time.

Everyone ran to the bank. The dog was already more than twenty feet out, and Cyrus Smith was urgently calling him back, when an enormous head rose out of the water, which did not seem to be deep at that spot.

Harbert recognized at once the kind of amphibious animal that head belonged to — conical, with big eyes, and adorned with whiskers of long, silky hairs.

"A manatee!" he cried.

It was not a manatee, but a specimen of the species, classed in the order of cetaceans, that goes by the name of "dugong," for its nostrils opened on the upper part of its snout.

The enormous animal had lunged at the dog, who tried in vain to escape by turning back toward the bank. His master could do nothing to save him, and before it had even occurred to Gédéon Spilett or Harbert to string their bows, Top, seized by the dugong, vanished beneath the water.

Nab, his iron-tipped spear in his hand, was ready to throw himself in to the dog's rescue, determined to take on the formidable animal even in its own element.

"No, Nab," said the engineer, holding back his brave servant.

Meanwhile a struggle was going on under the water — an inexplicable struggle, since in such conditions Top obviously could not hold out; a struggle that had to be terrible, as they could see from the churning of the surface; a struggle, in short, that could only end in the dog's death! But suddenly, in the middle of a ring of foam, Top appeared again. Hurled into the air by some unknown force, he rose ten feet above the surface of the lake, fell back into the wildly churning water, and soon made it back to the bank without any serious wounds, miraculously saved.

Cyrus Smith and his companions stared without understanding. And here was something even more inexplicable! It looked as though the fight was still going on under the water. No doubt the dugong, attacked by some powerful animal after it had let go of the dog, was now fighting for its own life.

But it did not last long. The water turned red with blood, and the dugong's body, rising out of a scarlet slick that spread far and wide, soon ran aground on a little beach at the southern corner of the lake.

The colonists ran to the spot. The dugong was dead. It was an enormous animal, fifteen or sixteen feet long, and it must have weighed three or four thousand pounds. In its neck gaped a wound that seemed to have been made with a sharp blade.

What amphibious creature could have destroyed the formidable dugong with that terrible blow? No one could have said. And, deeply troubled by the incident, Cyrus Smith and his companions went back to the Chimneys.
