Chapter 15: The Volcano Wakes

_The volcano wakes — The fine season — Work resumes — The evening of October 15 — A telegram — A question — An answer — Off to the corral — The note — The extra wire — The basalt coast — At high tide — At low tide — The cavern — A dazzling light_

The engineer had called out to the colonists. They had stopped their work and stood looking in silence at the summit of Mount Franklin.

So the volcano had woken up, and the vapors had broken through the layer of mineral matter heaped up at the bottom of the crater. But would the underground fires bring on some violent eruption? That was a possibility no one could guard against.

And yet, even supposing there were an eruption, it was likely that Lincoln Island as a whole would not suffer from it. Outpourings of volcanic matter are not always disastrous. The island had already been through this ordeal once, as the lava flows that striped the northern slopes of the mountain bore witness. Besides, the shape of the crater, with the great notch broken out of its upper rim, ought to throw whatever it spewed out toward the side opposite the fertile parts of the island.

Still, the past did not necessarily promise anything about the future. At the summits of volcanoes, old craters often close and new ones open. It has happened in both the Old World and the New — at Etna, at Popocatépetl, at Orizaba — and on the eve of an eruption anything may be feared. All it would take, in short, was an earthquake — a thing that sometimes goes along with volcanic outpourings — to change the inner layout of the mountain and clear new paths for the white-hot lava.

Cyrus Smith explained all this to his companions, and without making the situation sound worse than it was, he laid out the arguments on both sides.

After all, there was nothing they could do about it. Granite House did not seem to be in any danger, unless an earthquake shook the ground itself. But the corral would have everything to fear if some new crater opened in the southern flanks of Mount Franklin.

From that day on, the vapors never stopped crowning the top of the mountain like a plume, and it was even possible to see that they were growing taller and thicker, though no flame mixed with their heavy coils. The activity was still confined to the lower part of the central chimney.

Meanwhile, with the fine weather, the work had started up again. They pushed on with building the ship as fast as they could, and Cyrus Smith used the waterfall on the beach to set up a water-powered sawmill, which cut the tree trunks into planks and beams much more quickly. The machinery was as simple as that of the rustic sawmills that run in Norway. One horizontal motion to push the log forward, one vertical motion to work the saw up and down — that was all he needed to produce, and the engineer managed it with a wheel, two cylinders and some pulleys, properly arranged.

Toward the end of September the skeleton of the ship, which was to be rigged as a schooner, stood on the stocks in the shipyard. The frames were almost all in place, and since they were held together by temporary battens, the lines of the vessel could already be judged. This schooner, sharp at the bow and very clean in her run aft, would obviously be fit for a fairly long voyage if it came to that. But laying the planking, the inner planking and the deck would still take a good deal of time. Very luckily, the ironwork of the old brig had been saved after the underwater explosion. Pencroff and Ayrton had pulled the bolts and a great quantity of copper nails out of the damaged planks and knees. That was so much work saved for the blacksmiths, but the carpenters had plenty to do.

The shipbuilding had to be broken off for a week for the harvest, the haymaking and the bringing in of the various crops that grew in abundance on Prospect Plateau. Once that job was done, every moment from then on went to finishing the schooner.

When night came, the workers were truly worn out. So as not to lose any time, they had changed their mealtimes: they ate dinner at noon and did not have supper until the daylight gave out. Then they went back up to Granite House and hurried off to bed.

Sometimes, though, when the talk turned to some interesting subject, it kept them up a little past bedtime. The colonists let themselves talk about the future, and they liked to discuss the changes a voyage of the schooner to the nearest inhabited lands would bring to their situation. But in the middle of all these plans, one thought always came first: that they would come back to Lincoln Island afterward. They would never abandon this colony, founded with so much toil and so much success, to which a link with America would give a new life.

Pencroff and Nab especially fully expected to end their days there.

"Harbert," the sailor would say, "you'll never abandon Lincoln Island, will you?"

"Never, Pencroff — especially if you make up your mind to stay here!"

"It's made up, my boy," Pencroff would answer. "I'll wait for you! You'll bring me back your wife and your children, and I'll turn your little ones into a fine pack of rascals!"

"It's a deal," Harbert would reply, laughing and blushing at the same time.

"And you, Mr. Cyrus," Pencroff would go on, carried away, "you'll always be governor of the island! Now look here, how many people can she feed? Ten thousand at least!"

They would talk on like that, letting Pencroff run on, and one thing leading to another, the reporter would end up founding a newspaper — the New Lincoln Herald!

Such is the human heart. The need to make a work that lasts, that will outlive him, is the sign of man's superiority over everything else that lives here below. It is what has founded his dominion, and it is what justifies that dominion throughout the world.

After that, who knows whether Jup and Top did not have their own little dreams of the future too?

Ayrton, silent, would tell himself that he wanted to see Lord Glenarvan again and show himself to everyone, redeemed.

One evening, October 15, the conversation, launched among all these speculations, had gone on longer than usual. It was nine o'clock at night. Long yawns, badly hidden, were already announcing bedtime, and Pencroff had just headed for his bed when the electric bell set up in the great hall suddenly rang.

Everyone was there: Cyrus Smith, Gédéon Spilett, Harbert, Ayrton, Pencroff, Nab. So not one of the colonists was at the corral.

Cyrus Smith had stood up. His companions looked at one another, thinking they must have heard wrong.

"What does that mean?" cried Nab. "Is it the devil ringing?"

No one answered.

"The weather's stormy," Harbert pointed out. "Couldn't the electricity in the air..."

Harbert did not finish his sentence. The engineer, toward whom every eye had turned, was shaking his head.

"Let's wait," said Gédéon Spilett then. "If it's a signal, whoever is making it will make it again."

"But who do you think it could be?" cried Nab.

"Why," answered Pencroff, "the one who..."

The sailor's sentence was cut off by a fresh quivering of the hammer on the bell.

Cyrus Smith went over to the apparatus, sent the current along the wire, and dispatched this question to the corral:

    "What do you want?"

A few moments later the needle, moving around the alphabet dial, gave the occupants of Granite House this answer:

    "Come to the corral with all speed."

"At last!" cried Cyrus Smith.

Yes! At last! The mystery was going to be revealed! In the face of the overwhelming interest that was going to drive them to the corral, all the colonists' weariness vanished, all need of rest was gone. Without a word spoken, in a few moments they had left Granite House and were down on the beach. Only Jup and Top stayed behind. They could do without them.

The night was black. The moon, new that very day, had set along with the sun. As Harbert had pointed out, great storm clouds made a low, heavy vault overhead that blotted out every gleam of starlight. A few flashes of heat lightning, reflections of a distant storm, lit up the horizon.

It was possible that within a few hours the thunder would be crashing over the island itself. It was a threatening night.

But the darkness, however deep, could not stop people who knew the Corral Road as well as they did. They went up the left bank of the Mercy, reached the plateau, crossed the bridge over Glycerin Creek and pushed on through the forest.

They walked at a good pace, gripped by strong emotion. For them there was no doubt about it: they were at last going to learn the long-sought answer to the riddle, the name of that mysterious being who had entered so deeply into their lives, so generous in his influence, so powerful in his acts! Didn't this stranger have to be mixed up in their existence, didn't he have to know its smallest details, didn't he have to hear everything that was said in Granite House, to have always been able to act at exactly the right moment?

Each of them, lost in his thoughts, hurried on. Under that vault of trees the darkness was so thick that they could not even see the edge of the road. There was no sound in the forest, either. Beasts and birds, weighed down by the heaviness of the air, were still and silent. Not a breath stirred the leaves. Only the colonists' footsteps rang out in the dark on the hardened ground.

During the first quarter of an hour of the walk, the silence was broken only by this remark from Pencroff:

"We should have brought a lantern."

And by this reply from the engineer:

"We'll find one at the corral."

Cyrus Smith and his companions had left Granite House at twelve minutes past nine. At nine forty-seven they had covered three miles of the five that separated the mouth of the Mercy from the corral.

At that moment great whitish flashes of lightning were bursting over the island, cutting out the jagged shapes of the leaves in black. These intense flashes dazzled and blinded them. The storm obviously could not hold off much longer. Little by little the lightning came faster and brighter. Distant rumblings rolled through the depths of the sky. The air was stifling.

The colonists went on as though some irresistible force were pushing them forward.

At a quarter past nine a bright flash showed them the palisade, and they had not yet passed through the gate when the thunder broke with tremendous violence.

In an instant they had crossed the corral, and Cyrus Smith stood before the house.

It was possible that the stranger was in the house, since it was from the house itself that the telegram must have been sent. Yet no light shone in its window.

The engineer knocked at the door.

No answer.

Cyrus Smith opened the door, and the colonists went into the room, which was pitch dark.

Nab struck a light with his flint and steel, and a moment later the lantern was lit and carried into every corner of the room...

There was no one there. Everything was just as they had left it.

"Have we been taken in by an illusion?" murmured Cyrus Smith.

No! It wasn't possible! The telegram had plainly said:

    "Come to the corral with all speed."

They went over to the table set aside for working the line. Everything on it was in place — the battery and the box that held it, as well as the receiver and the transmitter.

"Who was the last one here?" asked the engineer.

"I was, Mr. Smith," answered Ayrton.

"And that was...?"

"Four days ago."

"Look — a note!" cried Harbert, pointing to a sheet of paper lying on the table.

On that paper these words were written, in English:

    "Follow the new wire."

"Let's go!" cried Cyrus Smith. He understood that the message had not been sent from the corral at all, but from the mysterious hiding place, which an extra wire, spliced onto the old one, connected directly with Granite House.

Nab took the lighted lantern, and they all left the corral.

The storm was now raging with tremendous violence. The gap between each flash of lightning and each clap of thunder was growing noticeably shorter. Soon the storm would be right over Mount Franklin and the whole island. By the glare of the flickering flashes they could see the summit of the volcano crowned with its plume of vapor.

In the part of the corral between the house and the palisade there was no telegraph line at all. But once he was through the gate, the engineer ran straight to the first pole, and by the light of a flash he saw a new wire hanging down from the insulator to the ground.

"There it is!" he said.

This wire lay along the ground, but along its whole length it was covered in an insulating substance, the way an undersea cable is, so that the current could travel freely through it. To judge by its direction, it seemed to make its way through the woods and the southern spurs of the mountain, and so it ran toward the west.

"Let's follow it!" said Cyrus Smith.

And now by the light of the lantern, now in the middle of the blazing lightning, the colonists set off along the path the wire laid out for them.

By then the rolling of the thunder was continuous, and so violent that no word could have been heard. Anyway, this was no time for talking, only for pressing on.

Cyrus Smith and his people first climbed the spur that rose between the valley of the corral and the valley of Fall River, and they crossed the river at its narrowest point. The wire, sometimes stretched over the low branches of the trees, sometimes running along the ground, guided them without fail.

The engineer had supposed that the wire might stop at the bottom of the valley, and that the unknown hiding place would be there.

Not at all. They had to climb the southwest spur and come down again onto that barren plateau which ended in the wall of strangely heaped-up basalt. Every so often one or another of the colonists bent down, felt the wire with his hand and corrected their course if need be. But there was no longer any doubt that the wire ran straight to the sea. There, no doubt, somewhere deep in the volcanic rock, lay the dwelling they had searched for in vain until now.

The sky was on fire. One flash did not wait for the next. Several struck the summit of the volcano and plunged into the crater through the thick smoke. At moments you might have thought the mountain was throwing out flames.

At a few minutes before ten the colonists reached the high edge that looked down over the ocean to the west. The wind had risen. The surf was roaring five hundred feet below.

Cyrus Smith worked out that he and his companions had covered a mile and a half since leaving the corral.

At this point the wire plunged in among the rocks, following the fairly steep slope of a narrow ravine that twisted every which way.

The colonists started down it, at the risk of setting off a slide of loosely balanced rocks and being hurled into the sea. The descent was extremely dangerous, but they took no account of the danger. They were no longer masters of themselves, and an irresistible pull drew them toward that mysterious spot, as a magnet draws iron.

So they went down that ravine almost without knowing it — a ravine that would have been all but impassable even in broad daylight. Stones rolled away and blazed like fiery meteors as they passed through the zones of light. Cyrus Smith was in the lead. Ayrton brought up the rear. Here they went step by step; there they slid on the smooth rock; then they picked themselves up and kept going.

At last the wire turned sharply and touched the rocks of the shore, a regular scattering of reefs that the high tides must pound. The colonists had reached the bottom of the basalt wall.

Here a narrow ledge ran level along the face, parallel to the sea. The wire followed it, and so did the colonists. They had not gone a hundred paces before the ledge, sloping gently downward, came right down to the level of the waves.

The engineer took hold of the wire and saw that it disappeared into the sea.

His companions, standing beside him, were dumbfounded.

A cry of disappointment, almost a cry of despair, burst from them! Were they going to have to throw themselves into the water and look for some undersea cavern? In the state of mental and physical overexcitement they were in, they would not have hesitated to do it.

A remark from the engineer stopped them.

Cyrus Smith led his companions under a hollow in the rocks, and there he said:

"Let's wait. The tide's high. At low tide the way will be open."

"But what makes you think...?" asked Pencroff.

"He wouldn't have called us if there were no way for us to reach him!"

Cyrus Smith had spoken with such conviction that no one raised an objection. And his reasoning was logical. They had to suppose that an opening, passable at low tide and blocked by the sea at that moment, lay at the foot of the wall.

That meant a wait of a few hours. So the colonists stayed huddled in silence under a kind of deep porch hollowed out of the rock. The rain was starting to fall by now, and soon the clouds, torn open by the lightning, were coming down in torrents. The echoes threw back the crash of the thunder and gave it a tremendous resonance.

The colonists' emotion was intense. A thousand strange, supernatural thoughts went through their minds, and they imagined some great, superhuman apparition — the only thing that could have lived up to the idea they had formed of the mysterious genie of the island.

At midnight Cyrus Smith took the lantern and went down to the level of the shore to look at how the rocks lay. The tide had already been going out for two hours.

The engineer had not been mistaken. The curve of a huge vault was beginning to show above the water. There the wire bent at a right angle and went in through that gaping mouth.

Cyrus Smith came back to his companions and said simply:

"In an hour the opening will be passable."

"So it does exist?" asked Pencroff.

"Did you doubt it?" answered Cyrus Smith.

"But this cave will be full of water up to a certain height," Harbert pointed out.

"Either the cave dries out completely," answered Cyrus Smith, "and in that case we'll walk through it, or it doesn't dry out, and some way of getting through it will be put at our disposal."

An hour went by. They all went down through the rain to the water's edge. In three hours the tide had dropped fifteen feet. The top of the arch stood at least eight feet above the water now. It was like the arch of a bridge, with the water flowing under it, flecked with foam.

Leaning over, the engineer saw a black object floating on the surface of the sea. He drew it toward him.

It was a boat, moored by a rope to some projection on the inside of the rock wall. It was made of bolted sheet iron. Two oars lay in the bottom, under the thwarts.

"Let's get in," said Cyrus Smith.

A moment later the colonists were in the boat. Nab and Ayrton took the oars, Pencroff the tiller. Cyrus Smith sat in the bow with the lantern set on the stem, lighting the way.

The roof was very low where the boat first passed under it, but then it rose sharply. Yet the darkness was too deep, and the lantern's light too weak, for them to make out how big the cave was — its width, its height, its depth. In the middle of this vault of basalt an imposing silence reigned. No sound from outside got in, and the crashes of the thunder could not pierce its thick walls.

There are, in a few parts of the globe, immense caverns of this kind, natural crypts of a sort that date from the geological ages of the earth. Some are invaded by the waters of the sea; others hold whole lakes inside them. Such is Fingal's Cave on the island of Staffa, one of the Hebrides; such are the caves of Morgat on the bay of Douarnenez in Brittany, the caves of Bonifacio in Corsica, and those of the Lysefjord in Norway; such is the immense Mammoth Cave in Kentucky, five hundred feet high and more than twenty miles long! In many places on the globe, nature has hollowed out these crypts and preserved them for man to marvel at.

As for the cave the colonists were now exploring — did it really reach all the way to the center of the island? For a quarter of an hour the boat had been moving forward, winding this way and that as the engineer called the turns to Pencroff in a few curt words, when all at once he gave the order:

"More to starboard!"

The boat changed course and at once ran along close to the right-hand wall. The engineer wanted, quite rightly, to make sure the wire was still running along that wall.

The wire was there, hooked onto the jutting rock.

"Go ahead!" said Cyrus Smith.

And the two oars, dipping into the black water, sent the boat on.

The boat went on for another quarter of an hour, and since the mouth of the cave it must have covered half a mile, when Cyrus Smith's voice was heard once more.

"Stop!" he said.

The boat stopped, and the colonists saw a brilliant light flooding the enormous crypt, hollowed out so deep in the bowels of the island.

Now they could look at this cavern, whose existence nothing had ever let them suspect.

A hundred feet up curved a vault, resting on shafts of basalt that all seemed to have been cast in the same mold. Irregular arches and fanciful ribs sprang from these columns, which nature had set up by the thousands in the earliest ages of the world's making. The basalt sections, fitted one into the other, stood forty to fifty feet high, and the water, calm despite the turmoil outside, lapped at their bases. The glare of the light the engineer had pointed out caught every edge of every prism and pricked it with points of fire. It seemed to shine right through the walls, as if they had been transparent, and it turned the least projection of the vault into a glittering polished gem.

By reflection, the water repeated all this brilliance on its surface, so that the boat seemed to float between two layers of sparkling light.

There was no mistaking the nature of the radiance pouring out from that bright center, whose sharp, straight rays broke against every angle and every rib of the crypt. This light came from an electric source; its white color gave away where it came from. It was the sun of this cavern, and it filled the whole of it.

At a sign from Cyrus Smith the oars dipped again, throwing up a perfect shower of sparkling gems, and the boat headed toward the source of the light, until it was soon no more than half a cable length away.

At this point the stretch of water was about three hundred and fifty feet wide, and beyond the dazzling center they could make out an enormous wall of basalt that closed off every way out on that side. So the cave had widened a great deal here, and the sea formed a little lake inside it. But the vault, the side walls, the wall at the far end, all those prisms, all those cylinders, all those cones were so bathed in the electric fluid that the glow seemed to be their own. You could have said of those stones, cut in facets like priceless diamonds, that they were sweating light!

In the middle of the lake a long, spindle-shaped object floated on the surface of the water, silent and motionless. The glow that came from it poured out of its sides, as if from two furnace mouths heated to a welding white heat. This machine, which looked like the body of an enormous whale, was about two hundred and fifty feet long and rose ten to twelve feet above the level of the sea.

The boat drew slowly nearer. In the bow, Cyrus Smith had risen to his feet. He was staring, gripped by violent agitation. Then, all at once, he seized the reporter's arm.

"But it's him! It can only be him!" he cried. "Him!..."

Then he dropped back onto his seat, murmuring a name that only Gédéon Spilett heard.

The reporter evidently knew that name, for it had an extraordinary effect on him, and he answered in a hollow voice:

"Him! An outlaw!"

"Him!" said Cyrus Smith.

At the engineer's order, the boat came up alongside this strange floating machine. It touched on the port quarter, where a beam of light streamed out through a thick pane of glass.

Cyrus Smith and his companions climbed onto the platform. A hatch stood open there. They all rushed through the opening.

At the bottom of the ladder a passageway ran inside, lit by electricity. At the end of the passageway was a door, which Cyrus Smith pushed open.

The colonists hurried through a richly decorated room that led into a library, where a luminous ceiling poured down a flood of light.

At the far end of the library was a wide door, also closed. The engineer opened it.

A vast salon, a kind of museum crammed with all the treasures of the mineral world, with works of art and marvels of industry, appeared before the colonists' eyes. They must have thought they had been carried off by magic into the world of dreams.

Stretched out on a rich divan they saw a man, who did not seem to notice they were there.

Then Cyrus Smith raised his voice, and to the utter amazement of his companions, he spoke these words:

"Captain Nemo, you asked for us? Here we are."
