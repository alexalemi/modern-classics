Chapter 11: An Inexplicable Mystery

_An inexplicable mystery — Harbert's recovery — The parts of the island still to explore — Preparations for departure — The first day — The night — The second day — The kauris — The pair of cassowaries — Footprints in the forest_

Gédéon Spilett took the box and opened it. Inside were about two hundred grains of a white powder. He touched a few specks of it to his lips. Its extreme bitterness could not deceive him. It was indeed the precious alkaloid of cinchona bark, the best febrifuge there is.

They had to give this powder to Harbert without a moment's hesitation. How it had come to be there could be argued about later.

"Coffee," said Gédéon Spilett.

A few moments later Nab brought a cup of the lukewarm brew. Gédéon Spilett stirred about eighteen grains of the quinine into it, and they managed to get Harbert to drink the mixture.

There was still time, for the third attack of the pernicious fever had not yet come!

And, if we may add it here, it was never going to come!

Besides, it must be said, every one of them had found hope again. The mysterious influence had made itself felt once more, and at the supreme moment, just when they had given up hope of it!...

After a few hours Harbert was resting more peacefully. Now the colonists could talk over what had happened. The unknown being's intervention was plainer than ever. But how had he managed to get inside Granite House during the night? It was absolutely inexplicable, and truly the way the "genie of the island" went about things was no less strange than the genie himself.

Throughout that day, roughly every three hours, Harbert was given a dose of the quinine sulfate.

By the very next day Harbert felt a certain improvement. He was certainly not cured. Intermittent fevers are apt to come back, often and dangerously. But he lacked no care. And then, the remedy was there — and no doubt the one who had brought it was not far off! In short, an immense hope came back into every heart.

That hope was not disappointed. Ten days later, on December 20, Harbert began his convalescence. He was still weak, and he had been put on a strict diet, but not a single attack had come back. And the obedient boy followed every order he was given so willingly! He wanted so badly to get well!

Pencroff was like a man who has been pulled up from the bottom of an abyss. He had fits of joy that came close to delirium. Once the hour of the third attack had gone by, he had hugged the reporter so hard he nearly smothered him. From then on he never called him anything but Dr. Spilett.

The real doctor remained to be found.

"We'll find him!" the sailor kept saying.

And certainly that man, whoever he might be, could expect a rough hug from the worthy Pencroff!

December came to an end, and with it the year 1867, in which the colonists of Lincoln Island had been so hard tried. They entered the year 1868 in magnificent weather — splendid heat, a tropical temperature, happily cooled by the sea breeze. Harbert was coming back to life. From his bed, set beside one of the windows of Granite House, he breathed in that healthy air, heavy with the smell of salt, and it gave him back his health. He was beginning to eat, and heaven knows what good little dishes, light and tasty, Nab cooked up for him!

"It was enough to make you wish you'd been dying!" said Pencroff.

During this whole period the convicts did not show themselves near Granite House a single time. Of Ayrton there was no news. The engineer and Harbert still kept some hope of finding him, but their companions no longer doubted that the unfortunate man had been killed. All the same, this uncertainty could not go on, and as soon as the boy was fit, the expedition would be undertaken — and its result was to be of great importance. But they would have to wait perhaps a month, because it would take the colony's full strength to get the better of the convicts.

Meanwhile Harbert was getting better and better. The congestion of the liver had cleared, and his wounds could be considered healed for good.

During that month of January important work was done on Prospect Plateau, but it consisted only of saving what could be saved of the ruined crops, wheat and vegetables alike. Seeds and plants were gathered up, so as to provide a new harvest in the coming season. As for rebuilding the poultry-yard sheds, the mill and the stables, Cyrus Smith preferred to wait. While he and his companions were out hunting the convicts, those villains might very well pay the plateau another visit, and it would not do to give them a reason to go back to their trade of plunderers and arsonists. Once the island had been cleared of these criminals, they would see about rebuilding.

The young convalescent had begun to get up in the second half of January — at first for one hour a day, then two, then three. His strength came back visibly, so vigorous was his constitution. He was eighteen by then. He was tall, and he promised to become a man of fine and noble bearing. From that time on his recovery, though it still called for some care — and Dr. Spilett was very strict — went steadily forward.

By the end of the month Harbert was already walking about Prospect Plateau and the beaches. A few sea baths that he took with Pencroff and Nab did him a world of good. Cyrus Smith thought he could already set the day of departure, and it was fixed for the coming February 15. The nights, very clear at that time of year, would favor the searches they meant to make over the whole island.

So the preparations for this exploration were begun, and they had to be thorough, because the colonists had sworn not to come back to Granite House until their double goal had been reached: on the one hand, to destroy the convicts and find Ayrton, if he was still alive; on the other, to discover the one who watched over the colony's fortunes so effectively.

Of Lincoln Island, the colonists knew thoroughly the whole east coast from Claw Cape to the Mandible Capes, the vast Shelduck Marsh, the surroundings of Lake Grant, Jacamar Wood between the Corral Road and the Mercy, the courses of the Mercy and Red Creek, and finally the spurs of Mount Franklin, among which the corral had been built.

They had explored, but only incompletely, the long shoreline of Washington Bay from Claw Cape to Reptile End, the strip of forest and marsh along the west coast, and those endless dunes that ended at the half-open jaws of Shark Gulf.

But they had not surveyed at all the broad wooded stretches that covered Serpentine Peninsula, the whole right bank of the Mercy, the left bank of Fall River, and the tangle of spurs and side valleys that held up three-quarters of the base of Mount Franklin to the west, north and east — where no doubt there were plenty of deep hiding places. That meant several thousand acres of the island had still escaped their investigations.

So it was decided that the expedition would cross the Far West, so as to take in the whole region lying on the right bank of the Mercy.

It might perhaps have been better to head first for the corral, where it was feared the convicts had taken refuge again, either to plunder it or to settle in. But either the corral had already been laid waste, and it was too late to prevent it, or the convicts had found it worth their while to dig in there, and there would always be time to go and drive them out of their hideout.

So, after some discussion, the first plan was kept, and the colonists resolved to make their way through the woods to Reptile End. They would cut their way with axes, and so lay out the first track of a road that would link Granite House with the tip of the peninsula, over a distance of sixteen or seventeen miles.

The cart was in perfect condition. The onaggas, well rested, could manage a long haul. Food, camping gear, the portable stove and various utensils were loaded onto the cart, along with weapons and ammunition chosen with care from Granite House's arsenal, so well stocked now. But they could not forget that the convicts might be roaming the woods, and that in the middle of those thick forests a shot was quickly fired — and quickly taken. So the little band of colonists had to stay together and not split up on any pretext.

It was also decided that no one would stay behind at Granite House. Even Top and Jup were to be part of the expedition. The inaccessible dwelling could guard itself.

February 14, the day before they left, was a Sunday. It was given over entirely to rest and made holy by the thanksgiving the colonists offered to the Creator. Harbert, completely cured but still a little weak, would have a place kept for him on the cart.

The next day, at daybreak, Cyrus Smith took the measures needed to keep Granite House safe from any invasion. The ladders that had once been used to climb up were carried to the Chimneys and buried deep in the sand, so that they could serve for the return — because the drum of the elevator was taken apart, and nothing was left of the machine. Pencroff stayed in Granite House last, to finish the job, and he came down by means of a doubled rope whose other end was held at the bottom. Once the rope had been pulled down to the ground, there was no longer any link at all between the upper landing and the beach.

The weather was magnificent.

"It's going to be a hot day!" said the reporter cheerfully.

"Bah! Dr. Spilett," answered Pencroff, "we'll be walking in the shade of the trees, and we won't even see the sun!"

"Let's go!" said the engineer.

The cart was waiting on the shore in front of the Chimneys. The reporter had insisted that Harbert ride in it, at least for the first few hours of the trip, and the boy had to obey his doctor's orders.

Nab went at the head of the onaggas. Cyrus Smith, the reporter and the sailor walked ahead. Top frisked about happily. Harbert had offered Jup a seat in his carriage, and Jup had accepted without any fuss. The moment of departure had come, and the little band set off.

The cart first rounded the corner at the river mouth. Then, after going a mile up the left bank of the Mercy, it crossed the bridge where the road to Balloon Harbor began. There the explorers left that road on their left and began to push in under the cover of the immense woods that made up the Far West.

For the first two miles the trees were widely spaced, and the cart could move about freely. Now and then they had to cut through a few creepers and thickets of brush, but nothing serious stopped the colonists' progress.

The thick branches overhead kept a cool shade on the ground. Deodars, Douglas firs, casuarinas, banksias, gum trees, dragon trees and other species they already knew followed one another as far as the eye could see. All the birds familiar on the island were there in full force — grouse, jacamars, pheasants, lories, and the whole chattering family of cockatoos, parakeets and parrots. Agoutis, kangaroos and capybaras darted through the grass, and all of it reminded the colonists of their first excursions when they had arrived on the island.

"All the same," Cyrus Smith pointed out, "I notice that these animals, four-footed and feathered alike, are shyer than they used to be. So these woods have been gone through recently by the convicts, and we're sure to find traces of them."

And indeed, in many places they could make out where a band of men had passed, more or less recently. Here, branches broken on the trees, perhaps to mark the way; there, the ashes of a dead fire, and footprints kept by patches of clayey ground. But all in all, nothing that looked like a permanent camp.

The engineer had told his companions not to hunt. Gunshots might have alerted the convicts, who were perhaps prowling the forest. Besides, the hunters would inevitably have been drawn some distance from the cart, and walking alone was strictly forbidden.

In the second part of the day, about six miles from Granite House, the going became rather hard. To get through certain thickets they had to fell trees and clear a path. Before going into these dense copses, Cyrus Smith took care to send Top and Jup in first, and they carried out their task conscientiously. When the dog and the orangutan came back without having raised any alarm, it meant there was nothing to fear, either from the convicts or from the wild beasts — two kinds of creature in the animal kingdom whose savage instincts put them on the same level.

On the evening of that first day the colonists camped about nine miles from Granite House, on the bank of a little tributary of the Mercy that they had not known existed. It must have belonged to the network of waters to which this soil owed its astonishing fertility.

They ate a hearty supper, for the colonists' appetites were very sharp, and measures were taken so that the night would pass without trouble. If the engineer had had to deal only with wild animals, jaguars or others, he would simply have lit fires around his camp, which would have been enough to protect it. But the convicts would have been drawn by those flames rather than kept off, and in that case it was better to wrap themselves in deep darkness.

The watch, besides, was strictly organized. Two of the colonists were to keep watch together, and it was agreed that they would be relieved by their comrades every two hours. Since Harbert, in spite of his protests, was let off guard duty, Pencroff and Gédéon Spilett on the one hand, and the engineer and Nab on the other, took turns standing guard at the approaches to the camp.

As it happened, there were barely a few hours of night. The darkness came more from the thickness of the branches than from the setting of the sun. The silence was hardly broken except by the hoarse howls of jaguars and the cackling of monkeys, which seemed to get on Master Jup's nerves in particular.

The night passed without incident, and the next day, February 16, the march through the forest resumed — slow rather than hard.

That day they could cover only six miles, because at every moment they had to hack out a road with the ax. Like true settlers, the colonists spared the tall, handsome trees — felling them would have cost them enormous effort anyway — and sacrificed the small ones. But the result was that the road did not run very straight and grew longer with many detours.

During that day Harbert discovered new species whose presence had not yet been noted on the island: tree ferns, their drooping fronds seeming to spill over like the water from a fountain basin, and carob trees, whose long pods the onaggas browsed greedily and which gave a sweet pulp with an excellent taste. There, too, the colonists came across magnificent kauris again, standing in groups, their cylindrical trunks, crowned with a cone of greenery, rising to a height of two hundred feet. These were indeed the royal trees of New Zealand, as famous as the cedars of Lebanon.

As for the animals, they showed no specimens other than those the hunters already knew. However, they caught a glimpse, without being able to get near them, of a pair of those large birds found only in Australia — a kind of cassowary called the emu — which stand five feet tall, have brown plumage, and belong to the order of wading birds. Top dashed after them as fast as his four legs would carry him, but the cassowaries easily left him behind, so prodigious was their speed.

As for the traces the convicts had left in the forest, a few more were found. Near a fire that seemed to have been put out recently, the colonists noticed footprints, and these were studied with the greatest attention. By measuring them one after another, length and breadth, they easily made out the tracks of five men's feet. The five convicts had clearly camped at this spot. But — and this was the whole point of so careful an examination! — they could not find a sixth footprint, which in that case would have been Ayrton's.

"Ayrton wasn't with them!" said Harbert.

"No," answered Pencroff, "and if he wasn't with them, it's because those villains had already killed him! But don't these scoundrels have a den, so we can go and hunt them down like tigers?"

"No," answered the reporter. "It's more likely they're wandering wherever chance takes them. It's in their interest to keep roaming like that until the moment they're masters of the island."

"Masters of the island!" cried the sailor. "Masters of the island!..." he repeated, and his voice was choked, as if an iron fist had seized him by the throat. Then, more calmly:

"Do you know, Mr. Cyrus," he said, "what bullet I've loaded into my gun?"

"No, Pencroff!"

"It's the bullet that went through Harbert's chest, and I promise you that one won't miss its mark!"

But that just revenge could not bring Ayrton back to life, and from this examination of the prints left on the ground they had, alas, to conclude that there was no longer any hope at all of ever seeing him again!

That evening the camp was set up fourteen miles from Granite House, and Cyrus Smith reckoned that it could not be more than five miles from Reptile End.

And in fact, the next day, they reached the tip of the peninsula, and the forest had been crossed from end to end. But not a single clue had led them to the hideout where the convicts had taken refuge — nor to the one, no less secret, that sheltered the mysterious stranger.
