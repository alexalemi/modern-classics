Chapter 1: Ruin or Rescue?

_Ruin or rescue? — Ayrton called back — An important discussion — It is not the Duncan — A suspicious ship — Precautions to take — The ship approaches — A cannon shot — The brig anchors in sight of the island — Night falls_

For two and a half years the castaways of the balloon had been stranded on Lincoln Island, and in all that time no contact of any kind had been made between them and the rest of humankind. Once, the reporter had tried to get in touch with the inhabited world by entrusting a bird with a note that held the secret of their situation, but that was a chance no one could seriously count on. Only Ayrton, under the circumstances we know, had come to join the members of the little colony. And now, on this very day — October 17 — other men had suddenly appeared within sight of the island, on that sea which was always empty!

There could be no more doubt about it! A ship was out there! But would she pass by out at sea, or would she put in? Within a few hours the colonists would certainly know where they stood.

Cyrus Smith and Harbert had at once called Gédéon Spilett, Pencroff and Nab into the great hall of Granite House and told them what was happening. Pencroff snatched up the spyglass and swept the horizon quickly, then stopped on the point they showed him — the spot that had made the almost invisible speck on the photographic plate.

"A thousand devils! It's a ship, all right!" he said, in a voice that did not sound especially pleased.

"Is she coming toward us?" asked Gédéon Spilett.

"No way of telling yet," answered Pencroff, "because only her masts show above the horizon. You can't see a scrap of her hull!"

"What should we do?" said the boy.

"Wait," answered Cyrus Smith.

And for a good long while the colonists stayed silent, given over to all the thoughts, all the emotions, all the fears, all the hopes that such an event could stir in them — the most serious thing that had happened since they had arrived on Lincoln Island.

Of course, the colonists were not in the position of those castaways left on some barren islet, who fight a cruel, stepmotherly nature for their miserable existence and are eaten up, every moment, by the longing to see inhabited lands again. Pencroff and Nab above all, who found themselves so happy and so rich at once, would not have left their island without regret. Besides, they had grown used to this new life, in the middle of this domain that their intelligence had, so to speak, civilized! But still — that ship meant news from the mainland, in any case. Perhaps it was a piece of their homeland coming out to meet them! She carried human beings like themselves, and anyone will understand how their hearts leaped at the sight of her!

Every so often Pencroff took up the spyglass again and stationed himself at the window. From there he studied the ship with the closest attention. She was twenty miles to the east, so the colonists still had no way at all to signal that they were there. A flag would not have been seen, a gunshot would not have been heard, a fire would not have been visible.

Still, it was certain that the island, with Mount Franklin towering over it, could not have escaped the eyes of the lookouts on board. But why would the ship land there? Wasn't it pure chance that had brought her into this part of the Pacific, where the charts showed no land at all except Tabor Island — and Tabor Island itself lay off the routes that ocean-going ships usually followed from the Polynesian archipelagos, New Zealand and the American coast?

The question everyone was asking himself was suddenly answered by Harbert.

"Could it be the Duncan?" he cried.

The Duncan, as no one will have forgotten, was the yacht of Lord Glenarvan, who had left Ayrton on the islet and who was supposed to come back for him one day. And the islet was not so far from Lincoln Island that a ship sailing for the one could not pass within sight of the other. Only a hundred and fifty miles of longitude separated them, and seventy-five miles of latitude.

"We must let Ayrton know," said Gédéon Spilett, "and send for him right away. He's the only one who can tell us if that's the Duncan."

Everyone agreed, and the reporter went to the telegraph that connected the corral with Granite House and sent this telegram:

"Come as fast as you can."

A few moments later the bell rang.

"I'm coming," Ayrton answered.

Then the colonists went on watching the ship.

"If it's the Duncan," said Harbert, "Ayrton will recognize her easily, since he sailed aboard her for a while."

"And if he does recognize her," added Pencroff, "it'll give him quite a shock!"

"Yes," answered Cyrus Smith, "but Ayrton is worthy now of going back aboard the Duncan, and heaven grant that it really is Lord Glenarvan's yacht, because any other ship would look suspicious to me! These seas have a bad reputation, and I'm always afraid our island will get a visit from Malay pirates."

"We'd defend it!" cried Harbert.

"No doubt, my boy," the engineer answered with a smile, "but it's better not to have to."

"Just one observation," said Gédéon Spilett. "Lincoln Island is unknown to sailors, since it isn't even marked on the most recent charts. Don't you think, Cyrus, that that's a reason for a ship that suddenly finds herself in sight of a new land to try to visit it rather than run away from it?"

"Certainly," answered Pencroff.

"I think so too," added the engineer. "You could even say that it's a captain's duty to report — and therefore to come and survey — any land or island not yet on the lists, and Lincoln Island is exactly that."

"Well, then," said Pencroff, "let's suppose this ship comes in and anchors there, a few cable lengths off our island. What do we do?"

The question, put so bluntly, at first got no answer. But Cyrus Smith, after thinking it over, replied in the calm tone that was usual with him:

"Here is what we'll do, my friends — what we ought to do. We'll make contact with the ship, we'll take passage aboard her, and we'll leave our island, after taking possession of it in the name of the States of the Union. Then we'll come back with everyone who wants to follow us, to colonize it for good and give the American republic a useful station in this part of the Pacific Ocean!"

"Hurrah!" cried Pencroff. "And that'll be no small present we'll be making our country! The colonizing is nearly finished already. Every part of the island has its name. There's a natural harbor, a watering place, roads, a telegraph line, a shipyard, a factory — and all that's left is to put Lincoln Island on the map!"

"But what if somebody takes it from us while we're away?" Gédéon Spilett pointed out.

"A thousand devils!" cried the sailor. "Then I'll stay here all by myself to guard it, and on Pencroff's word, nobody's going to steal it from me like a watch out of some gawker's pocket!"

For an hour it was impossible to say for certain whether the ship they had sighted was or was not heading for Lincoln Island. She had come nearer, though — but on what point of sail? That was something Pencroff could not make out. Still, since the wind was blowing from the northeast, it seemed likely that she was sailing on the starboard tack. Besides, the breeze was right for carrying her in toward the island's shores, and in so calm a sea she had no reason to be afraid of coming close, even though no soundings for those waters were marked on the chart.

At about four o'clock — an hour after he had been sent for — Ayrton arrived at Granite House. He came into the great hall, saying:

"At your service, gentlemen."

Cyrus Smith held out his hand to him, as he always did, and led him over to the window.

"Ayrton," he said, "we've asked you to come for a serious reason. There's a ship in sight of the island."

At first Ayrton went a little pale, and for a moment his eyes clouded. Then he leaned out of the window and swept the horizon, but he saw nothing.

"Take this spyglass," said Gédéon Spilett, "and look carefully, Ayrton, because this ship might be the Duncan, come into these seas to take you home."

"The Duncan!" Ayrton murmured. "Already!"

That last word slipped from Ayrton's lips as though in spite of himself, and he let his head fall into his hands.

So twelve years of abandonment on a desert islet did not seem to him atonement enough? Did the repentant man still not feel forgiven, either in his own eyes or in the eyes of others?

"No," he said, "no! It can't be the Duncan."

"Look, Ayrton," said the engineer then, "because it matters that we know ahead of time where we stand."

Ayrton took the glass and trained it in the direction they showed him. For several minutes he watched the horizon without moving, without saying a single word. Then:

"It's a ship, right enough," he said, "but I don't believe it's the Duncan."

"Why not?" asked Gédéon Spilett.

"Because the Duncan is a steam yacht, and I can't see any trace of smoke, either above that ship or near her."

"Maybe she's just under sail," Pencroff pointed out. "The wind's good for the course she seems to be on, and she'd want to save her coal, being so far from any land."

"You may be right, Mr. Pencroff," answered Ayrton, "and she may have let her fires go out. So let's wait for her to come in toward the coast, and we'll soon know where we stand."

With that, Ayrton went and sat down in a corner of the great hall and stayed there in silence. The colonists went on discussing the unknown ship, but Ayrton took no part in the discussion.

They were all in a state of mind that would not have let them carry on with their work. Gédéon Spilett and Pencroff were strangely on edge, coming and going, unable to keep still. Harbert felt curiosity more than anything. Nab alone kept his usual calm. Wasn't his country wherever his master was? As for the engineer, he remained lost in thought, and deep down he dreaded the ship's arrival more than he wished for it.

Meanwhile the ship had come a little closer to the island. With the help of the spyglass they had been able to make out that she was an ocean-going vessel, and not one of the Malay proas that the pirates of the Pacific usually sail in. So there was reason to believe that the engineer's fears would not turn out to be justified, and that this ship's presence in the waters of Lincoln Island meant no danger to it. Pencroff, after studying her with minute attention, felt he could say for certain that she was rigged as a brig, and that she was running at a slant to the coast on the starboard tack, under her courses, her topsails and her topgallants. Ayrton confirmed it.

But if she kept on that way she would soon disappear behind the tip of Claw Cape, because she was heading southwest, and to watch her then they would have to go up onto the heights above Washington Bay, near Balloon Harbor. That was a nuisance, since it was already five o'clock in the afternoon, and twilight would soon make any observation very difficult.

"What do we do once night comes?" asked Gédéon Spilett. "Do we light a fire to signal that we're here on this coast?"

It was a serious question, and yet, whatever forebodings the engineer still had, it was settled in favor of the fire. During the night the ship might vanish, sail off for good — and once she was gone, would another ever come back into the waters of Lincoln Island? And who could foresee what the future held for the colonists?

"Yes," said the reporter, "we have to let this ship know, whatever she is, that the island is inhabited. If we let this chance slip, we'll be making regrets for ourselves later!"

So it was decided that Nab and Pencroff would go to Balloon Harbor, and that there, once night had fallen, they would light a big fire whose blaze would be sure to catch the attention of the brig's crew.

But just as Nab and the sailor were getting ready to leave Granite House, the ship changed course, bore away and headed straight for the island, making for Union Bay. She was a fast sailer, that brig, for she drew near quickly.

So Nab and Pencroff put off their departure, and the spyglass was handed to Ayrton so that he could say once and for all whether this ship was or was not the Duncan. The Scottish yacht was rigged as a brig too. The question, then, was whether a funnel rose between the two masts of the ship they were watching, which was now only ten miles away.

The horizon was still very clear. The check was easy, and Ayrton soon let the glass drop, saying:

"It isn't the Duncan! It couldn't have been!..."

Pencroff framed the brig in the field of the spyglass once more, and he saw that she was a vessel of three to four hundred tons, wonderfully slender, boldly masted, beautifully built for speed — she had to be a swift runner of the seas. But what nation did she belong to? That was hard to say.

"And yet," added the sailor, "there's a flag flying from her gaff, but I can't make out the colors."

"Within half an hour we'll know about that," answered the reporter. "Anyway, it's obvious that this ship's captain means to put in here, and so, if not today, then tomorrow at the latest, we'll be making his acquaintance."

"Never mind that!" said Pencroff. "It's better to know who you're dealing with, and I wouldn't mind knowing that fellow's colors!"

And all the while he talked, the sailor never took his eye from the glass.

The daylight was beginning to fade, and with it the wind from the sea was dropping too. The brig's flag, no longer stretched out, kept tangling in the halyards and grew harder and harder to see.

"That's not an American flag," Pencroff kept saying every now and then, "and not an English one either — you'd see the red easily — and not French or German colors, and not the white flag of Russia, and not the yellow of Spain... It looks as if it's all one color... Let's see... in these seas... what would we be most likely to meet?... The Chilean flag? But that's three colors... Brazilian? That's green... Japanese? That's black and yellow... whereas this one..."

At that moment a breeze spread out the unknown flag. Ayrton seized the spyglass the sailor had let drop, put it to his eye, and in a hollow voice:

"The black flag!" he cried.

And in fact a dark bunting was unfurling at the brig's gaff, and now there was every reason to regard her as a suspicious ship!

Had the engineer been right in his forebodings, then? Was this a pirate ship? Was she scouring these lower seas of the Pacific, in competition with the Malay proas that still infest them? What had she come looking for off the shores of Lincoln Island? Did she see in it an unknown, unheard-of land, just the place to hide stolen cargoes? Had she come to these coasts for a harbor of refuge in the winter months? Was the colonists' honest domain destined to turn into a vile den — a sort of capital of piracy in the Pacific?

All these ideas came into the colonists' minds by instinct. Besides, there could be no doubt what the color of the flag she flew was meant to signify. It was the flag of the sea raiders, all right! It was the one the Duncan would have been flying if the convicts had succeeded in their criminal plans!

They wasted no time in discussion.

"My friends," said Cyrus Smith, "perhaps this ship means only to look over the island's coastline. Perhaps her crew won't come ashore. That's one chance. Whatever happens, we must do everything we can to hide the fact that we're here. The windmill on Prospect Plateau is far too easy to spot. Ayrton and Nab, go and take its sails down. Let's also cover the windows of Granite House with thicker branches. Put out every fire. Let nothing at all betray that there are human beings on this island!"

"And our boat?" said Harbert.

"Oh," answered Pencroff, "she's sheltered in Balloon Harbor, and I defy those scoundrels to find her there!"

The engineer's orders were carried out at once. Nab and Ayrton went up onto the plateau and took the steps needed to hide every sign that anyone lived there. While they were busy with that job, their companions went to the edge of Jacamar Wood and brought back a great quantity of branches and creepers which, seen from a certain distance, would look like natural foliage and would screen the openings in the granite wall quite well. At the same time the ammunition and the weapons were laid out so that they could be used at a moment's notice in case of a sudden attack.

When all these precautions had been taken:

"My friends," said Cyrus Smith — and you could hear in his voice how moved he was — "if those villains try to seize Lincoln Island, we'll defend it, won't we?"

"Yes, Cyrus," answered the reporter, "and if we have to, we'll all die defending it!"

The engineer held out his hand to his companions, and they gripped it warmly.

Only Ayrton, who had stayed in his corner, had not joined the colonists. Perhaps he, the former convict, still felt unworthy!

Cyrus Smith understood what was going on in Ayrton's soul, and he went over to him.

"And you, Ayrton," he asked him, "what will you do?"

"My duty," answered Ayrton.

Then he went and took up his post by the window and fixed his gaze out through the leaves.

It was half past seven by then. The sun had gone down behind Granite House some twenty minutes before, and so the eastern horizon was slowly darkening. Meanwhile the brig kept on toward Union Bay. She was no more than eight miles off by then, and exactly abreast of Prospect Plateau, because after coming about off Claw Cape she had made a good deal of ground to the north, helped along by the current of the rising tide. You could even say that at that distance she had already entered the vast bay, since a straight line drawn from Claw Cape to Mandible Cape now lay to the west of her, off her starboard quarter.

Would the brig sail deep into the bay? That was the first question. Once inside, would she anchor there? That was the second. Or would she be content to look over the coastline and then head back out to sea without landing her crew? They would know within the hour. So the colonists had nothing to do but wait.

Cyrus Smith had watched the suspicious ship hoist the black flag with deep anxiety. Wasn't it a direct threat to the work that he and his companions had carried through so far? Had these pirates — for there could be no doubt the brig's sailors were that — been to the island before, since they had run up their colors on approaching it? Had they made some landing here in the past, which would explain certain things that had remained inexplicable until now? Was there, in some part of the island not yet explored, an accomplice ready to make contact with them?

Cyrus Smith had no answer to any of the questions he silently asked himself, but he felt that the colony's situation could only be very seriously endangered by the arrival of this brig.

All the same, he and his companions were determined to resist to the very last. Were these pirates many, and better armed than the colonists? That was what it would have been so important to know! But how to get near them?

Night had come. The new moon, lost in the glare of the sun, had gone down with it. Deep darkness wrapped the island and the sea. Heavy clouds, heaped up on the horizon, let no glimmer of light filter through. The wind had died away completely with the dusk. Not a leaf stirred on the trees, not a wave whispered on the shore. Of the ship nothing could be seen. All her lights were blacked out, and if she was still in sight of the island, there was no way even to tell where she lay.

"Eh! Who knows?" said Pencroff then. "Maybe that damned ship will sail off during the night, and we won't find her again at daybreak!"

As if in answer to the sailor's remark, a bright flash burst out at sea, and a cannon shot rang out.

The ship was still there, and she had guns on board.

Six seconds had passed between the flash and the sound.

So the brig was about a mile and a quarter from the coast.

And at the same moment they heard the sound of chains running and grinding through the hawseholes.

The ship had just dropped anchor in sight of Granite House!
