Chapter 11: The Fuel of the Future

_Winter — Fulling the wool — The mill — Pencroff's fixed idea — The whalebone — What an albatross can be used for — The fuel of the future — Top and Jup — Storms — Damage to the poultry yard — An excursion to the marsh — Cyrus Smith alone — Exploring the well_

Meanwhile winter was arriving with the month of June, which is the December of the northern zones, and the great task of the season was making warm, sturdy clothes.

The mouflons in the corral had been sheared, so the colonists had their precious textile fiber. All that remained was to turn it into cloth.

It goes without saying that Cyrus Smith had no carding machines, no combing machines, no smoothing machines, no drawing frames, no twisting frames, no "spinning mule" or "self-acting" mule to spin the wool, and no loom to weave it. He had to go about it a simpler way, one that would save both spinning and weaving. And in fact he meant quite simply to make use of a property of wool fibers: when they are pressed together from every direction, they tangle up, and just by crisscrossing they form the fabric called felt. So felt could be made by simple fulling, that is, by pounding the wool. The process makes the fabric less supple, but it does a great deal to increase how well it holds in heat. And it so happened that the wool from the mouflons had very short fibers, which is just what makes for good felting.

The engineer, helped by his companions, Pencroff included (once again he had to leave his boat!), began with the preliminary work, whose purpose was to rid the wool of the oily, greasy substance it is soaked in, which is called suint. This degreasing was done in tubs full of water, heated to a temperature of a hundred and fifty-eight degrees Fahrenheit, and the wool soaked in them for twenty-four hours. After that it was washed thoroughly in baths of soda. Then, once the wool had been squeezed dry enough, it was ready to be fulled, that is, to be made into a strong fabric. It would be coarse, no doubt, and it would have had no value at all in a manufacturing center in Europe or America, but it was going to be prized very highly on "the markets of Lincoln Island."

Clearly this kind of fabric must have been known from the most ancient times, and in fact the first woolen fabrics ever made were made by the very process Cyrus Smith was about to use.

Where his training as an engineer served him best was in building the machine for fulling the wool. He cleverly took advantage of a mechanical power that had gone unused until then, the force of the waterfall on the beach, to drive a fulling mill.

Nothing could have been more primitive. A shaft fitted with cams, which lifted vertical pestles one after another and let them drop again; troughs to hold the wool, into which the pestles fell; a strong timber frame to hold and tie the whole system together: that was the machine in question. And that was what such machines had been for centuries, until someone had the idea of replacing the pestles with pressing rollers, so that the material was no longer beaten but truly rolled flat.

The operation, carefully directed by Cyrus Smith, was a complete success. The wool had first been soaked in a soapy solution. On the one hand this helped the fibers to slide, draw together, pack down and soften; on the other, it kept the beating from damaging them. The wool came out of the mill as a thick sheet of felt. The tiny ridges and rough scales that every wool fiber naturally carries had caught and tangled in one another so well that they made a fabric fit for clothes and blankets alike. Obviously it was not merino, or muslin, or Scotch cashmere, or worsted stuff, or rep, or China satin, or Orleans cloth, or alpaca, or broadcloth, or flannel! It was "Lincolnian felt," and Lincoln Island had one more industry.

So the colonists had good clothes and thick blankets, and they could watch the winter of 1866-67 approach without fear.

The real cold began to bite around June 20, and to his great regret Pencroff had to put off building the boat, which in any case was certain to be finished by the next spring.

The sailor's fixed idea was to make a voyage of exploration to Tabor Island. Cyrus Smith did not approve of the trip, which was pure curiosity, since there was obviously no help to be found on that bare, half-barren rock. A voyage of a hundred and fifty miles in a fairly small boat, across unknown seas, gave him a good deal of worry. Suppose the boat, once out at sea, found it impossible to reach Tabor and could not get back to Lincoln Island. What would become of her in the middle of the Pacific, an ocean so rich in disasters?

Cyrus Smith often talked over the plan with Pencroff, and he found in the sailor a rather odd stubbornness about making this voyage, a stubbornness that Pencroff himself may not have fully understood.

"After all," the engineer said to him one day, "I'll point out to you, my friend, that after singing the praises of Lincoln Island so often, after telling us so many times how sorry you'd be if you ever had to leave it, you're the first one who wants to go."

"Leave it for a few days, that's all," answered Pencroff, "just for a few days, Mr. Cyrus! Long enough to go and come back and see what that islet is!"

"But it can't be as good as Lincoln Island!"

"I'm sure of that already!"

"Then why take the risk?"

"To find out what's going on on Tabor Island!"

"But nothing's going on there! Nothing can be going on there!"

"Who knows?"

"And what if you're caught in a storm?"

"That's nothing to fear in the good season," answered Pencroff. "But, Mr. Cyrus, since we have to plan for everything, I'll ask your permission to take only Harbert with me on the voyage."

"Pencroff," said the engineer, putting his hand on the sailor's shoulder, "if anything happened to you and to that boy, whom chance has made our son, do you think we would ever get over it?"

"Mr. Cyrus," answered Pencroff with unshakable confidence, "we won't bring you that grief. Anyway, we'll talk about this voyage again when the time comes to make it. And then I imagine that when you've seen our boat well rigged and well fitted out, when you've watched how she behaves at sea, when we've sailed around our island — because we'll do that together — I imagine, I say, that you won't hesitate any longer to let me go! I won't hide it from you: she's going to be a masterpiece, that boat of yours!"

"At least say 'our boat,' Pencroff!" answered the engineer, disarmed for the moment.

There the conversation ended, to begin again later, without convincing either the sailor or the engineer.

The first snow fell toward the end of June. Before that, the corral had been well stocked with provisions and no longer needed daily visits, but it was decided that they would never let a week go by without going there.

The traps were set again, and the devices Cyrus Smith had made got their first trial. The curved strips of whalebone, locked in a sheath of ice and covered with a thick layer of fat, were laid at the edge of the forest, at the spot where animals usually passed on their way to the lake.

To the engineer's great satisfaction, this invention, borrowed from the Aleut hunters, worked perfectly. A dozen foxes, a few wild boars and even a jaguar were caught by it, and the animals were found dead, their stomachs pierced by the whalebone as it sprang straight.

Here belongs an experiment that deserves to be told, because it was the colonists' first attempt to communicate with their fellow men.

Gédéon Spilett had already thought several times of throwing a message into the sea, sealed in a bottle that the currents might carry to an inhabited coast, or else of entrusting it to pigeons. But how could anyone seriously hope that pigeons or bottles would cross the distance that separated the island from any land, which was twelve hundred miles? It would have been sheer folly.

But on June 30, not without trouble, they captured an albatross that a shot from Harbert's gun had slightly wounded in the foot. It was a magnificent bird of that family of great ocean fliers whose outspread wings measure ten feet from tip to tip, and which can cross seas as wide as the Pacific.

Harbert would have liked very much to keep the splendid bird. Its wound healed quickly, and he claimed he could tame it. But Gédéon Spilett made him understand that they could not pass up this chance to try to reach the lands of the Pacific by such a courier, and Harbert had to give in, for if the albatross had come from some inhabited region, it would surely go back there as soon as it was free.

Perhaps, deep down, Gédéon Spilett, in whom the newspaperman sometimes came back to the surface, was not sorry to launch, on the off chance, a gripping article telling the adventures of the colonists of Lincoln Island! What a triumph for the official reporter of the New York Herald, and for the issue that would carry the story, if it ever reached the desk of his editor, the Honorable John Bennett!

So Gédéon Spilett wrote a short account, which was put into a bag of strong gummed canvas, with an urgent request that whoever found it should forward it to the offices of the New York Herald. The little bag was tied around the albatross's neck, not its foot, because these birds have the habit of resting on the surface of the sea. Then this swift courier of the air was given its freedom, and it was not without some emotion that the colonists watched it vanish far off into the mists of the west.

"Where's he off to?" asked Pencroff.

"Toward New Zealand," answered Harbert.

"Bon voyage!" cried the sailor, who, for his part, did not expect much to come of this way of corresponding.

With the winter, the indoor work at Granite House had started up again: mending clothes, making all sorts of things, and among others the sails for the boat, which were cut from the balloon's inexhaustible envelope...

During the month of July the cold was severe, but they spared neither wood nor coal. Cyrus Smith had put a second fireplace into the great hall, and that was where they spent the long evenings. They talked while they worked, and read when their hands were idle, and the time passed profitably for everyone.

It was a real delight for the colonists when, in that room well lit with candles and well heated with coal, after a hearty dinner, with the elderberry coffee steaming in their cups and their pipes trailing plumes of fragrant smoke, they heard the storm roaring outside! Their comfort would have been complete, if comfort could ever be complete for people who are far from their fellow men and have no way of reaching them! They talked endlessly about their country, about the friends they had left behind, about the greatness of the American republic, whose influence could only grow. And Cyrus Smith, who had been deeply involved in the affairs of the Union, held his listeners spellbound with his stories, his insights and his predictions.

One day it happened that Gédéon Spilett was led to say to him:

"But really, my dear Cyrus, all this industrial and commercial activity that you predict will keep on growing — isn't it in danger of being stopped dead sooner or later?"

"Stopped! By what?"

"Why, by running out of coal, which has every right to be called the most precious of minerals!"

"Yes, the most precious, indeed," answered the engineer, "and it seems as though nature wanted to prove it, by making the diamond, which is nothing but pure carbon, crystallized."

"You don't mean, Mr. Cyrus," Pencroff put in, "that they'll burn diamonds instead of coal in the fireboxes of the boilers?"

"No, my friend," answered Cyrus Smith.

"All the same, I insist," Gédéon Spilett went on. "You don't deny that one day the coal will be entirely used up?"

"Oh, the coal deposits are still enormous, and the hundred thousand workers who dig ten million metric tons out of them every year are not close to exhausting them!"

"With the amount of coal being burned growing all the time," answered Gédéon Spilett, "we can foresee that those hundred thousand workers will soon be two hundred thousand, and that the output will double?"

"No doubt. But after the deposits in Europe, which new machines will soon let us mine more deeply, the coal mines of America and Australia will go on supplying industry for a long time yet."

"How long?" asked the reporter.

"Two hundred and fifty or three hundred years at least."

"That's reassuring for us," answered Pencroff, "but worrying for our great-grand-cousins!"

"They'll find something else," said Harbert.

"Let's hope so," answered Gédéon Spilett, "because without coal, no more machines, and without machines, no more railroads, no more steamships, no more factories, nothing that the progress of modern life demands!"

"But what will they find?" asked Pencroff. "Can you picture it, Mr. Cyrus?"

"More or less, my friend."

"And what will they burn instead of coal?"

"Water," answered Cyrus Smith.

"Water!" cried Pencroff. "Water to fire steamships and locomotives! Water to heat water!"

"Yes, but water broken down into the elements it is made of," answered Cyrus Smith, "and broken down, no doubt, by electricity, which by then will have become a powerful force that people know how to handle — for all the great discoveries, by some law nobody can explain, seem to arrive together and complete one another at the same moment. Yes, my friends, I believe that one day water will be used as fuel, and that hydrogen and oxygen, the two gases it is made of, used separately or together, will provide an inexhaustible source of heat and light, of an intensity that coal could never reach. One day the bunkers of steamships and the tenders of locomotives will be loaded, not with coal, but with these two gases, compressed, and they will burn in the fireboxes with enormous heating power. So there is nothing to fear. As long as this earth is inhabited, it will meet the needs of the people who live on it, and they will never lack light or heat, any more than they will lack the products of the vegetable, mineral or animal kingdoms. So I believe that when the coal deposits are exhausted, we will heat our engines and ourselves with water. Water will be the coal of the future."

"I'd like to see that," said the sailor.

"You were born too soon, Pencroff," answered Nab, and those were the only words he contributed to the discussion.

Still, it was not Nab's remark that ended the conversation, but Top's barking, which broke out again with that strange tone that had already troubled the engineer. At the same time Top began once more to circle around the opening of the well at the far end of the inside passage.

"What's Top barking like that about this time?" asked Pencroff.

"And why is Jup grunting that way?" added Harbert.

It was true: the orangutan had joined the dog and was showing unmistakable signs of agitation, and, a curious detail, the two animals seemed more uneasy than angry.

"It's obvious," said Gédéon Spilett, "that this well connects directly with the sea, and that some sea animal comes now and then to breathe at the bottom of it."

"That's obvious," answered the sailor, "and there's no other explanation to give... Come on, quiet, Top," Pencroff added, turning to the dog, "and you, Jup, off to your room!"

The ape and the dog fell silent. Jup went back to bed, but Top stayed in the great hall, and he went on growling under his breath for the rest of the evening.

Nobody spoke of the incident again, but it clouded the engineer's brow all the same.

For the rest of July there were spells of rain and spells of cold. The temperature did not drop as low as it had the winter before; at its coldest it went no lower than eight degrees Fahrenheit. But if this winter was less cold, it was at least more troubled by storms and gales. Once again the sea made violent attacks that more than once put the Chimneys in danger. You might have thought that a tidal wave, set off by some upheaval under the sea, was lifting these monstrous breakers and hurling them against the wall of Granite House.

When the colonists leaned out of their windows and watched those enormous masses of water breaking before their eyes, they could only admire the magnificent spectacle of the ocean's powerless fury. The waves bounced back in dazzling foam, the whole beach disappeared under the raging flood, and the cliff seemed to rise out of the sea itself, while the spray flew more than a hundred feet into the air.

During these storms it was hard to venture onto the island's roads, even dangerous, because trees came down often. Still, the colonists never let a week go by without visiting the corral. Luckily the enclosure, sheltered by the southeast shoulder of Mount Franklin, did not suffer much from the violence of the hurricane, which spared its trees, its sheds and its palisade. But the poultry yard, set up on Prospect Plateau and so directly exposed to the blasts of the east wind, took a good deal of damage. The dovecote lost its roof twice, and the fence was blown down as well. All of it would have to be rebuilt more solidly, because it was now clear that Lincoln Island lay in the worst waters of the Pacific. It truly seemed to be the center point of vast cyclones, which lashed it the way a whip lashes a spinning top. Only here it was the top that stood still, and the whip that went round.

During the first week of August the gusts died down little by little, and the air recovered a calm it had seemed to have lost forever. With the calm the temperature fell, the cold grew very sharp again, and the thermometer dropped to eight degrees below zero Fahrenheit.

On August 3 an excursion that had been planned for several days was made to the southeast of the island, toward Shelduck Marsh. The hunters were tempted by all the water birds that had set up their winter quarters there. Wild ducks, snipe, pintails, teal and grebes were plentiful, and it was decided that a day would be given to an expedition against these birds.

Not only Gédéon Spilett and Harbert but also Pencroff and Nab took part in the expedition. Cyrus Smith alone, pleading some work or other, did not join them and stayed at Granite House.

So the hunters took the road to Balloon Harbor to get to the marsh, after promising to be back by evening. Top and Jup went with them. As soon as they had crossed the bridge over the Mercy, the engineer raised it and went back, meaning to carry out a plan for which he wanted to be alone.

That plan was to make a thorough exploration of the inside well, whose opening was level with the passage of Granite House, and which connected with the sea, since it had once served as the channel for the waters of the lake.

Why did Top circle that opening so often? Why did he let out such strange barks when some kind of uneasiness drew him back to the well? Why did Jup join Top in a sort of shared anxiety? Did the well have other branches besides its straight connection down to the sea? Did it spread out toward other parts of the island? That was what Cyrus Smith wanted to know — and, to begin with, to be the only one to know. So he had made up his mind to try exploring the well while his companions were away, and now the chance had come.

It was easy to get down to the bottom of the well by using the rope ladder, which had not been in use since the elevator was put in and which was long enough. That is what the engineer did. He dragged the ladder to the hole, which was about six feet across, and after tying its upper end securely, he let it unroll down the shaft. Then, having lit a lantern, taken a revolver and stuck a cutlass in his belt, he started down the first rungs.

The wall was solid everywhere. But a few knobs of rock jutted out here and there, and by means of these projections it would really have been possible for some agile creature to climb up to the mouth of the well.

The engineer took note of that. But when he passed his lantern carefully over the projections, he found no mark, no chipped edge, nothing to suggest that they had ever been used for a climb, long ago or lately.

Cyrus Smith went deeper, lighting up every part of the wall.

He saw nothing suspicious.

When the engineer reached the last rungs, he felt the surface of the water, which was perfectly calm at that moment. Neither at the water's level nor anywhere else in the well was there any side passage that might branch off into the interior of the rock mass. The wall, when Cyrus Smith struck it with the handle of his cutlass, rang solid. It was compact granite, through which no living creature could force its way. To reach the bottom of the well and then climb to its opening, anything would have to come through that channel, always underwater, which linked it to the sea through the rocky ground beneath the beach — and that was possible only for sea animals. As for where the channel came out, at what point on the shore and how deep beneath the waves, there was no way to tell.

So Cyrus Smith, having finished his exploration, climbed back up, pulled up the ladder, covered the mouth of the well and went back to the great hall of Granite House, deep in thought, saying to himself:

"I saw nothing, and yet there is something there!"
