Chapter 4: The Chimneys

_The lithodomes — The river at its mouth — The "Chimneys" — The search goes on — The forest of evergreens — The supply of firewood — Waiting for the ebb — From the top of the cliff — The timber raft — Back to the shore_

First of all, the reporter told the sailor to wait for him right where he was, and that he would come back and join him there. Then, without losing a moment, he set off up the shore in the direction Nab had taken a few hours before. He quickly disappeared around a bend in the coast, so impatient was he for news of the engineer.

Harbert had wanted to go with him.

"Stay here, my boy," the sailor had told him. "We've got a camp to set up, and we need to see whether we can find something more solid to sink our teeth into than shellfish. Our friends will need to get their strength back when they return. Everybody has his job."

"I'm ready, Pencroff," Harbert answered.

"Good!" the sailor went on. "That'll do. Let's be methodical about this. We're tired, we're cold, we're hungry. So what we need is shelter, fire, and food. The forest has wood, the nests have eggs. All that's left is to find the house."

"All right," said Harbert. "I'll look for a cave in these rocks. I'm bound to find some hole we can squeeze ourselves into!"

"That's the idea," said Pencroff. "Let's go, my boy."

So off the two of them went, walking along the foot of the enormous wall, on the beach that the falling tide had left wide and bare. But instead of heading north, they went south. A few hundred paces below the spot where they had landed, Pencroff had noticed a narrow break in the coast, which, he thought, must be the outlet of a river or a stream. Now, on the one hand, it was important to settle near a supply of drinking water. And on the other, it was not impossible that the current had carried Cyrus Smith in that direction.

The high wall, as we have said, rose to a height of three hundred feet. But it was solid rock from end to end, and even at its base, which the sea barely licked, it did not offer the slightest crack that could serve as a temporary home. It was a sheer wall of very hard granite that the waves had never worn away. Around its top fluttered a whole world of seabirds, especially various species of the web-footed order, with long, narrow, pointed beaks — very noisy birds, and not much frightened by the presence of man, who was no doubt disturbing their solitude for the first time. Among these web-footed birds Pencroff recognized several skuas, a kind of gull sometimes called jaegers, and also some small, greedy sea gulls that nested in the crevices of the granite. One gunshot fired into that swarm of birds would have brought down a great many of them. But to fire a gunshot you need a gun, and neither Pencroff nor Harbert had one. Besides, these gulls and skuas are hardly fit to eat, and even their eggs taste dreadful.

Meanwhile Harbert, who had moved off a little to the left, soon pointed out some rocks carpeted with seaweed, which the high tide would cover again a few hours later. On these rocks, in among the slippery wrack, swarmed two-shelled mollusks of a kind that starving people could hardly turn up their noses at. So Harbert called Pencroff, who came running.

"Hey! Mussels!" cried the sailor. "That'll make up for the eggs we haven't got!"

"They're not mussels," answered young Harbert, who was carefully examining the shellfish clinging to the rocks. "They're lithodomes."

"Can you eat them?" asked Pencroff.

"Absolutely."

"Then let's eat lithodomes."

The sailor could rely on Harbert. The boy was very good at natural history and had always had a real passion for that science. His father had encouraged him in it, sending him to the lectures of the best teachers in Boston, who were fond of this intelligent, hardworking boy. His naturalist's instincts would prove useful more than once in the days to come, and on this first occasion he was not mistaken.

These lithodomes were oblong shellfish that hung in clusters and clung tightly to the rocks. They belonged to that species of boring mollusk that drills holes into the very hardest stone, and their shells were rounded at both ends — a shape you do not see in the ordinary mussel.

Pencroff and Harbert ate a good many of these lithodomes, which were half open in the sun just then. They ate them like oysters and found that they had a strong, peppery taste, which left them with no regret at all that they had neither pepper nor seasoning of any kind.

So their hunger was satisfied for the moment, but not their thirst, which only grew after eating these naturally spicy shellfish. Now they had to find fresh water, and it seemed unlikely that there would be none in a country so wildly broken up. Pencroff and Harbert took the precaution of laying in a large supply of lithodomes, filling their pockets and their handkerchiefs with them, and then made their way back to the foot of the high ground.

Two hundred paces farther on they reached the break in the cliff through which, just as Pencroff had guessed, a little river was flowing brimful. At this spot the wall looked as if it had been split apart by some violent volcanic upheaval. At its foot a small cove cut into the shore, and the inner end of it made a fairly sharp angle. The stream here was a hundred feet wide, and its banks on either side were barely twenty feet across. The river ran almost straight in between the two walls of granite, which grew lower as they went upstream from the mouth. Then it turned sharply and disappeared under a thicket half a mile away.

"Water here! Wood over there!" said Pencroff. "Well, Harbert, now all we need is the house!"

The river water was clear. The sailor made sure that at this stage of the tide — that is, at low water, when the rising sea did not push into it — it was fresh. With that important point settled, Harbert hunted for some hollow that could serve as a shelter, but it was no use. Everywhere the wall was smooth, flat, and sheer.

At the very mouth of the stream, however, above the high-water line, fallen rock had formed — not a cave, but a heap of enormous boulders, of the kind you often come across in granite country, and which go by the name of "Chimneys."

Pencroff and Harbert made their way fairly deep in among the rocks, along sandy passages that were not short of light, since it came in through the gaps the blocks of granite left between them — some of those blocks staying up only by a miracle of balance. But with the light came the wind too — a real hallway draft — and with the wind, the sharp cold from outside. Still, the sailor thought that by closing off certain parts of these passages, and stopping up some of the openings with a mixture of stones and sand, they could make the Chimneys livable. Their ground plan looked like the printer's sign &, the one used to abbreviate et cetera. Now, by walling off the upper loop of the sign, where the south and west winds came rushing in, they could no doubt make use of its lower part.

"This is just the thing," said Pencroff. "And if we ever see Mr. Smith again, he'll know how to make something of this maze."

"We will see him again, Pencroff!" cried Harbert. "And when he comes back he has to find a home here that's more or less bearable. It will be, if we can set up a fireplace in the left-hand passage and keep an opening there for the smoke."

"We can do that, my boy," the sailor answered, "and these Chimneys" — that was the name Pencroff kept for this temporary home — "will do just fine for us. But first, let's go and lay in a supply of fuel. I've a notion the wood will come in handy for blocking up these holes the devil's blowing his trumpet through!"

Harbert and Pencroff left the Chimneys, rounded the corner, and started up the left bank of the river. The current was fairly swift and was carrying along some dead wood. The rising tide — and it could already be felt at this point — must push the river back hard for quite a long way upstream. So the sailor thought they might be able to use this flow and ebb to carry heavy loads.

After walking for a quarter of an hour, the sailor and the boy reached the sharp bend the river made as it swung away to the left. From there on, its course ran through a forest of magnificent trees. These trees had kept their green, late in the season though it was, because they belonged to the conifer family, which is found in every region of the globe, from the northern climates to the tropics. The young naturalist recognized deodars in particular, a kind of cedar very common in the Himalayas, which gave off a pleasant fragrance. Between these fine trees grew clumps of pines, spreading their dense umbrellas wide. In the tall grass, Pencroff felt dry branches snap under his feet, crackling like fireworks.

"Well, my boy," he said to Harbert, "I may not know the names of these trees, but I do at least know how to file them under 'firewood,' and right now that's the only kind that's any use to us!"

"Let's gather our supply!" answered Harbert, and he set to work at once.

The harvest was easy. They did not even need to strip branches off the trees, since huge quantities of dead wood lay on the ground at their feet. But if there was no shortage of fuel, the means of carrying it left a good deal to be desired. Since the wood was very dry, it would burn quickly. So they would need to bring a large amount of it back to the Chimneys, and what two men could carry would not be enough. Harbert pointed this out.

"Ah, my boy," answered the sailor, "there's got to be some way of moving this wood. There's always a way to do anything! If we had a cart or a boat, it would be too easy."

"But we've got the river!" said Harbert.

"Right," said Pencroff. "The river will be a road that travels all by itself, and timber rafts weren't invented for nothing."

"Only," Harbert pointed out, "our road is traveling the wrong way just now, since the tide's coming in!"

"Then we'll just have to wait for it to go out," answered the sailor, "and it'll be the sea that delivers our fuel to the Chimneys. Let's get our raft ready anyway."

The sailor, with Harbert behind him, headed for the corner where the edge of the forest met the river. Each of them carried as heavy a load of wood as his strength allowed, tied up in bundles. On the bank, too, there was a great deal of dead branches, lying in grass where very likely no human foot had ever stepped. Pencroff set to work on his raft at once.

In a sort of eddy, made by a point of the bank that broke the current, the sailor and the boy laid down some fairly thick pieces of wood, which they had tied together with dry creepers. This made a kind of raft, and on it they piled the whole harvest, layer after layer — at least twenty men's worth of carrying. In an hour the work was done, and the raft, moored to the bank, had to wait for the tide to turn.

That left a few hours to fill, and Pencroff and Harbert agreed to climb up to the plateau above, to get a look at the country over a wider area.

As it happened, two hundred paces back from the bend in the river, the wall ended in a slide of fallen rocks that sloped gently down to the edge of the forest. It was like a natural staircase. So Harbert and the sailor began to climb. Thanks to their strong legs they reached the top in no time, and took up a position at the corner where the crest overlooked the mouth of the river.

When they got there, their first look was for the ocean they had just crossed under such terrible conditions! They gazed with deep emotion over all that northern stretch of the coast where the disaster had happened. That was where Cyrus Smith had vanished. They searched with their eyes to see whether some piece of wreckage from their balloon, something a man might have clung to, was still floating. Nothing! The sea was nothing but a vast desert of water. As for the coast, it was deserted too. Neither the reporter nor Nab was anywhere to be seen. But it was possible that at that moment they were both so far away that no one could have made them out.

"Something tells me," cried Harbert, "that a man with as much energy as Mr. Cyrus couldn't just let himself drown like anybody else. He must have reached the shore somewhere. Don't you think so, Pencroff?"

The sailor shook his head sadly. He had little hope left of ever seeing Cyrus Smith again. But since he wanted to leave Harbert some hope, he said:

"Of course, of course. Our engineer's the kind of man who gets himself out of a fix where anybody else would go under!..."

All the same, he was studying the coast with the closest attention. Below him spread the sandy beach, bounded on the right of the river mouth by lines of breakers. Those rocks, still out of the water, looked like herds of seals lying in the surf. Beyond the band of reefs the sea glittered in the sunlight. To the south a sharp point closed off the horizon, and it was impossible to tell whether the land went on in that direction or whether it turned southeast and southwest, which would have made this coast a kind of very long peninsula. At the northern end of the bay the outline of the shore went on for a great distance, in a more rounded curve. There the shore was low and flat, with no cliff, and with broad sandbanks that the ebb tide left uncovered.

Then Pencroff and Harbert turned around to face west. Their eyes were caught at once by the snow-capped mountain that rose six or seven miles away. From its lowest slopes to within two miles of the coast stretched vast wooded masses, set off by great patches of green where the trees kept their leaves all year. Then, from the edge of this forest to the coast itself, there spread a broad green plateau dotted with clumps of trees scattered about at random. To the left they could catch a glint, now and then, of the little river's waters through a few clearings, and its fairly winding course seemed to lead back toward the buttresses of the mountain, among which it must have its source. At the point where the sailor had left his timber raft, it began to flow between the two high walls of granite. But while on its left bank the walls stayed clean and steep, on the right bank, on the contrary, they sank little by little — the great masses turning into lone boulders, the boulders into stones, the stones into pebbles, all the way to the tip of the point.

"Are we on an island?" the sailor murmured.

"If we are, it seems pretty big!" the boy answered.

"An island, however big it is, is still only an island!" said Pencroff.

But that important question could not be settled yet. Its answer would have to wait for another time. As for the land itself, island or continent, it seemed fertile, pleasant to look at, and varied in what it produced.

"That's a piece of luck," Pencroff remarked, "and in all our misfortune we ought to thank Providence for it."

"Then God be praised!" answered Harbert, whose devout heart was full of gratitude to the Maker of all things.

For a long time Pencroff and Harbert studied this country where their fate had flung them, but after such a quick look it was hard to imagine what the future held in store for them.

Then they started back, following the southern crest of the granite plateau, which was edged with a long, scalloped line of fantastic rocks that took on the strangest shapes. Several hundred birds lived there, nesting in holes in the stone. Harbert, jumping from rock to rock, sent a whole flock of them flying.

"Ah!" he cried. "Those aren't gulls of any kind!"

"Then what birds are they?" asked Pencroff. "Upon my word, they look like pigeons!"

"That's right, but they're wild pigeons — rock pigeons," answered Harbert. "I can tell them by the double black band on the wing, the white rump, and the ash-blue feathers. Now, if the rock pigeon is good to eat, its eggs must be excellent, and if these birds have left any in their nests at all!..."

"We won't give them time to hatch, except as an omelet!" Pencroff answered cheerfully.

"But what are you going to make your omelet in?" asked Harbert. "Your hat?"

"Well!" answered the sailor. "I'm not enough of a wizard for that. We'll have to settle for boiled eggs, my boy, and I'll take care of the hardest ones myself!"

Pencroff and the boy searched the crevices of the granite carefully, and sure enough, they found eggs in some of the hollows! A few dozen were gathered and wrapped up in the sailor's handkerchief. Then, since it was nearly time for high tide, Harbert and Pencroff began to make their way back down to the river.

When they reached the bend in the river it was one o'clock in the afternoon. The current was already turning. So they had to take advantage of the ebb to bring the timber raft down to the mouth. Pencroff had no intention of letting the raft drift off on the current with no one steering it, but he did not mean to climb aboard and steer it himself either. A sailor, though, is never at a loss when it comes to cables and ropes, and Pencroff quickly braided a rope several fathoms long out of dry creepers. This vegetable cable was tied to the back end of the raft, and the sailor held it in his hand while Harbert, pushing the raft off with a long pole, kept it out in the current.

The method worked perfectly. The enormous load of wood, which the sailor held back as he walked along the bank, followed the flow of the water. The bank was very steep, so there was no fear that the raft would run aground, and before two o'clock it arrived at the mouth, a few steps from the Chimneys.
