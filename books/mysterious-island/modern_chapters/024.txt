Chapter 3: The Fever Trees

_The departure — The rising tide — Elms and nettle trees — Various plants — The jacamar — What the forest looked like — The giant eucalyptus trees — Why they are called "fever trees" — Troops of monkeys — The waterfall — Camp for the night_

The next day, October 30, everything was ready for the planned exploration, which recent events had made so urgent. Things had in fact turned out in such a way that the colonists of Lincoln Island could imagine they were no longer the ones in need of rescue, but the ones who might be able to bring it.

So it was agreed that they would go up the Mercy as far as the river's current allowed a boat to travel. That way a large part of the journey would be made without effort, and the explorers could carry their provisions and their weapons to a point well into the west of the island.

They had had to think, after all, not only about the things they were taking but also about the things that luck might allow them to bring back to Granite House. If there had been a shipwreck on the coast, as everything suggested, there would be no shortage of wreckage, and it would be well worth salvaging. With that in mind, the cart would no doubt have suited better than the fragile canoe. But the cart was heavy and clumsy and had to be pulled by hand, which made it much less practical. That led Pencroff to express his regret that the chest had not contained, along with "his half-pound of tobacco," a pair of those sturdy New Jersey horses, which would have been extremely useful to the colony!

The provisions, already loaded by Nab, consisted of preserved meat and a few gallons of beer and fermented liquor — enough to keep them going for three days, the longest time Cyrus Smith meant to give the exploration. Besides, they counted on restocking along the way if they needed to, and Nab took good care not to forget the little portable stove.

For tools, the colonists took the two woodsman's axes, which would serve to cut a path through the thick forest. For instruments, they took the spyglass and the pocket compass.

For weapons, they chose the two flintlock guns. On this island these were more useful than the percussion-cap guns would have been, because a flintlock needs only flints, which are easy to replace, while the others need percussion caps, and frequent use would soon have exhausted the supply. All the same, they also took one of the carbines and a few cartridges. As for the gunpowder, of which the barrels held about fifty pounds, they had to bring a certain amount, but the engineer was counting on making an explosive substance that would let them spare it. To the firearms they added the five cutlasses, well sheathed in leather. Equipped like that, the colonists could venture into the vast forest with a fair chance of getting themselves out of any trouble.

There is no need to add that Pencroff, Harbert, and Nab, armed this way, had nothing left to wish for — even though Cyrus Smith had made them promise not to fire a single shot unless they had to.

At six o'clock in the morning the canoe was pushed into the water. Everyone climbed aboard, Top included, and they headed for the mouth of the Mercy.

The tide had been coming in for only half an hour. That meant there were still a few hours of flood tide, and they had to make the most of them, because later the ebb would make it hard to go upriver. The tide was already running strongly, since the moon would be full in three days, and the canoe, which only had to be kept in the current, moved quickly between the two high banks without any need for the oars to speed it along.

In a few minutes the explorers reached the bend in the Mercy — the very angle where, seven months earlier, Pencroff had put together his first timber raft.

After that rather sharp angle the river curved round and turned southwest, and its course ran on in the shade of tall evergreen conifers.

The banks of the Mercy were magnificent. Cyrus Smith and his companions could only admire, without reservation, those beautiful effects that nature achieves so easily with water and trees. As they went on, the kinds of trees in the forest changed. On the right bank rose tier upon tier of splendid members of the elm family — true elms, those precious trees that builders prize so highly, because their wood keeps for a long time under water. Then came many clusters from the same family, among them nettle trees, whose kernel yields a very useful oil. Farther on, Harbert noticed some plants of the lardizabala family, whose supple branches, soaked in water, make excellent rope, and two or three trunks of the ebony family, a beautiful black streaked with wandering veins.

Every now and then, at places where it was easy to land, the canoe stopped. Then Gédéon Spilett, Harbert, and Pencroff, guns in hand and with Top running ahead, beat the bank. Quite apart from the game, they might come across some useful plant that was not to be sneezed at — and the young naturalist got exactly what he had hoped for. He discovered a kind of wild spinach from the goosefoot family, and many specimens of the mustard family belonging to the cabbage genus, which could certainly be "civilized" by transplanting them: there was watercress, horseradish, and turnips, and finally some little branching stems, slightly hairy, about three feet high, that bore seeds of an almost brown color.

"Do you know what that plant is?" Harbert asked the sailor.

"Tobacco!" cried Pencroff, who had obviously never seen his favorite plant anywhere but in the bowl of his pipe.

"No, Pencroff!" answered Harbert. "That's not tobacco, it's mustard."

"Mustard it is, then!" answered the sailor. "But if by any chance a tobacco plant should turn up, my boy, kindly don't turn your nose up at it."

"We'll find some one day!" said Gédéon Spilett.

"Really?" cried Pencroff. "Well, on that day I honestly don't know what our island will still be missing!"

These various plants were carefully dug up by the roots and carried to the canoe. Cyrus Smith had not left it; he was still absorbed in his own thoughts.

The reporter, Harbert, and Pencroff went ashore several times in this way, sometimes on the right bank of the Mercy, sometimes on the left. The left bank was less steep, the right bank more heavily wooded. By consulting his pocket compass, the engineer was able to establish that from the first bend the river ran roughly southwest to northeast, in a nearly straight line, for a distance of about three miles. But it seemed likely that its course changed farther on, and that the Mercy climbed toward the northwest, toward the foothills of Mount Franklin, which must feed it with their waters.

During one of these excursions, Gédéon Spilett managed to catch two pairs of live birds of the chicken family. They had long, slender beaks, long necks, and short wings, and no tail to speak of. Harbert, rightly, named them "tinamous," and it was decided that they would be the first residents of the future poultry yard.

So far, though, the guns had not spoken. The first shot to ring out in that forest of the Far West was set off by the appearance of a handsome bird that looked, in its build, very much like a kingfisher.

"I know that one!" cried Pencroff — and it is fair to say that his gun went off almost in spite of him.

"What do you know?" asked the reporter.

"The bird that got away from us on our first trip, the one we named this part of the forest after."

"A jacamar!" cried Harbert.

It was indeed a jacamar, a beautiful bird whose rather coarse plumage has a metallic sheen. A few pellets of shot had brought it down, and Top carried it back to the canoe, along with a dozen "touraco-lories," climbing birds about the size of a pigeon, painted all over in green, with part of their wings crimson and an upright crest edged with a white border. The honor of that fine shot went to the boy, and he was rather proud of it. The lories made better eating than the jacamar, whose flesh is a little tough, but it would have been hard to persuade Pencroff that he had not killed the king of edible birds.

It was ten o'clock in the morning when the canoe reached a second bend of the Mercy, about five miles from its mouth. They stopped there for lunch, and the halt, in the shelter of tall, beautiful trees, lasted half an hour.

The river was still sixty to seventy feet wide, and its bed five to six feet deep. The engineer had noticed that many tributaries swelled its flow, but they were only small streams, too shallow for any boat. As for the forest — whether you called it Jacamar Wood or the Far West forests — it stretched as far as the eye could see. Nowhere, neither under the tall timber nor among the trees along the banks of the Mercy, was there any sign that human beings had been there. The explorers could not find a single suspicious trace. It was obvious that no woodsman's ax had ever notched these trees, that no pioneer's knife had ever cut these creepers stretched from trunk to trunk among the dense undergrowth and the long grass. If some castaways had landed on the island, they had not yet left the shore, and it was not under this thick cover that they should look for the survivors of the shipwreck they suspected.

So the engineer showed a certain haste to reach the west coast of Lincoln Island, which by his reckoning was at least five miles away. They set off again by water. Although, in its present direction, the Mercy seemed to run not toward the coast but rather toward Mount Franklin, it was decided that they would keep using the canoe as long as there was enough water under its keel to float it. That would spare them a great deal of effort and save time as well, because otherwise they would have had to hack a path with the axes through the thick brush.

But soon the flood tide gave out altogether — either because the tide was going down, as it was in fact due to at that hour, or because it was no longer felt this far from the mouth of the Mercy. So they had to get out the oars. Nab and Harbert took their places on the thwart, Pencroff took the steering oar at the stern, and they went on up the river.

The forest now seemed to be thinning out on the Far West side. The trees there stood farther apart and were often quite alone. But precisely because they were spaced out, they had more of the free, clean air that moved around them, and they were magnificent.

What splendid examples of the plant life of that latitude! Their presence alone would have been enough for a botanist to name, without a moment's hesitation, the parallel that Lincoln Island lay on!

"Eucalyptus!" Harbert had cried out.

And that is what they were — those superb trees, the last giants of the zone just beyond the tropics, the cousins of the eucalyptus of Australia and New Zealand, both of which lie in the same latitude as Lincoln Island. Some of them rose to a height of two hundred feet. Their trunks measured twenty feet around at the base, and their bark, furrowed with networks of fragrant resin, was as much as five inches thick. Nothing could be more marvelous — or stranger — than these enormous members of the myrtle family, whose leaves turned their edges to the light and let the sun's rays reach all the way to the ground!

At the foot of these eucalyptus trees, fresh grass carpeted the ground, and out of the tufts burst flocks of little birds that shone in the shafts of light like winged jewels.

"Now those are trees!" cried Nab. "But are they good for anything?"

"Pooh!" answered Pencroff. "Giant plants must be like giant people. They're not much good for anything except showing off at fairs!"

"I think you're mistaken, Pencroff," Gédéon Spilett answered. "Eucalyptus wood is starting to be used to great advantage in fine furniture-making."

"And I'll add," said the boy, "that eucalyptus trees belong to a family with plenty of useful members. There's the guava tree, which gives guavas; the clove tree, which produces cloves; the pomegranate, which bears pomegranates; the Eugenia cauliflora, whose fruit makes a passable wine; the ugni myrtle, which contains an excellent alcoholic liquor; the caryophyllus myrtle, whose bark makes a highly valued cinnamon; the Eugenia pimenta, which is where Jamaica pepper comes from; the common myrtle, whose berries can stand in for pepper; the Eucalyptus robusta, which produces an excellent kind of manna; the Eucalyptus gunei, whose sap turns into beer when it ferments; and finally all those trees known as 'trees of life' or 'ironwood,' which belong to this same myrtle family — and it has forty-six genera and thirteen hundred species!"

They let the boy go on, and he rattled off his little botany lesson with great enthusiasm. Cyrus Smith listened with a smile, and Pencroff with a pride beyond words.

"Fine, Harbert," Pencroff answered, "but I'd be willing to swear that none of those useful specimens you just listed are giants like these!"

"That's true, Pencroff."

"So that backs up what I said," the sailor replied. "Giants are good for nothing!"

"That's where you're wrong, Pencroff," the engineer put in, "and it so happens that these very giant eucalyptus trees sheltering us are good for something."

"And what's that?"

"For making the country they grow in healthier. Do you know what they call them in Australia and New Zealand?"

"No, Mr. Cyrus."

"They call them 'fever trees.'"

"Because they give you fever?"

"No — because they prevent it!"

"Good. I'll make a note of that," said the reporter.

"Note it down, then, my dear Spilett, because it seems to be proved that the presence of eucalyptus trees is enough to neutralize the poisonous vapors of marshes. This natural safeguard has been tried in certain regions of southern Europe and North Africa where the soil was thoroughly unhealthy, and the health of the people who live there has gradually improved. No more recurring fevers in the areas covered by forests of these trees. The fact is now beyond doubt, and it's a lucky thing for us, the colonists of Lincoln Island."

"Ah! What an island! What a blessed island!" cried Pencroff. "I'm telling you, it doesn't lack a thing... Except..."

"That will come, Pencroff, it will turn up," answered the engineer. "But let's get back on the river and push on as far as it will carry our canoe!"

So the exploration went on for at least two more miles through country covered with eucalyptus, which towered over all the other woods in that part of the island. The area they covered stretched beyond the reach of sight on both sides of the Mercy, whose rather winding bed now ran deep between high green banks. The channel was often choked with tall weeds and even with sharp rocks, which made the going quite hard. The oars were hampered, and Pencroff had to push the boat along with a pole. They could also feel the bottom rising little by little, and the moment was not far off when the canoe would have to stop for lack of water. The sun was already sinking toward the horizon and throwing the trees' enormous shadows across the ground. Cyrus Smith, seeing that he could not reach the west coast of the island that day, decided to camp at the very spot where lack of water would force them to stop. He reckoned he must still be five or six miles from the coast, and that distance was too great to attempt crossing at night through these unknown woods.

So they kept the boat moving through the forest without a rest. Little by little the forest grew thicker again, and it seemed more inhabited as well — for, unless the sailor's eyes deceived him, he thought he saw troops of monkeys running through the underbrush. Sometimes two or three of these animals even stopped a short distance from the canoe and stared at the colonists without showing the slightest fear, as if, seeing human beings for the first time, they had not yet learned to dread them. It would have been easy to shoot these four-handed creatures, but Cyrus Smith would not allow a pointless slaughter, though the hot-headed Pencroff was a little tempted by it. And it was the prudent choice besides, because these monkeys were strong and extremely agile and could be dangerous. It was better not to provoke them with an attack that served no purpose at all.

It is true that the sailor looked at monkeys from a purely culinary point of view, and in fact these animals, which eat nothing but plants, make excellent game. But since there was plenty of food, there was no reason to waste ammunition for nothing.

Toward four o'clock, going up the Mercy became very difficult, because its course was clogged with water plants and rocks. The banks rose higher and higher, and the riverbed was already cutting its way between the first foothills of Mount Franklin. So its sources could not be far off, since they were fed by all the waters of the mountain's southern slopes.

"Within a quarter of an hour we'll have to stop, Mr. Cyrus," said the sailor.

"Well, then, we'll stop, Pencroff, and set up a camp for the night."

"How far can we be from Granite House?" asked Harbert.

"About seven miles," the engineer answered, "though that takes into account the windings of the river, which have carried us to the northwest."

"Are we going on?" asked the reporter.

"Yes, for as long as we can," answered Cyrus Smith. "Tomorrow, at daybreak, we'll leave the canoe. In two hours, I hope, we'll cover the distance that separates us from the coast, and we'll have almost the whole day to explore the shore."

"Onward!" answered Pencroff.

But soon the canoe scraped the pebbly bottom of the river, which was by then no more than twenty feet wide. A thick arch of greenery curved over its bed and wrapped it in half-darkness. They could also hear the fairly loud sound of falling water, which told them that a natural dam lay a few hundred paces upstream.

And sure enough, at a last bend in the river, a waterfall appeared through the trees. The canoe struck the bottom, and a few moments later it was tied to a trunk near the right bank.

It was about five o'clock. The last rays of the sun were slipping under the thick branches and striking the little waterfall at a slant, so that its damp spray glittered with all the colors of the rainbow. Beyond it, the bed of the Mercy disappeared into the thickets, where it was fed by some hidden spring. The various streams that flowed into it along the way made it a real river farther down, but here it was no more than a clear, shallow brook.

They camped on that very spot, which was charming. The colonists went ashore, and a fire was lit under a clump of broad nettle trees, in whose branches Cyrus Smith and his companions could, if they had to, have found a refuge for the night.

Supper was soon devoured, because everyone was hungry, and after that nobody thought of anything but sleep. But as night fell, a few roars of a suspicious kind were heard, so the fire was built up for the night, to protect the sleepers with its crackling flames. Nab and Pencroff even kept watch in turn and did not spare the fuel. Perhaps they were not mistaken when they thought they saw the shadows of animals prowling around the camp, either in the underbrush or among the branches. But the night passed without incident, and the next day, October 31, at five o'clock in the morning, everyone was up and ready to set off.
