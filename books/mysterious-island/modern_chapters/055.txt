Chapter 14: Three Years Have Passed

_Three years have passed — The question of the new ship — What is decided — The colony prospers — The shipyard — The cold of the southern hemisphere — Pencroff resigns himself — Washing the linen — Mount Franklin_

Three years had gone by since the prisoners of Richmond made their escape, and how often, in those three years, they had talked of their homeland, which was never far from their thoughts!

They had no doubt that the Civil War was over by now, and it seemed impossible to them that the just cause of the North had not won. But what had happened in that terrible war? How much blood had it cost? Which of their own friends had fallen in the struggle? That was what they often talked about, without yet glimpsing the day when they would be allowed to see their country again. To go back, if only for a few days; to tie the social bond with the inhabited world once more; to open a line of communication between their homeland and their island; and then to spend the longest and perhaps the best part of their lives in this colony they had founded, which would then belong to the mother country — was that, after all, a dream that could never come true?

But there were only two ways to make that dream come true. Either a ship would one day appear in the waters of Lincoln Island, or the colonists would build a vessel themselves, one strong enough to hold the sea as far as the nearest land.

"Unless," Pencroff would say, "our genie hands us the means of getting home himself!"

And truly, if someone had come and told Pencroff and Nab that a ship of three hundred tons was waiting for them in Shark Gulf or at Balloon Harbor, they would not even have made a gesture of surprise. Along those lines, they were ready for anything.

But Cyrus Smith, less trusting, advised them to come back down to earth. That was over the question of building a ship — a truly urgent job, since they needed to leave a notice on Tabor Island as soon as possible, telling where Ayrton was now living.

The Bonadventure was gone, and building a new ship would take six months at the very least. Now, winter was on its way, and the voyage could not be made before the following spring.

"So we have time to get ready for the fine season," said the engineer, who was talking these things over with Pencroff. "Now, my friend, since we have to build our boat over again, I think it would be better to make her considerably bigger. Whether the Scottish yacht will ever come to Tabor Island is very doubtful. She may even have come months ago and left again, after searching in vain for some trace of Ayrton. So wouldn't it be wise to build a ship that could carry us, if it came to that, either to the island groups of Polynesia or to New Zealand? What do you think?"

"I think, Mr. Cyrus," the sailor answered, "I think you're every bit as able to build a big ship as a little one. We're short of neither timber nor tools. It's only a question of time."

"And how many months would it take to build a ship of two hundred and fifty to three hundred tons?" asked Cyrus Smith.

"Seven or eight months at least," answered Pencroff. "But we mustn't forget that winter's coming, and in hard frost wood is difficult to work. So let's count on a few weeks of idleness, and if our ship is ready by next November we ought to think ourselves very lucky."

"Well," replied Cyrus Smith, "that would be exactly the right season to undertake a voyage of any length, whether to Tabor Island or to some land farther away."

"Just so, Mr. Cyrus," answered the sailor. "So draw up your plans. The workmen are ready, and I expect Ayrton can give us a good hand with it."

The colonists were consulted, and they approved the engineer's plan, which was, in truth, the best thing they could do. Building a ship of two to three hundred tons was a big undertaking, it is true, but the colonists had a confidence in themselves that many successes had already justified.

So Cyrus Smith set to work drawing the ship's plan and fixing her lines. Meanwhile his companions busied themselves felling and hauling the trees that were to supply the knees, the frames and the planking. It was the Far West forest that yielded the best kinds of oak and elm. They took advantage of the gap already cut during their last expedition to open a usable road, which took the name of the Far West Road, and the trees were carried to the Chimneys, where the shipyard was set up. As for the road itself, its course wandered somewhat whimsically, for it was more or less the choice of timber that laid it out; but it made a sizable part of Serpentine Peninsula easy to reach.

It was important to cut and saw the timber quickly, because it could not be used green; time had to be left to season and harden it. So the carpenters worked hard all through April, which was disturbed only by a few rather violent equinoctial gales. Master Jup helped them nimbly, either climbing to the top of a tree to fasten the felling ropes, or lending his sturdy shoulders to carry off the trimmed trunks.

All this timber was stacked under a large plank shed built beside the Chimneys, and there it waited for the moment it would be put to use.

April was quite fine, as October often is in the northern zone. At the same time, the farm work was pushed forward energetically, and soon every trace of the devastation had vanished from Prospect Plateau. The mill was rebuilt, and new buildings went up on the site of the poultry yard. It had seemed necessary to rebuild them on a larger scale, because the bird population was growing considerably. The stables now held five onaggas: four strong ones, well broken, that would let themselves be harnessed or ridden, and a little one just born. The colony's equipment had gained a plow, and the onaggas were put to plowing, just like real Yorkshire or Kentucky oxen. Each of the colonists took his share of the work, and nobody's hands sat idle. And what fine health these workers enjoyed, and what good humor they brought to their evenings in Granite House, making a thousand plans for the future!

It goes without saying that Ayrton shared the common life completely, and there was no longer any question of his going to live at the corral. Still, he remained sad and kept to himself, and joined in his companions' work more readily than in their pleasures. But he was a powerful worker on any job — strong, skillful, inventive, intelligent. He was respected and loved by everyone, and he could not have failed to know it.

The corral, however, was not abandoned. Every other day one of the colonists, driving the cart or riding one of the onaggas, went to look after the flock of mouflons and goats and brought back the milk that stocked Nab's pantry. These trips were also chances to hunt. So Harbert and Gédéon Spilett — with Top out in front — ran along the Corral Road more often than any of their companions, and with the excellent guns they now had at their disposal, the house was never short of game: capybaras, agoutis, kangaroos, wild boars and wild pigs for the large game; ducks, grouse, heath cocks, jacamars and snipe for the small. What came out of the warren, what came out of the oyster bed, a few turtles they caught, a new haul of those excellent salmon that once again came crowding into the waters of the Mercy, the vegetables from Prospect Plateau, the wild fruits of the forest — it was riches upon riches, and Nab, the head cook, could barely find room to store them all.

It goes without saying that the telegraph wire strung between the corral and Granite House had been repaired, and it was put to work whenever one or another of the colonists was at the corral and thought it necessary to spend the night there. Besides, the island was safe now, and no attack was to be feared — at least, not from men.

Still, what had happened once could happen again. A landing by pirates, or even by escaped convicts, was always to be feared. It was possible that companions of Bob Harvey's, accomplices still held on Norfolk Island, had been in on the secret of his plans and might be tempted to follow his example. So the colonists did not fail to watch the approaches to the island, and every day their spyglass swept the wide horizon that closed off Union Bay and Washington Bay. When they went to the corral, they studied the western part of the sea with no less care, and by climbing the mountain's shoulder they could take in a wide sweep of the western horizon.

Nothing suspicious appeared, but they still had to keep on their guard at all times.

So one evening the engineer told his friends about a plan he had formed for fortifying the corral. It seemed wise to him to raise its palisade and to flank it with a sort of blockhouse, in which, if it came to that, the colonists could hold out against an enemy band. Granite House had to be considered impregnable by its very position. The corral, then, with its buildings, its stores and the animals it held, would always be the target of any pirates, whoever they were, who landed on the island; and if the colonists were forced to shut themselves up there, they had to be able to hold out on equal terms.

That was a plan to be thought through, and in any case it had to be put off until the following spring.

Around May 15, the keel of the new ship lay stretched out in the shipyard, and soon the stem and the sternpost, mortised into each end of it, rose up from it almost vertically. This keel, made of good oak, was a hundred and ten feet long, which would allow the ship a beam of twenty-five feet at her widest point. But that was all the carpenters could manage before the cold and the bad weather arrived. During the following week they still managed to put the first frames of the stern in place; then the work had to stop.

During the last days of the month the weather was extremely bad. The wind blew from the east, sometimes with the violence of a hurricane. The engineer was somewhat worried about the shipyard's sheds — though he could not have put them anywhere else near Granite House — because the islet sheltered the shore only imperfectly against the fury of the open sea, and in great storms the waves broke straight against the foot of the granite wall.

But, very fortunately, these fears came to nothing. The wind backed instead toward the southeast, and in those conditions the shore below Granite House was completely sheltered by the jutting spur of Jetsam Point.

Pencroff and Ayrton, the two keenest builders of the new ship, kept at their work as long as they could. They were not men to be bothered by the wind twisting their hair or the rain soaking them to the bone, and a hammer blow is as good in foul weather as in fair. But when a very sharp cold followed that wet spell, the wood, whose fibers took on the hardness of iron, became extremely difficult to work, and around June 10 the building of the ship had to be given up for good.

Cyrus Smith and his companions had not failed to notice how harsh the winters of Lincoln Island were. The cold was comparable to what the states of New England feel, and they lie at about the same distance from the equator as the island. In the northern hemisphere — or at least in the part of it taken up by British North America and the northern United States — the cold is explained by the flatness of the lands that stretch toward the pole, where no rise in the ground stands in the way of the Arctic winds. But here, where Lincoln Island was concerned, that explanation could not hold.

"It's even been observed," Cyrus Smith told his companions one day, "that at the same latitude, islands and coastal regions suffer less from the cold than inland countries. I've often heard it said that the winters of Lombardy, for example, are harsher than those of Scotland, and that would be because the sea gives back in winter the heat it took in during the summer. So islands are in the best position to profit from that giving back."

"But then, Mr. Cyrus," asked Harbert, "why does Lincoln Island seem to escape the general rule?"

"That's hard to explain," replied the engineer. "Still, I'd be inclined to think that this oddity comes from the island's position in the southern hemisphere, which, as you know, my boy, is colder than the northern hemisphere."

"That's true," said Harbert, "and floating ice is found at lower latitudes in the south of the Pacific than in the north."

"So it is," said Pencroff, "and when I was in the whaling trade I saw icebergs as far up as off Cape Horn."

"Then perhaps," said Gédéon Spilett, "the hard cold that strikes Lincoln Island could be explained by ice floes or pack ice relatively close by."

"Your opinion is a very reasonable one, my dear Spilett," answered Cyrus Smith, "and it's clearly to the nearness of the pack ice that we owe our hard winters. But let me point out to you that a purely physical cause also makes the southern hemisphere colder than the northern. Since the sun is closer to this hemisphere during its summer, it is necessarily farther away during its winter. That explains why the temperature runs to extremes in both directions, and if we find the winters very cold on Lincoln Island, let's not forget that the summers here are very hot instead."

"But why, if you please, Mr. Smith," asked Pencroff, frowning, "why should our hemisphere, as you call it, get such a poor share? That isn't fair!"

"Friend Pencroff," answered the engineer, laughing, "fair or not, we have to put up with it, and here's where the peculiarity comes from. The earth doesn't travel around the sun in a circle, but in an ellipse, as the laws of mechanics require. The earth occupies one of the foci of the ellipse, and so at one point in its path the earth is at its apogee — that is, at its greatest distance from the sun — and at another point it's at its perigee — that is, at its shortest distance. Now, it so happens that it's precisely during the winter of the southern lands that the earth is at the point farthest from the sun, and therefore in just the conditions to make those regions suffer the greatest cold. There's nothing to be done about it, and men, Pencroff, however learned they may be, will never be able to change anything at all in the order of the heavens that God himself has set."

"And yet," Pencroff added, who showed a certain difficulty in resigning himself, "the world knows such a lot! What a big book you could make, Mr. Cyrus, out of everything people know!"

"And what a much bigger book out of everything they don't know," answered Cyrus Smith.

In any case, for one reason or another, June brought back the cold with its usual violence, and the colonists were confined to Granite House most of the time.

Ah, how hard that confinement seemed to all of them, and perhaps to Gédéon Spilett most of all!

"You see," he said to Nab one day, "I'd sign over to you, before a notary, every inheritance that's ever coming to me, if you'd be a good enough fellow to go off somewhere — anywhere — and take out a subscription to a newspaper for me — any newspaper! There's no doubt about it: what my happiness misses most is knowing every morning what happened the day before, somewhere other than here!"

Nab had burst out laughing.

"My word," he had answered, "what keeps me busy is the day's work!"

The truth is that indoors and out, there was no shortage of work.

The colony of Lincoln Island had now reached the height of its prosperity, and three years of steady labor had made it what it was. The episode of the destroyed brig had been a fresh source of riches. To say nothing of her complete rigging, which would serve the ship on the stocks, the storerooms of Granite House were now crammed with utensils and tools of every kind, arms and ammunition, clothes and instruments. There had been no further need even to make coarse felt cloth. If the colonists had suffered from the cold during their first winter, now the bad season could come without their having to dread its hardships. There was plenty of linen as well, and it was looked after with extreme care. From sodium chloride, which is nothing other than sea salt, Cyrus Smith had easily extracted soda and chlorine. The soda, which was easy to turn into sodium carbonate, and the chlorine, from which he made chloride of lime and other chlorides, were put to various household uses, and in particular to bleaching the linen. Besides, they now did only four big washes a year, as families used to do in the old days; and it may be added that Pencroff and Gédéon Spilett — the reporter while he waited for the mailman to bring him his newspaper — proved to be first-class laundrymen.

So the winter months went by: June, July and August. They were very harsh, and the average of the thermometer readings came to no more than eight degrees Fahrenheit, lower than the temperature of the winter before. And what a good fire blazed all the time in the fireplaces of Granite House, whose smoke streaked the granite wall with long black stripes! They did not spare the fuel, which grew naturally a few steps away. Besides, the leftover timber cut for building the ship let them save on coal, which was harder to haul.

Men and animals were all in good health. Master Jup did show himself a bit sensitive to the cold, it must be admitted. It was perhaps his only fault, and he had to be made a good, well-padded dressing gown. But what a servant — skillful, eager, tireless, not nosy, not a chatterbox! He could rightly have been held up as a model to all his two-legged fellow servants in the Old World and the New.

"After all," Pencroff would say, "when you've got four hands at your service, the least you can do is your work properly!"

And indeed, the intelligent four-handed creature did it well!

During the seven months that passed after the last searches around the mountain, and during September, which brought back the fine days, there was no question of the genie of the island at all. His hand did not show itself on any occasion. True, it would have been useless, for nothing happened that put the colonists to any painful test.

Cyrus Smith even observed that if by chance some communication had ever been established through the mass of granite between the stranger and the residents of Granite House, and if Top's instinct had somehow sensed it, there was no more of it during this period. The dog's growling had stopped completely, and so had the orangutan's uneasiness. The two friends — for friends they were — no longer prowled around the mouth of the inner well; they did not bark, and they no longer whined in that strange way that had put the engineer on the alert from the very beginning. But could he be sure that this was the end of the riddle, and that he would never find its answer? Could he be certain that some turn of events would not come about again and bring the mysterious being back onto the scene? Who could tell what the future held in store?

At last the winter came to an end. But an event whose consequences might well prove serious occurred in the very first days that marked the return of spring.

On September 7, Cyrus Smith, looking at the summit of Mount Franklin, saw smoke curling above the crater, its first wisps rising into the air.
