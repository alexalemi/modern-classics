Chapter 11: Lincoln Island

_At the top of the cone — Inside the crater — Sea all around — No land in sight — The coast from a bird's-eye view — Rivers and mountains — Is the island inhabited? — Christening the bays, gulfs, capes, rivers and so on — Lincoln Island_

Half an hour later Cyrus Smith and Harbert were back at the camp. The engineer told his companions only that the land where chance had thrown them was an island, and that they would decide what to do in the morning. Then everyone settled down to sleep as best he could, and in that hole in the basalt, two thousand five hundred feet above the level of the sea, on a peaceful night, "the islanders" slept deeply.

The next day, March 30, after a quick breakfast made entirely of the roast tragopan, the engineer wanted to climb back up to the summit of the volcano. He meant to take a careful look at the island where he and his friends were imprisoned — imprisoned for life, perhaps, if it lay a long way from any other land, or if it was off the track of the ships that visit the island groups of the Pacific Ocean. This time his companions went with him on the new exploration. They too wanted to see this island, which they were going to ask to supply their every need.

It must have been about seven o'clock in the morning when Cyrus Smith, Harbert, Pencroff, Gédéon Spilett and Nab left the camp. None of them seemed worried about the position he was in. No doubt they had faith in themselves; but it should be pointed out that this faith did not rest on the same ground in Cyrus Smith as in his companions. The engineer was confident because he felt able to wring from this wild nature everything that his companions' lives and his own would need. And they feared nothing precisely because Cyrus Smith was with them. The difference will be understood. Pencroff above all, ever since the business of the fire that had been lit again, would not have despaired for a single moment even if he had found himself on a bare rock, as long as the engineer was on that rock with him.

"Bah!" he said. "We got out of Richmond without permission from the authorities! It would be the very devil if we couldn't manage, one of these days, to get away from a place where nobody's going to stop us!"

Cyrus Smith took the same path as the day before. They went around the cone by way of the plateau that formed a kind of shoulder on the mountain, as far as the mouth of the enormous crevice. The weather was magnificent. The sun was climbing in a clear sky, and its rays covered the whole eastern side of the mountain.

They reached the crater. It was just as the engineer had made it out in the dark: a vast funnel that widened steadily as it rose, to a height of a thousand feet above the plateau. Below the crevice, broad, thick streams of old lava wound down the sides of the mountain, marking the road that the erupted matter had taken down to the lower valleys that cut across the northern part of the island.

The inside of the crater sloped at no more than thirty-five to forty degrees, and it offered neither difficulties nor obstacles to the climb. They could see traces of very ancient lava there, which had probably poured out over the top of the cone before the crevice in the side opened a new way for it.

As for the volcanic chimney that connected the layers deep underground with the crater, no one could judge its depth by eye, because it disappeared into darkness. But as for whether the volcano was completely extinct, there was no doubt about that.

Before eight o'clock Cyrus Smith and his companions were gathered at the top of the crater, on a cone-shaped swelling that bulged up from its northern rim.

"The sea! Sea everywhere!" they cried, as if their lips could not hold back the word that made islanders of them.

The sea indeed — the immense ring of water all around them! Perhaps, in climbing back to the top of the cone, Cyrus Smith had hoped to discover some coast, some nearby island, that he had not been able to make out the evening before in the dark. But nothing appeared, all the way to the edge of the horizon — that is, for more than fifty miles in every direction. No land in sight. Not a sail. All that immensity was empty, and the island sat at the center of a circle that seemed to have no end.

For several minutes the engineer and his companions, silent and motionless, swept their eyes over every point of the ocean. They searched it to its farthest limits. But Pencroff, who had such marvelous powers of sight, saw nothing — and certainly, if any land had risen above the horizon, even if it had shown only as a faint wisp of haze, the sailor would have recognized it beyond any doubt, for nature had set a pair of real telescopes under those eyebrows of his!

From the ocean their eyes turned back to the island, which they looked down on in its entirety, and the first question was asked by Gédéon Spilett, in these words:

"How big can this island be?"

Truly, it did not look very large in the middle of that immense ocean.

Cyrus Smith thought for a few moments. He studied the outline of the island carefully, taking into account the height at which he stood. Then:

"My friends," he said, "I don't think I'm wrong in saying that the island's coastline runs to more than a hundred miles."

"And so its area...?"

"That's hard to judge," the engineer replied, "because the coast is too irregularly cut up."

If Cyrus Smith's estimate was right, the island was very nearly the size of Malta or Zante in the Mediterranean. But it was at once much more irregular and less rich in capes, headlands, points, bays, coves and inlets. Its shape, which was truly strange, caught the eye, and when Gédéon Spilett, at the engineer's suggestion, had sketched its outline, they found that it looked like some fantastic animal — a sort of monstrous pteropod, one of those sea creatures that swim on winglike flaps, lying asleep on the surface of the Pacific.

Here, in fact, is the exact shape of the island, which it is important to describe. The reporter at once drew up a map of it, accurate enough for their needs.

The eastern part of the coast — the part where the castaways had come ashore — was cut back in a broad curve and edged a vast bay, which ended to the southeast in a sharp cape that a point of land had hidden from Pencroff on his first exploration. To the northeast two more capes closed off the bay, and between them ran a narrow gulf that looked like the half-open jaws of some fearsome shark.

From northeast to northwest the coast curved around like the flattened skull of a wild beast, then rose again into a sort of hump that gave no very definite outline to that part of the island, at whose center stood the volcanic mountain.

From there the shore ran fairly evenly north and south. Two-thirds of the way along this stretch it was cut by a narrow inlet, and from that point it ended in a long tail, like the tail of a gigantic alligator.

This tail formed a true peninsula, which stretched more than thirty miles out to sea, counting from the southeast cape of the island already mentioned. It curved around to form a wide-open bay, broadly exposed to the sea, which the lower coast of this so strangely shaped land traced out.

At its narrowest — that is, between the Chimneys and the inlet seen on the west coast at the same latitude — the island measured only ten miles across. But its greatest length, from the jaws in the northeast to the tip of the tail in the southwest, was no less than thirty miles.

As for the interior, its general look was this. The whole southern part, from the mountain down to the shore, was thickly wooded; the northern part was dry and sandy. Between the volcano and the east coast, Cyrus Smith and his companions were quite surprised to see a lake framed by a border of green trees, which they had never suspected was there. Seen from that height, the lake seemed to be at the same level as the sea. But after thinking it over, the engineer explained to his companions that this little sheet of water must lie three hundred feet up, because the plateau that held it was simply a continuation of the one along the coast.

"So it's a freshwater lake?" asked Pencroff.

"It must be," replied the engineer, "because it has to be fed by the water that runs down off the mountain."

"I can see a little river flowing into it," said Harbert, pointing to a narrow stream whose source had to lie somewhere in the foothills to the west.

"So there is," Cyrus Smith replied. "And since that stream feeds the lake, there's probably an outlet somewhere on the side toward the sea, where the overflow escapes. We'll see about that on our way back."

This small, winding stream and the river they already knew made up the island's whole system of waters — at least as far as the explorers could see. Still, under the masses of trees that turned two-thirds of the island into one immense forest, other streams might well be running down to the sea. One even had to suppose so, the region looked so fertile and so rich in the most magnificent specimens of temperate-zone plant life. In the northern part, on the other hand, there was no sign of running water. There might be standing water in the marshy part to the northeast, but that was all. Altogether it was dunes, sand, and a very marked barrenness that stood out sharply against the richness of the soil over the greater part of the island.

The volcano did not stand in the middle of the island. On the contrary, it rose in the northwest region and seemed to mark the border between the two zones. To the southwest, south and southeast, the lower slopes of its foothills disappeared under masses of greenery. To the north, on the other hand, one could follow their branching ridges until they died away in the sandy plains. It was on this side, too, that in the days of eruptions the outpourings had forced their way through, and a broad causeway of lava stretched all the way to the narrow jaws that formed the gulf in the northeast.

Cyrus Smith and his friends stayed an hour like this at the top of the mountain. The island spread out beneath them like a relief map in its different colors: green for the forests, yellow for the sands, blue for the waters. They took it in as a whole. Only the ground hidden under the immense greenery, the floors of the shady valleys, and the insides of the narrow gorges cut into the foot of the volcano escaped their search.

There remained one serious question to settle, a question that was bound to weigh heavily on the castaways' future.

Was the island inhabited?

It was the reporter who asked it, and after the careful examination they had just made of every region of the island, it seemed that the answer could already be no.

Nowhere could they see the work of a human hand. Not a cluster of huts, not a single lonely cabin, not a fishing station on the shore. No smoke rose into the air to betray the presence of man. It was true that some thirty miles separated the watchers from the farthest points — from that tail stretching out to the southwest — and it would have been difficult, even for Pencroff's eyes, to make out a dwelling there. Nor could they lift the curtain of greenery that covered three-quarters of the island and see whether or not it sheltered some village. But as a rule, islanders who live on these small specks of land risen out of the Pacific tend to live along the shore, and the shore seemed to be completely deserted.

Until they had explored it more fully, then, they could take it that the island was uninhabited.

But was it visited, at least from time to time, by the natives of neighboring islands? That question was hard to answer. No land appeared within about fifty miles. But fifty miles can easily be crossed, whether by Malay proas or by the great canoes of the Polynesians. So everything depended on where the island lay — on whether it was isolated in the Pacific or close to some group of islands. Would Cyrus Smith, with no instruments, manage later on to work out its position in latitude and longitude? That would be difficult. While they were in doubt, it was only sensible to take certain precautions against a possible landing by natives from nearby.

The exploration of the island was finished. Its shape had been worked out, its heights measured, its size calculated, its rivers and mountains surveyed. The lie of the forests and the plains had been roughly set down on the reporter's map. All that remained was to go back down the slopes of the mountain and to explore the land from three points of view: its mineral, its plant, and its animal resources.

But before giving his companions the signal to leave, Cyrus Smith spoke to them in his calm, grave voice.

"Here, my friends, is the narrow corner of the earth where the hand of the Almighty has thrown us. This is where we're going to live — for a long time, perhaps. Perhaps, too, some unexpected help will reach us, if a ship happens to pass by chance... I say by chance, because this island is of little importance. It doesn't even offer a harbor where ships could put in, and I'm afraid it lies outside the routes ships usually follow — that is, too far south for the vessels that sail among the island groups of the Pacific, and too far north for those that round Cape Horn on their way to Australia. I don't want to hide anything about our situation from you..."

"And you're right, my dear Cyrus," the reporter replied quickly. "You're dealing with men. They trust you, and you can count on them. Isn't that so, my friends?"

"I'll obey you in everything, Mr. Cyrus," said Harbert, seizing the engineer's hand.

"My master — always and everywhere!" cried Nab.

"As for me," said the sailor, "may I lose my name if I ever shirk the work! And if you're willing, Mr. Smith, we'll make this island into a little America! We'll build towns on it, we'll lay railroads, we'll put up telegraphs, and one fine day, when it's well transformed, well fitted out, well civilized, we'll go and offer it to the government of the Union! Only I ask one thing."

"What's that?" asked the reporter.

"That we stop thinking of ourselves as castaways, and think of ourselves as colonists who've come here to colonize!"

Cyrus Smith could not help smiling, and the sailor's motion was adopted. Then he thanked his companions and added that he was counting on their energy and on the help of Heaven.

"Well then! Off to the Chimneys!" cried Pencroff.

"One moment, my friends," replied the engineer. "I think it would be a good idea to give a name to this island, and to the capes, the headlands and the rivers we can see in front of us."

"A very good idea," said the reporter. "It will make any directions we have to give or follow a lot simpler in the future."

"Right," the sailor went on. "It's already something to be able to say where you're going and where you've come from. At least it feels like you're somewhere."

"The Chimneys, for instance," said Harbert.

"Exactly!" replied Pencroff. "That name was handier already, and it came to me all by itself. Shall we keep the name of the Chimneys for our first camp, Mr. Cyrus?"

"Yes, Pencroff, since that's what you christened it."

"Good. As for the others, that'll be easy," the sailor went on, warming to it. "Let's give them names the way the Robinsons did in those stories Harbert's read me more than once: 'Providence Bay,' 'Sperm Whale Point,' 'Cape Disappointed Hope'...!"

"Or rather the names of Mr. Smith," replied Harbert, "and Mr. Spilett, and Nab...!"

"My name!" cried Nab, with a dazzling grin.

"Why not?" said Pencroff. "'Port Nab' would sound very well! And 'Cape Gédéon'..."

"I'd rather have names taken from our own country," replied the reporter, "names that would remind us of America."

"Yes, for the main ones," said Cyrus Smith then. "For the bays and the seas, I'm all for it. If we give this vast bay to the east the name Union Bay, for example, and this wide indentation to the south the name Washington Bay, and the mountain we're standing on the name Mount Franklin, and that lake spread out below us the name Lake Grant — nothing could be better, my friends. Those names will remind us of our country and of the great citizens who have honored it. But for the rivers, the gulfs, the capes and the headlands we can see from the top of this mountain, let's choose names that call up their particular shapes instead. They'll stick better in our minds, and they'll be more practical too. The shape of the island is strange enough that we won't have any trouble thinking up names that fit the picture. As for the streams we don't know yet, the different parts of the forest that we'll explore later, and the coves we discover from here on, we'll christen them as they come along. What do you think, my friends?"

The engineer's proposal was adopted unanimously by his companions. The island lay there before their eyes like an unfolded map, and all they had to do was put a name to each of its coves and points and each of its heights. Gédéon Spilett would write them down as they went, and the island's geography would be fixed once and for all.

First of all, the two bays and the mountain were named Union Bay, Washington Bay and Mount Franklin, as the engineer had proposed.

"Now," said the reporter, "for that peninsula that juts out from the southwest of the island, I propose the name Serpentine Peninsula, and for the curled-up tail at the end of it, Reptile End — because it really is a reptile's tail."

"Adopted," said the engineer.

"Now," said Harbert, "the other end of the island, that gulf that looks so oddly like a pair of open jaws — let's call it Shark Gulf."

"Well found!" cried Pencroff. "And we'll finish the picture by calling the two sides of the jaws Mandible Cape."

"But there are two capes," the reporter pointed out.

"Well then," replied Pencroff, "we'll have North Mandible Cape and South Mandible Cape."

"They're written down," replied Gédéon Spilett.

"That leaves the point at the southeast end of the island to name," said Pencroff.

"You mean the end of Union Bay?" replied Harbert.

"Claw Cape!" Nab cried at once. He too wanted to be godfather to some piece of his new domain.

And truly Nab had found an excellent name, for that cape really did look like the powerful claw of the fantastic animal that this so strangely outlined island represented.

Pencroff was delighted with the way things were going, and their imaginations, a little overexcited by now, soon produced the following:

For the river that supplied the colonists with drinking water, and near which the balloon had thrown them, the name of the Mercy — a real word of thanks to Providence.

For the islet where the castaways had first set foot, the name of Safety Islet.

For the plateau that crowned the high granite wall above the Chimneys, from which the eye could take in the whole vast bay, the name of Prospect Plateau.

And finally, for the whole mass of impenetrable woods that covered Serpentine Peninsula, the name of the Far West forests.

That completed the naming of the parts of the island that could be seen and were known. Later on it would be filled out as new discoveries were made.

As for the island's orientation, the engineer had worked it out roughly from the height and position of the sun, which put Union Bay and the whole of Prospect Plateau to the east. But the next day, by taking the exact times of sunrise and sunset, and noting the sun's position at the moment exactly halfway between the two, he meant to fix the island's true north precisely. For since the island lay in the Southern Hemisphere, the sun at the precise moment of its highest point in the sky passed to the north, not to the south, as it seems to do in its apparent motion for places in the Northern Hemisphere.

So everything was done, and the colonists had only to go back down Mount Franklin and return to the Chimneys, when Pencroff suddenly cried out:

"Well! What a pack of scatterbrains we are!"

"Why's that?" asked Gédéon Spilett, who had closed his notebook and was getting up to leave.

"What about our island? What! We forgot to christen it!"

Harbert was about to propose giving it the engineer's name, and all his companions would have applauded, when Cyrus Smith said simply:

"Let's name it after a great citizen, my friends, the man who is fighting right now to defend the unity of the American republic! Let's call it Lincoln Island!"

Three hurrahs were the answer to the engineer's proposal.

And that evening, before they fell asleep, the new colonists talked about their faraway country. They spoke of the terrible war that was soaking it in blood. They could not doubt that the South would soon be beaten, and that the cause of the North, the cause of justice, would triumph — thanks to Grant, thanks to Lincoln!

Now, this was March 30, 1865, and they little knew that sixteen days later a dreadful crime would be committed in Washington, and that on Good Friday Abraham Lincoln would fall to the bullet of a fanatic.
