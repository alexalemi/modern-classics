Chapter 3: The Fog Lifts

_The fog lifts — The engineer's plan — Three posts — Ayrton and Pencroff — The first boat — Two more boats — On the islet — Six convicts ashore — The brig weighs anchor — The Speedy's cannonballs — A desperate situation — An unexpected ending_

The night passed without incident. The colonists had stayed on the alert and had not left their post at the Chimneys. The pirates, for their part, did not seem to have made any attempt to land. Since the last shots fired at Ayrton, not a gunshot, not even a sound, had given away the brig's presence off the island. One might almost have believed that she had weighed anchor, thinking she had met too strong an enemy, and had sailed away from those waters.

But nothing of the kind had happened, and when dawn began to break the colonists could make out a shapeless mass in the morning mist. It was the Speedy.

"My friends," the engineer said then, "here is the plan I think we ought to follow before this fog has lifted completely. It hides us from the pirates' eyes, and we can act without drawing their attention. What matters most is to make the convicts believe that the island has a good many inhabitants, and that those inhabitants are therefore able to fight them off. So I propose that we split into three groups. The first will take up its post right here at the Chimneys, the second at the mouth of the Mercy. As for the third, I think it would be wise to put it on the islet, to prevent any attempt at a landing, or at least to delay it. We have two carbines and four guns between us. So every one of us will be armed, and since we have plenty of powder and bullets, we won't spare our shots. We have nothing to fear from the brig's guns, or even from her cannon. What could they do against these rocks? And since we won't be firing from the windows of Granite House, the pirates won't think of sending shells there, where they might do damage that could never be repaired. What we must fear is having to fight hand to hand, because the convicts have numbers on their side. So it's every landing we must try to stop — but without showing ourselves. Let's not be sparing with our ammunition, then. Let's shoot often, but let's shoot straight. Each of us has eight or ten enemies to kill, and he must kill them!"

Cyrus Smith had laid out the situation in plain figures, speaking all the while in the calmest of voices, as if he were directing a piece of work and not a battle. His companions approved the plan without saying a word. All that was left was for each man to reach his post before the mist had completely cleared.

Nab and Pencroff went straight up to Granite House and brought down enough ammunition. Gédéon Spilett and Ayrton, both of them excellent shots, were given the two precision carbines, which could carry nearly a mile. The four other guns were shared out among Cyrus Smith, Nab, Pencroff and Harbert.

This is how the posts were manned.

Cyrus Smith and Harbert stayed hidden at the Chimneys, and from there they commanded a fairly wide stretch of the beach at the foot of Granite House.

Gédéon Spilett and Nab went to crouch among the rocks at the mouth of the Mercy — the bridge and the little bridges had all been raised — so as to stop any boat from getting through and even any landing on the opposite bank.

As for Ayrton and Pencroff, they pushed the canoe into the water and got ready to cross the channel and occupy two separate posts on the islet. In this way, shots ringing out from four different points would make the convicts think that the island was both well populated and fiercely defended.

If a landing was made that they could not prevent, or even if they saw themselves about to be cut off by one of the brig's boats, Pencroff and Ayrton were to come back in the canoe, regain the shore, and hurry to whichever spot was most threatened.

Before going to their posts, the colonists shook hands one last time. Pencroff managed to master himself enough to hold back his feelings as he hugged Harbert, his boy!... and they parted.

A few moments later Cyrus Smith and Harbert on one side, and the reporter and Nab on the other, had vanished behind the rocks, and five minutes after that Ayrton and Pencroff, having crossed the channel without mishap, landed on the islet and hid themselves in the hollows of its eastern shore.

None of them could have been seen, for they themselves could still barely make out the brig in the fog.

It was half past six in the morning.

Soon the fog began to tear apart, little by little, in the upper layers of the air, and the tops of the brig's masts rose out of the vapor. For a few moments more, great rolls of mist tumbled over the surface of the sea. Then a breeze sprang up and quickly scattered the whole bank of fog.

The Speedy appeared in full view, riding at two anchors with her bow to the north and her port quarter turned toward the island. Just as Cyrus Smith had estimated, she lay no more than a mile and a quarter from the shore.

The sinister black flag flew from her gaff.

Through his spyglass the engineer could see that the four cannon that made up her armament had been trained on the island. They were obviously ready to fire at the first signal.

Yet the Speedy stayed silent. Some thirty pirates could be seen coming and going on deck. A few had climbed onto the poop. Two others, perched on the main topgallant crosstrees with long telescopes, were studying the island with the closest attention.

Bob Harvey and his crew, certainly, could have had only a very confused idea of what had happened aboard the brig during the night. That half-naked man who had forced the door of the powder room, whom they had fought, who had emptied his revolver at them six times, who had killed one of their number and wounded two others — had he escaped their bullets? Had he managed to swim back to the coast? Where had he come from? What had he come aboard to do? Had he really meant to blow up the brig, as Bob Harvey believed? All of this must have been fairly muddled in the convicts' minds. But one thing they could no longer doubt: the unknown island off which the Speedy had dropped anchor was inhabited, and there might be a whole colony there ready to defend it. And yet nobody showed himself, either on the beach or on the heights. The shore seemed utterly deserted. In any case there was no sign of any dwelling. Had the inhabitants fled into the interior, then?

That was what the pirate chief must have been asking himself, and no doubt, being a cautious man, he wanted to scout the ground before he committed his gang to it.

For an hour and a half no sign of an attack or a landing could be caught aboard the brig. Bob Harvey was plainly hesitating. Even his best telescopes, no doubt, had not let him make out a single one of the colonists crouching in the rocks. It was not even likely that his attention had been caught by the screen of green branches and creepers that hid the windows of Granite House and stood out against the bare rock wall. After all, how could he have imagined that a dwelling had been hollowed out of the granite mass at that height? From Claw Cape all the way to the Mandible Capes, along the whole sweep of Union Bay, nothing could have told him that the island was occupied, or even could be.

At eight o'clock, however, the colonists noticed a certain stir aboard the Speedy. Men were hauling on the tackles of the boat davits, and a boat was being lowered into the sea. Seven men climbed down into it. They were armed with guns. One of them took the tiller, four took the oars, and the other two, crouching in the bow ready to fire, studied the island. Their aim, no doubt, was to make a first reconnaissance, not to land — for in that case they would have come in greater numbers.

The pirates perched in the rigging as high as the topgallant crosstrees must have been able to see that an islet lay in front of the coast, separated from it by a channel about half a mile wide. But it soon became clear to Cyrus Smith, watching the course the boat was taking, that it would not try to enter the channel at first. It would come alongside the islet instead — a prudent step, and a sensible one.

Pencroff and Ayrton, each hidden on his own in a narrow cleft in the rocks, saw it coming straight at them, and they waited until it was well within range.

The boat came on with extreme caution. The oars dipped into the water only at long intervals. One could see, too, that one of the convicts in the bow was holding a sounding line and was trying to find the channel scoured out by the current of the Mercy. That showed that Bob Harvey meant to bring his brig as close to the coast as he could. Some thirty pirates, scattered through the shrouds, did not miss a single movement of the boat, and they were taking bearings on certain landmarks that would let them come in to shore without danger.

The boat was no more than two cable lengths from the islet when it stopped. The man at the tiller, standing up, was looking for the best place to bring it in.

In an instant two shots rang out. A little puff of smoke swirled above the rocks of the islet. The man at the tiller and the man with the sounding line fell over backward into the boat. Ayrton's and Pencroff's bullets had struck them both at the same moment.

Almost at once a louder explosion was heard. A bright jet of smoke burst from the brig's side, and a cannonball struck the top of the rocks that sheltered Ayrton and Pencroff and sent splinters of stone flying — but neither of the two marksmen had been hit.

Horrible curses had broken out in the boat, which immediately got under way again. The man at the tiller was replaced at once by one of his comrades, and the oars bit sharply into the water.

But instead of going back to the ship, as one might have expected, the boat ran along the shore of the islet so as to round its southern point. The pirates were rowing hard to get out of range of the bullets.

In this way they went on as far as five cable lengths from the hollow in the coastline that ends at Jetsam Point. Then, after swinging round it in a half circle, still covered by the brig's cannon, they headed for the mouth of the Mercy.

Their obvious intention was to get into the channel this way and take the colonists posted on the islet from behind, so that, however many of them there were, they would be caught between the fire of the boat and the fire of the brig, in a very bad position indeed.

A quarter of an hour went by while the boat moved on in that direction. Absolute silence; perfect stillness in the air and on the water.

Pencroff and Ayrton understood that they were in danger of being cut off, yet they had not left their post. Perhaps they did not want to show themselves to the attackers yet and expose themselves to the Speedy's cannon; perhaps they were counting on Nab and Gédéon Spilett, keeping watch at the mouth of the river, and on Cyrus Smith and Harbert, lying in wait among the rocks of the Chimneys.

Twenty minutes after the first shots, the boat was abreast of the Mercy, less than two cable lengths off. The tide was starting to come in with its usual violence, made fiercer by the narrowness of the strait, and the convicts felt themselves being swept toward the river. Only by hard rowing did they manage to keep to the middle of the channel. But as they passed well within range of the mouth of the Mercy, two bullets greeted them on their way, and two more of them were laid flat in the boat. Nab and Spilett had not missed.

At once the brig sent a second cannonball at the post that the gun smoke had given away, but it did nothing more than chip a few rocks.

By now the boat held only three men still fit to fight. Caught by the current, it shot through the channel like an arrow and passed in front of Cyrus Smith and Harbert, who held their fire, judging it not well within range. Then, rounding the northern point of the islet with the two oars it had left, it made its way back toward the brig.

So far the colonists had no cause for complaint. The game was going badly for their enemies. The pirates already counted four men badly wounded, perhaps dead. The colonists, on the other hand, were without a scratch and had not wasted a single bullet. If the pirates kept attacking them this way, if they tried another landing by boat, they could be destroyed one by one.

It is easy to see how well chosen the engineer's plan had been. The pirates might well believe they were up against a large and well-armed enemy, one they would not easily overcome.

Half an hour passed before the boat, which had to fight the current running out to sea, got back to the Speedy. Dreadful shouts rang out when it came alongside with the wounded, and three or four cannon shots were fired that could not possibly have hit anything.

But then other convicts, drunk with rage — and perhaps still drunk from the night before — threw themselves into the boat, a dozen of them.

A second boat was lowered into the sea as well, and eight men took their places in it. While the first headed straight for the islet to drive the colonists out, the second maneuvered to force its way into the mouth of the Mercy.

The situation was obviously becoming very dangerous for Pencroff and Ayrton, and they understood that they had to get back to the mainland.

Still, they waited until the first boat was well within range, and two well-aimed bullets threw its crew into disorder once again. Then Pencroff and Ayrton left their post, not without drawing a dozen shots, ran across the islet as fast as their legs would carry them, threw themselves into the canoe, crossed the channel just as the second boat was reaching the islet's southern point, and ran to take cover in the Chimneys.

They had hardly rejoined Cyrus Smith and Harbert when the islet was overrun, and the pirates from the first boat were searching it from end to end.

Almost at the same moment, new shots broke out at the post on the Mercy, which the second boat had swiftly come up to. Two of the eight men in it were fatally hit by Gédéon Spilett and Nab, and the boat itself, swept helplessly onto the reefs, was smashed to pieces there at the mouth of the Mercy. But the six survivors, holding their weapons above their heads to keep them out of the water, managed to get a footing on the right bank of the river. Then, seeing that they were too close to the post's fire, they ran off as fast as they could toward Jetsam Point, out of range of the bullets.

So this was where things now stood. On the islet were twelve convicts, several of them no doubt wounded, but with a boat still at their disposal. On the island were six who had landed, but who had no way of reaching Granite House, since they could not cross the river, whose bridges were raised.

"It's going well!" Pencroff had said as he rushed into the Chimneys. "It's going well, Mr. Cyrus! What do you think?"

"I think," replied the engineer, "that the fight is about to take a new shape. We can't suppose these convicts are stupid enough to keep it up on terms so bad for them!"

"They still won't get across the channel," said the sailor. "Ayrton's and Mr. Spilett's carbines are there to stop them. You know they carry more than a mile!"

"No doubt," Harbert answered, "but what could two carbines do against the brig's cannon?"

"Ha! The brig isn't in the channel yet, I should think!" Pencroff replied.

"And if she comes in?" said Cyrus Smith.

"She can't. She'd risk running aground and being wrecked!"

"She can," Ayrton answered then. "The convicts can use the high tide to come into the channel, even if it means going aground at low tide. And then, under the fire of their cannon, our posts won't be worth holding."

"By all the thousand devils of hell!" cried Pencroff. "It really looks as if the scoundrels are getting ready to weigh anchor!"

"Maybe we'll be forced to take refuge in Granite House?" Harbert suggested.

"Let's wait!" replied Cyrus Smith.

"But Nab and Mr. Spilett?..." said Pencroff.

"They'll know how to join us in good time. Be ready, Ayrton. It's your carbine and Spilett's that must do the talking now."

It was only too true! The Speedy was beginning to swing round on her anchor and was showing that she meant to move closer to the islet. The tide would go on rising for another hour and a half, and since the flood current had already slackened, it would be easy for the brig to maneuver. But as for entering the channel, Pencroff, against Ayrton's opinion, could not believe that she would dare to try it.

Meanwhile the pirates who held the islet had gradually moved over to its far shore, and now only the channel separated them from the land. Armed with nothing but guns, they could do no harm to the colonists hidden either at the Chimneys or at the mouth of the Mercy. But since they did not know the colonists had long-range carbines, they did not think they were in any danger themselves either. So they walked about the islet in the open and paced along its edge.

Their mistake did not last long. Ayrton's and Gédéon Spilett's carbines spoke up then, and no doubt said something unpleasant to two of those convicts, for the two men fell over backward.

It was a general rout. The ten others did not even take the time to pick up their wounded or dead companions. They rushed back to the other side of the islet, threw themselves into the boat that had brought them, and rowed hard back to the ship.

"Eight fewer!" Pencroff had cried. "Honestly, you'd think Mr. Spilett and Ayrton had agreed to work as a team!"

"Gentlemen," Ayrton answered as he reloaded his carbine, "this is about to get more serious. The brig is getting under way!"

"The anchor's up and down!..." cried Pencroff.

"Yes, and it's already breaking out."

And indeed they could clearly hear the clank of the pawl striking against the windlass as the brig's crew hove in. At first the Speedy had moved up over her anchor. Then, once it had been torn out of the bottom, she began to drift toward the land. The wind was blowing in from the sea. The jib and the fore-topsail were hoisted, and the ship drew closer and closer to the shore.

From both posts, the Mercy and the Chimneys, they watched her maneuver without giving any sign of life — but not without a certain anxiety. The colonists' situation would be a terrible one once they were exposed, at short range, to the fire of the brig's cannon, with no means of answering it to any purpose. How could they stop the pirates from landing then?

Cyrus Smith felt this keenly, and he was asking himself what could be done. Before long he would have to make a decision. But what decision? Shut themselves up in Granite House, let themselves be besieged there, and hold out for weeks — for months, even — since there was food in plenty? Good! But after that? The pirates would be masters of the island all the same, free to lay it waste as they pleased, and in time they would get the better of the prisoners of Granite House.

Still, one chance remained: that Bob Harvey would not risk his ship in the channel and would keep to the outside of the islet. Half a mile would still separate him from the coast, and at that distance his shots might not do too much harm.

"Never," Pencroff kept saying, "never will that Bob Harvey come into the channel — not if he's a good seaman! He knows perfectly well he'd be risking the brig if the sea turned the least bit rough! And what would become of him without his ship?"

Yet the brig had come close to the islet, and they could see that she was trying to reach its lower end. The breeze was light, and since the current had by then lost much of its strength, Bob Harvey was entirely free to maneuver as he pleased.

The course the boats had followed earlier had let him find the deep water, and he had boldly entered it. His plan was only too easy to understand. He meant to moor broadside on in front of the Chimneys and from there answer with shells and cannonballs the bullets that had so far been cutting down his crew.

Soon the Speedy reached the point of the islet. She rounded it easily. Then her spanker was filled, and the brig, hauling close to the wind, found herself abreast of the Mercy.

"The bandits! They're coming in!" cried Pencroff.

At that moment Cyrus Smith, Ayrton, the sailor and Harbert were joined by Nab and Gédéon Spilett.

The reporter and his companion had judged it best to abandon the post at the Mercy, from which they could do nothing more against the ship, and they had acted wisely. It was better that the colonists should be together at the moment when a decisive action was surely about to begin. Gédéon Spilett and Nab had made their way there by slipping along under cover of the rocks, though not without drawing a hail of bullets, none of which had hit them.

"Spilett! Nab!" the engineer had cried. "You're not hurt?"

"No!" answered the reporter. "Just a few bruises — ricochets! But that damned brig is coming into the channel!"

"Yes!" Pencroff answered. "And in less than ten minutes she'll be anchored in front of Granite House!"

"Do you have a plan, Cyrus?" asked the reporter.

"We must take refuge in Granite House while there's still time, and while the convicts can't see us."

"That's my opinion too," Gédéon Spilett answered. "But once we're shut in..."

"We'll be guided by circumstances," replied the engineer.

"Let's go, then, and quickly!" said the reporter.

"You don't want Ayrton and me to stay here, Mr. Cyrus?" asked the sailor.

"What good would that do, Pencroff?" replied Cyrus Smith. "No. Let's not split up!"

There was not a moment to lose. The colonists left the Chimneys. A small jutting angle of the cliff wall kept them out of sight of the brig, but two or three explosions and the crash of cannonballs on the rocks told them that the Speedy was now only a short distance away.

To rush into the elevator, hoist themselves up to the door of Granite House, where Top and Jup had been shut in since the day before, and dash into the great hall was the work of a moment.

It was not a moment too soon, for through the branches the colonists saw the Speedy, wreathed in smoke, gliding up the channel. They even had to stand aside from the windows, because the firing never stopped. The cannonballs from the four guns were pounding blindly both at the post on the Mercy, though no one was there now, and at the Chimneys. The rocks were shattered, and every shot was greeted with cheers.

Still, they could hope that Granite House would be spared, thanks to the precaution Cyrus Smith had taken of hiding its windows — when a cannonball grazed the doorway and came flying into the passage.

"Curse it! Have they found us?" cried Pencroff.

Perhaps the colonists had not been seen, but it was certain that Bob Harvey had seen fit to send a shot through the suspicious foliage that masked that part of the high wall. Soon he was firing harder than ever, for another cannonball tore through the curtain of leaves and laid bare a gaping opening in the granite.

The colonists' situation was desperate. Their refuge had been discovered. They had no way to block these projectiles or to shield the stone, whose splinters were flying around them like grapeshot. There was nothing left for them but to take refuge in the upper passage of Granite House and abandon their home to whatever destruction might come — when a dull roar was heard, followed by dreadful screams!

Cyrus Smith and his companions rushed to one of the windows...

The brig, lifted irresistibly on a kind of waterspout, had just split in two, and in less than ten seconds she had sunk with all her criminal crew!
