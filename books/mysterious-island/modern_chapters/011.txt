Chapter 12: Setting the Watches

_Setting the watches — Pencroff is satisfied — A suspicious smoke — The course of Red Creek — The plants of Lincoln Island — The animals — Mountain pheasants — The kangaroo chase — The agouti — Lake Grant — Back to the Chimneys_

The colonists of Lincoln Island took one last look around them. They walked the circuit of the crater along its narrow rim, and half an hour later they were back down on the first plateau, at the place where they had camped for the night.

Pencroff decided it was time for lunch, and that brought up the question of setting the two watches, Cyrus Smith's and the reporter's.

Gédéon Spilett's watch, as we know, had been spared by the seawater, since the reporter had been thrown up on the sand at the very start, out of reach of the waves. It was an excellently made instrument, a true pocket chronometer, and Gédéon Spilett had never once forgotten to wind it carefully every day.

The engineer's watch, on the other hand, had of course stopped during the time Cyrus Smith had spent in the dunes.

So the engineer wound it, and, judging from the height of the sun that it must be about nine o'clock in the morning, he set his watch to that hour.

Gédéon Spilett was about to do the same when the engineer stopped him with a hand and said:

"No, my dear Spilett, wait. You've kept Richmond time, haven't you?"

"Yes, Cyrus."

"So your watch is set to the meridian of that city — a meridian that's very nearly the same as Washington's?"

"Of course."

"Well, then, keep it that way. Just wind it very carefully every day, but don't touch the hands. It may be useful to us."

"What good will that do?" thought the sailor.

They ate, and ate so well that the store of game and almonds was completely used up. But Pencroff was not the least bit worried. They would stock up again along the way. Top, whose share had been a very meager one, would surely find some new game under cover of the thickets. Besides, the sailor was thinking of simply asking the engineer to make some gunpowder and one or two hunting guns, and he imagined that this would present no difficulty at all.

As they left the plateau, Cyrus Smith proposed to his companions that they take a new route back to the Chimneys. He wanted to get a look at Lake Grant, so magnificently framed by its border of trees. So they followed the crest of one of the spurs of the mountain, between which the creek that fed the lake probably had its source. As they talked, the colonists were already using nothing but the names they had just chosen, and that made it remarkably easier to share their ideas. Harbert and Pencroff — the one young, the other a bit of a child himself — were delighted with them, and as they walked the sailor said:

"Ha! Harbert! Look how well it works! We can't possibly get lost now, my boy, because whether we take the road by Lake Grant or cut through the Far West forests to the Mercy, we're bound to come out on Prospect Plateau, and so at Union Bay!"

It had been agreed that the colonists, without marching in a tight group, would not stray too far from one another. It was quite certain that some dangerous animals lived in the island's thick forests, and it was wise to stay on guard. Most of the time Pencroff, Harbert, and Nab walked in front, with Top ahead of them nosing into every corner. The reporter and the engineer went along together: Gédéon Spilett ready to note down any incident, the engineer silent most of the time, stepping off his path only to pick up now one thing, now another — some mineral or plant substance — which he put in his pocket without a word of comment.

"What the devil does he keep picking up?" Pencroff muttered. "I can look all I like, and I don't see a thing worth bending over for!"

Toward ten o'clock the little band was coming down the last slopes of Mount Franklin. So far the ground was dotted only with bushes and a few scattered trees. They were walking on yellowish, scorched earth, which formed a plain about a mile long in front of the edge of the woods. Big blocks of basalt lay strewn across the plain, which was very rough and broken in places — basalt of the kind that, according to Bischof's experiments, needed three hundred and fifty million years to cool. Still, there was no trace of lava, which had poured out mostly down the northern slopes.

Cyrus Smith expected, then, to reach the course of the creek without incident. By his reckoning it ought to wind along under the trees at the edge of the plain. Just then he saw Harbert running back in a hurry, while Nab and the sailor ducked out of sight behind the rocks.

"What is it, my boy?" asked Gédéon Spilett.

"Smoke," answered Harbert. "We saw smoke rising between the rocks, a hundred paces from us."

"Men, here?" exclaimed the reporter.

"Let's not show ourselves until we know who we're dealing with," answered Cyrus Smith. "If there are natives on this island, I'd rather fear them than hope for them. Where's Top?"

"Top's up ahead."

"And he isn't barking?"

"No."

"That's odd. All the same, let's try to call him back."

In a few moments the engineer, Gédéon Spilett, and Harbert had joined their two companions, and like them they hid behind some broken chunks of basalt.

From there they could see, very clearly, a column of smoke swirling as it rose into the air — smoke of a very distinctive yellowish color.

Top, called back by a soft whistle from his master, returned, and his master, signaling to his companions to wait for him, slipped off between the rocks.

The colonists waited without moving, a little anxious to learn the result of this scouting, when a call from Cyrus Smith brought them running. They joined him at once, and the first thing that struck them was the unpleasant smell that filled the air.

That smell, easy to recognize, had been enough to tell the engineer what the smoke was — the smoke that must have worried him at first, and not without reason.

"This fire," he said, "or rather this smoke, is nature's doing and nobody else's. It's nothing but a sulfur spring, which will let us give our sore throats a proper treatment."

"Fine!" cried Pencroff. "What a shame I haven't got a cold!"

The colonists then made their way toward the spot the smoke was coming from. There they found a sodium-sulfur spring, flowing quite abundantly between the rocks. Its waters, once they had absorbed oxygen from the air, gave off a strong smell of hydrogen sulfide — the rotten-egg gas.

Cyrus Smith dipped his hand in and found the water oily to the touch. He tasted it and judged that it had a slightly sweetish flavor. As for its temperature, he put it at ninety-five degrees Fahrenheit. And when Harbert asked him what he based this estimate on, he said:

"Quite simply, my boy, on the fact that when I put my hand into this water, I felt no sensation of cold or of heat. So it's at the same temperature as the human body, which is about ninety-five degrees."

Then, since the sulfur spring offered nothing they could use for the moment, the colonists headed for the dense edge of the forest, which began a few hundred paces away.

There, just as they had guessed, the stream ran its lively, clear waters between high banks of red earth, whose color showed the presence of iron oxide. That color at once gave the stream its name: Red Creek.

It was really only a wide brook, deep and clear, formed by the waters coming off the mountain. Half river, half torrent — here flowing quietly over the sand, there roaring over the tops of rocks or plunging down in a cascade — it ran toward the lake for a mile and a half, varying between thirty and forty feet wide. Its water was fresh, which suggested that the lake's water must be fresh too. That was a lucky thing, in case they found on its shores a better place to live than the Chimneys.

As for the trees that shaded the banks of the creek a few hundred feet downstream, most of them belonged to species that are plentiful in the temperate zone of Australia or Tasmania, and no longer to the conifers that bristled over the part of the island already explored, a few miles from Prospect Plateau. At this time of year — the beginning of April, which in this hemisphere corresponds to October, that is, to the start of autumn — they had not yet lost their leaves. Most of them were casuarinas and eucalyptus trees, some of which would yield, the following spring, a sugary manna just like the manna of the East. Clumps of Australian cedars rose here and there in the clearings, which were covered in the tall grass known as "tussock" in New Holland, as Australia was then called. But the coconut palm, so plentiful in the archipelagos of the Pacific, seemed to be missing from the island, whose latitude was doubtless too high.

"What a pity!" said Harbert. "Such a useful tree, with such wonderful nuts!"

As for birds, they swarmed among the rather sparse branches of the eucalyptus and the casuarinas, which left them plenty of room to spread their wings. Black, white, and gray cockatoos; parrots and parakeets in plumage shaded with every color; "kings," a brilliant green and crowned with red; blue lories; "blue mountains" — they seemed to be seen only through a prism, and they fluttered about in a deafening chatter.

All of a sudden a strange concert of clashing voices rang out from the middle of a thicket. One after another, the colonists heard birdsong, the cries of four-footed animals, and a sort of clicking of the tongue that they might have thought came from the lips of a native. Nab and Harbert had rushed toward the bush, forgetting the most basic rules of caution. Very luckily there was no dangerous wild beast there, and no hostile native either — only half a dozen of those mocking, singing birds which they recognized as "mountain pheasants." A few well-aimed blows with a stick brought the imitation show to an end, and provided an excellent piece of game for that evening's dinner.

Harbert also pointed out some magnificent pigeons with bronze-colored wings, some topped with a superb crest, others draped in green like their cousins at Port Macquarie in Australia. But it was impossible to get at them, any more than at the crows and magpies that flew off in flocks. A single shot of birdshot would have brought down these birds by the dozen, but the hunters were still reduced to stones for throwing weapons and sticks for hand weapons, and those primitive tools fell a long way short.

Just how far short became even clearer when a troop of four-footed animals went bounding off over the thickets — hopping, leaping, making jumps of thirty feet, regular flying mammals — so nimbly and so high that you might have thought they were jumping from tree to tree like squirrels.

"Kangaroos!" cried Harbert.

"And can you eat them?" replied Pencroff.

"Stewed," answered the reporter, "they're as good as the finest venison!..."

Gédéon Spilett had not finished that exciting sentence before the sailor, followed by Nab and Harbert, was off on the kangaroos' trail. Cyrus Smith called them back, in vain. But it was in vain, too, that the hunters were going to chase this springy game, which bounced along like a rubber ball. After five minutes of running they were out of breath, and the troop was vanishing into the brush. Top had had no more luck than his masters.

"Mr. Cyrus," said Pencroff, once the engineer and the reporter had caught up with him, "Mr. Cyrus, you can see we absolutely have to make guns. Is that going to be possible?"

"Perhaps," the engineer answered. "But we'll start by making bows and arrows, and I have no doubt you'll get to be as skillful with them as the Australian hunters."

"Bows and arrows!" said Pencroff, with a scornful face. "That's for children!"

"Don't be so proud, friend Pencroff," the reporter answered. "For centuries bows and arrows were enough to drench the world in blood. Gunpowder only dates from yesterday, and war is as old as the human race — unfortunately!"

"Upon my word, that's true, Mr. Spilett," replied the sailor, "and I always talk too fast. You'll have to forgive me!"

Meanwhile Harbert, wrapped up in his favorite science, natural history, came back to the kangaroos.

"Anyway," he said, "those were the hardest kind to catch. They were giants with long gray fur. But if I'm not mistaken there are also black kangaroos and red kangaroos, rock kangaroos, and rat kangaroos, which are easier to get hold of. People count about a dozen species..."

"Harbert," the sailor replied solemnly, "as far as I'm concerned there's only one species of kangaroo — the kangaroo-on-a-spit — and that's exactly the one we'll be missing tonight!"

Nobody could help laughing at Master Pencroff's new system of classification. The good sailor made no secret of his regret at being reduced to singing pheasants for dinner. But fortune was about to smile on him once again.

Top, in fact, knew very well that his own interests were at stake, and he went ferreting about everywhere with an instinct sharpened by a ferocious appetite. It was even likely that if any game fell into his jaws there would not be much of it left for the hunters, and that Top was hunting on his own account just then. But Nab was keeping an eye on him, and a good thing too.

Toward three o'clock the dog disappeared into the brushwood, and soon some low growls told them that he was grappling with some animal.

Nab dashed in, and sure enough he found Top greedily devouring a four-footed creature which ten seconds later it would have been impossible to identify inside Top's stomach. But very luckily the dog had come upon a whole nest of them. He had made a triple catch, and two other rodents — for the animals in question belonged to that order — lay strangled on the ground.

So Nab came back in triumph, holding one of the rodents in each hand. They were bigger than a hare. Their yellow coats were mixed with greenish spots, and their tails were only stubs.

Citizens of the Union could not hesitate over the proper name for these rodents. They were "maras," a kind of agouti, a little bigger than their relatives in the tropical countries — true American rabbits, with long ears, and with jaws armed on each side with five molars, which is exactly what sets them apart from the agoutis.

"Hurrah!" cried Pencroff. "The roast has arrived! And now we can go home!"

The march, interrupted for a moment, began again. Red Creek still rolled its clear waters along under a vault of casuarinas, banksias, and gigantic gum trees. Superb lily plants rose as high as twenty feet. Other tree-like species, unknown to the young naturalist, leaned out over the stream, which they could hear murmuring beneath these green arbors.

Meanwhile the stream was growing noticeably wider, and Cyrus Smith was inclined to think they would soon reach its mouth. And indeed, as they came out of a thick stand of fine trees, there it was all at once.

The explorers had arrived on the western shore of Lake Grant. The place was well worth looking at. This sheet of water, about seven miles around and covering two hundred and fifty acres, lay in a border of many kinds of trees. To the east, through a curtain of greenery lifted here and there as if to frame a picture, a sparkling horizon of sea appeared. To the north the lake traced a gently curving inward line, which contrasted with the sharp outline of its lower tip. Large numbers of water birds frequented the shores of this little Ontario, whose namesake's "Thousand Islands" were represented by a single rock sticking up out of the water a few hundred feet from the southern shore. On it lived several pairs of kingfishers, all together, perched on some stone, grave and motionless, watching for fish to go by — then darting, diving with a shrill cry, and coming up again with their prey in their beaks. Elsewhere, on the shores and on the islet, strutted wild ducks, pelicans, moorhens, red-bills, and philedons, which are equipped with a tongue shaped like a paintbrush, and one or two specimens of those splendid lyrebirds whose tails spread out like the graceful arms of a lyre.

As for the water of the lake, it was fresh and clear, a little dark, and from certain bubblings, and from the rings that spread out and crossed one another on its surface, there could be no doubt that it was full of fish.

"It's really beautiful, this lake!" said Gédéon Spilett. "A person could live on its shores!"

"A person will!" answered Cyrus Smith.

The colonists, wanting now to go back to the Chimneys by the shortest way, went down to the corner formed at the south where the lake's shores met. They forced their way, not without trouble, through thickets and brushwood that no human hand had ever yet pushed aside, and in this way they headed for the shore, so as to come out at the northern end of Prospect Plateau. They covered two miles in that direction, and then, past the last curtain of trees, the plateau appeared, carpeted with thick grass, and beyond it the endless sea.

To get back to the Chimneys, they had only to cross the plateau on a slant for a mile and then go down to the elbow formed by the first bend of the Mercy. But the engineer wanted to find out how, and where, the lake's overflow escaped, and so the exploration was stretched out under the trees for a mile and a half to the north. It was likely, in fact, that there was a spillway somewhere, probably through a gap in the granite. The lake, after all, was nothing but an immense basin that had slowly filled up with the flow of the creek, and its overflow had to be draining away to the sea by some waterfall or other. If that was so, the engineer thought it might be possible to put that waterfall to use and borrow its power, which at present was going to waste and doing no one any good. So they kept following the shores of Lake Grant, climbing up the plateau. But after going another mile in that direction, Cyrus Smith had still not found the spillway, which nevertheless had to exist.

It was half past four by then. Dinner had to be got ready, and that meant the colonists had to go home. So the little band turned back, and by the left bank of the Mercy, Cyrus Smith and his companions reached the Chimneys.

There the fire was lit, and Nab and Pencroff, the colony's natural cooks, briskly grilled the agoutis, and everyone did them full justice.

When the meal was over, just as each of them was about to settle down to sleep, Cyrus Smith pulled out of his pocket some small samples of different kinds of minerals, and said only this:

"My friends, this is iron ore, this is pyrite, this is clay, this is lime, this is coal. That is what nature gives us, and that is nature's share of the common work! Tomorrow, ours!"
