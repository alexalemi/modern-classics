Chapter 1: The Lead Pellet

_About the lead pellet — Building a canoe — The hunts — At the top of a kauri — Nothing to show that man is here — A catch by Nab and Harbert — A turtle turned over — A turtle gone — Cyrus Smith's explanation_

It was seven months to the day since the passengers of the balloon had been thrown onto Lincoln Island. In all that time, however hard they had searched, not one human being had shown himself to them. Never had a wisp of smoke betrayed the presence of man anywhere on the island. Never had any work of human hands shown that someone had passed that way, whether long ago or lately. Not only did the island seem to be uninhabited; there was every reason to believe it had never been inhabited at all. And now that whole scaffolding of deductions was collapsing in front of a single pellet of metal found in the body of a harmless little animal!

For that lead had come out of a firearm, and who but a human being could have fired it?

When Pencroff set the pellet down on the table, his companions stared at it in deep astonishment. All the consequences of this incident, enormous for all its apparent smallness, had suddenly seized their minds. The sudden appearance of some supernatural being could not have struck them more sharply.

Cyrus Smith did not hesitate. He began at once to lay out the theories that so surprising and unexpected a fact was bound to raise. He picked up the pellet, turned it this way and that, and felt it between his thumb and forefinger. Then:

"You're sure," he asked Pencroff, "that the peccary this pellet wounded was barely three months old?"

"Barely, Mr. Cyrus," answered Pencroff. "It was still nursing at its mother when I found it in the pit."

"Well, then," said the engineer, "that proves, all by itself, that a gun has been fired on Lincoln Island within the last three months at most."

"And that a lead pellet hit this little animal," added Gédéon Spilett, "though not fatally."

"There's no doubt about it," Cyrus Smith went on, "and here are the consequences we ought to draw from it. Either the island was inhabited before we came, or men have landed on it within the last three months at most. Did those men come here on purpose or by accident? Did they put in to shore, or were they shipwrecked? That question can only be cleared up later. As for who they are — Europeans or Malays, enemies or friends to men like us — we have nothing at all to guess by. And whether they're still on the island or have left it, we don't know that either. But these questions concern us too closely for us to stay in doubt about them any longer."

"No! A hundred times no! A thousand times no!" cried the sailor, getting up from the table. "There are no other men on Lincoln Island but us! Hang it all, the island isn't big, and if anybody lived on it, we'd have caught sight of a few of them by now!"

"It would certainly be very surprising if we hadn't," said Harbert.

"But it would be a great deal more surprising, I should think," observed the reporter, "if this peccary had been born with a lead pellet inside it!"

"Unless," said Nab seriously, "Pencroff had..."

"Listen to him, Nab!" Pencroff shot back. "So for the last five or six months I've been carrying a lead pellet around in my jaw without noticing it! And where would it have been hiding?" the sailor added, opening his mouth to show off the splendid thirty-two teeth that filled it. "Take a good look, Nab, and if you find one hollow tooth in that set, you have my permission to pull out half a dozen!"

"Nab's theory can't be accepted, that's true," answered Cyrus Smith, who for all the seriousness of his thoughts could not hold back a smile. "It's certain that a gun has been fired on the island within the last three months at most. But I'm inclined to think that whoever landed on this coast has been here only a very short time, or else only stopped and went on. Because if the island had been inhabited when we were surveying it from the top of Mount Franklin, we would have seen them or they would have seen us. So most likely some castaways were thrown up somewhere on the coast by a storm only a few weeks ago. Whatever the truth is, we need to be certain about it."

"I think we ought to act carefully," said the reporter.

"I agree," answered Cyrus Smith, "because unfortunately there's reason to fear that it's Malay pirates who have landed on the island!"

"Mr. Cyrus," asked the sailor, "wouldn't it be a good idea, before we go out exploring, to build a boat that would let us go up the river, or around the coast if need be? We mustn't let ourselves be caught unprepared."

"That's a good idea, Pencroff," answered the engineer, "but we can't wait. It would take at least a month to build a boat..."

"A real boat, yes," the sailor answered. "But we don't need anything fit to go to sea. In five days at most I'll guarantee to build a canoe good enough to travel on the Mercy."

"In five days?" cried Nab. "Make a boat?"

"Yes, Nab, a boat the way the Indians make them."

"Out of wood?" asked Nab, not looking very convinced.

"Out of wood," answered Pencroff, "or rather out of bark. I'll say it again, Mr. Cyrus: in five days the job can be done!"

"Five days it is, then!" answered the engineer.

"But until then we'd better keep a sharp watch!" said Harbert.

"A very sharp watch, my friends," answered Cyrus Smith. "And I'll ask you to keep your hunting trips to the neighborhood of Granite House."

Dinner ended less cheerfully than Pencroff had hoped.

So the island was inhabited, or had been, by someone besides the colonists. Since the incident of the lead pellet, that was now a fact beyond dispute, and a discovery like that could only cause the colonists serious alarm.

Before going to bed, Cyrus Smith and Gédéon Spilett talked these things over for a long time. They wondered whether, by any chance, this incident might have some connection with the unexplained circumstances of the engineer's rescue, and with the other strange details that had struck them more than once already. In the end, though, after arguing the question both ways, Cyrus Smith said:

"To sum it up, would you like to know what I think, my dear Spilett?"

"Yes, Cyrus."

"Well, here it is. However carefully we explore the island, we won't find anything!"

The very next day Pencroff set to work. The point was not to build a proper boat with ribs and planking, but simply a flat-bottomed craft that would float, which would be excellent for traveling on the Mercy, especially up near its sources, where the water would be shallow. Pieces of bark sewn one to another would be enough to make the light little vessel, and if natural obstacles meant it had to be carried overland, it would be neither heavy nor awkward. Pencroff planned to join the strips of bark with riveted nails, and to make sure, by fitting them tightly together, that the craft would be perfectly watertight.

So they had to choose trees whose bark was supple and tough enough to lend itself to the work. Now, as it happened, the last storm had brought down a number of Douglas firs, which were perfectly suited to this kind of construction. Some of these firs were lying on the ground, and all that remained was to strip off the bark. But that turned out to be the hardest part, given how poor the colonists' tools were. Still, in the end they managed it.

While the sailor, with the engineer's help, was busy at this without losing an hour, Gédéon Spilett and Harbert were not idle either. They had made themselves the colony's providers. The reporter never tired of admiring the boy, who had become remarkably skillful with the bow and the spear. Harbert also showed great daring, together with a good deal of that coolness one might rightly call "the reasoning side of bravery." The two hunting companions, heeding Cyrus Smith's advice, no longer went more than two miles from Granite House in any direction. But the lower slopes of the forest supplied plenty of agoutis, capybaras, kangaroos, peccaries and so on. And though the traps were not bringing in much now that the cold had ended, the warren at least was giving its usual share, which would have been enough to feed the whole colony of Lincoln Island.

Often, during these hunts, Harbert talked with Gédéon Spilett about the incident of the lead pellet and the conclusions the engineer had drawn from it. One day — it was October 26 — he said to him:

"But, Mr. Spilett, don't you think it's very strange that if some castaways have landed on this island, they still haven't shown themselves anywhere near Granite House?"

"Very surprising, if they're still here," answered the reporter, "but not surprising at all if they're gone!"

"So you think those people have already left the island?" Harbert went on.

"It's more than likely, my boy. If they had stayed for long, and above all if they were still here, something would have given them away sooner or later."

"But if they were able to leave again," the boy pointed out, "then they weren't castaways?"

"No, Harbert. Or at least they were what I'd call temporary castaways. It's quite possible that a gale threw them onto the island without wrecking their boat, and that once the gale had passed they put to sea again."

"One thing has to be said," Harbert remarked. "Mr. Smith has always seemed more afraid of finding human beings on our island than eager to."

"That's true," answered the reporter. "He doesn't see who could be sailing these seas except the Malays, and those gentlemen are nasty scoundrels, best avoided."

"It isn't impossible, Mr. Spilett," Harbert went on, "that one of these days we'll come across some traces of their landing, and then perhaps we'll know for certain?"

"I won't say no, my boy. An abandoned camp, a dead fire, could put us on the trail, and that's what we'll be looking for on our next expedition."

On the day the two hunters had this talk, they were in a part of the forest near the Mercy that was remarkable for its magnificent trees. Among others, there rose up there, nearly two hundred feet above the ground, a few of those superb conifers that the Maori people of New Zealand call "kauris."

"I have an idea, Mr. Spilett," said Harbert. "If I climbed to the top of one of these kauris, maybe I could look out over the country for quite a long way around?"

"It's a good idea," answered the reporter, "but can you climb all the way to the top of those giants?"

"I can at least try," answered Harbert.

The boy, nimble and skillful, sprang up onto the lowest branches, which were arranged so as to make the kauri fairly easy to climb, and in a few minutes he had reached its crown, which rose clear of the immense plain of green formed by the rounded treetops of the forest.

From that high point the eye could range over the whole southern part of the island, from Claw Cape in the southeast to Reptile End in the southwest. To the northwest stood Mount Franklin, which blocked out a good quarter of the horizon.

But from the top of his lookout Harbert could survey exactly the part of the island that was still unknown, the part that might have sheltered, or might be sheltering still, the strangers whose presence they suspected.

The boy looked with the closest attention. At sea, first of all, there was nothing in sight. Not a sail, either on the horizon or in the waters close to the island. Still, since the mass of trees hid the shoreline, it was possible that a ship — especially one that had lost its masts — had come in very close to land and so could not be seen by Harbert.

In the middle of the Far West forests there was nothing either. The forest made an impenetrable dome several square miles across, without a clearing, without a gap. It was not even possible to follow the course of the Mercy, or to make out the spot on the mountain where it had its source. Perhaps other creeks ran toward the west, but there was nothing to show it.

But even if every sign of a camp escaped Harbert, might he not catch some smoke in the air that would betray the presence of man? The air was clear, and the faintest wisp of vapor would have stood out sharply against the sky.

For a moment Harbert thought he saw a thin thread of smoke rising in the west, but a more careful look showed him he was mistaken. He looked with the greatest care, and his eyesight was excellent... No, there was definitely nothing there.

Harbert climbed back down to the foot of the kauri, and the two hunters returned to Granite House. There Cyrus Smith listened to the boy's account, shook his head, and said nothing. It was quite clear that no one could settle the question until the island had been explored from end to end.

Two days later — October 28 — another incident took place, and its explanation, too, would leave something to be desired.

While roaming along the beach two miles from Granite House, Harbert and Nab were lucky enough to capture a magnificent specimen of the order of chelonians, the turtles. It was a green turtle, of the genus Mydas, and its shell gave off wonderful green gleams.

Harbert had spotted the turtle as it was slipping between the rocks to reach the sea.

"Over here, Nab! Over here!" he shouted.

Nab came running.

"What a beautiful animal!" said Nab. "But how are we going to catch it?"

"Nothing could be easier, Nab," answered Harbert. "We'll turn this turtle over on its back, and then it won't be able to get away. Take your spear and do what I do."

Sensing danger, the reptile had drawn itself in between its upper shell and its under-shell. Neither its head nor its legs could be seen any longer, and it lay as still as a rock.

Harbert and Nab then worked their poles in under the animal's breastbone, and by heaving together they managed, not without trouble, to turn it over on its back. The turtle was three feet long and must have weighed at least four hundred pounds.

"Good!" cried Nab. "That's going to make friend Pencroff happy!"

And indeed friend Pencroff could hardly fail to be happy, because the flesh of these turtles, which feed on eelgrass, is extremely tasty. At that moment all that could still be seen of this one was its head — small and flat, but greatly widened at the back by large hollows at the temples, hidden under an arch of bone.

"And now what are we going to do with our game?" said Nab. "We can't drag it all the way to Granite House!"

"Let's leave it here, since it can't turn itself back over," answered Harbert, "and we'll come back for it with the cart."

"All right."

All the same, to be extra careful, Harbert took the trouble — which Nab thought unnecessary — to wedge the animal in place with big stones. After that the two hunters went back to Granite House along the beach, which the tide, low just then, had laid wide open. Harbert, who wanted to surprise Pencroff, said nothing to him about the "superb specimen of the chelonians" he had left turned over on the sand. But two hours later, when he and Nab came back with the cart to the place where they had left it, the "superb specimen of the chelonians" was no longer there.

Nab and Harbert first looked at each other, then looked all around them. Yet this was certainly the spot where the turtle had been left. The boy even found the stones he had used, so he was sure he was not mistaken.

"Well, how about that!" said Nab. "So these creatures can turn themselves back over?"

"It looks that way," answered Harbert, who could make no sense of it at all and was staring at the stones scattered on the sand.

"Well, Pencroff won't be pleased, that's for sure!"

"And Mr. Smith may have a hard time explaining how it disappeared!" thought Harbert.

"All right," said Nab, who wanted to hide their misadventure, "we just won't mention it."

"On the contrary, Nab, we have to mention it," answered Harbert.

And the two of them took up the cart they had brought for nothing and went back to Granite House.

When he reached the work yard, where the engineer and the sailor were working together, Harbert told them what had happened.

"Ah! You clumsy pair!" cried the sailor. "To let at least fifty bowls of soup get away!"

"But Pencroff," Nab retorted, "it's not our fault the creature ran off, because I'm telling you we'd turned it over!"

"Then you didn't turn it over enough!" the incorrigible sailor shot back with a grin.

"Not enough!" cried Harbert.

And he told how he had taken care to wedge the turtle in place with stones.

"Then it's a miracle!" Pencroff replied.

"I thought, Mr. Cyrus," said Harbert, "that turtles couldn't get back on their feet once they'd been turned on their backs — especially big ones?"

"That's true, my boy," answered Cyrus Smith.

"Then how could it have happened...?"

"How far from the sea had you left the turtle?" asked the engineer, who had stopped his work and was thinking the incident over.

"About fifteen feet, at most," answered Harbert.

"And the tide was low at the time?"

"Yes, Mr. Cyrus."

"Well, then," answered the engineer, "what the turtle couldn't do on the sand, it may have been able to do in the water. It must have turned itself over when the rising tide reached it, and then calmly made its way back out to sea."

"Oh! What clumsy fools we are!" cried Nab.

"That's exactly what I had the honor of telling you!" answered Pencroff.

Cyrus Smith had given this explanation, and it was a reasonable one, no doubt. But was he really convinced that it was the right one? One would not dare to say so.
