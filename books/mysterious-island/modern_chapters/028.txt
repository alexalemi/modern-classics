Chapter 7: A Bridge over the Mercy

_Plans to carry out — A bridge over the Mercy — Making an island of Prospect Plateau — The drawbridge — The wheat harvest — The stream — The little bridges — The poultry yard — The dovecote — The two onaggas — The cart in harness — A trip to Balloon Harbor_

So the colonists of Lincoln Island had won back their home without having to go in by the old spillway, and that spared them a good deal of masonry work. It was lucky, really, that just as they were getting ready to do it, the band of monkeys had been seized by a terror as sudden as it was inexplicable, and that this terror had driven them out of Granite House. Had the animals somehow sensed that a serious attack was about to be made on them by another route? That was about the only way to explain their retreat.

During the last hours of the day the monkeys' bodies were carried into the woods and buried there. Then the colonists set to work repairing the disorder the intruders had left behind. It was disorder, not damage: the monkeys had turned the furniture of the rooms upside down, but at least they had broken nothing. Nab lit his stoves again, and the reserves in the pantry provided a solid meal, which everyone did full justice to.

Jup was not forgotten. He ate stone-pine nuts and rootstocks with a good appetite, and he found himself supplied with plenty of both. Pencroff had untied his arms, but he thought it wise to leave the hobbles on his legs until he could count on the ape having accepted his new life.

Then, before going to bed, Cyrus Smith and his companions sat around the table and talked over several projects that urgently needed doing.

The most important and the most pressing were two: building a bridge over the Mercy, so as to connect the southern part of the island with Granite House, and then setting up a corral to house the mouflons, or any other wool-bearing animals they ought to capture.

As anyone can see, both projects were aimed at the question of clothing, which was then the most serious one they faced. The bridge would make it easy to bring home the balloon's envelope, which would give them linen, and the corral would supply a harvest of wool, which would give them winter clothes.

As for the corral, Cyrus Smith meant to build it at the very sources of Red Creek, where the grazing animals would find pastures with plenty of fresh food. The road between Prospect Plateau and the sources had already been partly cleared, and with a better-built cart than the first one, hauling would be easier — especially if they managed to capture some animal that could pull it.

But while there was no harm in the corral being a long way from Granite House, the same could not be said of the poultry yard, which Nab now brought to the colonists' attention. The birds really did need to be within reach of the head cook, and no spot seemed better suited for the poultry yard than the stretch of the lakeshore next to the old spillway. Water birds would thrive there as well as the others, and the pair of tinamous caught on the last expedition would serve for a first try at taming.

The next day, November 3, the new work began with the building of the bridge, and every pair of hands was called on for this important job. Saws, axes, chisels, and hammers were loaded onto the colonists' shoulders, and, turned into carpenters, they went down to the beach.

There Pencroff had a thought.

"And what if, while we're away, Master Jup takes it into his head to pull up the ladder he was so kind as to send down to us yesterday?"

"Let's fasten down its lower end," Cyrus Smith answered.

This was done with two stakes driven firmly into the sand. Then the colonists went up the left bank of the Mercy and soon came to the bend the river made.

There they stopped, to see whether the bridge ought to be built at this point. The place seemed right.

From here to Balloon Harbor, which they had discovered the day before on the south coast, it was only three and a half miles, and from the bridge to the harbor it would be easy to clear a road a cart could travel, which would make it simple to go back and forth between Granite House and the south of the island.

Cyrus Smith now told his companions about a plan he had been turning over for some time — one that was very simple to carry out and very much worth doing. It was to cut Prospect Plateau off completely, so as to protect it from any attack by four-footed or four-handed creatures. That way Granite House, the Chimneys, the poultry yard, and the whole upper part of the plateau, which was meant for planting, would be safe from the raids of animals.

Nothing could be easier to carry out than this plan, and here is how the engineer meant to go about it.

The plateau was already defended on three sides by water, some of it man-made and some of it natural:

On the northwest, by the shore of Lake Grant, from the corner that rested against the mouth of the old spillway all the way to the cut made in the lake's east bank to let the water out.

On the north, from that cut down to the sea, by the new stream that had dug itself a bed across the plateau and the beach, above and below the waterfall. And to make that stream impassable to animals, all they had to do was dig its bed deeper.

Along the whole eastern edge, by the sea itself, from the mouth of that same stream to the mouth of the Mercy.

And finally on the south, from that river mouth up to the bend of the Mercy where the bridge was to be built.

That left only the western side of the plateau, between the bend of the river and the southern corner of the lake. It was less than a mile long, and it lay open to anything that came along. But nothing was easier than to dig a wide, deep ditch there. The lake would fill it, and the overflow would pour down through a second waterfall into the bed of the Mercy. The level of the lake would no doubt drop a little because of this new outlet for its water, but Cyrus Smith had made sure that Red Creek brought in enough water to allow his plan to go ahead.

"So you see," the engineer added, "Prospect Plateau will be a real island, surrounded by water on every side. It will connect with the rest of our domain only by the bridge we are about to put across the Mercy, by the two little bridges already built above and below the waterfall, and finally by two more little bridges still to be built — one over the ditch I'm proposing we dig, and the other on the left bank of the Mercy. Now, if this bridge and these little bridges can be raised whenever we like, Prospect Plateau will be safe from any surprise."

To make himself clearer, Cyrus Smith had drawn a map of the plateau, and his companions grasped the whole plan at once. They approved it unanimously, and Pencroff, brandishing his carpenter's ax, shouted:

"The bridge first!"

It was the most urgent job. Trees were chosen, felled, stripped of their branches, and sawed up into joists, thick planks, and boards. The bridge was to be fixed in the section that rested on the right bank of the Mercy, and movable in the section that joined the left bank, so that this part could be raised by means of counterweights, the way certain bridges over canal locks are.

As anyone will understand, it was a large piece of work, and although it was skillfully managed, it did take a certain amount of time, because the Mercy was about eighty feet wide. They had to drive piles into the riverbed to hold up the fixed deck of the bridge, and they had to set up a pile driver — a heavy weight hoisted and dropped again and again — to pound on the tops of the piles. These piles would form two arches, so that the bridge could bear heavy loads.

Very fortunately, they lacked nothing: not the tools for working wood, not the ironwork for strengthening it, not the ingenuity of a man who understood this kind of work wonderfully well, and not the zeal of his companions, who after seven months had naturally become very skillful with their hands. And it must be said that Gédéon Spilett was not the clumsiest of them. He competed in skill with the sailor himself, who "would never have expected so much from a mere journalist!"

The building of the bridge over the Mercy took three weeks, and they were very busy weeks. The colonists ate their midday meal right at the work site, and since the weather was magnificent then, they went back to Granite House only for supper.

During this time it was clear that Master Jup was settling in easily and growing used to his new masters, whom he still watched with an extremely curious eye. All the same, as a precaution, Pencroff did not yet give him complete freedom of movement. He wanted to wait, and rightly, until the edges of the plateau had been made impossible to cross by the planned works. Top and Jup got along splendidly and liked to play together, but Jup did everything with great seriousness.

On November 20 the bridge was finished. Its movable section, balanced by counterweights, swung up easily, and only a slight effort was needed to raise it. Between its hinge and the last crossbeam, where it came to rest when it was lowered again, there was a gap of twenty feet — wide enough that no animal could jump across it.

Next came the question of going to fetch the balloon's envelope, which the colonists were eager to put somewhere completely safe. But to carry it, they would have to take a cart all the way to Balloon Harbor, and that meant clearing a road through the dense thickets of the Far West. That would take a certain amount of time. So Nab and Pencroff first went ahead to scout as far as the harbor, and when they found that the "stock of cloth" was not suffering in the least in the cave where it had been stored, it was decided that the work on Prospect Plateau would go on without a break.

"That," Pencroff pointed out, "will let us set up our poultry yard under better conditions, since we won't have to be afraid of visits from foxes or attacks from any other pests."

"Not to mention," Nab added, "that we'll be able to clear the plateau, and plant wild plants there..."

"And get our second wheat field ready!" the sailor cried triumphantly.

The fact was that the first wheat field, sown with a single grain, had done wonderfully well, thanks to Pencroff's care. It had produced the ten ears the engineer had predicted, and since each ear held eighty grains, the colony now found itself the owner of eight hundred grains — in six months — which promised two harvests every year.

These eight hundred grains, less about fifty kept back to be safe, were therefore to be sown in a new field, and with no less care than the single grain had been.

The field was prepared and then surrounded by a strong palisade, tall and sharp-pointed, which four-footed animals would have had a very hard time getting over. As for the birds, they were kept away by screeching whirligigs and frightful scarecrows, products of Pencroff's fanciful imagination. The seven hundred and fifty grains were then laid in small, very regular furrows, and nature had to do the rest.

On November 21 Cyrus Smith began laying out the ditch that was to close off the plateau on the west, from the southern corner of Lake Grant to the bend of the Mercy. There were two or three feet of topsoil there, and granite underneath. So they had to make nitroglycerin all over again, and the nitroglycerin did its usual work. In less than two weeks a ditch twelve feet wide and six deep had been dug in the hard ground of the plateau. By the same means a new cut was made in the rocky rim of the lake, and the water rushed into this new bed, forming a little stream that was given the name "Glycerin Creek." It became a tributary of the Mercy. Just as the engineer had said, the level of the lake dropped, but by an amount you could barely see. Finally, to complete the enclosure, the bed of the stream on the beach was made much wider, and the sand was held back by a double palisade.

By the end of the first half of December this work was completely finished. Prospect Plateau — an irregular sort of pentagon about four miles around, with a belt of water all the way round it — was now entirely safe from any attack.

During that month of December the heat was intense. Even so, the colonists would not stop carrying out their plans, and since setting up the poultry yard had become urgent, they went ahead and set it up.

It goes without saying that once the plateau was completely closed off, Master Jup had been set free. He never left his masters now and showed no wish at all to escape. He was a gentle animal, yet very strong, and astonishingly agile. Ah! When it came to climbing the ladder of Granite House, nobody could have competed with him. He was already being put to some kinds of work: he hauled loads of wood and carted away the stones that had been dug out of the bed of Glycerin Creek.

"He's not a mason yet, but he's already a 'monkey'!" Harbert said jokingly. He was referring to the nickname "monkey" that French masons give their apprentices. And if ever a name was deserved, it was this one!

The poultry yard took up an area of two hundred square yards, chosen on the southeast shore of the lake. It was surrounded by a palisade, and various shelters were built for the animals that were to live in it. These were huts of branches, divided into compartments, and they were soon simply waiting for their guests.

The first were the pair of tinamous, which before long produced a large brood of young. They were joined by half a dozen ducks that lived along the shores of the lake. Some of these belonged to that Chinese species whose wings open like a fan, and whose brilliant, vivid plumage rivals that of the golden pheasant. A few days later Harbert caught a pair of gallinaceous birds — game birds of the chicken family — with rounded tails made of long feathers: magnificent "alectors," which soon became tame. As for the pelicans, the kingfishers, and the moorhens, they came of their own accord to the shore by the poultry yard. And this whole little world, after a few quarrels, cooing, squawking, and clucking, ended up getting along, and it multiplied at a rate that was reassuring for the colony's future food supply.

Cyrus Smith, wanting to round out the work, also built a dovecote in one corner of the poultry yard. In it they housed a dozen of the pigeons that haunted the high rocks of the plateau. These birds easily got into the habit of coming back to their new home every evening, and they showed more of a tendency to be domesticated than their relatives the wood pigeons, which in any case breed only in the wild.

At last the time had come to use the balloon's envelope for making linen. As for keeping it in its present form and risking a hot-air balloon to leave the island, over a sea that was practically without limits — that would have been thinkable only for people who lacked everything, and Cyrus Smith, a practical mind, could not consider it.

So the job was to bring the envelope back to Granite House, and the colonists set about making their heavy cart lighter and easier to handle. But while they did not lack a vehicle, they still had to find something to pull it! Wasn't there some native grazing animal on the island that could take the place of a horse, a donkey, an ox, or a cow? That was the question.

"Honestly," Pencroff said, "a draft animal would be very useful to us, until Mr. Cyrus is good enough to build us a steam wagon, or even a locomotive — because one day, for sure, we'll have a railroad from Granite House to Balloon Harbor, with a branch line up Mount Franklin!"

And the honest sailor believed what he was saying! Oh, imagination, when faith gets mixed up in it!

But, not to exaggerate, a simple four-footed animal that could be put in harness would have suited Pencroff perfectly well. And since Providence had a soft spot for him, it did not keep him waiting long.

One day, December 23, they heard Nab shouting and Top barking, each trying to outdo the other. The colonists, who were busy at the Chimneys, came running at once, afraid that something unpleasant had happened.

What did they see? Two handsome, good-sized animals, which had wandered carelessly onto the plateau, since the little bridges had not been closed. They looked like two horses, or at least two donkeys, a male and a female. They were finely built, with pale yellowish-gray coats, white legs and tails, and black stripes across the head, neck, and body. They were walking calmly forward without showing the least alarm, and they looked with bright eyes at these men, whom they could not yet recognize as their masters.

"They're onaggas!" Harbert cried. "Animals halfway between the zebra and the quagga!"

"Why not donkeys?" asked Nab.

"Because they don't have long ears, and their shape is more graceful!"

"Donkeys or horses," Pencroff shot back, "they're 'engines,' as Mr. Smith would say, and as such they're worth catching!"

Without frightening the two animals, the sailor slipped through the grass to the little bridge over Glycerin Creek and swung it up, and the onaggas were prisoners.

Now, should they be seized by force and made to submit to domestication? No. It was decided that for a few days they would be left to come and go freely on the plateau, where there was plenty of grass, and the engineer immediately had a stable built near the poultry yard, where the onaggas would find good bedding and shelter for the night.

So this magnificent pair was left entirely free to move about, and the colonists even avoided frightening them by coming too close. Several times, though, the onaggas seemed to feel a need to leave this plateau, which was too cramped for animals used to wide-open spaces and deep forests. Then they could be seen following the belt of water that stood in their way as a barrier they could not cross, letting out a few shrill brays, then galloping off through the grass. And when they had calmed down, they would stand for hours on end gazing at those great woods that were closed to them forever!

Meanwhile, harness and traces had been made out of plant fibers, and a few days after the onaggas were captured, not only was the cart ready to be hitched up, but a straight road — or rather a cutting — had been made through the Far West forest from the bend of the Mercy to Balloon Harbor. So the cart could now be driven there, and it was toward the end of December that they tried out the onaggas for the first time.

Pencroff had already coaxed the animals enough that they would come and eat out of his hand, and they let people come near them without any trouble. But once they were in harness they reared, and it was very hard to hold them. All the same, they would soon get used to this new work, because the onagga, less rebellious than the zebra, is often put to harness in the mountainous parts of southern Africa, and it has even been acclimatized in Europe, in relatively cold regions.

That day the whole colony, except Pencroff, who walked at the head of his animals, climbed into the cart and took the road to Balloon Harbor. It goes without saying that they were jolted about on that barely roughed-out road. But the cart arrived without mishap, and that same day they were able to load the envelope and the balloon's various riggings onto it.

At eight o'clock in the evening the cart, having crossed back over the bridge over the Mercy, came down the left bank of the river and stopped on the beach. The onaggas were unhitched and led back to their stable, and Pencroff, before falling asleep, let out a sigh of satisfaction so loud that it echoed through Granite House.
