Chapter 4: Salvage

_The colonists on the beach — Ayrton and Pencroff at the salvage work — Talk over lunch — Pencroff's reasoning — A careful inspection of the brig's hull — The powder magazine intact — The new riches — The last of the wreckage — A piece of a broken cylinder_

"They've blown up!" cried Harbert.

"Yes! Blown up as if Ayrton had set fire to the powder!" answered Pencroff, throwing himself into the elevator along with Nab and the boy.

"But what happened?" asked Gédéon Spilett, still stunned by this unexpected ending.

"Ah! This time we'll find out!..." the engineer answered quickly.

"Find out what?..."

"Later! Later! Come on, Spilett. What matters is that those pirates have been wiped out!"

And Cyrus Smith, drawing the reporter and Ayrton along with him, joined Pencroff, Nab and Harbert on the beach.

Nothing could be seen of the brig anymore, not even her masts. After being lifted up by that waterspout, she had fallen over on her side and sunk in that position, no doubt because some enormous leak had opened in her. But since the channel at that point was no more than twenty feet deep, it was certain that the sides of the sunken brig would show again at low tide.

A few pieces of wreckage were floating on the surface of the sea. There was a whole raft of spare masts and yards, chicken coops with their birds still alive, and crates and barrels that came bobbing up to the surface little by little after escaping through the hatches. But there was no debris drifting about — no planks from the deck, no planking from the hull — and that made the sudden sinking of the Speedy rather hard to explain.

Meanwhile the two masts, which had snapped a few feet above the deck where they passed through it, breaking their stays and shrouds as they went, soon rose to the surface of the channel with their sails, some spread and some furled. But they could not give the ebb time to carry off all these riches, and Ayrton and Pencroff jumped into the canoe, meaning to tie up all the floating wreckage either to the shore of the island or to the shore of the islet.

But just as they were about to push off, a thought from Gédéon Spilett stopped them.

"And the six convicts who landed on the right bank of the Mercy?" he said.

He was right. They must not forget that the six men whose boat had been smashed on the rocks had come ashore at Jetsam Point.

Everyone looked in that direction. None of the fugitives was in sight. Most likely, after watching the brig go down in the waters of the channel, they had fled into the interior of the island.

"We'll deal with them later," Cyrus Smith said then. "They may still be dangerous, because they're armed, but after all, it's six against six, so the odds are even. Let's see to the most urgent thing first."

Ayrton and Pencroff got into the canoe and rowed hard toward the wreckage.

The sea was at the slack just then, and very high, because the moon had been new for two days. So a good hour at least would have to pass before the hull of the brig came up out of the waters of the channel.

Ayrton and Pencroff had time to make fast the masts and spars with ropes, whose ends were carried to the beach below Granite House. There the colonists, pulling all together, managed to haul the wreckage ashore. Then the canoe picked up everything that was floating — chicken coops, barrels, crates — and it was all carried straight to the Chimneys.

A few bodies were floating too. Among them Ayrton recognized that of Bob Harvey, and he pointed it out to his companion, saying in a voice full of feeling:

"That's what I was, Pencroff!"

"But it's what you aren't anymore, my brave Ayrton!" answered the sailor.

It was rather strange that so few bodies were floating. They counted barely five or six, and the ebb was already starting to carry them out toward the open sea. Very probably the convicts, caught by the sudden sinking, had had no time to get away, and since the ship had gone over on her side, most of them had been trapped under the bulwarks. And so the outgoing tide, which was about to sweep the bodies of these wretched men out to sea, would spare the colonists the grim task of burying them in some corner of their island.

For two hours Cyrus Smith and his companions did nothing but haul the spars up onto the sand, unbend the sails from the yards, and spread them out to dry. The sails were perfectly undamaged. The colonists talked very little, so completely did the work absorb them — but what thoughts went through their minds! Owning this brig, or rather everything she held, was a fortune. A ship is like a small, complete world, and the colony's stock of equipment was about to grow by a great many useful things. It would be the chest found at Jetsam Point all over again, "on a grand scale."

"And besides," Pencroff was thinking, "why couldn't this brig be refloated? If she's got only a leak, well, a leak can be plugged, and a ship of three to four hundred tons — now that's a real ship next to our Bonadventure! You can go a long way in a ship like that! You can go wherever you like! Mr. Cyrus, Ayrton and I will have to look into it. It's worth the trouble!"

And indeed, if the brig was still fit to sail, the colonists' chances of getting home from Lincoln Island would be wonderfully improved. But to settle this important question they would have to wait until the tide was all the way out, so that every part of the brig's hull could be examined.

Once the wreckage was safe on the beach, Cyrus Smith and his companions allowed themselves a few moments for lunch. They were literally dying of hunger. Luckily the pantry was not far away, and Nab could pass for a ship's cook who got things on the table fast. So they ate beside the Chimneys, and during the meal, as you can well imagine, the only subject was the unexpected event that had saved the colony so miraculously.

"Miraculously is the word," Pencroff kept repeating, "because you have to admit those scoundrels blew up at exactly the right moment! Granite House was getting remarkably hard to live in!"

"And can you picture how it happened, Pencroff," asked the reporter, "and what could have set off that explosion on the brig?"

"Eh! Mr. Spilett, nothing simpler," answered Pencroff. "A pirate ship isn't run like a warship! Convicts aren't sailors! The brig's powder rooms must have been open, since they were firing at us nonstop, and it would only take one careless or clumsy fellow to blow the whole contraption sky-high!"

"Mr. Cyrus," said Harbert, "what surprises me is that the explosion didn't do more damage. The bang wasn't very loud, and when you come down to it, there's not much debris and not many planks torn off. It looks as if the ship sank rather than blew up."

"That surprises you, my boy?" asked the engineer.

"Yes, Mr. Cyrus."

"It surprises me too, Harbert," answered the engineer. "But when we inspect the brig's hull, we'll no doubt find the explanation."

"Now look here, Mr. Cyrus," said Pencroff, "you're not going to claim the Speedy simply sank like a ship that's run onto a reef?"

"Why not," Nab put in, "if there are rocks in the channel?"

"Come on, Nab," answered Pencroff. "You didn't have your eyes open at the right moment. An instant before she went down, the brig — I saw it perfectly well — rose up on an enormous wave and fell back, heeling over to port. Now if she'd only struck, she'd have sunk nice and quietly, like an honest ship going to the bottom."

"That's just it — she wasn't an honest ship!" answered Nab.

"Well, we'll see, Pencroff," said the engineer.

"We'll see," the sailor added, "but I'd bet my head there aren't any rocks in the channel. Come on, Mr. Cyrus, honestly — are you trying to tell me there's something marvelous about this business too?"

Cyrus Smith did not answer.

"In any case," said Gédéon Spilett, "collision or explosion, you'll grant, Pencroff, that it happened just in time!"

"Yes!... Yes!..." answered the sailor. "But that's not the question. I'm asking Mr. Smith if he sees anything supernatural in all this."

"I'm not saying either way, Pencroff," said the engineer. "That's all the answer I can give you."

That answer did not satisfy Pencroff in the least. He held to "an explosion," and nothing would shake him off it. He would never agree that there was some unknown reef in that channel, whose bed was fine sand like the beach itself, and which he had often crossed at low tide. Besides, at the moment the brig went down, the tide was high — which meant she had more than enough water under her to pass over, without touching, any rocks that did not show at low tide. Therefore there could not have been a collision. Therefore the ship had not struck. Therefore she had blown up.

And it must be admitted that the sailor's reasoning was not without a certain logic.

Toward half past one the colonists got into the canoe and went out to where the brig lay stranded. It was a pity that the brig's two boats could not have been saved. But one of them, as we know, had been smashed at the mouth of the Mercy and was completely useless, and the other had vanished when the brig went down and, no doubt crushed beneath her, had never come up again.

By now the hull of the Speedy was beginning to show above the water. The brig was lying worse than on her side. After snapping her masts under the weight of her ballast, which had shifted when she fell, she lay almost keel up. She had been truly turned over by that inexplicable but terrifying underwater force, which had at the same time shown itself by throwing up an enormous waterspout.

The colonists went all the way around the hull, and as the sea went down they were able to make out, if not the cause of the disaster, at least its effects.

In the bow, on both sides of the keel, seven or eight feet aft of the point where the stem begins, the brig's sides were horribly torn open along a length of at least twenty feet. There gaped two great holes that it would have been impossible to plug. Not only had the copper sheathing and the planking vanished — ground to powder, no doubt — but of the frames themselves, and of the iron bolts and wooden pegs that held them together, not a trace was left. All along the hull, right back to where it narrowed toward the stern, the strakes — the long rows of planking — were shredded and no longer held. The false keel had been torn away with an inexplicable violence, and the keel itself, ripped from the keelson — the inner timber that runs along on top of it — in several places, was broken along its whole length.

"A thousand devils!" cried Pencroff. "There's a ship that's going to be hard to refloat!"

"It will be impossible," said Ayrton.

"In any case," Gédéon Spilett pointed out to the sailor, "the explosion, if there was an explosion, has had some peculiar effects here! It has burst open the bottom of the ship's hull, instead of blowing off the deck and the upper works! These big holes look much more as if they'd been made by striking a reef than by a magazine blowing up!"

"There's no reef in the channel!" the sailor shot back. "I'll admit anything you like, except hitting a rock!"

"Let's try to get inside the brig," said the engineer. "Maybe we'll find out what to think about the cause of her destruction."

That was the best course to take, and besides, they needed to make an inventory of all the riches on board and arrange for getting everything out.

Getting inside the brig was easy now. The water was still dropping, and the underside of the deck — which, with the hull turned over, had now become the top — could be walked on. The ballast, made of heavy pigs of cast iron, had smashed through it in several places. They could hear the sea gurgling as it drained out through the cracks in the hull.

Cyrus Smith and his companions, axes in hand, made their way forward along the half-wrecked deck. Crates of every kind cluttered it, and since they had been in the water for only a very short time, perhaps their contents were not spoiled.

So they set about getting this whole cargo to a safe place. The tide would not come back for several hours, and those few hours were put to the most profitable use. Ayrton and Pencroff had rigged a block and tackle at the opening in the hull, and with it they hoisted out the barrels and crates. The canoe took them aboard and carried them straight to the beach. They took everything, without picking and choosing, leaving it until later to sort all these things out.

In any case, what the colonists could see at once, to their enormous satisfaction, was that the brig carried a very varied cargo — an assortment of goods of every sort: utensils, manufactured articles, tools, the kind of thing loaded by ships that trade from island to island across Polynesia. Most likely they would find a little of everything here, and you will agree that this was exactly what the colony on Lincoln Island needed.

All the same — and Cyrus Smith noticed it in silent astonishment — not only had the brig's hull, as has been said, suffered enormously from whatever blow had caused the disaster, but the inside of the ship was wrecked as well, especially toward the bow. Bulkheads and stanchions were shattered, as though some tremendous shell had burst inside the brig. The colonists could easily go from bow to stern once they had moved the crates, which were carried out one after another. These were not heavy bales, which would have been hard to shift, but ordinary packages, and in any case there was no telling anymore how they had been stowed.

The colonists then made their way to the stern of the brig, to the part that the poop deck used to cover. It was there, according to Ayrton, that they should look for the powder magazine. Since Cyrus Smith believed it had not exploded, it was possible that a few kegs could be saved, and that the powder, which is usually packed in metal casings, had not been harmed by the water.

And that, in fact, was what had happened. In the midst of a great quantity of shot and shells, they found about twenty kegs lined with copper, and these were carefully brought out. Pencroff saw with his own eyes that the destruction of the Speedy could not be put down to an explosion. The part of the hull where the magazine was located was exactly the part that had suffered least.

"Could be!" answered the stubborn sailor. "But as for a rock — there isn't any rock in the channel!"

"Then what happened?" asked Harbert.

"I have no idea," answered Pencroff. "Mr. Cyrus has no idea, and nobody has any idea, and nobody ever will!"

During all this searching, several hours had gone by, and the flood tide was starting to make itself felt. The salvage work had to be stopped. There was no fear, though, that the sea would carry the brig's carcass away. It was already sunk into the sand and held as firmly as if it had been moored with two anchors.

So they could safely wait for the next ebb to start work again. But as for the ship herself, she was certainly doomed, and they would even have to hurry to save the pieces of her hull, because before long it would disappear into the shifting sands of the channel.

It was five o'clock in the evening. The day had been a hard one for the workers. They ate with a hearty appetite, and however tired they were, after supper they could not resist the urge to look into the crates that made up the Speedy's cargo.

Most of them held ready-made clothes, which, as you can imagine, were very welcome. There was enough there to dress a whole colony — linen for every purpose, shoes for every foot.

"Now we're too rich!" Pencroff exclaimed. "But what are we going to do with all this?"

And every moment the jolly sailor burst out with another hurrah as he recognized kegs of tafia — a cheap rum made from sugarcane — hogsheads of tobacco, firearms and blades, bales of cotton, farming implements, tools for carpenters, joiners and blacksmiths, and boxes of seeds of every kind, which their short stay in the water had not harmed. Ah, two years earlier, how welcome all these things would have been! But even now, when these hardworking colonists had made their own tools, all these riches would find a use.

There was no shortage of room in the storerooms of Granite House, but that day there was no time to put everything away. They could not forget, though, that six survivors from the crew of the Speedy had set foot on the island, that they were very likely scoundrels of the first rank, and that the colonists would have to be on their guard against them. Even though the bridge over the Mercy and the little bridges had been raised, convicts like these were not the sort to let a river or a stream stop them, and driven by despair, such ruffians could be formidable.

They would see later what was best to do about them. Meanwhile, though, someone had to keep watch over the crates and packages piled up near the Chimneys, and the colonists took turns at it all night.

The night passed, however, without the convicts attempting any attack. Master Jup and Top, on guard at the foot of Granite House, would have given the alarm quickly enough.

The next three days, October 19, 20 and 21, were spent saving everything that might be of any value or any use at all, whether from the cargo or from the brig's rigging. At low tide they emptied the hold. At high tide they stored away what they had saved. A large part of the copper sheathing was stripped off the hull, which sank deeper into the sand every day. And before the sands could swallow up the heavy objects that had gone to the bottom, Ayrton and Pencroff dove down to the bed of the channel several times and found the brig's chains and anchors, the iron pigs of her ballast, and even her four cannons. They buoyed the cannons up with empty casks, and so managed to bring them ashore.

So you can see that the colony's arsenal had gained from the salvage no less than the pantries and storerooms of Granite House. Pencroff, always full of enthusiasm for his plans, was already talking about building a battery to command the channel and the mouth of the river. With four cannons, he promised, he would stop any fleet, "however powerful," from venturing into the waters of Lincoln Island!

In the meantime, when nothing was left of the brig but a useless shell, bad weather came along and finished destroying it. Cyrus Smith had meant to blow it up so as to collect the pieces along the shore, but a strong northeast wind and a heavy sea let him save his powder.

In fact, during the night of the 23rd to the 24th, the hull of the brig was completely broken up, and part of the wreckage washed up on the beach.

As for the ship's papers, it goes without saying that Cyrus Smith found no trace of them, even though he had carefully searched the cupboards in the poop. The pirates had obviously destroyed everything that had to do with either the captain or the owner of the Speedy, and since the name of her home port was not painted on her stern, there was nothing to suggest what country she came from. However, from certain lines of her bow, Ayrton and Pencroff seemed to think the brig must have been English-built.

A week after the disaster — or rather after the happy but inexplicable ending to which the colony owed its rescue — nothing more could be seen of the ship, even at low tide. Her remains had been scattered, and Granite House was rich with almost everything she had carried.

All the same, the mystery that hid her strange destruction would no doubt never have been cleared up if, on November 30, Nab, wandering along the beach, had not found a piece of a thick iron cylinder that showed traces of an explosion. The cylinder was twisted and torn along its edges, as if it had been subjected to the force of some explosive substance.

Nab brought this piece of metal to his master, who was busy just then with his companions in the workshop at the Chimneys.

Cyrus Smith examined the cylinder carefully. Then, turning to Pencroff:

"My friend," he said, "do you still insist that the Speedy didn't go down because she struck something?"

"Yes, Mr. Cyrus," answered the sailor. "You know as well as I do there aren't any rocks in the channel."

"But what if she had struck this piece of iron?" said the engineer, holding up the broken cylinder.

"What, this bit of pipe?" cried Pencroff, in a tone of complete disbelief.

"My friends," Cyrus Smith went on, "do you remember that before she sank, the brig rose up on the top of a regular waterspout?"

"Yes, Mr. Cyrus!" answered Harbert.

"Well, would you like to know what threw up that waterspout? This did," said the engineer, holding up the broken tube.

"This?" Pencroff shot back.

"Yes! This cylinder is all that's left of a torpedo — a mine, that is, laid under the water!"

"A torpedo!" cried the engineer's companions.

"And who put it there, this torpedo?" asked Pencroff, who would not give in.

"All I can tell you is that it wasn't me!" answered Cyrus Smith. "But it was there, and you've seen for yourselves how incomparably powerful it was!"
