Chapter 10: A Whale in Sight

_Building the boat — The second wheat harvest — Hunting koulas — A new plant, more pleasant than useful — A whale in sight — The harpoon from the Vineyard — Cutting up the whale — What the whalebone was for — The end of May — Pencroff has nothing left to wish for_

When Pencroff got an idea into his head, he gave himself no rest, and gave nobody else any, until it had been carried out. Now, he wanted to visit Tabor Island, and since a boat of some size was needed for the crossing, that boat had to be built.

Here is the plan the engineer settled on, in agreement with the sailor.

The boat would measure thirty-five feet along the keel and nine feet in the beam, which would make her a fast sailer if her bottom and her waterlines came out well. She should draw no more than six feet, enough depth in the water to keep her from drifting sideways off her course. She would be decked over her whole length, with two hatches opening into two cabins separated by a bulkhead. She would be rigged as a sloop, with a mainsail, a staysail, a square running sail, a topsail and a jib: a set of sails very easy to handle, quick to lower when a squall struck, and very good for sailing close to the wind. Finally, her hull would be carvel-built — that is, the planks would lie flush, edge to edge, instead of overlapping one another — and as for her frames, they would be bent into place hot, after the planking had been fitted over temporary molds.

What wood would they build her of? Elm or fir, both plentiful on the island? They decided on fir. It is a wood a little "splitty," as carpenters say, but it is easy to work, and it stands being soaked in water as well as elm does.

Once these details were settled, it was agreed that, since the fine season would not come back for another six months, Cyrus Smith and Pencroff would work on the boat by themselves. Gédéon Spilett and Harbert were to go on hunting, and neither Nab nor Master Jup, his assistant, would give up the household work that had fallen to them.

As soon as the trees were chosen, they were felled, cut into lengths and sawn into planks, the way pit sawyers would have done it. A week later a shipyard had been laid out in the recess between the Chimneys and the cliff, and a keel thirty-five feet long, with a sternpost at the after end and a stem at the forward end, lay stretched out on the sand.

Cyrus Smith had not gone blindly into this new job. He knew shipbuilding as he knew almost everything, and he had first worked out the shape of his boat on paper. Besides, he was well served by Pencroff, who had worked several years in a Brooklyn shipyard and knew the practical side of the trade. So it was only after strict calculation and careful thought that the temporary molds were set up on the keel.

Pencroff, as anyone will readily believe, was all on fire to see his new undertaking through, and he would not have left it for a moment.

Only one task had the privilege of tearing him away from his shipyard, and then for a single day. That was the second wheat harvest, which took place on April 15. It had done as well as the first and gave the yield of grain that had been predicted.

"Five bushels, Mr. Cyrus!" said Pencroff, after measuring his riches with the greatest care.

"Five bushels," answered the engineer, "and at a hundred and thirty thousand grains a bushel, that makes six hundred and fifty thousand grains."

"Well! This time we'll sow the lot," said the sailor, "except for a little reserve, all the same!"

"Yes, Pencroff, and if the next harvest gives a proportional yield, we'll have four thousand bushels."

"And we'll eat bread?"

"We'll eat bread."

"But we'll have to make a mill?"

"We'll make a mill."

So the third wheat field was incomparably larger than the first two, and the soil, prepared with the utmost care, received the precious seed. That done, Pencroff went back to his work.

Meanwhile Gédéon Spilett and Harbert went hunting in the surrounding country, and they ventured quite deep into the still unknown parts of the Far West, their guns loaded with ball, ready for any unpleasant encounter. It was a tangled mass of magnificent trees crowded against one another as though they were short of room. Exploring these great stands of timber was extremely difficult, and the reporter never risked it without taking the pocket compass, because the sun barely got through the thick branches and it would have been hard to find the way back. Game was naturally scarcer in such places, where it would not have had enough room to move freely. Still, three big plant-eaters were killed during the last two weeks of April. They were koulas, one of which the colonists had already seen north of the lake, and they let themselves be killed stupidly among the thick branches of the trees where they had taken refuge. Their skins were brought back to Granite House, and with the help of sulfuric acid they were put through a kind of tanning that made them usable.

On one of these excursions they also made a discovery, precious from another point of view altogether, and this one they owed to Gédéon Spilett.

It was April 30. The two hunters had pushed deep into the southwest of the Far West when the reporter, some fifty paces ahead of Harbert, came into a sort of clearing where the trees stood farther apart and let in a few rays of sun.

The first thing that struck Gédéon Spilett was the smell given off by certain plants with straight, round, branching stems, which bore their flowers in clusters and had very tiny seeds. The reporter pulled up one or two of these stems and went back to the boy.

"Look at this, Harbert. What is it?" he said.

"And where did you find this plant, Mr. Spilett?"

"Over there, in a clearing, where it grows in great quantities."

"Well, Mr. Spilett," said Harbert, "this is a find that gives you every right to Pencroff's gratitude!"

"So it's tobacco?"

"Yes, and if it isn't the finest quality, it's tobacco all the same!"

"Ah! Good old Pencroff! Won't he be happy! But he won't smoke the whole lot, hang it! He'll leave us our share!"

"Oh! I've got an idea, Mr. Spilett," answered Harbert. "Let's not say anything to Pencroff. We'll take the time to cure these leaves, and then one fine day we'll hand him a pipe all filled and ready!"

"Agreed, Harbert, and on that day our worthy companion won't have anything left in this world to wish for!"

The reporter and the boy gathered a good supply of the precious plant and went back to Granite House, where they smuggled it in "as contraband," with as many precautions as if Pencroff had been the strictest of customs officers.

Cyrus Smith and Nab were let in on the secret, and the sailor suspected nothing during the whole of the rather long time it took to dry the thin leaves, chop them, and roast them slightly on hot stones. That took two months; but all this handling could be done without Pencroff's knowing, because, busy as he was building the boat, he came up to Granite House only at rest time.

Once again, however, like it or not, his favorite work was interrupted on May 1 by a fishing adventure in which all the colonists had to take part.

For several days they had been able to watch an enormous animal swimming in the waters of Lincoln Island, two or three miles offshore. It was a whale of the largest size, which in all likelihood belonged to the southern species known as the "Cape whale."

"What a piece of luck it would be to catch her!" cried the sailor. "Ah! If we had a proper boat and a harpoon in good shape, I'd be saying, 'After that beast — she's worth the taking!'"

"Well, Pencroff," said Gédéon Spilett, "I'd have liked to see you handle a harpoon. It must be something to watch!"

"Very much something to watch, and not without danger," said the engineer. "But since we haven't the means to attack the animal, there's no point thinking about her."

"I'm surprised," said the reporter, "to see a whale in such a comparatively high latitude."

"Why's that, Mr. Spilett?" answered Harbert. "We're right in the part of the Pacific that English and American whalers call the 'Whale Field,' and it's here, between New Zealand and South America, that the whales of the southern hemisphere are found in the greatest numbers."

"Nothing truer," answered Pencroff, "and what surprises me is that we haven't seen more of them. After all, since we can't get near them, what does it matter!"

And Pencroff went back to his work, not without a sigh of regret. For in every sailor there is a fisherman, and if the pleasure of fishing goes up in direct proportion to the size of the fish, you can judge what a whaler feels in the presence of a whale!

And if it had only been the pleasure! But there was no hiding the fact that such a prize would have been very profitable to the colony, for the oil, the fat and the whalebone could all be put to a great many uses!

Now, it so happened that the whale they had sighted seemed unwilling to leave the waters around the island. So, from the windows of Granite House or from Prospect Plateau, Harbert and Gédéon Spilett when they were not out hunting, and Nab while he kept an eye on his stoves, never let go of the spyglass, and they followed every movement the animal made. The whale had come far into the wide expanse of Union Bay and was furrowing it at speed from Mandible Cape to Claw Cape, driven by her prodigiously powerful tail fin, which she bore down on to push herself forward in great surges, at a speed that sometimes reached twelve miles an hour. Sometimes, too, she came so close to the islet that they could make her out completely. It was indeed the southern right whale, which is entirely black, and whose head is flatter than that of the northern whales.

They could also see her shoot a cloud of vapor high into the air from her blowholes... or of water. For, odd as it may seem, naturalists and whalers still do not agree on the matter. Is it air or is it water that is forced out like that? The general view is that it is vapor, which condenses suddenly on meeting the cold air and falls back as rain.

All the same, the presence of this sea mammal was on the colonists' minds. It irritated Pencroff most of all and kept distracting him from his work. He ended up wanting that whale the way a child wants something it has been forbidden. At night he dreamed of her out loud, and certainly, if he had had any means of attacking her, if the boat had been fit to put to sea, he would not have hesitated to go after her.

But what the colonists could not do, chance did for them. On May 3, shouts from Nab, posted at his kitchen window, announced that the whale had run aground on the island's shore.

Harbert and Gédéon Spilett, who were just setting off to hunt, dropped their guns; Pencroff threw down his ax; Cyrus Smith and Nab joined their companions; and they all hurried toward the place where she had stranded.

She had run aground at high tide on the beach of Jetsam Point, three miles from Granite House. So it was likely that the whale would not be able to work herself free easily. In any case they had to hurry, so as to cut off her retreat if need be. They ran with picks and iron-tipped spears, crossed the bridge over the Mercy, went back down the right bank of the river, took the beach, and in less than twenty minutes the colonists stood beside the enormous animal, above which a whole world of birds was already swarming.

"What a monster!" cried Nab.

And the word was right, for it was a southern right whale eighty feet long, a giant of her kind, which could not have weighed less than a hundred and fifty thousand pounds!

Yet the monster, stranded as she was, did not stir, and did not thrash about trying to float herself off while the tide was still high.

The colonists soon had the explanation of why she lay so still, when at low tide they walked all the way around the animal.

She was dead, and a harpoon was sticking out of her left side.

"So there are whalers in our waters?" said Gédéon Spilett at once.

"Why do you say that?" asked the sailor.

"Because that harpoon is still there..."

"Eh, Mr. Spilett, that proves nothing," answered Pencroff. "Whales have been known to travel thousands of miles with a harpoon in their side, and if this one had been struck in the North Atlantic and come to die in the South Pacific, there'd be nothing surprising in it!"

"All the same..." said Gédéon Spilett, who was not satisfied by Pencroff's assurance.

"It's perfectly possible," answered Cyrus Smith, "but let's look at this harpoon. Perhaps, following a fairly common custom, the whalers engraved the name of their ship on it?"

And indeed, when Pencroff had pulled out the harpoon that was stuck in the animal's side, he read this inscription on it:

    Maria-Stella
    Vineyard.

"A ship from the Vineyard! A ship from my part of the world!" he cried. "The Maria-Stella! A fine whaler, upon my word! And one I know well! Ah, my friends, a ship from the Vineyard, a whaler from the Vineyard!"

And the sailor, brandishing the harpoon, kept repeating, not without emotion, that name so dear to his heart, the name of his native place!

But since they could hardly wait for the Maria-Stella to come and claim the animal she had harpooned, they decided to cut the whale up before she began to decompose. The birds of prey, which had been spying on this rich prize for several days, meant to take possession without further delay, and they had to be driven off with gunfire.

The whale was a female, and her udders yielded a large quantity of milk which, in the opinion of the naturalist Dieffenbach, could pass for cow's milk — and indeed it differs from it neither in taste, nor in color, nor in density.

Pencroff had once served aboard a whaling ship, and he was able to direct the cutting-up methodically. It was a rather unpleasant job, and it lasted three days, but not one of the colonists flinched from it — not even Gédéon Spilett, who, according to the sailor, would end up making "a very good castaway."

The blubber was cut into parallel slices two and a half feet thick, then divided into pieces that might weigh a thousand pounds each, and melted down in big earthenware vessels brought to the spot where the whale was being cut up — for they did not want to make the approaches to Prospect Plateau reek. In the melting it lost about a third of its weight. But there was blubber to spare: the tongue alone gave six thousand pounds of oil, and the lower lip four thousand. And besides this fat, which would keep them supplied with stearin and glycerin for a long time, there was the whalebone, which no doubt would find its uses too, even though nobody at Granite House carried an umbrella or wore a corset. The upper part of the whale's mouth was furnished, on both sides, with eight hundred horny plates, very springy, fibrous in texture, and fringed at their edges like two great combs whose teeth, six feet long, serve to strain out the thousands of tiny creatures, small fish and mollusks the whale lives on.

The job finished, to the great satisfaction of those who had done it, what was left of the animal was abandoned to the birds, which would make every last trace of it disappear, and the daily work at Granite House was taken up again.

Before going back to the shipyard, however, Cyrus Smith had the idea of making certain devices that greatly excited his companions' curiosity. He took a dozen strips of whalebone, cut each one into six equal pieces, and sharpened them at the ends.

"And those, Mr. Cyrus," asked Harbert, when the job was done, "what will they be good for...?"

"For killing wolves, foxes, and even jaguars," answered the engineer.

"Now?"

"No, this winter, when we have ice at our disposal."

"I don't understand..." answered Harbert.

"You will, my boy," answered the engineer. "This device isn't my invention. The Aleut hunters of Russian America use it a great deal. You see these strips of whalebone, my friends? Well, when it freezes, I'll bend them double and keep pouring water over them until each one is completely coated in a layer of ice that will hold it bent. Then I'll scatter them over the snow, after first hiding them under a layer of fat. Now, what will happen if a hungry animal swallows one of these baits? The heat of its stomach will melt the ice, and the whalebone will spring straight and pierce it with its sharpened ends."

"Now that's clever!" said Pencroff.

"And it will save powder and bullets," answered Cyrus Smith.

"It's better than traps!" added Nab.

"So let's wait for winter!"

"Let's wait for winter."

Meanwhile the building of the boat went forward, and toward the end of the month she was half planked. It could already be seen that her lines would be excellent for keeping the sea well.

Pencroff worked with unequaled energy, and it took his sturdy constitution to stand the strain. But his companions were secretly preparing a reward for all his trouble, and on May 31 he was to feel one of the greatest joys of his life.

That day, at the end of dinner, just as he was about to leave the table, Pencroff felt a hand come down on his shoulder.

It was the hand of Gédéon Spilett, who said to him:

"Just a moment, Master Pencroff! You don't walk out like that! What about the dessert you're forgetting?"

"Thank you, Mr. Spilett," answered the sailor, "but I'm going back to work."

"Well, then, a cup of coffee, my friend?"

"No more than that."

"A pipe, then?"

Pencroff had jumped to his feet, and his big, good-natured face went pale when he saw the reporter holding out a pipe all filled and ready, and Harbert holding out a glowing ember.

The sailor tried to say something and could not get a word out. But he seized the pipe and carried it to his lips; then, putting the ember to it, he drew five or six puffs, one after another.

A bluish, fragrant cloud spread out, and from the depths of that cloud came a voice in raptures, repeating:

"Tobacco! Real tobacco!"

"Yes, Pencroff," answered Cyrus Smith, "and excellent tobacco at that!"

"Oh, divine Providence! Holy Maker of all things!" cried the sailor. "Now there's nothing missing on our island!"

And Pencroff smoked, and smoked, and smoked!

"And who made this discovery?" he asked at last. "You, I suppose, Harbert?"

"No, Pencroff, it was Mr. Spilett."

"Mr. Spilett!" cried the sailor, crushing the reporter to his chest in a hug such as the reporter had never had to endure before.

"Oof! Pencroff," answered Gédéon Spilett, getting back his breath, which had been in some danger for a moment. "Save a share of your gratitude for Harbert, who recognized the plant, for Cyrus, who prepared it, and for Nab, who had a terrible time keeping the secret!"

"Well, my friends, I'll pay you back for this some day!" answered the sailor. "From now on, it's for life and death between us!"
