Chapter 15: Iron and Steel

_Wintering on the island is settled once and for all — The metallurgical question — Exploring Safety Islet — The seal hunt — An echidna captured — The koula — What is called the Catalan method — Making iron — How steel is obtained_

The next morning, April 17, the sailor's first words were for Gédéon Spilett.

"Well, sir," he asked, "what are we going to be today?"

"Whatever Cyrus pleases," the reporter answered.

And so the engineer's companions, who until then had been brickmakers and potters, were about to become metalworkers.

The day before, after lunch, they had carried their exploration as far as the tip of Mandible Cape, nearly seven miles from the Chimneys. There the long line of dunes came to an end, and the ground took on a volcanic look. There were no more high walls of rock like those at Prospect Plateau. Instead, a strange, ragged border framed the narrow gulf between the two capes, and it was made of minerals the volcano had once spewed out. Having reached the point, the colonists had turned back, and they got home to the Chimneys as night was falling. But they did not go to sleep until they had settled, once and for all, whether or not they should think of leaving Lincoln Island.

The twelve hundred miles that lay between the island and the Pomotou Archipelago were a great distance. A small boat would not have been enough to cross it, especially with the bad season coming on. Pencroff had said so flatly. Besides, building even a simple boat was hard work, even with the right tools, and the colonists had no tools at all. They would have to begin by making hammers, axes, adzes, saws, augers, planes and so on, and that would take time. So it was decided that they would spend the winter on Lincoln Island, and that they would look for a more comfortable home than the Chimneys to live in through the winter months.

First of all, they had to put to use the iron ore that the engineer had spotted in a few deposits in the northwest part of the island, and turn that ore into iron or into steel.

As a rule, the ground does not hold metals in a pure state. Most of them are found combined with oxygen or with sulfur. As it happened, of the two samples Cyrus Smith had brought back, one was magnetic iron ore, not a carbonate, and the other was pyrite — in other words, iron sulfide. So it was the first, the iron oxide, that had to be reduced with charcoal or coal — that is, stripped of its oxygen — to get the iron pure. This reduction is done by heating the ore to a high temperature in the presence of the coal. There are two ways of going about it. One is the quick, easy "Catalan method," which has the advantage of turning the ore straight into iron in a single operation. The other is the blast-furnace method, which first turns the ore into cast iron, and then turns the cast iron into iron by taking out of it the three or four percent of carbon combined with it.

Now, what did Cyrus Smith need? Iron, not cast iron, and he had to find the quickest method of reduction. What was more, the ore he had collected was very pure and very rich in itself. It was the kind of magnetic oxide that turns up in shapeless dark-gray masses, gives a black powder when it is ground, crystallizes in regular octahedrons — eight-sided crystals — supplies natural magnets, and is used in Europe to make the finest grades of iron, which Sweden and Norway have in such abundance. Not far from this deposit lay the coal beds the colonists had already been working. That made it very easy to treat the ore, since everything needed for the job was close at hand. It is exactly this that makes the mines of the United Kingdom so prodigiously rich: there the coal that smelts the metal is dug out of the same ground, at the same time, as the ore itself.

"So, Mr. Cyrus," Pencroff said to him, "we're going to work the iron ore?"

"Yes, my friend," the engineer answered, "and to do that — which won't displease you — we'll begin with a seal hunt on the islet."

"A seal hunt!" cried the sailor, turning to Gédéon Spilett. "So it takes seals to make iron?"

"If Cyrus says so!" the reporter answered.

But the engineer had already left the Chimneys, and Pencroff got ready for the seal hunt without getting any further explanation.

Soon Cyrus Smith, Harbert, Gédéon Spilett, Nab and the sailor were gathered on the beach at a spot where the channel left a kind of ford at low tide. The tide was at the very bottom of the ebb, and the hunters were able to cross the channel without getting wet above the knee.

So this was the first time Cyrus Smith had set foot on the islet, and the second time for his companions, since it was there that the balloon had first thrown them down. As they came ashore, a few hundred auks looked at them with innocent eyes. The colonists, armed with sticks, could easily have killed them, but they never thought of carrying out a slaughter that would have been doubly pointless — the more so because it mattered not to frighten the seals, which were lying on the sand a few cable lengths away. They also spared certain perfectly harmless penguins, whose wings had shrunk to stumps and lay flat like flippers, covered with feathers that looked like scales.

So the colonists moved carefully toward the northern point, walking over ground pocked with little hollows, each of them the nest of some water bird. Toward the end of the islet they could see big black spots swimming at the surface of the water. They looked like the tops of reefs on the move.

These were the seals they had come to capture. They had to let them come ashore first. With their narrow hips, their short, close-set fur and their spindle-shaped bodies, seals are excellent swimmers and hard to catch in the sea, while on land their short webbed feet let them do no more than crawl, and not quickly.

Pencroff knew the habits of these animals, and he advised waiting until they had stretched out on the sand in the sunshine, which would soon put them into a deep sleep. Then they would maneuver to cut off their retreat and strike them on the nose.

So the hunters hid behind the rocks along the shore and waited in silence.

An hour went by before the seals came out to play on the sand. There were half a dozen of them. Pencroff and Harbert then slipped away to go around the point of the islet, so as to come up behind the animals and cut off their retreat. Meanwhile Cyrus Smith, Gédéon Spilett and Nab crawled along the rocks and crept toward what was about to become the field of battle.

All of a sudden the sailor's tall figure rose to its full height. Pencroff gave a shout. The engineer and his two companions threw themselves as fast as they could between the sea and the seals. Two of the animals, struck hard, were left dead on the sand, but the others managed to get back to the sea and swim off.

"The seals you ordered, Mr. Cyrus!" said the sailor, walking up to the engineer.

"Good," Cyrus Smith answered. "We'll make forge bellows out of them!"

"Forge bellows!" cried Pencroff. "Well! There's a couple of lucky seals for you!"

It was in fact a blowing machine — a bellows, which the treatment of the ore required — that the engineer meant to make from the skins of these animals. They were of middling size, no more than six feet long, and their heads looked like dogs' heads.

Since there was no point in loading themselves down with the considerable weight of two whole seals, Nab and Pencroff decided to skin them on the spot, while Cyrus Smith and the reporter finished exploring the islet.

The sailor and Nab managed the job skillfully, and three hours later Cyrus Smith had two sealskins at his disposal. He meant to use them just as they were, without tanning them at all.

The colonists had to wait for the tide to go down again, and then they crossed the channel and went home to the Chimneys.

It was no small job to stretch those skins over wooden frames that would hold them open, and to sew them up with fibers so that they could store air without leaking too much. They had to try again several times. Cyrus Smith had nothing to work with but the two steel blades from Top's collar. Even so, he was so skillful, and his companions helped him so intelligently, that three days later the little colony's equipment had grown by one blowing machine. Its job was to drive air into the middle of the ore while it was being heated — a condition the operation could not succeed without.

It was on April 20, first thing in the morning, that what the reporter called in his notes "the metallurgical period" began. The engineer, as we know, had made up his mind to do the work right at the deposits of coal and ore. By his observations, these lay at the foot of the northeast spurs of Mount Franklin, which meant six miles away. So there was no question of coming back to the Chimneys every day, and it was agreed that the little colony would camp in a hut of branches, so that the important operation could be watched over night and day.

With this plan settled, they set out first thing in the morning. Nab and Pencroff dragged the blowing machine on a hurdle — a sledge of woven branches — along with a supply of vegetable and animal food, which they could top up along the way anyway.

The route they took ran through Jacamar Wood, which they crossed on a slant from southeast to northwest, through its thickest part. They had to cut a path, and that path was later to become the most direct road between Prospect Plateau and Mount Franklin. The trees, all of kinds they had already identified, were magnificent. Harbert pointed out some new ones, among them dragon trees, which Pencroff called "stuck-up leeks" — because, for all their height, they belonged to the same lily family as the onion, the chive, the shallot and the asparagus. These dragon trees could supply woody roots that are excellent cooked and that, when left to ferment a certain way, make a very pleasant liquor. They laid in a supply.

This march through the woods was long. It took the whole day, but it gave them a chance to observe the animals and plants. Top, who was more particularly in charge of the animals, ran through the grass and the brush and flushed every kind of game without distinction. Harbert and Gédéon Spilett killed two kangaroos with arrows, and also an animal that looked a great deal like both a hedgehog and an anteater: like the first, because it rolled itself into a ball and bristled with spines; like the second, because it had digging claws, a long thin snout ending in a bird's beak, and a tongue it could shoot out, covered with little barbs that held on to insects. It was an echidna.

"And when he's in the stewpot," Pencroff naturally remarked, "what will he taste like?"

"Like an excellent piece of beef," Harbert answered.

"We won't ask any more of him than that," the sailor answered.

During this outing they caught sight of a few wild boars, which made no attempt to attack the little party. It did not seem likely that they would meet any dangerous beasts — until, in a thick patch of brush, the reporter thought he saw, a few paces away, in the lowest branches of a tree, an animal he took for a bear. He calmly started to sketch it. Very luckily for Gédéon Spilett, the animal in question did not belong to that formidable family of flat-footed walkers the bears belong to. It was only a "koula," better known as a "sloth." It was the size of a big dog, with bristly fur of a dirty color and feet armed with strong claws, which let it climb trees and live on their leaves. Once the creature's identity had been checked — and nobody disturbed it in its business — Gédéon Spilett crossed out "bear" in the caption of his sketch, wrote "koula" in its place, and they went on their way.

At five in the evening Cyrus Smith gave the signal to halt. He was now outside the forest, where the mighty spurs that propped up Mount Franklin on its eastern side began. A few hundred paces off ran Red Creek, so drinking water was close by.

The camp was set up at once. In less than an hour, at the edge of the forest, among the trees, a hut of branches woven through with creepers and plastered with clay offered a good enough shelter. The geological search was put off until the next day. Supper was made ready, a good fire blazed in front of the hut, the spit turned, and by eight o'clock, while one of the colonists stayed awake to keep the fire going in case some dangerous animal came prowling around, the others were sound asleep.

The next day, April 21, Cyrus Smith, with Harbert, went to look for the very old rock formations where he had already found his sample of ore. He came on the deposit right at the surface, almost at the source of the creek, at the foot of the side of one of those northeast spurs. This ore, very rich in iron and set in a gangue — the waste rock around it — that melted easily, was perfectly suited to the method of reduction the engineer meant to use: the Catalan method, but in the simplified form used in Corsica.

The Catalan method proper calls for building furnaces and crucibles, where the ore and the charcoal, laid in alternate layers, are transformed and reduced. But Cyrus Smith intended to save himself all that construction. He simply wanted to build a cube-shaped heap of ore and coal and drive the wind from his bellows into the middle of it. This was no doubt the process used by Tubal-cain, the Bible's first worker in metals, and by the first metalworkers of the inhabited world. And what had worked for the grandsons of Adam, and what still gave good results in countries rich in ore and fuel, could hardly fail in the circumstances the colonists of Lincoln Island found themselves in.

Like the ore, the coal was gathered without trouble, close by and right on the surface. First they broke the ore into small pieces and cleaned the dirt off their surfaces by hand. Then coal and ore were stacked in a heap, in layer after layer — just as a charcoal burner stacks the wood he wants to turn into charcoal. That way, with the air blown in by the bellows, the coal would turn first into carbonic acid and then into carbon monoxide, and the carbon monoxide would reduce the iron oxide — that is, take its oxygen away.

That is how the engineer went about it. The sealskin bellows, fitted at its end with a pipe of fireclay that had been made beforehand in the pottery kiln, was set up next to the heap of ore. It was worked by a mechanism made of frames, fiber ropes and counterweights, and it drove a steady supply of air into the heap. This air raised the temperature, and it also played its part in the chemical change that was to yield pure iron.

The operation was hard. It took all the colonists' patience and all their ingenuity to carry it through, but in the end it succeeded. The final result was a bloom of iron — a lump of spongy metal — which had to be shingled and worked, that is, forged under the hammer, to drive out the melted gangue. Obviously these makeshift blacksmiths had no first hammer to start with. But after all, they were in the same position as the very first metalworker, and they did what he must have done.

The first bloom, fixed onto a stick for a handle, served as a hammer for forging the second on a granite anvil, and in this way they managed to get a metal that was rough but usable.

At last, after a great deal of effort and a great deal of weariness, by April 25 several bars of iron had been forged, and they were being turned into tools — crowbars, pincers, picks, mattocks and so on — which Pencroff and Nab declared to be real jewels.

But it was not as plain iron that this metal could be of most service. It was above all as steel. Now, steel is a combination of iron and carbon, and it is made in one of two ways: either from cast iron, by taking out the carbon it has too much of, or from iron, by adding the carbon it lacks. The first kind, made by decarburizing cast iron, gives natural or puddled steel. The second, made by carburizing iron, gives cementation steel.

So it was this second kind that Cyrus Smith had reason to try to make, since what he had was pure iron. He succeeded by heating the metal with powdered charcoal in a crucible made of fireclay.

Then he worked this steel, which can be shaped by hammering both hot and cold, under the hammer. Nab and Pencroff, skillfully guided, made axe heads. Heated red-hot and plunged suddenly into cold water, these took an excellent temper.

Other tools were made the same way — roughly shaped, it goes without saying: plane blades, axes, hatchets, strips of steel that were to be turned into saws, carpenter's chisels, and then the iron heads for mattocks, shovels and picks, hammers, nails and so on.

At last, on May 5, the first metallurgical period was over. The blacksmiths went home to the Chimneys, and new work would soon entitle them to take on yet another title.
