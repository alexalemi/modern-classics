Chapter 4: A Forest for a Coastline

_Heading for the coast — Troops of monkeys — A new stream — Why the tide does not reach it — A forest for a coastline — Reptile End — Gédéon Spilett makes Harbert envious — The bamboo fireworks_

At six o'clock in the morning, after a first breakfast, the colonists set off again, meaning to reach the west coast of the island by the shortest way. How long would it take them to get there? Cyrus Smith had said two hours, but that obviously depended on what kind of obstacles they met. This part of the Far West looked crammed with trees, like an immense thicket made up of every possible kind of wood. So they would probably have to clear a path through grass, brush and creepers, and walk with an ax in hand — and with a gun, too, no doubt, judging by the cries of wild beasts they had heard in the night.

The exact position of the camp could be fixed by the position of Mount Franklin. Since the volcano rose to the north at a distance of less than three miles, all they had to do was strike out in a straight line to the southwest to reach the west coast.

They set off, after carefully making sure the canoe was well moored. Pencroff and Nab carried provisions that would feed the little party for at least two days. There was no more talk of hunting. The engineer even urged his companions to avoid any unnecessary shot, so as not to give away their presence anywhere near the shore.

The first blows of the ax fell in the brush, among clumps of mastic shrubs, a little above the waterfall, and Cyrus Smith, compass in hand, pointed out the way.

The forest here was made up of trees most of which they had already identified around the lake and Prospect Plateau. There were deodars, Douglas firs, casuarinas, gum trees, eucalyptus, dragon trees, hibiscus, cedars and other kinds, mostly of middling size, because there were so many of them that they had stunted one another's growth. So the colonists could move only slowly along this road, which they were cutting as they walked, and which the engineer had in mind to link up later with the road to Red Creek.

Since leaving the camp, the colonists had been going down the low slopes that made up the island's mountain system, over very dry ground. But its lush growth hinted either at a network of water running underground or at some stream flowing nearby. All the same, Cyrus Smith did not remember seeing any watercourse but Red Creek and the Mercy on his expedition to the crater.

During the first hours of the trip they saw troops of monkeys again, which seemed utterly astonished at the sight of these men, whose appearance was new to them. Gédéon Spilett jokingly asked whether these agile, sturdy four-handed creatures might not take him and his companions for degenerate brothers! And frankly, mere pedestrians, hampered by the brush at every step, tangled in creepers, blocked by tree trunks, did not cut much of a figure next to those supple animals, which leaped from branch to branch and let nothing stop them. The monkeys were numerous, but, very fortunately, they showed no sign of hostility.

They also saw a few wild boars, some agoutis, some kangaroos and other rodents, and two or three koulas, which Pencroff would gladly have treated to a few loads of shot.

"But hunting season isn't open," he said. "So frisk around, my friends, jump and fly in peace! We'll have a word or two with you on the way back!"

At half past nine in the morning, the road, which ran straight to the southwest, was suddenly cut off by an unknown stream, thirty to forty feet wide. Its swift current, driven by the slope of its bed and broken by a great many rocks, rushed along with a harsh roar. This creek was deep and clear, but it would have been completely impossible to navigate.

"We're cut off!" cried Nab.

"No," answered Harbert, "it's only a brook, and we'll manage to swim across it."

"What for?" answered Cyrus Smith. "This creek obviously runs to the sea. Let's stay on its left bank and follow it, and I'll be very surprised if it doesn't take us to the coast in no time. Let's go!"

"Just a moment," said the reporter. "What about this creek's name, my friends? Let's not leave our geography incomplete."

"Right!" said Pencroff.

"Name it, my boy," said the engineer, turning to the boy.

"Wouldn't it be better to wait until we've followed it all the way to its mouth?" Harbert pointed out.

"Very well," answered Cyrus Smith. "Then let's follow it without stopping."

"One moment more!" said Pencroff.

"What is it?" asked the reporter.

"If hunting is forbidden, fishing is allowed, I suppose," said the sailor.

"We have no time to lose," answered the engineer.

"Oh! Five minutes!" Pencroff replied. "All I ask is five minutes, in the interest of our lunch!"

And Pencroff lay down on the bank, plunged his arms into the running water, and soon sent a few dozen fine crayfish flying out from among the rocks, where they were swarming.

"Now that's going to be good!" cried Nab, coming to help the sailor.

"Didn't I tell you? Except for tobacco, there's everything on this island!" Pencroff murmured with a sigh.

It took less than five minutes to make a miraculous catch, because the creek was teeming with crayfish. These crustaceans had cobalt-blue shells and a beak armed with a little tooth. They filled a bag with them, and set off again.

Once they were following the bank of this new stream, the colonists walked more easily and more quickly. Besides, its banks were untouched by any human footprint. From time to time they spotted tracks left by large animals that came regularly to drink at the stream, but nothing more. It was not in this part of the Far West, then, that the peccary had taken the lead pellet that had cost Pencroff a molar.

Meanwhile, looking at that swift current racing off toward the sea, Cyrus Smith began to suspect that he and his companions were much farther from the west coast than they thought. In fact, at that hour the tide was rising along the shore, and it ought to have pushed back the creek's current if its mouth had been only a few miles off. But no such thing was happening, and the water kept flowing along the natural slope of its bed. The engineer must have been very surprised, and he kept checking his compass to make sure that some bend in the stream was not leading him back into the heart of the Far West.

Little by little, however, the creek grew wider and its waters less turbulent. The trees on its right bank were as crowded as those on its left, and the eye could not see past them. But those wooded masses were certainly empty of people, because Top was not barking, and the clever animal would not have failed to signal any stranger in the neighborhood of the stream.

At half past ten, to Cyrus Smith's great surprise, Harbert, who had gone a little way ahead, suddenly stopped and shouted:

"The sea!"

And a few moments later the colonists, halted at the edge of the forest, saw the western shore of the island spread out before their eyes.

But what a contrast between this coast and the east coast, where chance had first thrown them! No more granite wall, no reef offshore, not even a sandy beach. The forest made the shore, and its last trees, lashed by the waves, leaned out over the water. It was not a shore of the kind nature usually makes, either by spreading out wide carpets of sand or by piling up rocks. It was a wonderful border made of the most beautiful trees in the world. The bank stood high enough to overlook the level of the highest tides, and over all that rich soil, which rested on a base of granite, the splendid forest trees seemed as firmly rooted as the ones massed in the island's interior.

The colonists were standing at the notch of a small, insignificant cove, which could not have held even two or three fishing boats, and which served as the outlet of the new creek. But — a curious arrangement — instead of running into the sea down a gently sloping mouth, its waters fell from a height of more than forty feet. That explained why the rising tide had not made itself felt anywhere up the creek. In fact, the tides of the Pacific, even at their very highest, could never reach the level of the river, whose bed formed a sort of upper reach, like the stretch of a canal above a lock. Millions of years, no doubt, would still go by before the water wore away that granite sill and carved out a mouth a boat could use. So, by common consent, they gave the stream the name of Fall River.

Beyond it, to the north, the forest border went on for about two miles. Then the trees grew sparser, and beyond them a line of very picturesque heights stood out, running almost straight north and south. In the other direction, along the whole stretch of shore between Fall River and Reptile End, there was nothing but masses of woods, magnificent trees, some upright, some leaning, whose roots were bathed by the long swell of the sea. And it was in that direction — that is, along the whole of the Serpentine Peninsula — that the exploration had to go on, because that part of the shore offered places of refuge that the other, bare and wild, would obviously have refused to castaways of any kind.

The weather was fine and clear. From the top of a cliff, where Nab and Pencroff set out lunch, the eye could see a long way. The horizon was perfectly sharp, and there was not a sail out at sea. Along the whole shore, as far as the eye could reach, not a ship, not even a piece of wreckage. But the engineer would not consider the question settled until he had explored the coast all the way to the very tip of the Serpentine Peninsula.

Lunch was quickly dispatched, and at half past eleven Cyrus Smith gave the signal to leave. Instead of traveling along the crest of a cliff or over a sandy beach, the colonists had to keep under cover of the trees in order to follow the shore.

The distance from the mouth of Fall River to Reptile End was about twelve miles. On a walkable beach, without hurrying, the colonists could have covered that distance in four hours. But it took them twice as long to reach their goal, because the trees they had to get around, the brush they had to cut, the creepers they had to break, kept stopping them, and so many detours made their road much longer.

For that matter, there was nothing on this shore to show that a ship had been wrecked there recently. It is true, as Gédéon Spilett pointed out, that the sea could have carried everything away, and that just because no trace of a wreck could be found anymore, they should not conclude that no ship had been thrown ashore on this part of Lincoln Island.

The reporter's reasoning was sound. Besides, the incident of the lead pellet proved beyond any doubt that a gun had been fired on the island within the last three months at most.

It was already five o'clock, and the tip of the Serpentine Peninsula was still two miles from where the colonists were. Clearly, once Cyrus Smith and his companions reached Reptile End, they would not have time to get back before sunset to the camp they had set up near the sources of the Mercy. So they would have to spend the night on the promontory itself. But they had plenty of provisions, and that was lucky, because furred game no longer showed itself along this strip of forest, which was only a shoreline, after all. Birds, on the other hand, swarmed there — jacamars, couroucous, tragopans, grouse, lories, parrots, cockatoos, pheasants, pigeons and a hundred others. Not a tree without a nest, not a nest that was not full of beating wings!

At about seven o'clock in the evening, worn out with fatigue, the colonists reached Reptile End, a sort of scroll strangely carved out against the sea. Here the shore forest of the peninsula ended, and along the whole southern stretch the coast took on the usual look of a coast again, with its rocks, its reefs and its beaches. So it was possible that a disabled ship had run aground on this part of the island. But night was coming on, and the exploration had to be put off until the next day.

Pencroff and Harbert at once hurried off to look for a good spot to make camp. The last trees of the Far West forest came to an end at this point, and among them the boy recognized thick clumps of bamboo.

"Good!" he said. "Now that's a valuable find."

"Valuable?" answered Pencroff.

"Of course," Harbert went on. "I won't tell you, Pencroff, that bamboo bark, cut into flexible strips, is used for making baskets and hampers; that the bark, pulped and soaked, is used for making Chinese paper; that the stems, depending on how thick they are, give you canes, pipe stems and water pipes; that big bamboos make excellent building material, light and strong and never attacked by insects. I won't even add that if you saw bamboo stems between the joints, and keep part of the crosswise wall that forms the joint as a bottom, you get strong, handy containers that the Chinese use a great deal! No! None of that would satisfy you. But..."

"But...?"

"But I'll tell you, in case you didn't know, that in India people eat bamboo like asparagus."

"Thirty-foot asparagus!" cried the sailor. "And is it good?"

"Excellent," answered Harbert. "Only it isn't the thirty-foot stems people eat. It's the young bamboo shoots."

"Perfect, my boy, perfect!" answered Pencroff.

"I'll also add that the pith of the new stems, pickled in vinegar, makes a much-prized relish."

"Better and better, Harbert."

"And finally, that between their joints bamboos ooze a sweet liquid you can make into a very pleasant drink."

"Is that all?" asked the sailor.

"That's all!"

"And you can't smoke it, by any chance?"

"You can't smoke it, my poor Pencroff!"

Harbert and the sailor did not have to look long for a good place to spend the night. The rocks of the shore were deeply split — the sea must batter them violently when the southwest winds blew — and they held hollows where the colonists could sleep sheltered from the weather. But just as the two of them were about to go into one of these caves, a tremendous roaring stopped them in their tracks.

"Back!" cried Pencroff. "We've got nothing but birdshot in our guns, and animals that roar like that wouldn't care any more about it than about a grain of salt!"

And the sailor, seizing Harbert by the arm, dragged him behind the shelter of the rocks, just as a magnificent animal appeared at the mouth of the cave.

It was a jaguar, at least as large as its Asian relatives — that is, it measured more than five feet from the tip of its head to the root of its tail. Its tawny coat was set off by several rows of black spots, regularly ringed like eyes, which stood out against the white fur of its belly. Harbert recognized it as that ferocious rival of the tiger, far more formidable than the cougar, which is only the rival of the wolf!

The jaguar came forward and looked around, its fur bristling, its eyes blazing, as if this were not the first time it had smelled a man.

Just then the reporter was coming around the high rocks. Harbert, thinking he had not seen the jaguar, was about to rush toward him. But Gédéon Spilett signaled to him with his hand and kept on walking. This was not his first tiger. He came up to within ten paces of the animal and stood still, his carbine at his shoulder, without a single muscle twitching.

The jaguar gathered itself and sprang at the hunter. But at the very moment it leaped, a bullet struck it between the eyes, and it dropped dead.

Harbert and Pencroff rushed over to the jaguar. Nab and Cyrus Smith came running from their side, and for a few moments they all stood looking at the animal stretched out on the ground. Its magnificent skin would be an ornament for the great hall of Granite House.

"Oh, Mr. Spilett! How I admire you, and how I envy you!" cried Harbert, in a burst of enthusiasm that was only natural.

"Come now, my boy," answered the reporter, "you'd have done the same."

"Me! With nerves like that...!"

"Just imagine, Harbert, that a jaguar is a hare, and you'll shoot it as calmly as you please."

"There you are!" answered Pencroff. "Nothing more to it than that!"

"And now," said Gédéon Spilett, "since this jaguar has left its den, my friends, I don't see why we shouldn't move into it for the night."

"But others might come back!" said Pencroff.

"It'll be enough to light a fire at the mouth of the cave," said the reporter, "and they won't risk crossing the threshold."

"To the jaguars' house, then!" answered the sailor, dragging the animal's body along behind him.

The colonists headed for the abandoned den, and there, while Nab skinned the jaguar, his companions piled a great heap of dry wood at the entrance, which the forest supplied in plenty.

But Cyrus Smith, having noticed the clump of bamboo, went and cut a good quantity of it and mixed it in with the fuel for the fire.

That done, they settled into the cave, whose sandy floor was strewn with bones. The guns were loaded, just in case of a sudden attack. They had supper, and then, when it was time to rest, they set fire to the woodpile at the mouth of the cave.

At once a regular volley of bangs burst out into the air! It was the bamboo, caught by the flames, going off like firecrackers! That racket alone would have been enough to terrify the boldest wild beasts!

And this way of making loud explosions was not the engineer's invention. According to Marco Polo, the Tartars have used it successfully for many centuries to drive the fearsome wild beasts of Central Asia away from their camps.
