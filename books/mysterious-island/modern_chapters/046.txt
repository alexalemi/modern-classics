Chapter 5: Pencroff's Grand Theories

_What the engineer affirms — Pencroff's grand theories — A battery in the air — The four shots — About the surviving convicts — Ayrton hesitates — Cyrus Smith's generous feelings — Pencroff gives in reluctantly_

So everything was explained by the underwater explosion of that torpedo. Cyrus Smith, who had had occasion during the war for the Union to try out these terrible engines of destruction, could not be mistaken. It was the blast of that cylinder, packed with some explosive substance — nitroglycerin, picrate, or something of the same kind — that had thrown the water of the channel up like a waterspout and torn open the brig's bottom so that she sank on the spot. And that was why it had been impossible to refloat her: the damage to her hull had been so enormous. Against a torpedo that could have destroyed an ironclad frigate as easily as a little fishing boat, the Speedy had never stood a chance!

Yes! Everything was explained, everything... except what that torpedo had been doing in the waters of the channel!

"My friends," Cyrus Smith went on, "we can no longer doubt that some mysterious being is here — a castaway like us, perhaps, abandoned on our island. I say this so that Ayrton will know all the strange things that have happened over the past two years. Who is this unknown benefactor whose help, so fortunate for us, has shown itself on so many occasions? I can't imagine. What reason does he have for acting this way, for hiding himself after doing us so many services? I can't understand it. But his services are no less real for that, and they are the kind that only a man with prodigious power at his command could have done us. Ayrton is in his debt as much as we are. For if it was the stranger who saved me from the waves after the balloon fell, then it was obviously he who wrote the document, who put that bottle in the path of our boat in the channel, and who let us know where our companion was. I will add this: it was he who guided that chest, so well stocked with everything we lacked, and beached it at Jetsam Point; it was he who lit the fire on the heights of the island that let you make land; it was he who fired the lead pellet we found in the peccary's body; it was he who sank the torpedo in the channel that destroyed the brig. In a word, all those inexplicable facts we could never account for are owed to this mysterious being. So whoever he may be, castaway or exile on this island, we would be ungrateful if we thought ourselves released from all gratitude toward him. We have run up a debt, and I hope we will pay it one day."

"You're right to speak that way, my dear Cyrus," replied Gédéon Spilett. "Yes, there is someone, someone almost all-powerful, hidden somewhere on the island, whose influence has been remarkably useful to our colony. I'll add that this stranger seems to me to have means of action at his disposal that would border on the supernatural, if the supernatural could be accepted among the facts of practical life. Is it he who communicates with us in secret through the well of Granite House, and so knows all our plans? Is it he who held out that bottle to us when the canoe made her first trip out to sea? Is it he who threw Top out of the lake and killed the dugong? Is it he — as everything leads us to believe — who saved you from the waves, Cyrus, and in circumstances where anyone who was only a man could have done nothing? If it is, then he has a power that makes him master of the elements."

The reporter's remark was a fair one, and they all felt it.

"Yes," replied Cyrus Smith. "If the intervention of a human being is no longer in doubt, I agree that he has means of action at his disposal beyond those that humanity commands. There is a mystery still. But if we find the man, we'll find the answer to the mystery too. So the question is this: should we respect the incognito of this generous being, or should we do everything we can to reach him? What do you think?"

"What I think," replied Pencroff, "is that whoever he is, he's a good man, and he has my respect!"

"Fair enough," said Cyrus Smith, "but that's not an answer, Pencroff."

"My master," Nab said then, "I have an idea that we can look for this gentleman as long as we like, but we won't find him until he pleases."

"That's not a stupid thing to say, Nab," said Pencroff.

"I agree with Nab," said Gédéon Spilett, "but that's no reason not to try. Whether we find this mysterious being or not, we will at least have done our duty toward him."

"And you, my boy, give us your opinion," said the engineer, turning to Harbert.

"Oh!" cried Harbert, his eyes lighting up. "I'd like to thank him — the one who saved you first, and then saved the rest of us!"

"You and me both, my boy," Pencroff shot back, "and all of us! I'm not the curious sort, but I'd give one of my eyes to see that fellow face to face! I picture him handsome, tall, strong, with a fine beard and hair like rays of light, lying on the clouds with a big ball in his hand!"

"Why, Pencroff," replied Gédéon Spilett, "that's a portrait of God the Father you're painting for us!"

"Could be, Mr. Spilett," the sailor answered, "but that's how I imagine him!"

"And you, Ayrton?" asked the engineer.

"Mr. Smith," replied Ayrton, "I can hardly give you my opinion in this matter. Whatever you do will be well done. Whenever you want me to join your search, I'll be ready to follow you."

"Thank you, Ayrton," said Cyrus Smith, "but I'd like a more direct answer to the question I asked you. You are our companion. You have already risked yourself for us more than once, and like everyone here, you must be consulted when an important decision is to be made. So speak."

"Mr. Smith," replied Ayrton, "I think we must do everything we can to find this unknown benefactor. Perhaps he's alone? Perhaps he's suffering? Perhaps there's a life to be renewed? I too, as you said, have a debt of gratitude to pay him. It was he — it can only have been he — who came to Tabor Island, who found the wretch you knew there, who let you know there was an unhappy man to be saved!... So it's thanks to him that I became a man again. No, I will never forget it!"

"Then it's settled," said Cyrus Smith. "We'll begin our search as soon as possible. We won't leave one part of the island unexplored. We'll search it down to its most secret hiding places — and may our unknown friend forgive us, for the sake of our intentions!"

For several days the colonists were hard at work bringing in the hay and the harvest. Before carrying out their plan to explore the parts of the island still unknown, they wanted every essential job finished. This was also the season for gathering the various vegetables grown from the plants brought back from Tabor Island. So everything had to be stored, and fortunately there was no lack of room in Granite House, where they could have stored all the riches of the island. The colony's produce was kept there, methodically arranged and in a safe place — you can believe it — as secure from beasts as from men. There was no damp to fear in the middle of that thick mass of granite. Several of the natural hollows along the upper passage were enlarged or hollowed out, some with the pick, some by blasting, and Granite House became a general warehouse as well, holding the provisions, the ammunition, the spare tools and utensils — in a word, all the colony's equipment.

As for the cannons from the brig, they were handsome pieces of cast steel. At Pencroff's urging they were hoisted with tackles and cranes right up to the doorsill of Granite House. Gunports were cut between the windows, and soon the guns could be seen thrusting their gleaming muzzles out through the granite wall. From that height these pieces of artillery truly commanded the whole of Union Bay. It was a little Gibraltar, and any ship that anchored broadside to the shore off the islet would have been exposed, with no escape, to the fire of this battery in the air.

"Mr. Cyrus," Pencroff said one day — it was November 8 — "now that the guns are mounted, we really have to test their range."

"Do you think that would be useful?" replied the engineer.

"It's more than useful, it's necessary! Otherwise, how will we know how far we can send one of those fine cannonballs we've got such a stock of?"

"Let's try, then, Pencroff," replied the engineer. "But I think we should make the experiment using not ordinary gunpowder, since I want to keep our supply of that intact, but guncotton, which we'll never run short of."

"Can these cannons stand the blast of guncotton?" asked the reporter, who was no less eager than Pencroff to try out the artillery of Granite House.

"I think so. Besides," added the engineer, "we'll go about it carefully."

The engineer had good reason to think the cannons were of excellent make, and he knew what he was talking about. They were made of forged steel and loaded at the breech, so for that very reason they ought to stand a considerable charge, and therefore have an enormous range. For in practical terms, the path the shot travels should be as flat as possible, and that flatness can only be had if the projectile leaves the gun at a very high initial speed.

"Now," Cyrus Smith told his companions, "the initial speed depends on the amount of powder used. In making guns, the whole question comes down to using the strongest metal possible, and of all metals, steel is beyond question the one that holds up best. So I have reason to think our cannons will safely withstand the expanding gases of the guncotton and will give excellent results."

"We'll be a lot surer of that once we've tried it!" replied Pencroff.

It goes without saying that the four cannons were in perfect condition. Ever since they had been hauled out of the water, the sailor had made it his job to clean them conscientiously. How many hours he had spent rubbing them, greasing them, polishing them, cleaning the breech mechanism — the breechblock, the bolt, the clamping screw! And now the guns shone as brightly as if they had been aboard a frigate of the United States Navy.

So that day, in the presence of the whole colony, Master Jup and Top included, the four cannons were tested one after another. They were loaded with guncotton, allowing for its explosive power, which, as we have said, is four times that of ordinary gunpowder. The projectile they were to fire was cylindrical, with a conical nose.

Pencroff, holding the cord of the friction primer, stood ready to fire.

At a sign from Cyrus Smith, the shot went off. The ball, aimed out to sea, passed over the islet and vanished far offshore, at a distance that could not be judged with any accuracy.

The second cannon was trained on the farthest rocks of Jetsam Point, and the projectile struck a sharp-pointed stone nearly three miles from Granite House and blew it to pieces.

It was Harbert who had aimed the cannon and fired it, and he was very proud of his first attempt. Only Pencroff was prouder than he was! A shot like that — and the honor of it went to his dear boy!

The third projectile, fired this time at the dunes that formed the upper shore of Union Bay, struck the sand at a distance of at least four miles; then it ricocheted and was lost at sea in a cloud of spray.

For the fourth gun, Cyrus Smith increased the charge a little, to test its utmost range. Then, with everyone standing well aside in case it burst, the primer was fired by means of a long cord.

There was a violent explosion, but the gun held. The colonists rushed to the window and saw the projectile chip the rocks of Mandible Cape, nearly five miles from Granite House, and disappear into Shark Gulf.

"Well, Mr. Cyrus," cried Pencroff, whose hurrahs could have rivaled the explosions themselves, "what do you say about our battery? All the pirates of the Pacific can come and show themselves in front of Granite House! Not a single one of them will land now without our permission!"

"If you take my advice, Pencroff," replied the engineer, "it's better not to put that to the test."

"By the way," the sailor went on, "what about the six scoundrels prowling around the island? What are we going to do with them? Are we going to let them run loose through our forests, our fields, our meadows? They're real jaguars, those pirates, and it seems to me we shouldn't hesitate to treat them as such. What do you think, Ayrton?" Pencroff added, turning to his companion.

At first Ayrton hesitated to answer, and Cyrus Smith regretted that Pencroff had put the question to him so thoughtlessly. So he was deeply moved when Ayrton answered in a humble voice:

"I was one of those jaguars, Mr. Pencroff, and I have no right to speak..."

And he walked slowly away.

Pencroff had understood.

"What a blasted fool I am!" he cried. "Poor Ayrton! He has as much right to speak here as anybody!..."

"Yes," said Gédéon Spilett, "but his reserve does him credit, and we ought to respect the feeling he has about his unhappy past."

"Understood, Mr. Spilett," replied the sailor, "and you won't catch me at it again! I'd sooner swallow my tongue than hurt Ayrton's feelings! But let's get back to the question. It seems to me those bandits have no right to any pity, and we ought to rid the island of them as soon as we can."

"Is that really your opinion, Pencroff?" asked the engineer.

"Absolutely my opinion."

"And before hunting them down without mercy, you wouldn't wait until they had committed some fresh act of hostility against us?"

"So what they've already done isn't enough?" asked Pencroff, who could make no sense of all this hesitation.

"They may come to feel differently!" said Cyrus Smith. "And perhaps repent..."

"Repent — them!" cried the sailor, shrugging his shoulders.

"Pencroff, think of Ayrton!" Harbert said then, taking the sailor's hand. "He became an honest man again!"

Pencroff looked at his companions one after another. He would never have believed that his proposal could raise the slightest hesitation. His rough nature could not accept any compromise with the scoundrels who had landed on the island, the accomplices of Bob Harvey, the murderers of the Speedy's crew. He thought of them as wild beasts that had to be destroyed without hesitation and without remorse.

"Well, there it is!" he said. "Everybody's against me! You want to be generous with those villains! All right. Let's hope we don't live to regret it!"

"What danger are we in," said Harbert, "if we take care to stay on our guard?"

"Hmm!" said the reporter, who was not committing himself too far. "There are six of them, and well armed. If each of them lay in ambush in some corner and fired at one of us, they'd soon be masters of the colony!"

"Then why haven't they done it?" replied Harbert. "No doubt because it wasn't in their interest to. Besides, there are six of us too."

"Fine! Fine!" replied Pencroff, whom no argument could have convinced. "Let's leave those fine fellows to go about their little business, and not think about them anymore!"

"Come on, Pencroff," said Nab, "don't make yourself out to be so mean! If one of those poor wretches were here, right in front of you, within easy range of your gun, you wouldn't shoot him..."

"I'd shoot him like a mad dog, Nab," Pencroff replied coldly.

"Pencroff," the engineer said then, "you have often shown great respect for my advice. In this case, will you rely on me once again?"

"I'll do as you please, Mr. Smith," replied the sailor, who was not convinced in the least.

"Well then, let's wait, and attack only if we're attacked."

So that was how they decided to act toward the pirates, though Pencroff predicted nothing good from it. They would not attack them, but they would stay on their guard. After all, the island was large and fertile. If any feeling of honesty remained in the depths of their souls, those wretches might perhaps reform. Given the conditions they had to live in, wasn't it in their own best interest to make a new life for themselves? In any case, for humanity's sake if nothing else, they had to wait. The colonists might no longer be able to come and go as freely and trustingly as before. Until now they had only had wild beasts to guard against, and now six convicts, perhaps of the worst kind, were prowling about their island. It was serious, no doubt, and for less courageous people it would have meant the end of all their security.

No matter! For the present, the colonists were right and Pencroff was wrong. Would they be right in the future? Time would tell.
