Chapter 9: No News of Nab

_No news of Nab — A proposal by Pencroff and the reporter that is not accepted — A few sorties by Gédéon Spilett — A scrap of cloth — A message — A hasty departure — Arrival at Prospect Plateau_

The young patient's recovery was going steadily. Only one thing was left to wish for now: that his condition would allow them to take him back to Granite House. However well fitted out and well stocked the house at the corral might be, it could not offer the comfort of their healthy granite home. Besides, it was not as safe, and the people inside it, for all their watchfulness, were always under the threat of a shot from the convicts. Back there, on the other hand, in the middle of that impregnable, inaccessible mass of rock, they would have nothing to fear, and any attempt against them would be bound to fail. So they were waiting impatiently for the moment when Harbert could be moved without danger to his wound, and they had made up their minds to move him, even though getting through Jacamar Wood was very difficult.

They had no news of Nab, but they were not worried about him. The brave fellow, well entrenched deep inside Granite House, would not let himself be taken by surprise. Top had not been sent back to him. It had seemed pointless to expose the faithful dog to some gunshot that would have robbed the colonists of their most useful helper.

So they waited, but the colonists were in a hurry to be together again at Granite House. It pained the engineer to see his forces divided, because that was playing into the pirates' hands. Since Ayrton's disappearance there were only four of them against five, for Harbert could not be counted yet — and that was not the least of the worries of the brave boy, who understood very well the trouble he was causing!

The question of how to act against the convicts, as things stood, was talked through thoroughly on November 29 by Cyrus Smith, Gédéon Spilett and Pencroff, at a moment when Harbert was dozing and could not hear them.

"My friends," said the reporter, after they had spoken of Nab and of the impossibility of getting in touch with him, "I agree with you that venturing out on the Corral Road would mean risking a bullet without being able to return it. But don't you think that what we ought to do now is go after those wretches openly and hunt them down?"

"That's just what I was thinking," answered Pencroff. "I don't suppose we've come to the point of being afraid of a bullet, and for my part, if Mr. Cyrus agrees, I'm ready to throw myself into the forest! What the devil! One man's as good as another!"

"But is he as good as five?" asked the engineer.

"I'll go with Pencroff," answered the reporter, "and the two of us, well armed, along with Top..."

"My dear Spilett, and you too, Pencroff," Cyrus Smith went on, "let's think this through coolly. If the convicts were holed up in one spot on the island, if we knew where that spot was, and if all we had to do was flush them out of it, I'd understand a direct attack. But isn't there reason to fear, on the contrary, that they'd be sure of firing the first shot?"

"Oh, Mr. Cyrus," cried Pencroff, "a bullet doesn't always reach its address!"

"The one that hit Harbert didn't go astray, Pencroff," answered the engineer. "Besides, notice that if the two of you left the corral, I'd be left here alone to defend it. Can you promise me that the convicts won't see you leave it, that they won't let you go deep into the forest and then attack the corral while you're gone, knowing there's no one here but a wounded boy and one man?"

"You're right, Mr. Cyrus," answered Pencroff, his chest swelling with a smothered anger. "You're right. They'll do anything to take back the corral, because they know it's well stocked! And on your own you couldn't hold out against them! Oh, if only we were at Granite House!"

"If we were at Granite House," answered the engineer, "things would be very different! There I wouldn't be afraid to leave Harbert with one of us, and the other three could go and search the island's forests. But we're at the corral, and the right thing is to stay here until we can all leave it together!"

There was no answer to Cyrus Smith's reasoning, and his companions knew it.

"If only Ayrton were still with us!" said Gédéon Spilett. "Poor man! His return to the society of men didn't last long!"

"If he's dead?..." Pencroff added, in a rather odd tone.

"So, Pencroff, you hope those scoundrels spared him?" asked Gédéon Spilett.

"Yes! If they had some reason to!"

"What! You'd suppose that Ayrton, finding his old accomplices again, forgetting everything he owes us..."

"Who knows?" answered the sailor, who did not put forward this ugly suggestion without hesitating.

"Pencroff," said Cyrus Smith, taking the sailor by the arm, "that's an unworthy thought, and you'd hurt me deeply if you kept on talking that way! I vouch for Ayrton's loyalty!"

"So do I," the reporter put in quickly.

"Yes... yes!... Mr. Cyrus... I'm wrong," answered Pencroff. "That was an unworthy thought I had there, and nothing justifies it! But what do you expect? My head's not quite my own anymore. Being shut up in this corral weighs on me something terrible, and I've never been so worked up in my life!"

"Be patient, Pencroff," answered the engineer. "How soon, my dear Spilett, do you think Harbert can be moved to Granite House?"

"That's hard to say, Cyrus," answered the reporter, "because one careless move could have fatal consequences. But still, his recovery is coming along steadily, and if his strength has come back a week from now — well, we'll see!"

A week! That put off the return to Granite House until the first days of December at the earliest.

By that time spring was already two months old. The weather was fine, and the heat was beginning to grow strong. The island's forests were in full leaf, and the time was coming when the usual harvests would have to be brought in. So their return to Prospect Plateau would be followed by a great deal of farm work, interrupted only by the expedition they had planned across the island.

You can see, then, how much harm this confinement at the corral was doing the colonists. But if they were forced to bow to necessity, they did not do it without impatience.

Once or twice the reporter ventured out onto the road and made a circuit around the palisade. Top went with him, and Gédéon Spilett, his carbine cocked, was ready for anything.

He had no bad encounters and found no suspicious tracks. His dog would have warned him of any danger, and since Top did not bark, it was fair to conclude that there was nothing to fear, at the moment at least, and that the convicts were busy in some other part of the island.

On his second sortie, however, on November 27, Gédéon Spilett, who had ventured a quarter of a mile into the woods south of the mountain, noticed that Top was onto something. The dog had dropped his casual manner. He was running back and forth, nosing through the grass and the brush, as if his sense of smell had told him of something suspicious.

Gédéon Spilett followed Top, egging him on and urging him with his voice, while keeping a sharp lookout, his carbine at his shoulder, and using the trees for cover. It was not likely that Top had scented a man, because in that case he would have announced it with half-held-back barks and a kind of muffled fury. And since he was not growling at all, the danger was neither near nor close at hand.

About five minutes went by like this, with Top nosing about and the reporter cautiously following, when all of a sudden the dog dashed into a thick bush and dragged out a scrap of cloth.

It was a piece of clothing, stained and torn, and Gédéon Spilett brought it straight back to the corral.

There the colonists examined it, and they recognized it as a piece of Ayrton's jacket, a piece of the felt that was made nowhere but in the workshop at Granite House.

"You see, Pencroff," Cyrus Smith pointed out, "the unlucky Ayrton put up a fight. The convicts dragged him off against his will! Do you still doubt his honesty?"

"No, Mr. Cyrus," answered the sailor, "and I got over that moment of distrust a long time ago! But it seems to me there's a conclusion to be drawn from this."

"What's that?" asked the reporter.

"That Ayrton wasn't killed at the corral! That they dragged him off alive, since he fought back! So maybe he's still alive!"

"Maybe so, indeed," answered the engineer, and he stayed lost in thought.

Here was a hope that Ayrton's companions could cling to. Until now they had had to believe that Ayrton, taken by surprise at the corral, had fallen to some bullet, just as Harbert had. But if the convicts had not killed him at once, if they had carried him off alive to some other part of the island, wasn't it possible that he was still their prisoner? Perhaps one of them had even recognized in Ayrton an old companion from Australia — Ben Joyce, the leader of the escaped convicts! And who knows whether they had not formed the impossible hope of winning Ayrton back to their side! He would have been so useful to them if they could have made a traitor of him!...

So this incident was taken as a good sign at the corral, and it no longer seemed impossible that they would find Ayrton again. For his part, if he was only a prisoner, Ayrton would no doubt do everything he could to escape from those bandits' hands, and he would be a powerful ally to the colonists!

"In any case," Gédéon Spilett pointed out, "if by good luck Ayrton manages to get away, he'll go straight to Granite House. He doesn't know about the attempt to murder Harbert, and so he can't imagine we're shut up here at the corral."

"Oh! I wish he were there, at Granite House!" cried Pencroff. "And us too! Because after all, even if those scoundrels can't do anything against our home, they can at least lay waste to the plateau — our plantations, our poultry yard!"

Pencroff had become a real farmer, attached heart and soul to his crops. But it must be said that Harbert was more impatient than any of them to get back to Granite House, because he knew how badly the colonists were needed there. And he was the one keeping them at the corral! So a single idea filled his mind: to leave the corral, to leave it no matter what! He believed he could stand being moved to Granite House. He insisted that his strength would come back faster in his own room, with the sea air and the view of the sea!

Several times he pressed Gédéon Spilett. But the reporter was afraid — and rightly — that Harbert's wounds, still badly healed, would open again on the way, and he did not give the order to leave.

Then something happened that led Cyrus Smith and his two friends to give in to the boy's wishes — and God knows what grief and remorse that decision might bring them!

It was November 29. It was seven o'clock in the morning. The three colonists were talking in Harbert's room when they heard Top barking loudly.

Cyrus Smith, Pencroff and Gédéon Spilett seized their guns, which were always loaded and ready to fire, and went out of the house.

Top had run to the foot of the palisade and was jumping and barking — but with pleasure, not anger.

"Someone's coming!"

"Yes!"

"It isn't an enemy!"

"Nab, maybe?"

"Or Ayrton?"

These words had barely passed between the engineer and his two companions when a body came leaping over the palisade and landed on the ground inside the corral.

It was Jup — Master Jup in person — and Top gave him a true friend's welcome!

"Jup!" cried Pencroff.

"Nab's sent him to us!" said the reporter.

"Then," answered the engineer, "he must have a note on him."

Pencroff rushed over to the orangutan. Clearly, if Nab had had something important to tell his master, he could not have used a surer or swifter messenger — one who could get through where neither the colonists nor perhaps even Top himself could have gone.

Cyrus Smith had not been mistaken. A little bag hung around Jup's neck, and in the bag was a note in Nab's hand.

Imagine the despair of Cyrus Smith and his companions when they read these words:

    "Friday, 6 a.m.
    Plateau overrun by the convicts!
    Nab."

They looked at one another without saying a word, and then they went back into the house. What were they to do? The convicts on Prospect Plateau — that meant disaster, devastation, ruin!

When Harbert saw the engineer, the reporter and Pencroff come back in, he understood that the situation had just taken a turn for the worse, and when he caught sight of Jup, he no longer doubted that some misfortune was threatening Granite House.

"Mr. Cyrus," he said, "I want to go. I can stand the trip! I want to go!"

Gédéon Spilett went over to Harbert. Then, after looking at him for a moment:

"Let's go, then!" he said.

The question of whether Harbert should be carried on a stretcher or in the cart that Ayrton had brought to the corral was quickly settled. The stretcher would have moved more gently for the wounded boy, but it needed two bearers, which meant two guns fewer for their defense if they were attacked on the way.

Couldn't they, on the other hand, use the cart and leave every pair of hands free? Was it really impossible to lay the mattresses Harbert was lying on inside it, and to go forward so carefully that he would be spared every jolt? It could be done.

The cart was brought round. Pencroff harnessed the onagga to it. Cyrus Smith and the reporter lifted Harbert's mattresses and laid them on the floor of the cart, between its two side rails.

The weather was fine. Bright rays of sunlight were slipping through the trees.

"Are the weapons ready?" asked Cyrus Smith.

They were. The engineer and Pencroff, each armed with a double-barreled gun, and Gédéon Spilett, holding his carbine, had only to set off.

"Are you comfortable, Harbert?" asked the engineer.

"Oh, Mr. Cyrus," answered the boy, "don't worry, I won't die on the way!"

As he said this, anyone could see that the poor boy was calling up all his energy, and that by a supreme effort of will he was holding on to a strength that was ready to go out.

The engineer felt his heart clench painfully. He still hesitated to give the signal to leave. But that would have driven Harbert to despair — perhaps killed him.

"Let's go!" said Cyrus Smith.

The gate of the corral was opened. Jup and Top, who knew when to keep quiet, rushed out ahead. The cart went out, the gate was shut again, and the onagga, led by Pencroff, moved forward at a slow walk.

It would certainly have been better to take some road other than the one that ran straight from the corral to Granite House, but the cart would have had great difficulty getting through the woods. So they had to follow that road, even though the convicts were bound to know it.

Cyrus Smith and Gédéon Spilett walked on either side of the cart, ready to answer any attack. Still, it was not likely that the convicts had left Prospect Plateau yet. Nab's note had obviously been written and sent off the moment the convicts showed themselves there. Now, the note was dated six o'clock in the morning, and the nimble orangutan, used to coming to the corral often, had taken barely three quarters of an hour to cover the five miles that lay between it and Granite House. So the road ought to be safe for the moment, and if there was any shooting to be done, it would most likely be only as they drew near Granite House.

All the same, the colonists kept strictly on their guard. Top and Jup — Jup armed with his stick — now ranging ahead, now beating the woods along the sides of the road, gave no sign of danger.

The cart moved slowly forward, with Pencroff at its head. It had left the corral at half past seven. An hour later, four of the five miles had been covered without anything happening.

The road was as deserted as all that part of Jacamar Wood that stretched between the Mercy and the lake. There was no alarm of any kind. The thickets seemed as empty as on the day the colonists first landed on the island.

They were drawing near the plateau. One more mile, and they would see the little bridge over Glycerin Creek. Cyrus Smith had no doubt that this bridge would be down and in place — either because the convicts had come in that way, or because, after crossing one of the streams that closed off the plateau, they had taken the precaution of lowering it to leave themselves a way of retreat.

At last a gap in the last of the trees opened onto a view of the sea's horizon. But the cart kept on going, since none of its defenders could think of leaving it.

At that moment Pencroff stopped the onagga, and in a terrible voice he cried:

"Oh! The villains!"

And he pointed to a thick smoke swirling over the mill, the stables and the buildings of the poultry yard.

A man was moving about in the middle of that smoke.

It was Nab.

His companions gave a shout. He heard them and ran toward them...

The convicts had left the plateau about half an hour before, after laying it waste!

"And Mr. Harbert?" cried Nab.

Gédéon Spilett came back to the cart at that moment.

Harbert had lost consciousness!
