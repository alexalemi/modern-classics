Chapter 12: An Unexpected Document

_Rigging the boat — An attack by culpeos — Jup wounded — Jup nursed — Jup cured — The boat finished — Pencroff's triumph — The Bonadventure — A first trial south of the island — An unexpected document_

That same evening the hunters came home. They had had good hunting, and they were literally loaded down with game, carrying everything four men could carry.

Top had a string of pintails around his neck, and Jup had belts of snipe wound around his body.

"There, my master!" cried Nab. "There's something to keep us busy! Preserves, pâtés — we'll have a fine store of them! But somebody has to help me. I'm counting on you, Pencroff."

"No, Nab," the sailor answered. "The boat's rigging needs me, and you'll have to manage without me."

"What about you, Mr. Harbert?"

"I have to go to the corral tomorrow, Nab," the boy answered.

"Then it'll be you who helps me, Mr. Spilett?"

"To oblige you, Nab," the reporter answered. "But I warn you — if you let me in on your recipes, I'll publish them."

"As you please, Mr. Spilett," said Nab. "As you please!"

And that is how, the next day, Gédéon Spilett became Nab's assistant and was set up in his culinary laboratory. Before that, though, the engineer had told him the result of the exploration he had carried out the day before, and on that point the reporter shared Cyrus Smith's opinion: even though he had found nothing, there was still a secret waiting to be discovered!

The cold held on for another week, and the colonists did not leave Granite House except to look after the poultry yard. The whole dwelling was filled with the good smells given off by the expert operations of Nab and the reporter. Not all the game from the marsh hunt was turned into preserves, though. In that intense cold the meat kept perfectly, so wild ducks and the rest were eaten fresh, and they were declared superior to every other water bird in the known world.

During that week Pencroff, helped by Harbert, who handled a sailmaker's needle with skill, worked so hard that the boat's sails were finished. There was no shortage of hemp rope, thanks to the rigging that had been recovered along with the balloon's envelope. The cables and the cords of the net were all made of excellent line, and the sailor put it to good use. The sails were edged with strong bolt ropes — the ropes sewn around a sail's border to take the strain — and there was still enough left over to make the halyards, the shrouds, the sheets, and so on. As for the blocks, Cyrus Smith made the ones they needed on the lathe the sailor had set up, following Pencroff's advice. So it happened that the rigging was entirely ready long before the boat was finished. Pencroff even made a flag of blue, red, and white, its colors supplied by certain dye plants that were very plentiful on the island. Only, to the thirty-seven stars that stand for the thirty-seven states of the Union and shine on the blue corner of the American flag, the sailor had added a thirty-eighth: the star of "the State of Lincoln," because he already considered his island part of the great republic.

"And it is, at heart," he would say, "even if it isn't yet in fact!"

Meanwhile the flag was raised at the middle window of Granite House, and the colonists saluted it with three hurrahs.

By now the cold season was nearing its end, and it seemed that this second winter would pass without any serious incident, when, on the night of August 11, Prospect Plateau was threatened with complete devastation.

After a well-filled day the colonists were sound asleep when, at about four o'clock in the morning, they were suddenly wakened by Top's barking.

This time the dog was not barking near the mouth of the well, but at the threshold of the door, and he was throwing himself against it as if he wanted to break it down. Jup, for his part, was letting out shrill cries.

"Hey there, Top!" cried Nab, who was the first one awake.

But the dog went on barking even more furiously.

"What is it?" asked Cyrus Smith.

They all dressed in a hurry and rushed to the windows of the room and threw them open.

Before their eyes lay a sheet of snow that looked barely white in that very dark night. The colonists could see nothing, but they could hear strange barks bursting out of the darkness. It was obvious that the beach had been invaded by a number of animals that could not be made out.

"What is it?" cried Pencroff.

"Wolves, jaguars, or monkeys!" answered Nab.

"The devil! But they could get up onto the plateau!" said the reporter.

"And our poultry yard," cried Harbert, "and our plantations..."

"Which way did they get in?" asked Pencroff.

"They must have crossed the little bridge on the beach," the engineer answered. "One of us must have forgotten to close it."

"That's right," said Spilett. "I remember now — I left it down..."

"That was a fine piece of work, Mr. Spilett!" cried the sailor.

"What's done is done," Cyrus Smith answered. "Let's think about what has to be done now!"

Those were the questions and answers that flew back and forth between Cyrus Smith and his companions. It was certain that the little bridge had been crossed, that the beach had been invaded by animals, and that these animals, whatever they were, could reach Prospect Plateau by climbing along the left bank of the Mercy. So they had to get there first and fight them off, if need be.

"But what are these creatures?" someone asked a second time, just as the barking rang out louder.

The barking made Harbert start. He remembered having heard it before, during his first visit to the sources of Red Creek.

"They're culpeos — foxes!" he said.

"Forward!" cried the sailor.

And all of them, arming themselves with axes, carbines, and revolvers, crowded into the elevator's basket and set foot on the beach.

These culpeos are dangerous animals when there are a great many of them and hunger has made them savage. All the same, the colonists did not hesitate to throw themselves into the middle of the pack, and their first revolver shots, flashing quick bursts of light through the darkness, drove the leading attackers back.

What mattered above all was to keep these raiders from climbing up to Prospect Plateau. If they got there, the plantations and the poultry yard would be at their mercy, and immense damage — perhaps damage beyond repair, especially to the wheat field — would inevitably follow. But since the plateau could only be invaded by way of the left bank of the Mercy, it was enough to set up an impassable barrier against the culpeos on the narrow strip of bank between the river and the granite wall.

Everyone understood this, and at an order from Cyrus Smith they made for the spot he named, while the pack of culpeos leaped about in the dark.

So Cyrus Smith, Gédéon Spilett, Harbert, Pencroff, and Nab took up positions to form a line that nothing could get through. Top, his tremendous jaws wide open, stood out in front of the colonists, and behind him came Jup, armed with a knotty cudgel that he swung like a war club.

The night was extremely dark. Only in the flash of the shots — and every one of them had to count — could the attackers be seen. There must have been at least a hundred of them, and their eyes glowed like live coals.

"They mustn't get through!" cried Pencroff.

"They won't get through!" the engineer answered.

But if they did not get through, it was not for want of trying. The rear ranks pushed the front ones forward, and it was a ceaseless battle of revolver shots and ax blows. Many culpeo bodies must already have been strewn on the ground, but the pack did not seem to shrink. It was as if it were endlessly renewed by fresh arrivals over the little bridge on the beach.

Soon the colonists had to fight hand to hand, and they did not get away without a few wounds — slight ones, luckily. With one revolver shot Harbert had rid Nab of a culpeo that had just sprung onto his back like a wildcat. Top fought with real fury, leaping at the foxes' throats and strangling them outright. Jup, armed with his stick, laid about him like a madman, and it was no use trying to keep him in the rear. He must have had eyes that could pierce that darkness, because he was always where the fighting was thickest, and every now and then he let out a shrill whistle, which in him was the sign of tremendous glee. At one point he even pushed so far forward that, in the flash of a revolver shot, they could see him surrounded by five or six big culpeos, holding them off with rare coolness.

The fight was bound to end in the colonists' favor, but only after they had held out for two long hours! It was probably the first glimmers of dawn that decided the attackers to retreat. They bolted off to the north, back over the little bridge, which Nab ran to raise at once.

When the daylight was strong enough to light up the battlefield, the colonists counted some fifty bodies scattered across the beach.

"And Jup!" cried Pencroff. "Where's Jup?"

Jup had disappeared. His friend Nab called him, and for the first time Jup did not answer his friend's call.

Everyone set out to look for Jup, dreading to find him among the dead. They cleared the ground of the bodies, which were staining the snow with their blood, and Jup was found in the middle of a regular heap of culpeos. Their smashed jaws and broken backs showed that they had had to deal with the terrible cudgel of that fearless animal. Poor Jup was still holding the stump of his broken stick in his hand. But once he had lost his weapon, he had been overwhelmed by their numbers, and deep wounds furrowed his chest.

"He's alive!" cried Nab, bending over him.

"And we'll save him," the sailor answered. "We'll take care of him just as if he were one of us!"

It seemed as though Jup understood, for he leaned his head on Pencroff's shoulder as if to thank him. The sailor was wounded himself, but his wounds, like his companions', were trifling, since thanks to their firearms they had almost always been able to keep the attackers at a distance. So the orangutan was the only one in serious condition.

Carried by Nab and Pencroff, Jup was brought to the elevator, and barely a faint moan escaped his lips. They hoisted him gently up to Granite House. There he was laid on one of the mattresses taken from one of the bunks, and his wounds were washed with the greatest care. They did not seem to have reached any vital organ, but Jup had been badly weakened by loss of blood, and a fairly high fever set in.

So after his wounds were dressed they put him to bed and placed him on a strict diet — "just like a real person," said Nab — and they gave him a few cups of a cooling herbal tea, its ingredients supplied by Granite House's pharmacy of plants.

At first Jup's sleep was restless, but little by little his breathing became more regular, and they left him to rest in perfect quiet. From time to time Top came walking — on tiptoe, you might say — to visit his friend, and he seemed to approve of all the care being taken of him. One of Jup's hands hung over the edge of the bed, and Top licked it with a sorrowful air.

That same morning they buried the dead. The bodies were dragged to the Far West forest and buried deep.

This attack, which could have had such serious consequences, taught the colonists a lesson. From then on they never went to bed until one of them had made sure that all the bridges were raised and that no invasion was possible.

Meanwhile Jup, after giving them serious cause for worry for a few days, fought back hard against his illness. His constitution won out. The fever went down little by little, and Gédéon Spilett, who was something of a doctor, soon considered him out of danger. On August 16 Jup began to eat. Nab made him good little sweet dishes, which the patient savored with great relish, for if he had one endearing fault it was that he was a bit of a glutton, and Nab had never done a thing to cure him of it.

"What do you expect?" he would say to Gédéon Spilett, who sometimes scolded him for spoiling the ape. "The only pleasure poor Jup has is eating, and I'm only too happy to be able to repay him for his services this way!"

Ten days after taking to his bed, on August 21, Master Jup got up. His wounds had healed over, and it was plain that before long he would get back his usual agility and strength. Like every convalescent, he was seized with a ravenous hunger, and the reporter let him eat as much as he liked, trusting to the instinct that reasoning creatures so often lack and that would keep the orangutan from any excess. Nab was delighted to see his pupil's appetite coming back.

"Eat, my Jup," he would tell him, "and don't deny yourself a thing! You shed your blood for us, and the least I can do is help you make more!"

At last, on August 25, they heard Nab's voice calling his companions.

"Mr. Cyrus, Mr. Gédéon, Mr. Harbert, Pencroff — come here! Come here!"

The colonists, who were gathered in the great hall, got up at Nab's call. He was in the room set aside for Jup.

"What is it?" asked the reporter.

"Look!" Nab answered, with a huge burst of laughter.

And what did they see? Master Jup, sitting cross-legged in the doorway of Granite House, calmly and seriously smoking!

"My pipe!" cried Pencroff. "He's taken my pipe! Ah, my good Jup, I'm giving it to you! Smoke, my friend, smoke!"

And Jup solemnly puffed out thick clouds of tobacco smoke, which seemed to give him unequaled pleasure.

Cyrus Smith did not seem especially surprised by the incident, and he cited several cases of tame monkeys that had grown used to tobacco.

But from that day on, Master Jup had his own pipe — the sailor's former pipe — which was hung in his room beside his supply of tobacco. He filled it himself, he lit it with a glowing coal, and he seemed to be the happiest of all four-handed creatures. As you might expect, this shared taste only tightened the close bonds of friendship that already joined the worthy ape and the honest sailor.

"Maybe he's a man," Pencroff would sometimes say to Nab. "Would it surprise you if one day he started talking to us?"

"Goodness, no," Nab would answer. "What surprises me is that he doesn't talk. After all, the only thing he's missing is speech!"

"All the same, it would tickle me," the sailor went on, "if one fine day he said to me, 'How about we swap pipes, Pencroff?'"

"Yes," Nab answered. "What a pity he was born mute!"

With the month of September the winter was over for good, and work started up again in earnest.

The building of the boat moved quickly. Her outer planking was already all in place, and now they fitted her with frames on the inside, so as to tie every part of the hull together. The frames were made of timbers softened with steam, which bent to every curve the boat's lines called for.

Since there was no shortage of wood, Pencroff suggested to the engineer that they line the inside of the hull with a second, watertight layer of planking — what shipwrights call a ceiling — which would make the boat completely sound.

Cyrus Smith, not knowing what the future held in store, approved the sailor's idea of making their boat as strong as possible.

The ceiling and the deck were entirely finished around September 15. To caulk the seams they made oakum out of dried eelgrass, which was driven in with mallet blows between the planks of the hull, the ceiling, and the deck. Then the seams were coated with boiling tar, which the pines of the forest supplied in plenty.

The fitting out of the boat was as simple as could be. First she was ballasted with heavy pieces of granite, set in a bed of lime mortar, and they stowed about twelve thousand pounds of it. A floor was laid over this ballast, and the inside was divided into two cabins, each with a bench running along it that doubled as a storage chest. The foot of the mast was to serve as a prop for the partition between the two cabins, which were reached through two hatches opening onto the deck and fitted with hatch covers.

Pencroff had no trouble finding a suitable tree for the mast. He chose a young fir, good and straight and free of knots, which he only had to square off at the heel, where it would sit in its step, and round off at the top. The ironwork for the mast, the rudder, and the hull had been made at the forge in the Chimneys — roughly, but solidly. At last the yards, the topmast, the boom, the spars, the oars, and so on were all finished in the first week of October, and it was agreed that they would try the boat out in the waters around the island, to find out how she behaved at sea and how far they could trust her.

All this time the necessary work had not been neglected. The corral had been rearranged, since the flock of mouflons and goats now counted a number of young ones that had to be housed and fed. The colonists had kept up their visits to the oyster bed, to the warren, to the coal and iron deposits, and to certain parts of the Far West forests, which were full of game and had not yet been explored.

Some more native plants were discovered, too, and even if they were of no immediate use, they helped to vary the vegetable stores of Granite House. They were kinds of ice plant, some like the ones at the Cape of Good Hope, with fleshy leaves you could eat, and others producing seeds that held a sort of flour.

On October 10 the boat was launched. Pencroff was beaming. The operation went off perfectly. The boat, fully rigged, was pushed on rollers to the edge of the shore, where the rising tide took her, and she floated to the applause of the colonists — and especially of Pencroff, who showed no modesty whatever on this occasion. Besides, his pride was bound to outlast the boat's completion, since, having built her, he was now going to be called on to command her. The rank of captain was conferred on him with everyone's approval.

To satisfy Captain Pencroff, the first thing to do was give the boat a name. After several proposals had been argued over at length, the votes came together on Bonadventure, which was the honest sailor's baptismal name.

As soon as the rising tide had lifted the Bonadventure, they could see that she sat perfectly on her waterline and ought to sail well on every point of sailing.

In any case, she would be put to the test that very day, on an excursion off the coast. The weather was fine, the breeze fresh, and the sea easy, especially along the southern shore, since the wind had been blowing from the northwest for an hour already.

"All aboard! All aboard!" Captain Pencroff kept shouting.

But they had to have lunch before they left, and it even seemed wise to take provisions on board, in case the excursion stretched on into the evening.

Cyrus Smith was just as eager to try out this boat. The plans were his, though on the sailor's advice he had often changed parts of them. But he did not have the confidence in her that Pencroff showed, and since the sailor had not mentioned the voyage to Tabor Island again, Cyrus Smith even hoped he had given it up. He would have hated, in fact, to see two or three of his companions venture far out to sea in this boat, which was small, after all, and measured no more than fifteen tons.

At half past ten everyone was on board, even Jup, even Top. Nab and Harbert weighed the anchor, which was biting into the sand near the mouth of the Mercy. The mainsail was hoisted, the Lincolnian flag flew from the masthead, and the Bonadventure, steered by Pencroff, stood out to sea.

To get out of Union Bay they first had to run before the wind, and they could see that on that course the boat's speed was satisfactory.

After rounding Jetsam Point and Claw Cape, Pencroff had to sail close-hauled — as near into the wind as the boat would go — to run along the southern coast of the island. After a few tacks he observed that the Bonadventure could sail within about five points of the wind and held up well against drifting to leeward. She came about very well, having plenty of "way," as sailors say, and even gained ground as she turned.

The Bonadventure's passengers were truly delighted. They had a good boat here, one that could do them great service if the need arose, and in such fine weather, with such a steady breeze, the outing was a delight.

Pencroff stood out to sea three or four miles from the coast, abreast of Balloon Harbor. From there the island appeared in its full extent and in a new light: the varied panorama of its shoreline from Claw Cape to Reptile End; its forests in the foreground, where the conifers still stood out against the young leaves of the other trees, barely in bud; and Mount Franklin, towering over everything, its head still whitened with a little snow.

"How beautiful it is!" cried Harbert.

"Yes, our island is beautiful and good," Pencroff answered. "I love it the way I loved my poor mother! It took us in when we were poor and lacking everything, and what do these five children who dropped on it out of the sky lack now?"

"Nothing!" Nab answered. "Nothing, Captain!"

And the two good fellows gave three tremendous hurrahs in honor of their island!

Meanwhile Gédéon Spilett, leaning against the foot of the mast, was sketching the panorama spread out before his eyes.

Cyrus Smith watched in silence.

"Well, Mr. Cyrus," asked Pencroff, "what do you say about our boat?"

"She seems to handle well," the engineer answered.

"Good! And do you think now that she could make a voyage of some length?"

"What voyage, Pencroff?"

"To Tabor Island, for instance?"

"My friend," answered Cyrus Smith, "I believe that in an emergency we shouldn't hesitate to trust ourselves to the Bonadventure, even for a longer crossing. But, as you know, I would be sorry to see you leave for Tabor Island, since nothing obliges you to go there."

"A person likes to know his neighbors," Pencroff answered, digging in on his idea. "Tabor Island is our neighbor, and our only one! Good manners say we ought to go and pay it a visit, at least!"

"The devil!" said Gédéon Spilett. "Our friend Pencroff is a stickler for etiquette!"

"I'm not a stickler for anything at all," retorted the sailor. The engineer's opposition annoyed him a little, but he would not have wanted to cause him any pain.

"Remember, Pencroff," Cyrus Smith answered, "that you can't go to Tabor Island alone."

"One companion will be enough for me."

"Very well," answered the engineer. "So you're risking depriving the colony of Lincoln Island of two colonists out of five?"

"Out of six!" Pencroff answered. "You're forgetting Jup."

"Out of seven!" added Nab. "Top's worth as much as anybody!"

"There's no risk, Mr. Cyrus," Pencroff went on.

"That may be, Pencroff. But I'll say it again: it means exposing yourselves to danger for no need!"

The stubborn sailor made no reply and let the conversation drop, fully determined to take it up again. But he had no idea that an incident was about to come to his aid and turn what had been nothing but a whim — and a debatable one, after all — into a work of humanity.

After standing out to sea, the Bonadventure had just come back in toward the coast, heading for Balloon Harbor. It was important to check the channels that ran between the sandbanks and the reefs, and to mark them with buoys if necessary, since this little cove was to be the boat's home port.

They were now only half a mile from the coast, and they had had to beat their way in against the wind. The Bonadventure's speed was quite moderate at that point, because the breeze, partly blocked by the high ground, barely filled her sails. The sea, smooth as a mirror, rippled only when the cat's-paws passed fitfully across it.

Harbert had stationed himself in the bow, to point out the course to follow through the channels, when suddenly he cried:

"Luff, Pencroff, luff!"

"What's the matter?" answered the sailor, getting to his feet. "A rock?"

"No... wait," said Harbert. "I can't see well... luff a little more... good... bear off a bit..."

And as he said it Harbert, lying flat along the gunwale, plunged his arm quickly into the water and straightened up, saying:

"A bottle!"

He was holding a corked bottle in his hand, which he had just snatched from the water a few cable lengths from the coast.

Cyrus Smith took the bottle. Without saying a single word, he popped out the cork and drew out a damp piece of paper, on which these words could be read:

    Castaway... Tabor Island: 153° W. long. — 37° 11′ S. lat.
