Chapter 8: Is Cyrus Smith Alive?

_Is Cyrus Smith alive? — Nab's story — The footprints — A question with no answer — Cyrus Smith's first words — The footprints checked — The return to the Chimneys — Pencroff aghast!_

Nab did not move. The sailor threw him just one word.

"Alive?" he cried.

Nab did not answer. Gédéon Spilett and Pencroff went pale. Harbert clasped his hands and stood frozen where he was. But it was plain that poor Nab, swallowed up in his grief, had neither seen his companions nor heard the sailor's words.

The reporter knelt beside the motionless body, opened the engineer's clothes, and laid his ear against his chest. A minute went by — it felt like a century! — while he tried to catch some beat of the heart.

Nab had straightened up a little and was staring without seeing. Despair could not have changed a man's face more. Nab was unrecognizable, worn out with fatigue, broken with grief. He believed his master was dead.

After a long and careful examination, Gédéon Spilett stood up.

"He's alive!" he said.

Pencroff knelt in his turn beside Cyrus Smith. His ear, too, caught a few heartbeats, and his lips felt a faint breath escaping from the engineer's lips.

At a word from the reporter, Harbert dashed outside to look for water. A hundred paces away he found a clear stream, obviously swollen by the rains of the day before, filtering through the sand. But there was nothing to carry the water in — not one shell in those dunes! The boy had to make do with soaking his handkerchief in the stream, and he ran back to the cave.

Luckily the wet handkerchief was all Gédéon Spilett needed, since he only wanted to moisten the engineer's lips. Those few drops of fresh water had an almost immediate effect. A sigh escaped from Cyrus Smith's chest, and he even seemed to be trying to say a few words.

"We'll save him!" said the reporter.

At those words Nab began to hope again. He undressed his master to see whether his body showed any wound. There were no bruises on the head, the chest or the limbs, not even a scrape — which was surprising, since Cyrus Smith's body must have been rolled about among the rocks. Even his hands were unmarked, and it was hard to explain how the engineer showed no trace of the struggle he must have gone through to get past the line of reefs.

But the explanation of this would come later. When Cyrus Smith could talk, he would tell them what had happened. For the moment the job was to bring him back to life, and rubbing him down would probably do it. So they rubbed him with the sailor's jacket. Warmed by this rough massage, the engineer moved his arm slightly, and his breathing began to settle into a steadier rhythm. He had been dying of exhaustion, and if the reporter and his companions had not arrived, that would certainly have been the end of Cyrus Smith.

"So you thought he was dead, your master?" the sailor asked Nab.

"Yes! Dead!" Nab answered. "And if Top hadn't found you, if you hadn't come, I would have buried my master and died beside him!"

You can see what a thread Cyrus Smith's life had hung by!

Then Nab told them what had happened. The day before, after leaving the Chimneys at dawn, he had gone up the coast heading north and reached the stretch of shore he had already searched.

There, without any hope, as he admitted, Nab had searched the shore, among the rocks and on the sand, for the slightest clue that might guide him. Above all he had examined the part of the beach that the high tide did not cover, because along its edge the rise and fall of the tide would have wiped out every trace. Nab no longer hoped to find his master alive. What he was looking for was a body — a body he wanted to bury with his own hands!

Nab had searched a long time, and all his efforts came to nothing. That deserted coast did not seem ever to have been visited by a human being. The shells that the sea could not reach — and there were millions of them above the high-tide line — lay untouched. Not one shell was crushed. Over a stretch of two or three hundred yards there was no sign of anyone having come ashore, either long ago or recently.

So Nab had decided to go on up the coast for a few miles. The currents might have carried a body to some point farther along. When a body is floating not far from a flat shore, it is very rare for the waves not to throw it up on the beach sooner or later. Nab knew that, and he wanted to see his master one last time.

"I walked along the coast for two more miles," he said. "I checked the whole line of reefs at low tide and the whole beach at high tide, and I'd given up hope of finding anything, when yesterday, around five o'clock in the evening, I noticed footprints in the sand."

"Footprints?" cried Pencroff.

"Yes!" answered Nab.

"And did these footprints start right at the reefs?" asked the reporter.

"No," Nab answered, "only at the high-tide line. Between the tide line and the reefs, the others must have been washed away."

"Go on, Nab," said Gédéon Spilett.

"When I saw those footprints, I went half out of my mind. They were very easy to make out, and they headed toward the dunes. I followed them for a quarter of a mile, running, but taking care not to step on them. Five minutes later, just as night was falling, I heard a dog barking. It was Top, and Top led me right here, to my master!"

Nab finished his story by telling them how he had grieved when he found that lifeless body. He had tried to catch some last sign of life in it! Now that he had found him dead, he wanted him alive! All his efforts had been useless! There was nothing left for him to do but pay his last respects to the man he loved so much!

Then Nab had thought of his companions. They would surely want to see the unfortunate man one last time! Top was there. Couldn't he rely on the cleverness of that faithful animal? Nab said the reporter's name several times over — of all the engineer's companions, he was the one Top knew best. Then he pointed south along the coast, and the dog dashed off in the direction he had been shown.

We know how Top got to the Chimneys all the same, guided by an instinct that could almost be called supernatural, since the animal had never been there before.

Nab's companions had listened to his story with the closest attention. To them there was something inexplicable in the fact that Cyrus Smith, after the struggle he must have gone through to escape the waves and cross the reefs, did not have so much as a scratch. And what was no easier to explain was how the engineer could have reached this cave, lost among the dunes more than a mile from the shore.

"So, Nab," said the reporter, "it wasn't you who carried your master here?"

"No, it wasn't me," Nab answered.

"It's pretty obvious Mr. Smith got here on his own," said Pencroff.

"Obvious, yes," remarked Gédéon Spilett, "but not believable!"

Only the engineer himself could explain it, and for that they would have to wait until he could speak again. Fortunately, life was already flowing back into him. The rubbing had got his blood circulating again. Cyrus Smith moved his arms once more, then his head, and once again a few words they could not understand escaped his lips.

Nab, bending over him, kept calling to him, but the engineer did not seem to hear, and his eyes stayed shut. Movement was the only sign of life in him. His senses had not yet come back.

Pencroff badly regretted having no fire, and nothing to make one with, because he had unfortunately forgotten to bring the burnt linen, which he could easily have lit by striking two pebbles together. As for the engineer's pockets, they were completely empty, except for his vest pocket, which held his watch. So they would have to carry Cyrus Smith to the Chimneys, and as soon as possible. On that, everyone agreed.

But the care they lavished on the engineer was to bring him round faster than they could have hoped. The water they moistened his lips with was reviving him little by little. Pencroff also had the idea of mixing the water with juice from the grouse meat he had brought along. Harbert ran down to the shore and came back with two big bivalve shells. The sailor made a sort of mixture in them and slipped it between the engineer's lips, and he seemed to drink it down eagerly.

Then his eyes opened. Nab and the reporter were bending over him.

"My master! My master!" cried Nab.

The engineer heard him. He recognized Nab and Spilett, then his two other companions, Harbert and the sailor, and his hand gave theirs a faint squeeze.

A few more words escaped his lips — words he had no doubt been saying before, and which showed what thoughts were tormenting his mind, even then. This time the words were understood.

"Island or continent?" he murmured.

"Ah!" cried Pencroff, who could not hold the exclamation back. "By all the devils, who cares, as long as you're alive, Mr. Cyrus! Island or continent? We'll find out later."

The engineer gave a slight nod and seemed to fall asleep.

They let him sleep, and the reporter immediately began making arrangements to carry the engineer back in the best possible way. Nab, Harbert and Pencroff left the cave and headed for a tall dune crowned with a few stunted trees. And as they went, the sailor could not stop himself from repeating:

"Island or continent! Thinking about that when he's got hardly a breath left in him! What a man!"

When they reached the top of the dune, Pencroff and his two companions, with no tools but their bare arms, stripped the main branches off a rather puny tree, a kind of maritime pine worn thin by the wind. Out of those branches they made a stretcher, which, once it was covered with leaves and grass, would let them carry the engineer.

It took them about forty minutes, and it was ten o'clock when the sailor, Nab and Harbert came back to Cyrus Smith, whom Gédéon Spilett had not left.

The engineer was just then waking from the sleep — or rather the stupor — in which they had found him. The color was coming back into his cheeks, which until then had been as pale as death. He raised himself a little, looked around him, and seemed to be asking where he was.

"Can you listen to me without tiring yourself, Cyrus?" said the reporter.

"Yes," answered the engineer.

"If you ask me," the sailor put in, "Mr. Smith will hear you even better if he has another go at this grouse jelly — because it is grouse, Mr. Cyrus," he added, holding out a little of the jelly, into which, this time, he had mixed a few shreds of meat.

Cyrus Smith chewed the bits of grouse, and what was left was shared among his three companions, who were suffering from hunger and found it a rather skimpy lunch.

"Good!" said the sailor. "There's food waiting for us at the Chimneys. Because you ought to know, Mr. Cyrus, that down there, to the south, we've got a house with rooms, beds and a fireplace, and in the pantry a few dozen birds that our Harbert calls couroucous. Your stretcher's ready, and as soon as you feel strong enough, we'll carry you home."

"Thank you, my friend," answered the engineer. "Another hour or two and we can go... And now, tell me, Spilett."

So the reporter told him what had happened. He told him everything Cyrus Smith could not have known: the balloon's last fall; the landing on this unknown land, which seemed deserted, whatever it turned out to be, island or continent; the discovery of the Chimneys; the search for the engineer; Nab's devotion; everything they owed to the intelligence of faithful Top; and so on.

"But," asked Cyrus Smith, his voice still weak, "you didn't pick me up on the beach, then?"

"No," answered the reporter.

"And it wasn't you who brought me to this cave?"

"No."

"How far is this cave from the reefs?"

"About half a mile," answered Pencroff. "And if you're amazed, Mr. Cyrus, we're no less surprised ourselves to find you here!"

"Indeed," answered the engineer, who was reviving little by little and taking an interest in these details, "indeed, that's very strange!"

"But," the sailor went on, "can you tell us what happened after that wave carried you off?"

Cyrus Smith gathered his memories. He knew very little. The wave had torn him out of the balloon's net. At first he had sunk several fathoms deep. When he came back up to the surface in the half darkness, he felt a living creature thrashing beside him. It was Top, who had leaped in to save him. Looking up, he could no longer see the balloon, which, relieved of his weight and the dog's, had shot away like an arrow. He found himself in the middle of the raging waves, at a distance from the coast that could not have been less than half a mile. He tried to fight the waves, swimming hard. Top held him up by his clothes. But a current as fast as lightning caught him and swept him north, and after half an hour of struggling he went under, dragging Top down with him into the depths. From that moment until the moment he had just found himself in his friends' arms, he remembered nothing at all.

"Still," Pencroff went on, "you must have been thrown up on the shore, and you must have had the strength to walk all the way here, since Nab found your footprints!"

"Yes... I must have..." answered the engineer, thinking it over. "And you didn't see any trace of human beings on this coast?"

"Not a trace," answered the reporter. "Besides, if by chance some rescuer had happened to be there at just the right moment, why would he have abandoned you after pulling you out of the waves?"

"You're right, my dear Spilett." Then, turning to his servant, the engineer added: "Tell me, Nab, it wasn't you who... you couldn't have had a moment's blackout... during which... No, that's absurd... Are any of those footprints still there?" asked Cyrus Smith.

"Yes, my master," answered Nab. "Right there at the entrance, on the back slope of this very dune, in a spot sheltered from the wind and rain. The others were wiped out by the storm."

"Pencroff," said Cyrus Smith, "would you take my shoes and see whether they fit those footprints exactly?"

The sailor did what the engineer asked. He and Harbert, guided by Nab, went to the place where the footprints were, while Cyrus Smith said to the reporter:

"Some inexplicable things have happened here!"

"Inexplicable indeed!" answered Gédéon Spilett.

"But let's not dwell on it now, my dear Spilett. We'll talk about it later."

A moment later the sailor, Nab and Harbert came back in.

There was no possible doubt. The engineer's shoes fitted the surviving footprints exactly. So it was Cyrus Smith who had left them in the sand.

"Well, then," he said, "it must have been me who had that hallucination, that blackout I was blaming on Nab! I must have walked like a sleepwalker, without knowing I was taking a step, and it must have been Top who led me here by instinct, after pulling me out of the waves... Come here, Top! Come, boy!"

The magnificent animal bounded up to his master, barking, and he was not short of pats and praise.

It will be agreed that there was no other explanation for the events that had led to Cyrus Smith's rescue, and that all the credit for the business belonged to Top.

Toward noon, when Pencroff asked Cyrus Smith whether he could be moved, Cyrus Smith's only answer was to stand up, with an effort that showed the most powerful willpower. But he had to lean on the sailor, or he would have fallen.

"Fine! Fine!" said Pencroff. "The stretcher for the engineer, please!"

The stretcher was brought. Its crosspieces had been covered with moss and long grass. They laid Cyrus Smith on it and set off toward the coast, Pencroff at one end of the poles, Nab at the other.

There were eight miles to cover, but since they could not go fast and might have to stop often, they would have to allow at least six hours to reach the Chimneys.

The wind was still violent, but fortunately it was no longer raining. Though he was lying down, the engineer propped himself on his elbow and studied the coast, especially the landward side, away from the sea. He did not speak, but he looked, and there is no doubt that the shape of that country — its rises and hollows, its forests, its different kinds of plants — was printed on his mind. After two hours on the way, however, fatigue won, and he fell asleep on the stretcher.

At half past five the little party reached the cut-off corner of the cliff, and a little later they stood in front of the Chimneys.

Everyone stopped, and the stretcher was set down on the sand. Cyrus Smith was sound asleep and did not wake.

Then, to his great surprise, Pencroff saw that the terrible storm of the day before had changed the look of the place. There had been some fairly big rockfalls. Large blocks of stone lay scattered on the beach, and a thick carpet of seaweed — wrack and algae — covered the whole shore. It was obvious that the sea had swept right over the islet and rolled all the way to the foot of the enormous granite rampart.

In front of the opening of the Chimneys the ground had been deeply gouged; the waves had battered it violently.

A kind of foreboding flashed through Pencroff's mind. He rushed into the passage.

Almost at once he came back out and stood frozen, staring at his companions...

The fire was out. The drowned ashes were nothing but mud. The burnt linen that was to serve as tinder was gone. The sea had pushed all the way to the back of the passages and turned everything upside down, wrecked everything, inside the Chimneys!
