Chapter 5: A Single Match

_Fitting out the Chimneys — The all-important question of fire — The box of matches — A search on the beach — The reporter and Nab return — A single match! — The crackling hearth — The first supper — The first night on land_

As soon as the timber raft had been unloaded, Pencroff's first concern was to make the Chimneys fit to live in by blocking up the passages that let the wind blow through. Sand, stones, woven branches and wet earth sealed off tight the galleries of the "&" that lay open to the south winds, and shut off its upper loop. A single narrow, winding gut that opened on one side was left clear, to carry the smoke outside and give the fire a draft. In this way the Chimneys were divided into three or four rooms — if you can call them rooms, those gloomy dens that a wild animal would hardly have put up with. But they were dry, and you could stand upright in them, at least in the main room, which took up the center. Fine sand covered the floor, and all things considered they could make do with it until they found something better.

Harbert and Pencroff talked as they worked.

"Maybe our friends have found a better place than ours," Harbert said.

"Could be," the sailor answered, "but when in doubt, don't hold back! Better an extra string to your bow than no string at all!"

"Oh," Harbert said again, "if only they bring Mr. Smith back — if only they find him — then all we'll have left to do is thank heaven!"

"Yes!" Pencroff murmured. "He was a man, that one, a real one!"

"He was?" said Harbert. "Have you given up hope of ever seeing him again?"

"God forbid!" the sailor answered.

The work of fitting out the place was soon done, and Pencroff declared himself very pleased with it.

"Now," he said, "our friends can come back. They'll find shelter enough."

That left the hearth to build and the meal to make. A simple, easy job, really. Large flat stones were laid at the back of the first passage on the left, at the mouth of the narrow gut that had been kept open. Whatever heat the smoke did not carry outside would obviously be enough to keep the inside at a comfortable temperature. The supply of wood was stored in one of the rooms, and the sailor laid a few logs on the hearthstones, mixed in with kindling.

The sailor was busy at this when Harbert asked him whether he had any matches.

"Certainly," Pencroff answered, "and I'll add: luckily, because without matches or tinder we'd be in a real fix!"

"We could always make fire the way the islanders do, couldn't we," Harbert answered, "by rubbing two pieces of dry wood together?"

"Well! Try it, my boy, and we'll see if you manage anything besides wearing your arms out!"

"But it's a very simple method, and they use it all the time in the islands of the Pacific."

"I'm not saying they don't," Pencroff answered, "but the islanders must know the trick of it, or else they use a special kind of wood, because more than once I've tried to get a fire going that way and I've never once pulled it off! So I'll admit I prefer matches! Where are my matches?"

Pencroff felt in his jacket for the box that never left him, for he was a dedicated smoker. He did not find it. He went through his trouser pockets, and to his utter astonishment he found no box there either.

"Now that's stupid, worse than stupid!" he said, looking at Harbert. "That box must have dropped out of my pocket, and I've lost it! But you, Harbert, haven't you got anything — a flint and steel, anything at all that could make a fire?"

"No, Pencroff!"

The sailor went out, with the boy behind him, scratching his forehead hard.

On the sand, among the rocks, along the riverbank, the two of them searched with the greatest care, but it was no use. The box was made of copper and would never have escaped their eyes.

"Pencroff," Harbert asked, "didn't you throw that box out of the basket?"

"I made very sure not to," the sailor answered. "But when you've been shaken up the way we just were, a little thing like that can easily go missing. Why, even my pipe has deserted me! Blasted box! Where can it be?"

"Well, the tide's going out," said Harbert. "Let's run to the place where we came ashore."

It was not very likely they would find the box, which the waves must have rolled among the pebbles at high tide, but it was worth taking the chance into account. Harbert and Pencroff went quickly to the spot where they had landed the day before, some two hundred paces from the Chimneys.

There, among the pebbles and in the hollows of the rocks, they searched every inch. Nothing. If the box had fallen there, the waves must have carried it off. As the sea went out, the sailor poked into every crack in the rocks and found nothing. Under the circumstances it was a serious loss, and for the moment one that could not be made good.

Pencroff did not hide how bitterly disappointed he was. His forehead was deeply furrowed. He did not say a word. Harbert tried to comfort him by pointing out that the matches would most likely have been soaked by the seawater and impossible to use anyway.

"No, my boy," the sailor answered. "They were in a copper box that shut tight! And now what are we going to do?"

"We'll find some way to get fire, I'm sure of it," said Harbert. "Mr. Smith or Mr. Spilett won't be caught short the way we are!"

"Yes," Pencroff answered, "but in the meantime we've got no fire, and our friends will come back to a sorry meal!"

"But," Harbert said quickly, "they can't possibly be without tinder or matches!"

"I doubt it," the sailor answered, shaking his head. "For one thing, Nab and Mr. Smith don't smoke, and I'm very much afraid Mr. Spilett held on to his notebook sooner than his box of matches!"

Harbert did not answer. Losing the box was obviously a misfortune. Still, the boy was confident they would get fire one way or another. Pencroff, who had more experience, did not see it that way, even though he was not a man to be put out by small troubles or by large ones. In any case, there was only one thing to do: wait for Nab and the reporter to come back. But he would have to give up the meal of hard-boiled eggs he had meant to make for them, and a diet of raw meat did not strike him as a pleasant prospect, either for them or for himself.

Before going back to the Chimneys, in case they should end up with no fire at all, the sailor and Harbert gathered another load of lithodomes, and then they silently took the road back to their lodging.

Pencroff kept his eyes fixed on the ground, still hunting for his vanished box. He even went back up the left bank of the river from its mouth to the bend where the raft had been moored. He returned to the plateau above, crisscrossed it in every direction, and searched the tall grass along the edge of the forest — all in vain.

It was five o'clock in the evening when he and Harbert came back to the Chimneys. Needless to say, the passages were searched down to their darkest corners, and in the end they had to give it up for good.

Toward six o'clock, just as the sun was disappearing behind the high ground to the west, Harbert, who was pacing up and down the beach, called out that Nab and Gédéon Spilett were coming back.

They were coming back alone! ... The boy felt an indescribable tightening of the heart. The sailor's forebodings had not been wrong. The engineer Cyrus Smith had not been found!

When the reporter arrived, he sat down on a rock without a word. Worn out, dying of hunger, he did not have the strength to speak!

As for Nab, his reddened eyes showed how much he had wept, and the fresh tears he could not hold back said all too plainly that he had lost all hope!

The reporter told the story of their search for Cyrus Smith. He and Nab had covered more than eight miles of the coast, and so had gone well beyond the place of the balloon's next-to-last fall — the fall after which the engineer and the dog Top had disappeared. The beach was deserted. No trace, no footprint. Not a pebble freshly turned over, not a sign on the sand, not one mark of a human foot along that whole stretch of shoreline. It was obvious that no inhabitant ever came to that part of the coast. The sea was as empty as the shore, and it was there, a few hundred feet off the coast, that the engineer had found his grave.

At that moment Nab stood up, and in a voice that showed how stubbornly hope held out in him, he cried:

"No! No! He isn't dead! No! It can't be! Him? Come on! Me — anybody else — maybe! But him? Never. He's a man who comes back from anything! ..."

Then, his strength leaving him:

"Oh, I can't go on!" he murmured.

Harbert ran to him.

"Nab," the boy said, "we'll find him! God will give him back to us! But for now, you're hungry! Eat — please, eat a little something!"

And as he said it he held out a few handfuls of shellfish to poor Nab — a thin, meager meal!

Nab had not eaten for many hours, but he refused. Without his master, Nab could not, or would not, go on living!

Gédéon Spilett, on the other hand, devoured the shellfish; then he lay down on the sand at the foot of a rock. He was exhausted, but calm.

Then Harbert went over to him and took his hand.

"Sir," he said, "we've found a shelter where you'll be better off than here. Night's coming. Come and rest! Tomorrow we'll see ..."

The reporter got up and, with the boy guiding him, made his way toward the Chimneys.

At that moment Pencroff came up to him and, in the most offhand tone in the world, asked whether he happened to have a match on him.

The reporter stopped, felt in his pockets, found nothing, and said:

"I had some, but I must have thrown everything out ..."

Then the sailor called Nab over, asked him the same question, and got the same answer.

"Damnation!" cried the sailor, unable to hold the word back.

The reporter heard him and came over to Pencroff.

"Not one match?" he said.

"Not one, and so no fire!"

"Oh!" cried Nab. "If he were here, my master, he'd know how to make you one!"

The four castaways stood motionless and looked at one another, not without alarm. It was Harbert who broke the silence first.

"Mr. Spilett," he said, "you're a smoker, you always carry matches! Maybe you didn't look properly? Look again! One single match is all we need!"

The reporter went through his pockets again — trousers, vest, coat — and at last, to Pencroff's great joy and to his own utter surprise, he felt a little stick of wood caught in the lining of his vest. His fingers had hold of the little stick through the cloth, but they could not get it out. Since it had to be a match, and only one, the whole point was not to scrape off its phosphorus tip.

"Will you let me do it?" the boy said to him.

And very deftly, without breaking it, he managed to work out that little stick of wood — that wretched, precious splinter that meant so much to these poor men! It was whole.

"A match!" cried Pencroff. "Why, it's as good as having a whole cargo of them!"

He took the match and, with his companions behind him, went back to the Chimneys.

This little stick of wood, which in inhabited countries people throw away so carelessly and which is worth nothing at all, had to be used here with the utmost care. The sailor made sure it was perfectly dry. Then, that done:

"We'll need some paper," he said.

"Here," answered Gédéon Spilett, who, after some hesitation, tore a page out of his notebook.

Pencroff took the scrap of paper the reporter held out to him and crouched in front of the hearth. There, a few handfuls of dry grass, leaves and moss were placed under the bundles of sticks and arranged so that the air could move freely through them and set the dead wood quickly alight.

Then Pencroff folded the scrap of paper into a cone, as pipe smokers do in a high wind, and pushed it in among the moss. Next he picked up a slightly rough pebble and wiped it carefully, and, not without his heart pounding, he struck the match gently, holding his breath.

The first stroke did nothing. Pencroff had not pressed hard enough, for fear of scraping off the phosphorus.

"No, I can't do it," he said. "My hand's shaking ... The match would misfire ... I can't ... I won't! ..." And getting up, he handed the job to Harbert.

Certainly the boy had never in his life been so stirred. His heart was pounding. Prometheus going to steal fire from heaven can hardly have been more nervous! He did not hesitate, though, and struck the match quickly against the pebble. There was a little sputtering sound, and a small bluish flame sprang up, giving off a sharp, acrid smoke. Harbert gently turned the match over to feed the flame, then slid it into the paper cone. In a few seconds the paper caught, and the moss went up at once.

A few moments later the dry wood was crackling, and a cheerful blaze, fanned by the sailor's mighty breath, was growing in the middle of the darkness.

"At last!" cried Pencroff, getting to his feet. "I've never been so worked up in my life!"

There is no doubt that this fire looked fine on its hearth of flat stones. The smoke went out easily through the narrow flue, the chimney drew, and a pleasant warmth soon spread through the place.

As for the fire, they would have to take care never to let it go out again, and always to keep a few live embers banked under the ashes. But that was only a matter of care and attention, since there was no shortage of wood and the supply could always be renewed in good time.

Pencroff's first thought was to put the hearth to use by making a supper more nourishing than a plate of lithodomes. Harbert brought two dozen eggs. The reporter, propped up in a corner, watched these preparations without saying anything. Three questions strained his mind. Is Cyrus still alive? If he is alive, where can he be? If he survived his fall, how can it be that he has found no way to let them know he exists? As for Nab, he was wandering along the beach. He was nothing now but a body without a soul.

Pencroff, who knew fifty-two ways of cooking eggs, had no choice in the matter this time. He had to settle for burying them in the hot ashes and letting them harden slowly.

In a few minutes the cooking was done, and the sailor invited the reporter to take his share of the supper. Such was the castaways' first meal on that unknown coast. The hard-boiled eggs were excellent, and since an egg contains every element that human beings need for nourishment, these poor men felt much the better for them, and their strength came back.

Ah, if only one of them had not been missing from that meal! If all five of the prisoners who had escaped from Richmond had been there, under those heaped-up rocks, in front of that bright, crackling fire, on that dry sand, they might have had nothing but thanks to give to heaven! But the cleverest of them, and the most learned too, their undisputed leader, Cyrus Smith, was missing, alas — and his body had not even been granted a grave!

So passed that day, March 25. Night had fallen. Outside they could hear the wind whistling and the monotonous surf pounding the coast. The pebbles, pushed up and dragged back by the waves, rolled with a deafening roar.

The reporter had withdrawn to the back of a dark passage after jotting down a brief record of the day's events — the first sight of this new land, the disappearance of the engineer, the exploration of the coast, the business of the matches, and so on — and, with fatigue to help him, he managed to find some rest in sleep.

Harbert, for his part, soon fell asleep. As for the sailor, he spent the night beside the hearth sleeping with one eye open, and he did not spare the fuel.

Only one of the castaways did not rest in the Chimneys. It was Nab — inconsolable, despairing Nab — who, the whole night through, in spite of everything his companions said to persuade him to rest, wandered the beach calling for his master!
