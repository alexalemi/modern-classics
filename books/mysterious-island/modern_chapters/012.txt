Chapter 13: What They Found on Top

_What they found on Top — Making bows and arrows — A brickworks — The pottery kiln — Various cooking pots — The first stew — Mugwort — The Southern Cross — An important astronomical observation_

"Well, Mr. Cyrus, where do we start?" Pencroff asked the engineer the next morning.

"At the beginning," answered Cyrus Smith.

And in fact it was at the very "beginning" that these colonists were going to be forced to start. They did not even have the tools needed to make tools. They were not even in the position of nature, which, "having time, saves effort." Time was exactly what they lacked, since they had to provide at once for the needs of daily life. Thanks to the experience of the human race they had nothing to invent, but at least they had everything to make. Their iron and their steel were still only ore, their pottery was still only clay, and their linen and their clothes were still only raw fiber.

It must be said, though, that these colonists were "men" in the finest and strongest sense of the word. The engineer could not have had more intelligent companions to help him, or more devoted and eager ones. He had questioned them. He knew what each of them could do.

Gédéon Spilett, a reporter of great talent, who had learned everything so that he could talk about everything, would contribute a great deal, with his head and with his hands, to the colonizing of the island. He would shrink from no task, and as a passionate hunter he would now make a trade of what until then had been only a pleasure.

Harbert, a fine boy, already remarkably well schooled in the natural sciences, was going to be a real asset to the common cause.

Nab was devotion itself. Skillful, intelligent, tireless, strong, with an iron constitution, he knew something of blacksmith's work and could only be extremely useful to the colony.

As for Pencroff, he had been a sailor on every ocean, a carpenter in the Brooklyn shipyards, a tailor's assistant aboard navy ships, and a gardener and a farmhand during his shore leaves, and so on. Like all seamen, he was fit for anything and knew how to do everything.

It would truly have been hard to bring together five men better fitted to struggle against fate, or more certain of winning.

"At the beginning," Cyrus Smith had said. And the beginning the engineer meant was building a piece of equipment that could be used to transform natural materials. Everyone knows the part that heat plays in such transformations. Fuel, whether wood or coal, could be used at once. So the task was to build a kiln to burn it in.

"What will the kiln be for?" asked Pencroff.

"For making the pottery we need," answered Cyrus Smith.

"And what will we make the kiln out of?"

"Bricks."

"And the bricks?"

"Clay. Let's go, my friends. To save ourselves hauling things back and forth, we'll set up our workshop right where the materials are. Nab will bring provisions, and there'll be no shortage of fire for cooking food."

"No," the reporter answered, "but what if there's a shortage of food, for lack of anything to hunt with?"

"Ah! If only we had a knife!" cried the sailor.

"Well?" asked Cyrus Smith.

"Well! I'd make a bow and arrows in no time, and there'd be plenty of game in the pantry!"

"Yes, a knife, a sharp blade..." said the engineer, as though he were talking to himself.

At that moment his eyes fell on Top, who was running back and forth along the shore.

Suddenly Cyrus Smith's face lit up.

"Top, here!" he said.

The dog came running at his master's call. The engineer took Top's head in his hands, unfastened the collar the animal wore around his neck, and broke it in two, saying:

"There are two knives, Pencroff!"

The sailor answered with two cheers. Top's collar was made of a thin strip of tempered steel. All they had to do was grind it first on a sandstone to bring the edge to a bare, sharp angle, and then take off the wire edge, the rough burr the grinding leaves, on a finer sandstone. Rock of that gritty kind was plentiful on the beach, and two hours later the colony's tool kit consisted of two sharp blades, which it had been easy to fit into sturdy handles.

The winning of this first tool was hailed as a triumph. And a precious winning it was, and it came at just the right moment.

They set off. Cyrus Smith meant to go back to the western shore of the lake, where the day before he had noticed the clayey soil he had brought a sample of. So they followed the bank of the Mercy, crossed Prospect Plateau, and after a walk of five miles at most they came to a clearing two hundred paces from Lake Grant.

On the way, Harbert had found a tree whose branches the Indians of South America use to make their bows. It was the "crejimba," a member of the palm family that bears no edible fruit. They cut long, straight branches, stripped them of leaves, and trimmed them, leaving them thicker in the middle and thinner at the ends. Now all that remained was to find a plant fit to make the bowstring. They found it in a species of the mallow family, an "Hibiscus heterophyllus," which supplied fibers so remarkably tough they could have been compared to animal sinews. In this way Pencroff got himself bows of considerable power, and the only thing they still lacked was arrows. The shafts were easy to make from straight, stiff branches without knots. But the head that had to tip them, something that could take the place of iron, was not going to be so easy to come by. Pencroff told himself, though, that since he had done his share of the work, luck would do the rest.

The colonists had arrived at the patch of ground they had explored the day before. It was made of the potter's clay that is used to make bricks and tiles, so it was very well suited to the job they had in hand. The work itself presented no difficulty. They only had to temper the clay with sand, so that it would not be too sticky, mold the bricks, and bake them in the heat of a wood fire.

Usually bricks are pressed into molds, but the engineer was satisfied to make them by hand. The whole day, and the next one, went into this work. The clay was soaked with water, then worked and kneaded with the feet and fists of the workmen, and then divided into blocks of equal size. A practiced workman can turn out as many as ten thousand bricks in twelve hours without a machine. But in their two days of work the five brickmakers of Lincoln Island made no more than three thousand. These were laid out side by side until they were completely dry and ready to be fired, which would be in three or four days.

It was on April 2 that Cyrus Smith set about fixing the island's orientation — finding exactly where north lay.

The evening before, he had noted the exact time at which the sun disappeared below the horizon, allowing for the refraction of the air, which makes the sun seem higher than it is. That morning he noted just as exactly the time at which it reappeared. Between that sunset and that sunrise, twelve hours and twenty-four minutes had passed. So six hours and twelve minutes after it rose, the sun, on that day, would cross the meridian exactly, and the point in the sky it occupied at that moment would be north. (At that time of year, and in that latitude, the sun was rising at 5:33 in the morning and setting at 6:17 in the evening.)

At the appointed time Cyrus took a sighting on that point. He lined up two trees with the sun, to serve him as markers, and in this way he obtained a fixed north-south line for all his later work.

During the two days before the bricks were fired, everyone was busy laying in fuel. Branches were cut all around the clearing, and they gathered up all the dead wood that had fallen under the trees. This did not happen without a little hunting in the neighborhood, all the more since Pencroff now had a few dozen arrows tipped with very sharp points. It was Top who had supplied those points, by bringing back a porcupine. As game it was fairly poor, but it was undeniably valuable for the quills that bristled all over it. These quills were fixed firmly to the ends of the arrows, and feathers from cockatoos were fitted to the other ends to make them fly true. The reporter and Harbert soon became very skillful archers. And so game of every kind, furred and feathered, poured into the Chimneys: capybaras, pigeons, agoutis, heath cocks, and more. Most of these animals were killed in the part of the forest on the left bank of the Mercy, which they named Jacamar Wood, in memory of the bird Pencroff and Harbert had chased on their first exploration.

This game was eaten fresh, but they saved the capybara hams. They seasoned them with fragrant leaves and then smoked them over a fire of green wood. Still, this very nourishing food was always roast after roast, and the diners would have been happy to hear a simple stew bubbling on the hearth. But for that they had to wait until the pot was made — which meant waiting until the kiln was built.

During these outings, which never went more than a short distance from the brickworks, the hunters found signs that large animals had passed by recently, animals armed with powerful claws, though they could not tell what species they were. So Cyrus Smith urged them to be extremely careful, because it was likely that the forest sheltered some dangerous wild beasts.

He was right to do it. One day, in fact, Gédéon Spilett and Harbert caught sight of an animal that looked like a jaguar. Luckily the beast did not attack them, because they might not have got away without some serious wound. But Gédéon Spilett promised himself that as soon as he had a real weapon — one of those guns Pencroff kept asking for — he would wage relentless war on the wild beasts and rid the island of them.

During those few days nothing was done to make the Chimneys more comfortable, since the engineer expected to discover, or if need be to build, a better place to live. They were content to spread a fresh bed of moss and dry leaves over the sand of the passages, and on these rather primitive beds the exhausted workers slept the sleep of the just.

They also worked out how many days had passed on Lincoln Island since the colonists had landed there, and from then on they kept a regular count. On April 5, which was a Wednesday, it had been twelve days since the wind had thrown the castaways onto this coast.

On April 6, at dawn, the engineer and his companions gathered in the clearing at the spot where the bricks were to be fired. Naturally this operation had to be done in the open air and not in kilns. Or rather, the pile of bricks would itself be one enormous kiln that would bake itself. The fuel, made of carefully prepared bundles of brushwood, was laid out on the ground, and around it they built several courses of dried bricks, which soon formed a great cube, with vents left in its outer walls to let the fire draw. This work took the whole day, and only in the evening did they set fire to the brushwood.

That night nobody went to bed, and they watched carefully to make sure the fire did not die down.

The firing lasted forty-eight hours and was a complete success. Then the smoking mass had to be left to cool. In the meantime Nab and Pencroff, guided by Cyrus Smith, hauled several loads of calcium carbonate — that is, limestone, a very common stone, which was plentiful north of the lake — on a hurdle made of woven branches. When these stones were broken down by heat, they gave a quicklime that was very rich, that swelled up a great deal when it was slaked with water, and that was in the end as pure as if it had been made by burning chalk or marble. Mixed with sand, which keeps the paste from shrinking as it hardens, this lime made an excellent mortar.

The result of all this work was that on April 9 the engineer had a good quantity of lime ready to use, and a few thousand bricks.

So, without losing a moment, they began building a kiln for firing the various kinds of pottery they needed for everyday household use. They managed it without much trouble. Five days later the kiln was loaded with coal from the open-air seam the engineer had discovered near the mouth of Red Creek, and the first smoke was pouring out of a chimney some twenty feet high. The clearing had been turned into a factory, and Pencroff was not far from believing that every product of modern industry was about to come out of that kiln.

In the meantime, what the colonists made first was a plain, coarse pottery, but one very well suited for cooking. The raw material was the very clay of the ground, to which Cyrus Smith had them add a little lime and some quartz. This paste was in fact real "pipe clay," the white clay that tobacco pipes are made from. Out of it they made pots; cups, molded over pebbles of suitable shapes; plates; big jars and tubs to hold water; and so on. These objects were clumsy and lopsided. But once they had been fired at a high temperature, the kitchen of the Chimneys found itself supplied with a number of utensils as precious as if they had been made of the finest porcelain clay.

It must be mentioned here that Pencroff, eager to find out whether this clay, prepared in this way, deserved its name of "pipe clay," made himself a few rather rough pipes. He thought they were charming, but alas, there was no tobacco to put in them! And it has to be said that this was a great hardship for Pencroff.

"But the tobacco will come, like everything else!" he kept saying, in his bursts of absolute confidence.

This work lasted until April 15, and it will be understood that the time was conscientiously spent. The colonists, turned potters, did nothing but make pottery. When it suited Cyrus Smith to turn them into blacksmiths, they would be blacksmiths. But since the next day was a Sunday, and Easter Sunday at that, they all agreed to keep it holy by resting. These Americans were religious men, who carefully observed the teachings of the Bible, and the situation they had been placed in could only deepen their trust in the Maker of all things.

On the evening of April 15, then, they went back to the Chimneys for good. The rest of the pottery was carried along with them, and the kiln was let go out until it was needed for something new. The return was marked by a lucky event: the engineer discovered something that could take the place of tinder. Everyone knows that tinder, that spongy, velvety flesh, comes from a certain mushroom, a bracket fungus of the genus Polyporus. Properly prepared, it catches fire extremely easily, especially if it has first been soaked in gunpowder or boiled in a solution of potassium nitrate or potassium chlorate. But until then they had not found any of those fungi, nor even any of the morels that can stand in for them. That day, though, the engineer recognized a certain plant belonging to the genus Artemisia, the mugworts, whose chief species include wormwood, southernwood, tarragon, genepi, and others. He pulled up several clumps, and holding them out to the sailor, he said:

"Here, Pencroff, this will make you happy."

Pencroff looked closely at the plant, which was covered with long, silky hairs, and whose leaves were coated in a cottony down.

"Eh! What's this, Mr. Cyrus?" asked Pencroff. "Good heavens! Is it tobacco?"

"No," answered Cyrus Smith. "It's artemisia — Chinese mugwort to the scientists — and for us it's going to be tinder."

And in fact this mugwort, once properly dried, did give them a substance that caught fire very easily, especially later on, when the engineer had soaked it in potassium nitrate, of which the island had several beds — and potassium nitrate is nothing other than saltpeter.

That evening all the colonists gathered in the central chamber and ate a proper supper. Nab had made an agouti stew and a seasoned capybara ham, and with them they had the boiled tubers of "Caladium macrorhizum," a kind of herbaceous plant of the arum family, which in the tropics would have grown into the shape of a tree. These rootstocks tasted excellent and were very nourishing, much like the substance sold in England under the name of "Portland sago," and up to a point they could take the place of bread, which the colonists of Lincoln Island still did not have.

When supper was over, before turning in for the night, Cyrus Smith and his companions went out to take the air on the beach. It was eight o'clock in the evening. The night promised to be magnificent. The moon, which had been full five days before, had not yet risen, but the horizon was already silvering with those soft, pale tints that might be called the moon's dawn. At the southern zenith the constellations that circle the pole were shining in all their splendor, and among them all the Southern Cross, which the engineer had greeted a few days before from the top of Mount Franklin.

Cyrus Smith watched that splendid constellation for some time. It carries two stars of the first magnitude at its top and its foot, a star of the second magnitude on its left arm, and one of the third magnitude on its right.

Then, after some thought, he asked the boy:

"Harbert, isn't today April 15?"

"Yes, Mr. Cyrus," answered Harbert.

"Well, unless I'm mistaken, tomorrow will be one of the four days of the year when true time and mean time are the same. That means, my boy, that tomorrow, to within a few seconds, the sun will cross the meridian exactly at noon by the clocks. So if the weather's fine, I think I'll be able to get the island's longitude to within a few degrees."

"Without instruments? Without a sextant?" asked Gédéon Spilett.

"Yes," the engineer went on. "And since the night is clear, I'm going to try this very evening to get our latitude, by working out the height of the Southern Cross — that is, of the south pole — above the horizon. You understand, my friends, that before we take on any serious work of settling in, it isn't enough to have found out that this land is an island. We have to find out, as nearly as we can, how far it lies from the American continent, or from the Australian continent, or from the main island groups of the Pacific."

"Right," said the reporter. "Instead of building a house, it might be in our interest to build a boat, if we happen to be only a hundred miles or so from an inhabited coast."

"That's why," Cyrus Smith continued, "I'm going to try tonight to get the latitude of Lincoln Island, and tomorrow at noon I'll try to work out its longitude."

If the engineer had had a sextant — an instrument that uses mirrors to measure the angle between two distant objects with great precision — the operation would have presented no difficulty at all. That evening, from the height of the pole, and the next day, from the sun's passage across the meridian, he would have had the island's coordinates. But since he had no such instrument, he had to make up for it.

So Cyrus Smith went back into the Chimneys. By the light of the fire he whittled two small flat rulers and joined them together at one end, so as to make a kind of pair of compasses whose legs could be spread apart or brought together. The joint was held by a strong acacia thorn, taken from the dead wood of the woodpile.

When this instrument was finished, the engineer went back to the beach. But he had to take the height of the pole above a sharply drawn horizon — that is, a sea horizon — and Claw Cape hid the horizon to the south from him, so he had to look for a better place to observe from. The best would obviously have been the stretch of coast facing directly south, but to get there he would have had to cross the Mercy, which was deep at that point, and that was a problem.

Cyrus Smith therefore decided to make his observation from Prospect Plateau, and to allow for its height above sea level — a height he planned to work out the next day by a simple method of elementary geometry.

So the colonists made their way up onto the plateau, climbing along the left bank of the Mercy, and took their places on the edge that ran from northwest to southeast — that is, on the line of oddly jagged rocks that bordered the river.

This part of the plateau stood some fifty feet higher than the heights on the right bank, which sloped down two ways, to the tip of Claw Cape and to the island's southern coast. So nothing blocked the view, which took in half the horizon, from the cape all the way to Reptile End. To the south, that horizon, lit from below by the first glow of the moon, stood out sharply against the sky and could be sighted on with fair precision.

At that moment the Southern Cross appeared to the observer upside down, with the star Alpha marking its foot, the end nearer the south pole.

This constellation does not lie as close to the south pole as the North Star does to the north pole. Alpha is about twenty-seven degrees from it. But Cyrus Smith knew this and meant to allow for that distance in his calculation. He also took care to observe it at the moment when it was crossing the meridian below the pole, which would make his work simpler.

So Cyrus Smith aimed one leg of his wooden compasses at the sea horizon and the other at Alpha, as he would have aimed the telescopes of a repeating circle, and the opening between the two legs gave him the angle that separated Alpha from the horizon. To fix the angle so that it could not change, he pinned the two little boards of his instrument with thorns onto a third one laid across them, so that the spread between them was held firm.

Once that was done, all that remained was to work out the angle he had obtained, correcting the observation to sea level so as to allow for the dip of the horizon — the way a horizon seen from a height lies a little below the true level — and that meant measuring the height of the plateau. The value of this angle would then give the height of Alpha above the horizon, and from it the height of the pole above the horizon — in other words, the island's latitude, since the latitude of any point on the globe is always equal to the height of the pole above that point's horizon.

These calculations were put off until the next day, and by ten o'clock everyone was sound asleep.
