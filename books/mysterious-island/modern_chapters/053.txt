Chapter 12: A Lighted Window

_Exploring Serpentine Peninsula — Camp at the mouth of Fall River — Six hundred paces from the corral — Gédéon Spilett and Pencroff scout ahead — Their return — Everyone forward! — An open gate — A lighted window — By the light of the moon_

The next day, February 18, was given over to exploring the whole wooded stretch that made up the coast from Reptile End to Fall River. The colonists were able to search this forest thoroughly, since it was only three to four miles wide, lying as it did between the two shores of Serpentine Peninsula. The trees, with their great height and their thick spreading branches, bore witness to the power of the soil to make things grow, and here it was more astonishing than anywhere else on the island. It looked like a corner of the virgin forests of America or Central Africa, carried bodily into this temperate zone. All of which suggested that these magnificent trees found in the soil, damp in its upper layer but heated from within by volcanic fires, a warmth that could not belong to a temperate climate. The commonest kinds were exactly those kauris and eucalyptus trees, which grew here to gigantic size.

But the colonists had not come to admire these splendors of the plant world. They already knew that in this respect Lincoln Island would have deserved a place among the Canaries, whose first name was the Fortunate Isles. Now, alas, their island no longer belonged wholly to them. Others had taken possession of it; scoundrels were treading its soil, and they had to be destroyed to the last man.

On the west coast they found no further traces, however carefully they looked. No more footprints, no more broken branches on the trees, no more cold ashes, no more abandoned camps.

"That doesn't surprise me," Cyrus Smith told his companions. "The convicts landed somewhere near Jetsam Point, and they went straight into the Far West forests after crossing Shelduck Marsh. So they followed more or less the route we took when we left Granite House. That explains the traces we found in the woods. But once they reached the coast, the convicts saw clearly that they wouldn't find a decent hideout there, and that's when they headed back north and discovered the corral..."

"Where they may have gone back..." said Pencroff.

"I don't think so," answered the engineer, "because they must guess that our search will turn in that direction. For them the corral is only a place to get supplies, not a permanent camp."

"I agree with Cyrus," said the reporter. "In my opinion, it's among the spurs of Mount Franklin that the convicts will have looked for a den."

"Then straight to the corral, Mr. Cyrus!" cried Pencroff. "We've got to finish this, and so far we've wasted our time!"

"No, my friend," answered the engineer. "You're forgetting that we needed to know whether the Far West forests might hold some dwelling. Our exploration has a double purpose, Pencroff. If on the one hand we must punish crime, on the other we have a debt of gratitude to pay!"

"Well spoken, Mr. Cyrus," answered the sailor. "All the same, it's my belief we won't find that gentleman unless he wants us to!"

And really, Pencroff was only saying what they all thought. It was likely that the stranger's hiding place would prove no less mysterious than the stranger himself!

That evening the cart stopped at the mouth of Fall River. They set up camp for the night in the usual way and took the usual precautions. Harbert, once again the strong, healthy boy he had been before his illness, was making the most of this open-air life between the ocean breezes and the bracing air of the forests. His place was no longer in the cart but at the head of the caravan.

The next day, February 19, the colonists left the coast, where beyond the river's mouth basalt rocks of every shape were piled up so picturesquely, and went up the river along its left bank. The way had been partly cleared already by earlier trips made from the corral to the west coast. The colonists were now six miles from Mount Franklin.

The engineer's plan was this: to examine with great care the whole valley whose lowest line formed the bed of the river, and to approach the neighborhood of the corral cautiously. If the corral was occupied, they would take it by force. If it was not, they would dig in there and make it the base for operations whose goal would be the exploration of Mount Franklin.

The colonists approved this plan unanimously, and indeed they were impatient to have their island wholly back in their own hands!

So they made their way along the narrow valley that separated two of the mightiest spurs of Mount Franklin. The trees crowded the banks of the river but grew sparser toward the upper slopes of the volcano. It was hilly, rather broken ground, made for ambushes, and they ventured across it only with the greatest caution. Top and Jup went ahead as scouts, plunging to right and left into the thick undergrowth, each trying to outdo the other in cleverness and skill. But nothing showed that anyone had been along the banks of the stream lately; nothing announced that the convicts were there or anywhere near.

At about five in the evening the cart stopped roughly six hundred paces from the palisade. A half-circle curtain of tall trees still hid it from view.

The thing to do, then, was to scout the corral and find out whether it was occupied. To go there openly, in broad daylight, would be asking to be shot, if the convicts were lying in wait there at all — just as had happened to Harbert. It was better to wait until night had fallen.

Gédéon Spilett, however, wanted to look over the approaches to the corral without any more delay, and Pencroff, at the end of his patience, offered to go with him.

"No, my friends," answered the engineer. "Wait for nightfall. I won't let any of you expose himself in broad daylight."

"But, Mr. Cyrus..." the sailor replied, not much inclined to obey.

"I'm asking you, Pencroff," said the engineer.

"Have it your way!" answered the sailor, who found another outlet for his anger by treating the convicts to the roughest names in the whole seafaring vocabulary.

So the colonists stayed close to the cart and kept a careful watch on the nearby parts of the forest.

Three hours went by like that. The wind had dropped, and an absolute silence reigned under the great trees. The snap of the thinnest twig, a footstep on dry leaves, a body sliding through the grass, would all have been heard easily. Everything was quiet. Besides, Top, lying on the ground with his head stretched out on his paws, showed no sign of uneasiness.

At eight o'clock the evening seemed far enough along for the scouting to be done under good conditions. Gédéon Spilett said he was ready to go, with Pencroff. Cyrus Smith agreed. Top and Jup had to stay behind with the engineer, Harbert and Nab, because a bark or a cry at the wrong moment must not give the alarm.

"Don't take any rash chances," Cyrus Smith told the sailor and the reporter. "You're not there to take the corral, only to find out whether it's occupied or not."

"Agreed," answered Pencroff.

And the two of them set off.

Under the trees, because the foliage was so thick, a certain darkness already made it impossible to see anything more than thirty or forty feet away. The reporter and Pencroff moved forward with the most extreme care, stopping the moment any sound seemed suspicious to them.

They walked some distance apart, to make a smaller target for gunfire. And to tell the whole truth, they expected a shot to ring out at any moment.

Five minutes after leaving the cart, Gédéon Spilett and Pencroff reached the edge of the woods, facing the clearing at the far end of which the palisade rose.

They stopped. A little faint light still washed over the treeless meadow. Thirty paces away stood the gate of the corral, which seemed to be shut. Those thirty paces they would have to cross between the edge of the woods and the palisade were the danger zone, to borrow an expression from the science of gunnery. One or more bullets fired from the top of the palisade would have knocked down anyone who ventured into that zone.

Gédéon Spilett and the sailor were not men to hang back, but they knew that any recklessness on their part, though they would be its first victims, would fall afterward on their companions. If the two of them were killed, what would become of Cyrus Smith, Nab and Harbert?

But Pencroff, worked up at feeling himself so close to the corral, where he supposed the convicts had taken refuge, was about to push forward when the reporter held him back with a firm hand.

"In a few moments it'll be completely dark," Gédéon Spilett whispered in Pencroff's ear, "and that will be the time to act."

Pencroff, his hand clenched tight on the stock of his gun, held himself in and waited, grumbling.

Soon the last glimmers of twilight faded away entirely. The darkness, which seemed to pour out of the thick forest, flooded the clearing. Mount Franklin rose like an enormous screen against the western horizon, and night came on quickly, as it does in regions already fairly low in latitude. This was the moment.

Ever since they had taken up their post at the edge of the woods, the reporter and Pencroff had not taken their eyes off the palisade. The corral seemed to be completely deserted. The top of the palisade made a line a little blacker than the surrounding shadow, and nothing broke its sharp edge. Still, if the convicts were there, they would have had to post one of their number as a lookout, to protect themselves against any surprise.

Gédéon Spilett pressed his companion's hand, and the two of them crawled toward the corral, their guns ready to fire.

They reached the gate of the enclosure without a single streak of light cutting through the darkness.

Pencroff tried to push the gate, which, as he and the reporter had supposed, was shut. However, the sailor was able to make sure that the outside bars had not been put in place.

From this they could conclude that the convicts were in the corral at that moment, and that they had most likely made the gate fast from the inside so that it could not be forced.

Gédéon Spilett and Pencroff listened.

Not a sound inside the enclosure. The mouflons and the goats, no doubt asleep in their stables, did nothing at all to disturb the calm of the night.

Hearing nothing, the reporter and the sailor wondered whether they ought to climb the palisade and get into the corral. That would be going against Cyrus Smith's instructions.

True, the attempt might succeed — but it might fail, too. And if the convicts suspected nothing, if they knew nothing of the expedition launched against them, if in short there was at this moment a chance of taking them by surprise, ought they to throw that chance away by rashly venturing over the palisade?

The reporter thought not. He judged it sensible to wait until the colonists were all together before trying to get into the corral. One thing was certain: it was possible to get right up to the palisade without being seen, and the enclosure did not seem to be guarded. With that settled, all that remained was to go back to the cart and decide what to do.

Pencroff probably shared this view, for he raised no objection to following the reporter when the reporter fell back into the woods.

A few minutes later the engineer had been told how matters stood.

"Well," he said, after thinking it over, "I now have reason to believe that the convicts are not at the corral."

"We'll find out soon enough," answered Pencroff, "once we've climbed the palisade."

"To the corral, my friends!" said Cyrus Smith.

"Do we leave the cart in the woods?" asked Nab.

"No," answered the engineer. "It's our supply wagon for ammunition and food, and if need be it will serve us as a breastwork."

"Forward, then!" said Gédéon Spilett.

The cart came out of the woods and began to roll silently toward the palisade. The darkness was deep now, the silence as complete as when Pencroff and the reporter had crawled away over the ground. The thick grass smothered the sound of their footsteps entirely.

The colonists were ready to fire. Jup, on Pencroff's orders, kept to the rear. Nab held Top on a leash so that he would not dash ahead.

The clearing soon came into view. It was deserted. Without hesitating, the little band headed for the enclosure. In a short space of time they had crossed the danger zone. Not a shot had been fired. When the cart reached the palisade, it stopped. Nab stayed at the onaggas' heads to keep them still. The engineer, the reporter, Harbert and Pencroff then went toward the gate, to see whether it was barricaded on the inside...

One of the two halves of the gate was open!

"But what were you telling me?" asked the engineer, turning to the sailor and Gédéon Spilett.

The two of them were dumbfounded.

"On my soul," said Pencroff, "that gate was shut a little while ago!"

At that the colonists hesitated. Had the convicts been in the corral, then, at the very moment Pencroff and the reporter were scouting it? There could be no doubt of it, since the gate, which had been shut then, could only have been opened by them! Were they still inside, or had one of them just gone out?

All these questions leaped into everyone's mind at once, but how were they to answer them?

At that moment Harbert, who had gone a few steps inside the enclosure, drew back hastily and seized Cyrus Smith's hand.

"What is it?" asked the engineer.

"A light!"

"In the house?"

"Yes!"

All five of them moved up to the gate, and sure enough, through the panes of the window facing them, they saw a faint glimmer flickering.

Cyrus Smith made up his mind quickly.

"It's a unique chance," he told his companions, "to find the convicts shut up in that house, expecting nothing! They're ours! Forward!"

The colonists slipped into the enclosure, guns ready to raise to their shoulders. The cart had been left outside in the care of Jup and Top, who had been tied to it as a precaution.

Cyrus Smith, Pencroff and Gédéon Spilett on one side, Harbert and Nab on the other, moved along the palisade and looked over that part of the corral, which was completely dark and deserted.

In a few moments they were all near the house, in front of its door, which was shut.

Cyrus Smith signaled to his companions with his hand not to move, and he went up to the window, which was faintly lit by the light inside.

He looked into the single room that made up the ground floor of the house.

On the table a lantern was burning. Beside the table was the bed that Ayrton used to sleep in.

On the bed lay the body of a man.

Suddenly Cyrus Smith drew back, and in a stifled voice he cried:

"Ayrton!"

At once the door was burst in rather than opened, and the colonists rushed into the room.

Ayrton seemed to be asleep. His face showed that he had suffered long and cruelly. On his wrists and ankles there were broad bruises.

Cyrus Smith bent over him.

"Ayrton!" cried the engineer, gripping the arm of the man he had just found again in such unexpected circumstances.

At the sound of his name Ayrton opened his eyes, and, looking straight at Cyrus Smith, then at the others, he cried:

"You? You?"

"Ayrton! Ayrton!" Cyrus Smith repeated.

"Where am I?"

"In the house at the corral!"

"Alone?"

"Yes!"

"But they're coming back!" cried Ayrton. "Defend yourselves! Defend yourselves!"

And Ayrton fell back, exhausted.

"Spilett," the engineer said then, "we may be attacked at any moment. Bring the cart into the corral. Then barricade the gate, and all of you come back here."

Pencroff, Nab and the reporter hurried to carry out the engineer's orders. There was not a moment to lose. Perhaps the cart was already in the convicts' hands!

In an instant the reporter and his two companions had crossed the corral and reached the gate in the palisade, behind which they could hear Top growling low.

The engineer, leaving Ayrton for a moment, went out of the house, ready to shoot. Harbert was at his side. Both of them kept watch on the crest of the spur that overlooked the corral. If the convicts were lying in wait up there, they could pick the colonists off one after another.

At that moment the moon appeared in the east above the black curtain of the forest, and a white sheet of light spread across the inside of the enclosure. The whole corral lit up, with its clumps of trees, the little stream that watered it, and its broad carpet of grass. On the mountain side, the house and part of the palisade stood out white. On the opposite side, toward the gate, the enclosure remained dark.

Soon a black mass came into view. It was the cart, rolling into the circle of light, and Cyrus Smith could hear the sound of the gate as his companions shut it and made its two halves fast from the inside.

But at that moment Top, snapping his leash with a violent jerk, began to bark furiously and rushed toward the far end of the corral, to the right of the house.

"Look out, my friends! Take aim!..." shouted Cyrus Smith.

The colonists had shouldered their guns and were waiting for the moment to fire. Top was still barking, and Jup, running toward the dog, gave a series of shrill whistles.

The colonists followed him and came out on the bank of the little brook, shaded by tall trees.

And there, in the full light of the moon, what did they see?

Five bodies, stretched out on the bank!

They were the bodies of the convicts who, four months before, had landed on Lincoln Island!
