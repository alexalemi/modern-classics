Chapter 7: A Sure and Faithful Messenger

_The reporter and Pencroff in the corral — Harbert carried in — The sailor's despair — The reporter and the engineer confer — The treatment — Some hope returns — How to warn Nab? — A sure and faithful messenger — Nab's answer_

At Harbert's cry, Pencroff had dropped his gun and rushed to him.

"They've killed him!" he cried. "My boy! They've killed him!"

Cyrus Smith and Gédéon Spilett had run to Harbert. The reporter listened to find out whether the poor boy's heart was still beating.

"He's alive," he said. "But we've got to move him..."

"To Granite House? That's impossible!" answered the engineer.

"To the corral, then!" cried Pencroff.

"One moment," said Cyrus Smith.

And he dashed off to the left, to work his way around the palisade. There he found himself face to face with a convict, who took aim at him and put a bullet through his hat. A few seconds later, before the man even had time to fire his second shot, he fell, struck to the heart by Cyrus Smith's dagger, which was even surer than his gun.

Meanwhile Gédéon Spilett and the sailor hoisted themselves up at the corners of the palisade, swung their legs over the top, and jumped down inside. They knocked away the props that held the gate shut from within, rushed into the house, which was empty, and soon poor Harbert was lying on Ayrton's bed.

A few moments later Cyrus Smith was beside him.

When he saw Harbert lying there senseless, the sailor's grief was terrible. He sobbed, he wept, he wanted to dash his head against the wall. Neither the engineer nor the reporter could calm him. Their own emotion was choking them. They could not speak.

All the same, they did everything in their power to fight death for the poor boy who lay dying before their eyes. After all the adventures his life had been strewn with, Gédéon Spilett had some practical knowledge of everyday medicine. He knew a little about everything, and more than once he had found himself having to treat wounds made by a blade or by a gun. So, with Cyrus Smith's help, he set about giving Harbert the care his condition called for.

The first thing that struck the reporter was the deep stupor that held the boy, a stupor caused either by loss of blood or else by shock, if the bullet had hit a bone hard enough to jolt the whole body.

Harbert was extremely pale, and his pulse was so weak that Gédéon Spilett could feel it beat only at long intervals, as though it were about to stop. At the same time his senses and his mind seemed almost entirely gone. These were very grave symptoms.

They bared Harbert's chest, stanched the blood with handkerchiefs, and washed the chest with cold water.

The wound appeared: a bruised, torn wound where the bullet had struck. There was an oval hole in the chest between the third and fourth ribs. That was where the bullet had hit Harbert.

Cyrus Smith and Gédéon Spilett then turned the poor boy over. He let out a moan so faint that it might have been his last breath.

A second wound was bleeding on Harbert's back, and the bullet that had struck him dropped out of it at once.

"Thank God!" said the reporter. "The bullet didn't stay in his body, and we won't have to take it out."

"But the heart?..." asked Cyrus Smith.

"The heart wasn't touched, or Harbert would be dead!"

"Dead!" cried Pencroff, with a roar.

The sailor had heard only the reporter's last words.

"No, Pencroff," Cyrus Smith answered. "No! He isn't dead. His pulse is still beating! He even moaned. But for your boy's own sake, calm down. We need to keep our heads. Don't make us lose them, my friend."

Pencroff fell silent, but a reaction set in, and great tears flooded down his face.

Meanwhile Gédéon Spilett was trying to gather his memories and go about things methodically. From what he had seen, he had no doubt that the bullet had gone in at the front and come out at the back. But what damage had it done on its way through? Which vital organs had it reached? That was something a professional surgeon could hardly have said at that moment, let alone the reporter.

Still, he knew one thing: he would have to keep the injured tissue from swelling up and choking itself with inflammation, and then fight the local inflammation and the fever that would follow such a wound — a wound that might well be fatal! And what dressings, what remedies against inflammation could he use? How could he head that inflammation off?

In any case, the important thing was to dress both wounds without delay. Gédéon Spilett did not think it necessary to start the blood flowing again by washing them in warm water and pressing their edges. The bleeding had been very heavy, and Harbert was already far too weak from the loss of blood.

So the reporter decided to do no more than wash the two wounds with cold water.

Harbert was laid on his left side, and they kept him in that position.

"He mustn't move," said Gédéon Spilett. "He's in the best position for the wounds in his back and chest to drain freely, and he needs absolute rest."

"What! We can't carry him to Granite House?" asked Pencroff.

"No, Pencroff," answered the reporter.

"Damnation!" cried the sailor, shaking his fist at the sky.

"Pencroff!" said Cyrus Smith.

Gédéon Spilett had gone back to examining the wounded boy with the greatest care. Harbert was still so dreadfully pale that the reporter felt shaken.

"Cyrus," he said, "I'm not a doctor... I'm terribly unsure what to do... You have to help me with your advice, your experience!..."

"Steady... my friend," answered the engineer, gripping the reporter's hand. "Judge it coolly... think of only one thing: we have to save Harbert!"

These words gave Gédéon Spilett back the self-command that a sharp sense of his responsibility had made him lose in a moment of discouragement. He sat down by the bed. Cyrus Smith stood. Pencroff had torn up his shirt and, without thinking, was picking it apart into lint for dressings.

Gédéon Spilett then explained to Cyrus Smith that, above all, he thought he ought to stop the bleeding — but not close the two wounds or try to make them heal over at once. There had been a puncture inside, and the pus must not be allowed to collect in the chest.

Cyrus Smith agreed completely, and it was decided that they would dress the two wounds without trying to close them by drawing their edges together. Fortunately, it did not seem that they would need to be opened up any further.

And now, did the colonists have anything that would work against the inflammation that was bound to come?

Yes! They had something, for nature has given it out generously. They had cold water — the most powerful soothing agent that can be used against inflamed wounds, the most effective remedy in serious cases, and one that every doctor has now adopted. Cold water has the further advantage of leaving the wound perfectly at rest and sparing it any premature dressing, a considerable advantage, since experience has shown that contact with the air does harm during the first few days.

Gédéon Spilett and Cyrus Smith reasoned it out this way, by plain common sense, and they acted as the best surgeon would have done. Linen compresses were laid on poor Harbert's two wounds, and these had to be kept constantly soaked in cold water.

The sailor had first of all lit a fire in the fireplace of the house, which was well stocked with the necessities of life. Maple sugar and medicinal plants — the very ones the boy had gathered on the banks of Lake Grant — let them make a few cooling herbal teas, and they got him to drink them without his knowing it. His fever was extremely high, and the whole day and the whole night went by without his regaining consciousness. Harbert's life hung by a thread, and that thread might snap at any moment.

The next day, November 12, Cyrus Smith and his companions began to have some hope. Harbert had come out of his long stupor. He opened his eyes. He recognized Cyrus Smith, the reporter, Pencroff. He said two or three words. He did not know what had happened. They told him, and Gédéon Spilett begged him to keep absolutely still, telling him that his life was not in danger and that his wounds would heal over in a few days. Besides, Harbert was in almost no pain, and the cold water they kept pouring over the wounds without a break was holding off all inflammation. The wounds were draining steadily, the fever showed no sign of rising, and there was reason to hope that this terrible wound would not end in disaster. Little by little the weight lifted from Pencroff's heart. He was like a Sister of Charity, like a mother at her child's bedside.

Harbert dozed off again, but his sleep seemed better now.

"Tell me again that you've got hope, Mr. Spilett!" said Pencroff. "Tell me again that you'll save Harbert!"

"Yes, we'll save him!" answered the reporter. "The wound is serious, and the bullet may even have gone through the lung, but a hole in that organ isn't fatal."

"God hear you!" Pencroff said again.

As can easily be imagined, in the twenty-four hours since they had come to the corral, the colonists had had no other thought than caring for Harbert. They had worried neither about the danger that might threaten them if the convicts came back nor about the precautions to take for the future.

But that day, while Pencroff watched at the sickbed, Cyrus Smith and the reporter talked over what ought to be done.

First of all, they went over the whole corral. There was no trace of Ayrton. Had the unfortunate man been dragged off by his former accomplices? Had they taken him by surprise in the corral? Had he fought and been killed in the fight? This last guess was only too likely. As he was climbing the palisade, Gédéon Spilett had clearly seen one of the convicts running away along the southern shoulder of Mount Franklin, with Top racing after him. It was one of the men whose boat had been smashed on the rocks at the mouth of the Mercy. Besides, the one Cyrus Smith had killed, whose body was found outside the palisade, was certainly one of Bob Harvey's gang.

As for the corral, it had not yet been damaged in any way. The gates were shut, and the animals had not been able to scatter into the forest. Nor was there any sign of a struggle, any damage, either to the house or to the palisade. Only the ammunition Ayrton had been supplied with had disappeared along with him.

"The poor man must have been taken by surprise," said Cyrus Smith, "and since he was a man who would defend himself, he must have been killed."

"Yes! That's what I'm afraid of!" answered the reporter. "And then, no doubt, the convicts settled in at the corral, where they found plenty of everything, and they ran off only when they saw us coming. It's obvious, too, that by then Ayrton, dead or alive, wasn't here anymore."

"We'll have to beat the forest," said the engineer, "and rid the island of these scoundrels. Pencroff's instincts weren't wrong when he wanted us to hunt them down like wild beasts. It would have spared us a great deal of misery!"

"Yes," answered the reporter, "but now we have the right to show no mercy!"

"In any case," said the engineer, "we're forced to wait a while and stay at the corral until Harbert can be carried safely to Granite House."

"But what about Nab?" asked the reporter.

"Nab is safe."

"And if he gets worried because we haven't come back, and risks coming here?"

"He mustn't come!" Cyrus Smith answered quickly. "He'd be murdered on the way!"

"But it's very likely he'll try to join us!"

"Ah! If the telegraph were still working, we could warn him! But that's impossible now! And we can't leave Pencroff and Harbert here alone!... All right, I'll go to Granite House by myself."

"No, no, Cyrus!" answered the reporter. "You mustn't put yourself in danger! Your courage wouldn't do any good. Those scoundrels are obviously watching the corral. They're lying in wait in the thick woods all around it, and if you left, we'd soon have two disasters to mourn instead of one!"

"But Nab?" the engineer kept saying. "He's had no news of us for twenty-four hours! He'll want to come!"

"And since he'll be even less on his guard than we would be ourselves," answered Gédéon Spilett, "they'll shoot him!..."

"Is there no way at all to warn him?"

While the engineer was thinking, his eyes fell on Top, who was going back and forth and seemed to be saying, "Well, what about me? Aren't I here?"

"Top!" cried Cyrus Smith.

The dog bounded up at his master's call.

"Yes, Top will go!" said the reporter, who had understood the engineer. "Top will get through where we couldn't! He'll take the news from the corral to Granite House, and he'll bring us back the news from Granite House!"

"Quick!" answered Cyrus Smith. "Quick!"

Gédéon Spilett had swiftly torn a page out of his notebook, and on it he wrote these lines:

    "Harbert wounded. We are at the corral. Be on your guard. Do not leave Granite House. Have the convicts shown themselves near you? Answer by Top."

This brief note held everything Nab needed to learn, and at the same time it asked him everything the colonists needed to know. It was folded and tied to Top's collar where it could not be missed.

"Top! Good dog," said the engineer, stroking him. "Nab, Top! Nab! Go! Go!"

Top leaped up at these words. He understood; he guessed what was being asked of him. He knew the Corral Road by heart. He could cover it in less than half an hour, and there was reason to hope that where neither Cyrus Smith nor the reporter could have ventured without danger, Top, running through the grass or along the edge of the woods, would get by unseen.

The engineer went to the gate of the corral and pushed open one of its two halves.

"Nab! Top, Nab!" the engineer said once more, stretching out his hand toward Granite House.

Top sprang outside and vanished almost at once.

"He'll get there!" said the reporter.

"Yes, and he'll come back, the faithful animal!"

"What time is it?" asked Gédéon Spilett.

"Ten o'clock."

"He could be here in an hour. We'll watch for him."

The gate of the corral was shut again. The engineer and the reporter went back into the house. Harbert was sound asleep by then. Pencroff kept his compresses wet all the time.

Seeing that there was nothing more to be done for the moment, Gédéon Spilett busied himself with getting some food ready, while keeping a careful watch on the part of the palisade that backed onto the mountain's shoulder, where an attack might come from.

The colonists waited for Top's return, and not without anxiety. A little before eleven o'clock, Cyrus Smith and the reporter were standing behind the gate, carbines in hand, ready to open it at the first bark of their dog. They had no doubt that if Top had reached Granite House safely, Nab would have sent him straight back.

The two of them had been there for about ten minutes when a shot rang out, followed at once by a burst of barking.

The engineer opened the gate, saw a last wisp of smoke a hundred paces off in the woods, and fired in that direction.

Almost at the same moment Top bounded into the corral, and the gate was quickly shut again.

"Top! Top!" cried the engineer, taking the dog's big, good-natured head in his arms.

A note was tied to his neck, and Cyrus Smith read these words, written in Nab's large handwriting:

    "No pirates around Granite House. I won't budge. Poor Mr. Harbert!"
