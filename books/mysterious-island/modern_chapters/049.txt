Chapter 8: The Convicts Near the Corral

_The convicts near the corral — A temporary arrangement — Harbert's treatment continues — Pencroff's first rejoicings — Looking back at the past — What the future holds — Cyrus Smith's ideas on the subject_

So the convicts were still there, spying on the corral, determined to kill the colonists one after another! There was nothing left to do but treat them as wild beasts. Yet great precautions had to be taken, because at that moment the wretches held every advantage of position. They could see without being seen. They could take their enemies by surprise with a sudden attack, and they could not be taken by surprise themselves.

Cyrus Smith therefore arranged things so that they could live at the corral, whose stores, besides, would last for a fairly long time. Ayrton's house had been furnished with everything needed for daily life, and the convicts, frightened off by the colonists' arrival, had not had time to plunder it. It was likely, as Gédéon Spilett pointed out, that things had happened this way. The six convicts, once landed on the island, had followed its southern coast. After going along both shores of Serpentine Peninsula, and being in no mood to venture into the Far West forests, they had reached the mouth of Fall River. From there they had gone up the right bank of the stream and come to the spurs of Mount Franklin, where it was natural that they should look for some hiding place among them, and they could not have been long in discovering the corral, which was empty at the time. There, very probably, they had settled in to wait for the moment to carry out their abominable plans. Ayrton's arrival had surprised them, but they had managed to seize the unfortunate man, and... what followed was easy enough to guess!

Now the convicts — down to five, it was true, but well armed — were prowling in the woods, and to venture in among the trees was to lay oneself open to their shots, with no way of either warding them off or seeing them coming.

"Wait! There's nothing else we can do!" Cyrus Smith kept repeating. "When Harbert is well again, we can organize a general sweep of the island and settle with these convicts. That will be the purpose of our great expedition, and at the same time..."

"...the search for our mysterious protector," added Gédéon Spilett, finishing the engineer's sentence. "Ah! You have to admit, my dear Cyrus, that this time his protection let us down — and at the very moment we needed it most!"

"Who knows?" answered the engineer.

"What do you mean?" asked the reporter.

"That we haven't come to the end of our troubles, my dear Spilett, and that this powerful intervention may yet have a chance to act. But that isn't the question now. Harbert's life comes before everything."

That was the colonists' most painful concern. Several days went by, and the poor boy's condition, fortunately, had not grown worse. Now, every day won from the illness counted for a great deal. The cold water, always kept at the right temperature, had entirely prevented the wounds from becoming inflamed. It even seemed to the reporter that this water, which was slightly sulfurous — the nearness of the volcano explained that — acted more directly to help the wounds heal over. There was much less pus, and thanks to the unceasing care that surrounded him, Harbert was coming back to life, and his fever was tending to fall. He was kept, besides, on a strict diet, and so his weakness was extreme, as it had to be. But he never lacked for herbal teas, and complete rest was doing him the greatest good.

Cyrus Smith, Gédéon Spilett and Pencroff had become very skillful at dressing the young patient's wounds. Every scrap of linen in the house had been sacrificed. Harbert's wounds were covered with compresses and lint and bound neither too tightly nor too loosely, so as to help them heal without setting off any inflammation. The reporter brought extreme care to these dressings, knowing well how much they mattered, and he kept repeating to his companions what most doctors readily admit: that a well-made dressing is perhaps rarer to see than a well-done operation.

After ten days, on November 22, Harbert was noticeably better. He had begun to take a little food. The color was coming back into his cheeks, and his kind eyes smiled at his nurses. He chatted a little, in spite of the efforts of Pencroff, who talked the whole time himself to keep him from speaking and told him the most unlikely stories. Harbert had asked him about Ayrton, whom he was surprised not to see near him, thinking he must be at the corral. But the sailor, not wanting to distress Harbert, had simply answered that Ayrton had gone to join Nab and help defend Granite House.

"Ha!" he would say. "Those pirates! Those gentlemen have lost all right to any consideration! And Mr. Smith wanted to win them over with kindness! I'll send them some kindness, all right — good lead, the proper caliber!"

"And nobody's seen them since?" asked Harbert.

"No, my boy," answered the sailor, "but we'll find them again, and when you're well, we'll see whether those cowards who shoot people in the back will dare to attack us face to face!"

"I'm still very weak, my poor Pencroff!"

"Eh! Your strength will come back little by little! What's a bullet through the chest? A mere joke! I've seen plenty worse, and I'm none the worse for it!"

In short, things seemed to be going as well as they could, and as long as no complications set in, Harbert's recovery could be considered certain. But what would the colonists' situation have been if his condition had worsened — if, for example, the bullet had stayed lodged in his body, or if his arm or his leg had had to be amputated!

"No," Gédéon Spilett said more than once, "I've never thought of a thing like that without shuddering!"

"And yet, if you'd had to act," Cyrus Smith answered him one day, "you wouldn't have hesitated?"

"No, Cyrus!" said Gédéon Spilett. "But God be thanked for sparing us that!"

As in so many other situations, the colonists had called on that logic of plain common sense which had served them so often, and once again, thanks to their general knowledge, they had succeeded! But would the moment not come when all their science would fail them? They were alone on this island. Yet people complete one another by living in society; they need one another. Cyrus Smith knew that well, and sometimes he wondered whether some circumstance might not arise that they would be powerless to overcome!

It seemed to him, besides, that he and his companions, so fortunate until then, had entered an unlucky period. In the more than two and a half years since they had escaped from Richmond, one could say that everything had gone their way. The island had supplied them generously with minerals, plants and animals, and if nature had showered gifts on them without end, their science had known how to make use of what she offered. The material well-being of the colony was, so to speak, complete. More than that, on certain occasions an inexplicable influence had come to their aid!... But all of that could last only for a time!

In short, Cyrus Smith believed he could see their luck beginning to turn against them.

And indeed, the convicts' ship had appeared in the waters of the island, and if those pirates had been destroyed almost by a miracle, six of them, at least, had escaped the disaster. They had landed on the island, and the five who survived were all but impossible to catch. Ayrton had beyond any doubt been murdered by those wretches, who had firearms, and the very first time they used them, Harbert had fallen, struck almost to death. Were these, then, the first blows that hostile fortune was dealing the colonists? That was what Cyrus Smith asked himself! That was what he often repeated to the reporter. And it seemed to them, too, that the intervention which had been so strange, but so effective, and which had served them so well until now, was failing them at last. This mysterious being, whoever he might be, whose existence they could not deny — had he left the island, then? Had he died in his turn?

To these questions no answer was possible. But let no one imagine that Cyrus Smith and his companion, because they talked over such things, were the kind of men to despair! Far from it. They looked the situation in the face, they weighed the chances, they prepared themselves for whatever might happen, they stood firm and upright before the future, and if adversity was at last to strike them, it would find in them men ready to fight it.
