Chapter 1: The Hurricane of 1865

_The hurricane of 1865 — Cries in the air — A balloon carried off by a waterspout — The torn envelope — Nothing but sea in sight — Five passengers — What happens in the basket — A coast on the horizon — How the drama ends_

"Are we going up again?"

"No! Just the opposite! We're going down!"

"Worse than that, Mr. Cyrus! We're falling!"

"For God's sake, throw out ballast!"

"That was the last sack!"

"Is the balloon rising?"

"No!"

"I can hear something like waves splashing!"

"The sea's right under the basket!"

"It can't be five hundred feet below us!"

Then a powerful voice tore through the air, and these words rang out:

"Throw out everything that has any weight! Everything! And God help us!"

Those were the words that burst out in the sky above the vast watery desert of the Pacific at about four o'clock in the afternoon on March 23, 1865.

No doubt nobody has forgotten the terrible northeasterly gale that broke loose at the height of that year's spring equinox, when the barometer fell to seven hundred and ten millimeters. It was a hurricane, without a single lull, and it lasted from March 18 to March 26. The damage it did was immense, in America, in Europe, and in Asia, across a band eighteen hundred miles wide that ran slantwise to the equator from the thirty-fifth parallel north to the fortieth parallel south. Cities flattened, forests torn up by the roots, shores laid waste by mountains of water that came rushing in like tidal bores, ships flung up on the coast — the shipping registers of the Bureau Veritas counted them by the hundreds — whole districts leveled by waterspouts that ground up everything in their path, several thousand people crushed on land or swallowed at sea: that was the evidence of its fury that this tremendous hurricane left behind it. In the scale of its disasters it outdid even the two that had devastated Havana and Guadeloupe so horribly, the one on October 25, 1810, the other on July 26, 1825.

Now, at the very moment when so many catastrophes were taking place on land and sea, a drama no less gripping was being played out in the churning air.

A balloon, carried like a ball on the top of a waterspout and caught in the whirling motion of that column of air, was racing through space at ninety miles an hour, spinning round and round as though some aerial Maelström had seized it.

Beneath the neck at the bottom of the balloon swung a basket holding five passengers, barely visible in the thick vapors, mixed with spray, that trailed down to the surface of the ocean.

Where had this balloon come from, this plaything of the frightful storm? From what point on the globe had it set out? It obviously could not have taken off during the hurricane. But the hurricane had been raging for five days already, and its first signs had shown themselves on the 18th. So one would have been justified in thinking that the balloon had come from very far away, since it could not have covered less than two thousand miles in every twenty-four hours.

In any case, the passengers could have had no way of estimating how far they had come since setting out, because they had nothing at all to take their bearings by. In fact, a curious thing must have happened: carried along in the midst of the storm's violence, they did not feel it. They were moving, they were spinning, without sensing anything of the rotation or of their headlong travel sideways through the air. Their eyes could not pierce the thick fog that heaped up beneath the basket. All around them was mist. The clouds were so dense that they could not have said whether it was day or night. No glint of light, no sound from inhabited lands, no roar of the ocean could have reached them in that dark immensity as long as they stayed in the upper heights. Only their rapid descent could have told them of the danger they were in above the waves.

Meanwhile the balloon, lightened of heavy things — ammunition, weapons, provisions — had risen again into the upper layers of the atmosphere, to a height of four thousand five hundred feet. Once they realized that the sea lay beneath the basket, the passengers decided that the dangers above were less fearsome than the dangers below. They had not hesitated to throw even their most useful possessions overboard, and they were trying not to lose another breath of the gas — the very soul of their machine — that held them up over the abyss.

The night passed in an anxiety that would have killed less determined spirits. Then day returned, and with the day the hurricane began to slacken. From the very start of that day, March 24, there were signs of calm. At dawn the clouds, thinner and more vaporous, had climbed back into the heights of the sky. Within a few hours the waterspout spread out at the top and broke apart. The wind dropped from a hurricane to what sailors call "a strong gale" — that is, the speed at which the layers of air were rushing along was cut in half. It was still what sailors call "a three-reef breeze," a wind that makes them shorten sail three times over, but the improvement in the turmoil of the elements was considerable all the same.

Toward eleven o'clock the lower air had noticeably cleared. The atmosphere had that damp clarity which you can see, and even feel, after the passage of great storms. The hurricane did not seem to have gone any farther west. It seemed to have killed itself. Perhaps, after the waterspout broke, it had drained away in sheets of electricity, as sometimes happens with the typhoons of the Indian Ocean.

But at about that same hour, anyone watching could have seen once more that the balloon was slowly sinking, in one continuous movement, into the lower layers of the air. It even seemed to be deflating little by little, its envelope stretching and sagging, changing from the shape of a sphere to the shape of an egg.

By noon the balloon was floating only two thousand feet above the sea. It held fifty thousand cubic feet of gas, and thanks to that great capacity it had clearly been able to stay aloft for a long time, whether by climbing to great altitudes or by traveling horizontally.

Now the passengers threw out the last things still weighing the basket down: the little food they had kept back, everything, down to the small odds and ends in their pockets. And one of them, hoisting himself up onto the ring where the cords of the net came together, tried to tie the neck of the balloon shut as tightly as he could.

It was plain that the passengers could no longer keep the balloon in the upper air. They were running out of gas!

So they were lost!

For it was neither a continent nor even an island that stretched out below them. The whole expanse offered not a single place to land, not one solid surface for their anchor to bite into.

It was the immense sea, its waves still smashing together with incomparable violence! It was the ocean with no limits in sight, even for them, high above it, their gaze sweeping a circle forty miles in radius! It was that liquid plain, beaten without mercy and lashed by the hurricane, which must have looked to them like a stampede of wild-maned waves with a vast net of white crests flung over them! Not a scrap of land in sight, not a ship!

So they had to stop the descent at any cost, to keep the balloon from being swallowed up by the waves. And that urgent task was clearly what the passengers in the basket were working at. But in spite of their efforts the balloon kept sinking, while at the same time it was driven along with tremendous speed in the direction of the wind — that is, from northeast to southwest.

A terrible situation for these unfortunate men! They had obviously lost all control of the balloon. Nothing they tried could work. The envelope was deflating more and more. The gas was escaping, and there was no possible way to hold it in. Their descent was visibly speeding up, and at one o'clock in the afternoon the basket was hanging no more than six hundred feet above the ocean.

The truth was that nothing could stop the gas from leaking, because it was pouring freely out through a tear in the envelope.

By lightening the basket of everything it held, the passengers had been able to keep themselves in the air a few hours longer. But the inevitable catastrophe could only be put off, and if no land appeared before nightfall, passengers, basket, and balloon would vanish for good beneath the waves.

The only maneuver left to try was tried at this point. The balloon's passengers were clearly people of great energy, people who knew how to look death in the face. Not a single complaint could be heard from their lips. They were resolved to fight to the last second and to do everything they could to delay their fall. The basket was nothing but a kind of wicker box, useless as a boat, and if it fell into the sea there would be no way at all to keep it afloat.

At two o'clock the balloon was barely four hundred feet above the waves.

At that moment a manly voice — the voice of a man whose heart fear could not reach — made itself heard. Other voices, no less determined, answered it.

"Has everything been thrown out?"

"No! There's still ten thousand francs in gold!"

A heavy bag dropped into the sea at once.

"Is the balloon rising?"

"A little, but it won't be long before it falls again!"

"What's left to throw out?"

"Nothing!"

"Yes there is! The basket!"

"Grab hold of the net! Overboard with the basket!"

That was in fact the one last way left to lighten the balloon. The ropes that tied the basket to the ring were cut, and the balloon, after its fall, shot back up two thousand feet.

The five passengers had hauled themselves up into the net above the ring and were clinging there in the mesh, staring down into the abyss.

Everyone knows how sensitive balloons are to any change of weight. Throw out the lightest object and the balloon moves up or down. Floating in the air, it behaves like a scale of mathematical precision. So it is easy to understand that when it is relieved of a relatively large weight, it moves a long way, and suddenly. That is what happened now.

But after hanging in balance for a moment up in the higher air, the balloon began to sink again. The gas was escaping through the tear, and there was no way to mend it.

The passengers had done everything they could do. No human means could save them now. They had nothing left to count on but the help of God.

At four o'clock the balloon was only five hundred feet above the surface of the water.

A loud bark rang out. A dog was traveling with the passengers, hanging on in the mesh of the net beside his master.

"Top's seen something!" cried one of the passengers.

And at once a strong voice rang out:

"Land! Land!"

Driven steadily southwest by the wind, the balloon had covered a considerable distance since dawn — hundreds of miles — and a fairly high piece of land had in fact just appeared in that direction.

But that land was still thirty miles away to leeward. It would take a good hour to reach it, and that only if they did not drift off course. An hour! Wouldn't the balloon lose all the gas it had left before then?

That was the terrible question! The passengers could clearly see that solid spot, which they had to reach at any cost. They did not know what it was, island or continent, since they barely knew what part of the world the hurricane had carried them to! But that land, whether inhabited or not, whether it would welcome them or not — they had to get there!

By four o'clock, though, it was plain that the balloon could not hold itself up any longer. It was skimming the surface of the sea. The crests of the enormous waves had already licked the bottom of the net several times, making it heavier still, and the balloon now lifted only halfway, like a bird with lead shot in its wing.

Half an hour later the land was only a mile off, but the balloon, exhausted, limp, stretched out of shape and crumpled into great folds, kept gas only in its upper part. The passengers clinging to the net were still too heavy for it, and soon, half plunged in the sea, they were being battered by the furious waves. Then the envelope bellied out like a pocket, and the wind, rushing into it, drove it along like a ship running before the wind. Perhaps it would reach the coast that way!

It was only two cable lengths from shore when terrible cries burst from four throats at once. The balloon, which had seemed as if it would never rise again, had just made one more unexpected leap after being struck by a tremendous wave. As though suddenly relieved of yet another part of its weight, it climbed to a height of fifteen hundred feet, and there it met a kind of eddy in the wind which, instead of carrying it straight to the coast, sent it along a course almost parallel to the shore. At last, two minutes later, it slanted back in toward the land and came down for good on the sand of the beach, out of reach of the waves.

Helping one another, the passengers managed to free themselves from the mesh of the net. Relieved of their weight, the balloon was caught up again by the wind, and like a wounded bird that finds one last moment of life, it vanished into the sky.

The basket had carried five passengers, plus a dog, and the balloon had thrown only four onto the shore.

The missing passenger had obviously been swept away by the wave that had just struck the net, and that was what had let the lightened balloon climb one last time and then, a few moments later, reach land.

The four castaways — for that is what we can call them now — had barely set foot on solid ground when all of them, thinking of the one who was gone, cried out:

"Maybe he's trying to swim ashore! Let's save him! Let's save him!"
