Chapter 18: Through the Granite

_Pencroff doubts nothing anymore — The lake's old spillway — A descent underground — The road through the granite — Top has vanished — The central cavern — The lower well — Mystery — With blows of the pick — The return_

Cyrus Smith's plan had succeeded. But, as was his habit, he showed no sign of satisfaction; he stood motionless, lips pressed together, eyes fixed. Harbert was overjoyed. Nab was leaping with delight. Pencroff wagged his big head and muttered:

"Well, well, our engineer sure knows his business!"

And indeed the nitroglycerin had done its work with tremendous force. The breach blown in the lake was so large that the volume of water now pouring out through this new spillway was at least three times what had flowed through the old one. The result had to be that, a short while after the operation, the level of the lake would have dropped by at least two feet.

The colonists went back to the Chimneys to fetch picks, iron-tipped spears, fiber ropes, a flint and steel, and some tinder; then they returned to the plateau. Top went with them.

On the way, the sailor could not keep from saying to the engineer:

"But do you realize, Mr. Cyrus, that with that charming liquid you've made, a person could blow up our whole island?"

"No doubt about it — the island, the continents, and the earth itself," replied Cyrus Smith. "It's only a question of quantity."

"Then couldn't you use this nitroglycerin to load firearms?" asked the sailor.

"No, Pencroff, because it's too violent a substance — it would shatter the gun. But it would be easy to make guncotton, or even ordinary gunpowder, since we have nitric acid, saltpeter, sulfur, and charcoal. Unfortunately, it's the guns we don't have."

"Oh, Mr. Cyrus," replied the sailor, "with a little goodwill...!"

There was no doubt about it: Pencroff had struck the word "impossible" out of the dictionary of Lincoln Island.

When they reached Prospect Plateau, the colonists headed straight for the point of the lake near which the mouth of the old spillway opened. By now it ought to be uncovered. And since the water would no longer be rushing into it, the spillway should have become passable, and it would no doubt be easy to find out how it was laid out inside.

In a few moments the colonists had reached the lower corner of the lake, and one glance was enough to tell them that they had got the result they wanted.

There, in the granite wall of the lake and now above the level of the water, appeared the opening they had searched for so long. A narrow ledge, laid bare by the falling water, made it possible to reach it. The opening was about twenty feet wide but only two feet high. It looked like a storm drain in the edge of a sidewalk. So it could not have given the colonists an easy way in; but Nab and Pencroff took up their picks, and in less than an hour they had made it high enough.

The engineer then came closer and saw that, in its upper part, the walls of the spillway sloped at no more than thirty to thirty-five degrees. That meant they could be walked, and as long as the slope did not get steeper it would be easy to climb down them all the way to sea level. And if, as was very likely, some vast hollow existed inside the granite mass, they might find a way to put it to use.

"Well, Mr. Cyrus, what's stopping us?" asked the sailor, impatient to venture into the narrow passage. "You see Top's already gone ahead of us!"

"Fine," replied the engineer. "But we need to be able to see. Nab, go cut some resinous branches."

Nab and Harbert ran to the shores of the lake, which were shaded by pines and other evergreens, and soon came back with branches, which they made up into torches. The torches were lit from the flint and steel, and then, with Cyrus Smith in the lead, the colonists started down the dark tunnel that the lake's overflow had filled not long before.

Contrary to what one might have expected, the tunnel grew wider as it went, so that almost at once the explorers could stand upright as they descended. The granite walls, worn smooth by the water over endless ages, were slippery, and they had to watch out for falls. So the colonists had tied themselves to one another with a rope, the way mountain climbers do. Luckily, some ledges in the granite formed real steps and made the descent less dangerous. Droplets still hanging from the rock glittered in rainbow colors here and there in the torchlight, and one might have thought the walls were covered with countless stalactites. The engineer studied this black granite. He could not see a single layer in it, not a single crack. The mass was solid and extremely fine-grained. So this tunnel dated from the very birth of the island. It was not the water that had hollowed it out little by little. Pluto, the god of the fires underground, and not Neptune, the god of the sea, had bored it with his own hand, and on the wall they could still make out the traces of volcanic action that the scouring of the water had not been able to wipe away entirely.

The colonists went down very slowly. They could not help feeling a certain emotion at venturing like this into the depths of the rock, where human beings had obviously never been before. They did not talk, but they were thinking, and more than one of them must have had the thought that some octopus or other gigantic cephalopod might be living in the hollows inside, since they were connected with the sea. So they had to go forward with a certain caution.

Besides, Top was at the head of the little troop, and they could rely on the dog's keen instincts. He would not fail to give the alarm if there was need.

After they had gone down about a hundred feet, along a rather winding route, Cyrus Smith, who was in front, stopped, and his companions caught up with him. The spot where they had halted was hollowed out to form a cave of modest size. Drops of water were falling from its roof, but they were not seeping through the rock. They were simply the last traces left by the torrent that had roared through this hollow for so long. The air was slightly damp but gave off no foul or poisonous smell.

"Well, my dear Cyrus?" said Gédéon Spilett. "Here's a retreat that nobody knows about, well hidden down in these depths — but when all's said and done, it's not fit to live in."

"Why not fit to live in?" asked the sailor.

"Because it's too small and too dark."

"Can't we make it bigger, dig it out, cut openings in it for daylight and air?" replied Pencroff, who no longer doubted anything.

"Let's go on," replied Cyrus Smith. "Let's go on with our exploring. Maybe lower down nature will have spared us that work."

"We're only a third of the way down so far," Harbert pointed out.

"About a third," replied Cyrus Smith, "since we've come down roughly a hundred feet from the opening, and it isn't impossible that a hundred feet lower..."

"Where's Top?" asked Nab, interrupting his master.

They searched the cave. The dog was not there.

"He's probably gone on ahead," said Pencroff.

"Let's catch up with him," replied Cyrus Smith.

They took up the descent again. The engineer carefully watched every turn the spillway took, and despite its many windings he could keep track fairly easily of its general direction, which was toward the sea.

The colonists had gone down roughly another fifty feet, measured straight down, when their attention was caught by faraway sounds coming up from deep inside the rock. They stopped and listened. Carried along the passage the way a voice travels through a speaking tube, the sounds reached their ears clearly.

"That's Top barking!" cried Harbert.

"Yes," replied Pencroff, "and our good dog's barking like mad!"

"We have our iron-tipped spears," said Cyrus Smith. "Let's be on our guard — and forward!"

"This is getting more and more interesting," Gédéon Spilett murmured into the sailor's ear, and the sailor nodded.

Cyrus Smith and his companions rushed down to help the dog. Top's barking grew louder and louder. There was a strange fury in his jerky, broken yelps. Was he locked in a fight with some animal whose lair he had disturbed? It is fair to say that the colonists, without a thought for the danger they were exposing themselves to, were now in the grip of an irresistible curiosity. They were no longer climbing down the passage; they were practically sliding down its wall. And in a few minutes, sixty feet lower, they had caught up with Top.

There the passage opened into a vast and magnificent cavern. There Top was running back and forth, barking furiously. Pencroff and Nab shook their torches and flung great flashes of light across every jutting point of the granite, while Cyrus Smith, Gédéon Spilett, and Harbert stood with their spears raised, ready for anything.

The enormous cavern was empty. The colonists went over it in every direction. There was nothing — not an animal, not a living creature! And yet Top kept on barking. Neither petting nor threats could make him stop.

"There must be an exit somewhere that the lake water drained out through to the sea," said the engineer.

"Right," replied Pencroff, "and let's be careful not to fall into a hole."

"Go on, Top, go on!" cried Cyrus Smith.

The dog, roused by his master's words, ran toward the far end of the cavern, and there his barking doubled.

They followed him, and by the light of the torches they saw the mouth of a real well opening in the granite. This was surely where the water that had once run through the rock used to escape. And this time it was no longer a slanting passage that could be climbed down, but a straight vertical shaft that it would have been impossible to venture into.

They held their torches out over the opening. They could see nothing. Cyrus Smith broke off a burning branch and threw it into the abyss. The blazing resin, which shone all the brighter for the speed of its fall, lit up the inside of the well, but still nothing appeared. Then the flame went out with a faint hiss that showed it had reached a layer of water — that is, sea level.

By timing how long the fall took, the engineer was able to estimate the depth of the well, which turned out to be about ninety feet.

So the floor of the cavern lay ninety feet above sea level.

"Here's our home," said Cyrus Smith.

"But something was living in it," replied Gédéon Spilett, whose curiosity was not satisfied.

"Well, whatever it was, amphibian or something else, it has escaped through this exit," replied the engineer, "and left the place to us."

"All the same," added the sailor, "I'd have liked to be Top a quarter of an hour ago, because he didn't bark like that for no reason!"

Cyrus Smith was looking at his dog, and any of his companions who had come close to him would have heard him murmur these words:

"Yes, I really do think Top knows more than we do about a good many things!"

Meanwhile, the colonists' wishes had for the most part come true. Chance, helped along by their leader's marvelous shrewdness, had served them well. Here, at their disposal, they had a vast cavern. They could not yet judge its size by the feeble light of the torches, but it would certainly be easy to divide it into rooms with brick partitions and to fit it out, if not as a house, then at least as a roomy apartment. The water had left it and could never come back. The place was theirs.

Two difficulties remained: first, how to bring light into a hollow carved out of solid rock; second, how to make it easier to get in and out. As for light, there was no point in thinking of letting it in from above, since an enormous thickness of granite roofed the cavern over. But perhaps they could cut through the front wall, the one that faced the sea. During the descent Cyrus Smith had judged fairly closely how steep the spillway was, and so how long; and that gave him good reason to believe that the front part of the wall could not be very thick. If they could get light that way, they would get access that way too, since it was just as easy to cut a door as windows, and to set up a ladder on the outside.

Cyrus Smith shared his ideas with his companions.

"Then let's get to work, Mr. Cyrus!" replied Pencroff. "I've got my pick, and I'll find a way to make daylight through that wall. Where do I hit it?"

"Here," replied the engineer, pointing out to the sturdy sailor a fairly deep recess in the wall, which ought to make it thinner at that spot.

Pencroff attacked the granite, and for half an hour, by the light of the torches, he sent chips flying all around him. The rock threw off sparks under his pick. Nab took over from him, and then Gédéon Spilett after Nab.

The work had been going on for two hours already, and they were beginning to fear that at this spot the wall might be thicker than the pick was long, when, at one last blow struck by Gédéon Spilett, the tool went right through the wall and fell outside.

"Hurrah! And hurrah again!" cried Pencroff.

At that point the wall was only three feet thick.

Cyrus Smith came and put his eye to the opening, which was eighty feet above the ground. Before him stretched the edge of the shore, the islet, and beyond it the boundless sea.

The hole was fairly wide, because the rock around it had crumbled a good deal, and through it the light came pouring in and flooded this splendid cavern with a magical effect! On its left side it was no more than thirty feet high and thirty feet wide, over a length of a hundred feet. But on its right side, by contrast, it was enormous, and its vault curved up to a height of more than eighty feet. Here and there, granite pillars, set irregularly, held up the springing of the arches, like those in the nave of a cathedral. Resting on side piers of a sort — here flattening into low round arches, there soaring up on pointed Gothic ribs, vanishing into dark bays whose fanciful arches could just be glimpsed in the shadows, and richly adorned with jutting knobs of rock that hung down like so many pendants — the vault offered a picturesque mixture of everything that Byzantine, Romanesque, and Gothic architecture have ever produced under the hand of man. And yet here it was only the work of nature! Nature alone had hollowed out this fairy-tale Alhambra in a mass of granite!

The colonists were struck dumb with wonder. Where they had expected to find nothing but a cramped hollow, they had found a kind of marvelous palace, and Nab had taken off his hat, as if he had been carried into a temple!

Cries of admiration burst from every mouth. Their hurrahs rang out and went echoing on and on until they died away in the depths of the dark naves.

"Ah, my friends!" cried Cyrus Smith. "Once we've let plenty of light into the inside of this rock, once we've laid out our bedrooms, our storerooms, and our kitchen and pantries in its left-hand part, we'll still have this splendid cavern left over, and we'll make it our study and our museum!"

"And what will we call it?" asked Harbert.

"Granite House," replied Cyrus Smith — a name his companions greeted with another round of hurrahs.

By now the torches were almost completely burned down, and since, to get back, they would have to climb up the passage to the top of the plateau, it was decided to put off until the next day the work of fitting out their new home.

Before leaving, Cyrus Smith came and leaned once more over the dark well that dropped straight down to sea level. He listened closely. There was no sound at all — not even the sound of the water, which the swell must sometimes stir down in those depths. Another flaming piece of resin was thrown down. The walls of the well lit up for a moment, but this time, no more than the first time, nothing suspicious came to light. If some sea monster had been caught by surprise when the water drained away, it had by now made its way back to the open sea through the underground channel that ran on beneath the beach — the channel the lake's overflow had followed before a new exit was opened for it.

Still, the engineer stood motionless, ears straining, eyes fixed on the depths, and did not say a single word.

At last the sailor came up to him and touched his arm.

"Mr. Smith?" he said.

"What is it, my friend?" replied the engineer, as if he were coming back from the land of dreams.

"The torches are about to go out."

"Let's go!" replied Cyrus Smith.

The little troop left the cavern and began its climb up through the dark spillway. Top brought up the rear, still letting out strange growls. The climb was hard going. The colonists stopped for a few moments at the upper cave, which made a kind of landing halfway up this long granite staircase. Then they started climbing again.

Soon they could feel fresher air. The droplets, dried up by evaporation, no longer glittered on the walls. The smoky light of the torches was growing paler. Nab's went out, and if they did not want to find themselves in deep darkness, they had to hurry.

So hurry they did, and a little before four o'clock, just as the sailor's torch went out in its turn, Cyrus Smith and his companions came out through the mouth of the spillway.
