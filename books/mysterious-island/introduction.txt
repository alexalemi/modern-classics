Jules Verne (1828–1905) published The Mysterious Island in three parts in 1874 and 1875, first in instalments in the family magazine run by his publisher Pierre-Jules Hetzel and then as a book. It opens in March 1865, in the last weeks of the American Civil War. Five Northerners trapped in besieged Richmond, Virginia, two of them prisoners of war, steal a Confederate balloon during a hurricane and escape in it, along with a dog. The storm carries them for days and drops them on an unknown island in the South Pacific, more than a thousand miles from any other land, with almost nothing in their pockets.

The leader of the five is Cyrus Smith, an engineer, and the book is really about what an engineer knows. Starting from one match, then from none, the castaways make fire, bricks, pottery, iron, steel, glass, soap, candles, explosives and in the end a telegraph. Verne gives every step. The long chapters of chemistry and metalwork are the heart of the book's argument, which is that knowledge is the one thing a shipwreck cannot take from you, and they are kept here in full.

From the start, though, things happen on the island that nobody can explain. The answer, when it comes, ties this book to two earlier ones. Captain Grant's Children (1867–68) left a criminal marooned on a nearby island, and Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Sea (1870) ended with Captain Nemo's submarine vanishing into a whirlpool. Verne could not make the dates of the three books agree, and his publisher printed notes admitting as much. Those notes are left out here.

In the earlier book Nemo hides where he comes from. Verne had wanted him to be a Polish nobleman at war with Russia, and Hetzel talked him out of it. Here Verne tells the story at last: Nemo was an Indian prince who fought the British in the rising of 1857 and lost his whole family to it. W. H. G. Kingston's English translation of 1875, the one most English readers have known, cut and softened that history. This edition is translated complete from Verne's French and gives it in full.

Some things in this edition differ from the original. The heroes are American, so Verne measured in feet, miles and degrees Fahrenheit and added metric conversions for French readers. The American units are kept and the conversions dropped. Nab, Smith's servant, is a freed slave who chose to stay with him. Verne's narrator often calls him by a racial term rather than his name, and this edition uses his name. Two asides that mock his race have been cut, and so has a comparison between an ape and named human peoples. What Nab says and does is unchanged.
