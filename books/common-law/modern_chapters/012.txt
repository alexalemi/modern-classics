Lecture VI: Possession
(Part 1 of 3)

Possession is a conception only less important than contract. But the interest of the theory of possession does not stop at its practical importance in the body of English law. The theory has fallen into the hands of the philosophers, and with them it has become a cornerstone of more than one elaborate structure. It will be a service to sound thinking to show that a far more civilized system than the Roman is built on a plan that cannot be reconciled with the a priori doctrines of Kant and Hegel—doctrines, that is, reasoned out in advance of experience. Those doctrines were worked out in careful correspondence with German views of Roman law. And most of the speculative jurists of Germany, from Savigny to Ihering, have been at once professors of Roman law and deeply influenced, if not controlled, by some form of Kantian or post-Kantian philosophy. So everything has combined to give German speculation a special bent, and that bent deprives it of any claim to universal authority.

Why does the law protect possession when the possessor is not also the owner? That is the general problem, and it has greatly exercised the German mind. Kant, as is well known, was deeply influenced in his views on ethics and law by the speculations of Rousseau. Kant, Rousseau, and the Massachusetts Bill of Rights agree that all men are born free and equal, and from that day to this one branch or the other of that declaration has supplied the answer to the question why possession should be protected. Kant and Hegel start from freedom. The freedom of the will, Kant said, is the essence of man. It is an end in itself. It needs no further explanation; it is to be respected absolutely; and to realize and affirm it is the very end and object of all government. Possession is to be protected because a man, by taking possession of an object, has brought it within the sphere of his will. He has extended his personality into or over that object. As Hegel would have put it, possession is the objective realization of free will. And by Kant's postulate, the will of any individual, once manifested in this way, is entitled to absolute respect from every other individual. It can be overcome or set aside only by the universal will—that is, by the state, acting through its organs, the courts.

Savigny did not follow Kant on this point. He said that every act of violence is unlawful, and he seemed to treat the protection of possession as a branch of the protection of the person. But the answer came back that possession is protected against disturbance by fraud as well as by force, and his view is discredited. Those who have been content with humble grounds of expediency seem to have been few, and they have either recanted or fallen out of favor.

The majority have gone in the direction Kant pointed out. Bruns, an admirable writer, expresses a characteristic yearning of the German mind when he demands an internal juristic necessity, drawn from the nature of possession itself, and on that account rejects reasons drawn from experience. He finds the necessity he is looking for in the freedom of the human will, which the whole legal system does nothing but recognize and carry out. To constrain that will is a wrong, and the wrong must be righted without asking whether the will conforms to law—and so on, in a Kantian vein. So Gans, a favorite disciple of Hegel: "The will is of itself a substantial thing to be protected, and this individual will has only to yield to the higher common will." So Puchta, a great master: "The will which wills itself, that is, the recognition of its own personality, is to be protected."

Footnote: Further Hegelian discourse may be found in Dr. J. Hutchison Stirling's Lectures on the Philosophy of Law.

The chief variation on this view is Windscheid's, a writer now in vogue. He prefers the other branch of the declaration in the Bill of Rights. He thinks that possession is protected on the same grounds as a person is protected against injuria, a wrong to his personal standing: everyone in the state is the equal of everyone else, and no one shall raise himself over another. Ihering, to be sure, a man of genius, made an independent start. He said that possession is ownership on the defensive: in the owner's interest, the person who is exercising ownership in fact (that is, the possessor) is spared the need of proving his title against someone who stands in an unlawful position. But Bruns, in his later work, answered this well. It assumes that the title of disseisors—those who have wrongfully put another out of his land—is generally worse than the title of the disseisees they put out. That cannot be taken for granted, and it is probably not true in fact.

It follows from the Kantian doctrine that a man in possession is to be confirmed and maintained in it until he is put out by an action brought for that purpose. Perhaps one more fact, besides those already mentioned, has influenced this reasoning: the sharp division in Continental procedure between possessory actions or defenses, which deal only with who holds the thing, and petitory ones, which deal with who has the right to it. When a defendant in a possessory action is not allowed to set up his own title, a theorist readily finds a mystical importance in possession.

But when does a man become entitled to this absolute protection? On Kant's principle it is not enough that he has custody of a thing. A protection founded on the sacredness of human personality requires that the object have been brought within the sphere of that personality—that the free will have set itself into the object without restraint. There must therefore be an intent to appropriate it: that is, to make it part of oneself, or one's own.

Here the prevailing view of Roman law comes in to fortify principle with precedent. We are told that, of all the people who might have the actual charge or custody of a thing, Roman law recognized as possessor only the owner, or one holding as owner and on his way to becoming owner by the lapse of time. In later days it made a few exceptions on practical grounds. But beyond the pledgee (the person holding goods as security for a debt) and the sequester (a receiver appointed by the court), these exceptions are unimportant and disputed. Some of the Roman jurists say in so many words that depositaries—people with whom goods are left for safekeeping—and borrowers do not have possession of the things entrusted to them. Whether or not the German reading of the sources goes too far, it must be taken into account in examining the German theories.

By denying possession to bailees in general, philosophy cunningly fitted itself to Roman law, and so put itself in a position to claim the authority of that law for the theory—a theory of which the treatment of bailees was merely a corollary. That is why I say it is important to show that a far more developed, more rational, and mightier body of law than the Roman gives no sanction to either the premise or the conclusion held by Kant and his successors.

In the first place, English law has always had the good sense to allow title to be set up as a defense to a possessory action. In the assize of novel disseisin—the old proceeding for recovering land from which one had lately and wrongfully been put out—which was a true possessory action, the defendant could always rely on his title. Even when possession is taken or kept in a way the criminal law punishes, as in forcible entry and detainer (taking or holding land by force), proof of title lets the defendant keep it, and in many cases it has been held an answer to an action of trespass. So in trespass for taking goods the defendant may set up title in himself. There might seem to be a trace of the Continental distinction in the general rule that title cannot be tried in trespass quare clausum. But this is an exception, and it is commonly put on the ground that the judgment in that action cannot change the property in the land, as a judgment in trespass for chattels or in trover can. The rule that you cannot go into title in a possessory action presupposes great difficulty of proof—the probatio diabolica, or "devil's proof," of the Canon law—delays in the process, and great importance in possession for the time being. All of these mark a stage of society long since passed. In ninety-nine cases out of a hundred it is about as easy and cheap to prove at least a prima facie title as it is to prove possession.

Footnote: Heusler thinks this merely a result of English formalism, and of a narrow reading of the word suo, "his," in the writ, which charged that the defendant had disseised the plaintiff of "his" tenement. But there was no such narrowness in dealing with catalla sua, "his chattels," in trespass, as will be seen below.

In the next place—and this was the bearing of the last Lecture on this subject—the common law has always given the possessory remedies to all bailees without exception. The right to these remedies extends not only to pledgees, lessees, and those having a lien, who can exclude their bailor, but also to simple bailees, as they have been called. These have no interest in the chattels, no right to hold them against the owner, and neither give nor receive any reward.

Modern German statutes have followed the same path so far as to give the possessory remedies to tenants and some others. Bruns says, as the spirit of the Kantian theory required him to say, that this is a sacrifice of principle to convenience. But I cannot see what is left of a principle that avows itself inconsistent with convenience and with the actual course of legislation. The first call on a theory of law is that it should fit the facts. It must explain the observed course of legislation. And since it is pretty certain that men will make the laws that seem convenient to them without troubling themselves much about what principles their legislation runs into, a principle that defies convenience is likely to wait some time before it finds itself permanently realized.

It remains, then, to look for some ground for protecting possession outside the Bill of Rights or the Declaration of Independence—a ground consistent with the larger scope that modern law gives the conception.

The courts have said little on the subject. In one case it was laid down that the protection of possession was an extension of the protection the law throws around the person, and on that ground it was held that the right to sue in trespass quare clausum did not pass to an assignee in bankruptcy. It has also been said that to deny a bankrupt the action of trover against strangers for goods that came into his possession after his bankruptcy would be "an invitation to all the world to scramble for the possession of them"; and the court referred to "grounds of policy and convenience." I may also refer to the cases of capture, some of which will be cited again. In the Greenland whale fishery, by the English custom, if the first striker lost his hold on the whale and it was then killed by another, the first had no claim; but he had the whole whale if he kept fast to it until it was struck by the other, even though it then broke away from the first harpoon. By the custom in the Galápagos, on the other hand, the first striker had half the whale even though he had lost control of the line. The English courts have upheld and acted on each of these customs, and Judge Lowell has decided in accordance with still a third, which gives the whale to the vessel whose iron first remains in it, provided the claim is made before cutting in—before the whale is cut up for its blubber. The ground, as Lord Mansfield put it, is simply that without such customs there would have to be a sort of warfare perpetually going on between the adventurers. If courts adopt different rules on similar facts, according to the point at which men will fight in the different cases, that tends, so far as it goes, to shake any a priori theory of the matter.

Those who see in the history of law the formal expression of the development of society will be apt to think that the proximate ground of law must be empirical—drawn from experience—even when that ground is the fact that a certain ideal or theory of government is generally held. Law, being a practical thing, must found itself on actual forces. It is quite enough for the law, therefore, that man, by an instinct he shares with the domestic dog, and of which the seal gives a most striking example, will not allow himself to be dispossessed of what he holds, either by force or by fraud, without trying to get it back. Philosophy may find a hundred reasons to justify the instinct, but it would make no difference at all if philosophy condemned it and told us to surrender without a murmur. As long as the instinct remains, it will be more comfortable for the law to satisfy it in an orderly way than to leave people to themselves. If the law did otherwise, it would become a matter for pedagogues, wholly devoid of reality.

I think we are now in a position to begin the analysis of possession. It will be instructive first to say a word on a preliminary question that has been debated with much zeal in Germany: Is possession a fact or a right? The question must be taken to mean possession and right in the sense the law gives those words, and not something else that philosophers or moralists may mean by them; for as lawyers we have nothing to do with either except in a legal sense. If this had always been kept steadily in mind, the question would hardly have been asked.

A legal right is nothing but a permission to exercise certain natural powers and, on certain conditions, to obtain protection, restitution, or compensation by the aid of the public force. Just so far as the aid of the public force is given to a man, he has a legal right, and the right is the same whether his claim is founded in righteousness or in iniquity. Just so far as possession is protected, it is as much a source of legal rights as ownership is when ownership secures the same protection.

Every right is a consequence the law attaches to one or more facts that the law defines. Wherever the law gives anyone special rights not shared by the body of the people, it does so because certain special facts, not true of the rest of the world, are true of him. When a group of facts singled out by the law in this way exists in the case of a given person, he is said to be entitled to the corresponding rights. What is meant is that the law helps him to constrain his neighbors, or some of them, in a way it would not if all the facts in question were not true of him. It follows that any word which denotes such a group of facts connotes the rights attached to it as legal consequences, and any word which denotes the rights attached to a group of facts connotes the group of facts in the same way.

The word "possession" denotes such a group of facts. So when we say of a man that he has possession, we state directly that all the facts of a certain group are true of him, and we convey indirectly, by implication, that the law will give him the advantage of the situation. Contract, or property, or any other substantive notion of the law, may be analyzed in the same way, and should be treated in the same order. The only difference is this. Possession denotes the facts and connotes the consequence; property always, and contract with more uncertainty and wavering, denote the consequence and connote the facts. When we say that a man owns a thing, we state directly that he has the benefit of the consequences attached to a certain group of facts, and, by implication, that the facts are true of him. The important thing to grasp is that each of these legal compounds—possession, property, and contract—is to be analyzed into fact and right, antecedent and consequent, just like every other. It does not matter at all that one word puts the accent on one element and the other two words on the other. We are not studying etymology, but law. There are always two things to ask: first, what are the facts that make up the group in question; and then, what consequences does the law attach to that group. The first generally offers the only difficulties.

So it is almost a tautology to say that the protection the law attaches to possession as its consequence is as truly a right, in the legal sense, as the consequences attached to adverse holding for the period of prescription—the long, open holding of another's property that ripens into title—or to a promise made for value or under seal. If the statement needs dramatic reinforcement, I may add that possessory rights pass by descent or by will, as well as by conveyance, and that in some of the States they are taxed as property.

We are now ready to analyze possession as the common law understands it. To discover the facts that make it up, it will be best to study them at the moment when possession is first gained. For then they must all be present, in the same way that both consideration and promise must be present at the moment a contract is made. But when we turn to the continuance of possessory rights—or, as it is commonly put, the continuance of possession—all schools will agree that fewer than all the facts needed to call those rights into being must remain true at present in order to keep them alive.

To gain possession, then, a man must stand in a certain physical relation to the object and to the rest of the world, and he must have a certain intent. These relations and this intent are the facts we are looking for.

The physical relation to others is simply a relation of manifested power that reaches as far as the intent does, and little will need to be said about it once the nature of the intent is settled. When I come to the intent, I shall not attempt an analysis like the one pursued earlier with regard to intent as an element of liability. The principles worked out about intent in that connection have nothing to do with the present subject, and any such analysis, so far as it did not fail, would be little more than a discussion of evidence. The intent inquired into here must perhaps be outwardly manifested; but all theories of the grounds on which possession is protected seem to agree in requiring that it be actual—subject, of course, to the necessary limits of legal investigation.

But besides our power and intent with regard to our fellow men, there must be a certain degree of power over the object. If there were only one other man in the world, and he was safely under lock and key in jail, the person holding the key would not possess the swallows that flew over the prison. Cases of capture illustrate this element, although no doubt where the line is drawn is affected by the degree of power gained against other people, as well as by the power gained over the object. Roman law and the common law agree that, in general, fresh pursuit of wild animals does not give the pursuer the rights of possession. Until escape has been made impossible by some means, another may step in and kill or catch the game and carry it off if he can. So it has been held that no action lies against a person for killing and taking a fox that another man had been pursuing, and that was at that moment actually in view of the man who had found it, started it, and chased it. The Court of Queen's Bench went so far as to decide, in spite of a jury's verdict the other way, that fish nearly surrounded by a seine—a large fishing net—with an opening of seven fathoms between its ends, where boats were stationed to frighten them from escaping, had not been reduced to possession as against a stranger who rowed in through the opening and helped himself. But the difference between power over the object that is enough for possession and power that is not is plainly one of degree only, and the line may be drawn at different places at different times, on the grounds just mentioned. Thus we are told that the legislature of New York enacted in 1844 that anyone who started and pursued deer in certain counties of that State should be deemed in possession of the game as long as he kept up fresh pursuit of it, and to that extent modified the New York decisions just cited. So too, while Justinian decided that a wild beast so badly wounded that it might easily be taken must actually be taken before it belongs to its captors, Judge Lowell, with equal reason, has upheld the contrary custom of the American whalemen in the Arctic Ocean, mentioned above, which gives a whale to the vessel whose iron first remains in it, provided the claim is made before cutting in.

We may leave the physical relation to the object with these few examples, because it can rarely come into question except with living and wild things. And so we come to the intent, which is the really troublesome matter. It is just here that we find the German jurists unsatisfactory, for the reasons I have already given. The best-known theories have been framed as theories of the German interpretation of Roman law, under the influence of some form of Kantian or post-Kantian philosophy. The type of Roman possession, in German opinion, was that of an owner, or of one on his way to becoming owner. Following this out, Savigny—the only writer on the subject whom English readers generally know—said that the animus domini, the intent to deal with the thing as owner, is in general necessary to turn a mere physical holding into possession in the legal sense. We need not stop to ask whether this modern form, or the Greek phrase of Theophilus and the Greek sources (animus dominantis or animus dominandi, the intent of one who is master), is more exact. Either one excludes most bailees and termors—tenants for a term of years—from the list of possessors, as the civilians and canonists (the scholars of Roman and of Church law) exclude them, and as the German theories must.

Footnote: I shall use animus domini here as a short way of indicating the general nature of the intent required, even by those who deny that the expression fits; and especially because Savigny's opinion is the one English writers have adopted.

The effect of this exclusion, as interpreted by the Kantian philosophy of law, has been to lead the German lawyers to regard the intent necessary to possession as primarily self-regarding. Their philosophy teaches them that a man's physical power over an object is protected because he has the will to make it his, so that it has become part of his very self, the outward manifestation of his freedom. Once the possessor's will is conceived as self-regarding in this way, the intent with which he must hold is pretty clear: he must hold for his own benefit. Furthermore, the self-regarding intent must rise to the height of an intent to appropriate; for otherwise, it seems to be implied, the object would not truly be brought under the possessor's personality.

The grounds for rejecting the tests of Roman law have been shown above. Let us begin afresh. Legal duties are logically prior to legal rights. What their relation may be to moral rights, if there are any, and whether moral rights are not in the same way the logical offspring of moral duties, are questions that do not concern us here. They belong to the philosopher, who approaches the law from outside, as one part of a larger series of human manifestations. The business of the jurist is to make known the content of the law—that is, to work on it from within, or logically, arranging and distributing it in order, from its summum genus, its highest class, down to its infima species, its narrowest kind, so far as that can be done. Legal duties, then, come before legal rights. To put it more broadly, and to avoid the word duty, which is open to objection: the direct working of the law is to limit, in certain specified ways, the freedom of action or choice of a greater or smaller number of persons. The power of removing or enforcing this limitation, which is generally entrusted to certain other private persons—in other words, a right corresponding to the burden—is not a necessary or universal counterpart. Again, a large part of the advantages enjoyed by one who has a right are not created by the law. The law does not enable me to use or abuse this book that lies before me. That is a physical power I have without the law's help. What the law does is simply to prevent other men, to a greater or lesser extent, from interfering with my use or abuse. And this analysis and example apply to possession as well as to ownership.
