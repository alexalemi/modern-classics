Lecture III: Torts—Trespass and Negligence
(Part 2 of 3)

The general principle of our law is that loss from accident must lie where it falls, and this principle is not affected by the fact that a human being is the instrument of misfortune. But relative to a given human being, anything is an accident which he could not fairly have been expected to contemplate as possible, and therefore to avoid. In the words of the late Chief Justice Nelson of New York: "No case or principle can be found, or if found can be maintained, subjecting an individual to liability for an act done without fault on his part.... All the cases concede that an injury arising from inevitable accident, or, which in law or reason is the same thing, from an act that ordinary human care and foresight are unable to guard against, is but the misfortune of the sufferer, and lays no foundation for legal responsibility." If this were not so, any act would be enough, however remote, that set in motion or opened the door to a chain of physical events ending in damage—riding the horse, in the case of the runaway, or even coming to a place where one is seized with a fit and strikes the plaintiff in an unconscious spasm. Indeed, why need the defendant have acted at all? Why is it not enough that his mere existence has been at the plaintiff's expense? The requirement of an act is the requirement that the defendant should have made a choice. But the only possible purpose of bringing in this moral element is to make the power of avoiding the harm complained of a condition of liability. And there is no such power where the harm cannot be foreseen. Here we reach the argument from policy, and so I shall put off for a moment the discussion of trespasses upon land and of conversions, and will take up the liability for cattle separately at a later stage.

A man need not, it is true, do this or that particular act—the term act implies a choice—but he must act somehow. Furthermore, the public generally profits by individual activity. Since action cannot be avoided, and since it tends to the public good, there is obviously no sound policy in throwing the risk of what is at once desirable and inevitable upon the man who acts. The state might conceivably make itself a mutual insurance company against accidents, and spread the burden of its citizens' mishaps among all its members. There might be a pension for the paralyzed, and state aid for those who suffered in person or property from storms or wild beasts. As between individuals, it might adopt the mutual insurance principle pro tanto—to that extent—and divide the damages when both parties were at fault, as admiralty does under its rule of splitting the loss in a collision; or it might throw all loss upon the actor regardless of fault. The state does none of these things, however, and the prevailing view is that its cumbersome and expensive machinery ought not to be set in motion unless some clear benefit is to be gained by disturbing the status quo. State interference is an evil, where it cannot be shown to be a good. Universal insurance, if people want it, can be provided better and more cheaply by private enterprise. An attempt to redistribute losses simply on the ground that they resulted from the defendant's act would be open to all these objections. It would also, as I hope the preceding discussion has shown, be open to the still graver one of offending the sense of justice. Unless my act is of a kind to threaten others—unless, under the circumstances, a prudent man would have foreseen the possibility of harm—it is no more justifiable to make me indemnify my neighbor against the consequences than to make me do the same if I had fallen upon him in a fit, or to compel me to insure him against lightning.

I must now return to the conclusions drawn from innocent trespasses upon land and from conversions—the wrongful dealing with another man's goods as if they were one's own—and to the supposed analogy between those cases and trespasses against the person. Otherwise the law concerning the latter might be thought to sit between two contradictory principles, each driving with equal force to the opposite conclusion from the other.

Take first the case of a trespass upon land that does actual damage. When a man goes upon his neighbor's land thinking it is his own, he intends the very act or consequence complained of. He means to meddle with a certain thing in a certain way, and it is exactly that intended meddling for which he is sued. But if he accidentally hits a stranger as he raises his staff in self-defense, the fact that is the heart of the action—namely, the contact between the staff and his neighbor's head—was not intended, and could not have been foreseen. It might be answered, to be sure, that a man is sued not for meddling with property, but for meddling with the plaintiff's property. On that view, in the cases supposed, just as much as in that of the accidental blow, the defendant is ignorant of one of the facts that make up the whole situation, and which must be present to make his action wrong. He does not know, that is, that the true owner has or claims any interest in the property in question. And so he does not intend a wrongful act, because he does not mean to deal with his neighbor's property. But the answer to this is that he does intend to do the damage complained of. One who lessens the value of property by intentional damage knows that it belongs to somebody. If he thinks it belongs to himself, he expects whatever harm he does to come out of his own pocket. It would be odd if he were to escape the burden by discovering that it belonged to his neighbor. It is a very different thing to say that he who intentionally does harm must bear the loss, from saying that one whose acts happen to lead to harm, as a consequence that could not have been foreseen, must bear it.

Next, suppose the act complained of is an exercise of control over the plaintiff's property, such as a merely technical trespass or a conversion. If the defendant thought the property belonged to himself, there seems to be no abstract injustice in requiring him to know the limits of his own titles. If he thought it belonged to someone else, there is none in holding him bound to get proof of title before acting. Consider, too, what the defendant's liability amounts to when the act—whether an entry upon land or a conversion of goods—has done no damage to the property, and the thing has come back into the true owner's hands. The sum recovered is merely nominal, and the payment is nothing more than a formal acknowledgment of the owner's title. Considering how prescription and the statutes of limitation can turn repeated acts of control into ownership, that is no more than right. All appearance of injustice disappears when the defendant is allowed to escape the costs of a lawsuit by a tender—an offer of payment made in advance—or in some other way.

But suppose the property has not come back into the true owner's hands. If the thing remains with the defendant, it is clearly right that he should surrender it. And if, instead of the thing itself, he holds the proceeds of a sale, it is as reasonable to make him pay over its value in trover (the action for the value of goods wrongfully converted) or in assumpsit (the action upon a promise) as it would have been to compel him to surrender the thing. But whether the defendant has afterward paid the proceeds of the sale of a chattel over to a third person cannot affect the rights of the chattel's true owner. Take the supposed case of an auctioneer. If he had paid the true owner, that would have been an answer to the claim of his bailor, the man who gave him the goods to sell. If he has paid his bailor instead, he has paid someone he was not bound to pay, and no general principle requires that this should be held to take away the plaintiff's right.

There is another point bearing on the argument that the law of trespasses upon property establishes a general principle. Whether the defendant knew of the plaintiff's title is likely to lie entirely in his own breast, and so hardly admits of satisfactory proof. Indeed, in many cases it cannot have been open to evidence at all at the time the law was settled, before the parties to a suit were allowed to testify. So in Basely v. Clarkson, an action of trespass quare clausum, the defense was that the defendant, in mowing his own land, had involuntarily and by mistake mowed down some of the plaintiff's grass. The plaintiff had judgment on demurrer. "For it appears the fact was voluntary, and his intention and knowledge are not traversable; they can't be known."

This language suggests that it would be enough to explain the law of trespass upon property historically, without attempting to justify it. For it seems to be admitted that if the defendant's mistake could be proved, it might matter. It will be noticed, further, that any general argument from the law of trespass upon land to the law governing trespass against the person is shown to be misleading by the law as to cattle. The owner is bound at his peril to keep them off his neighbor's land, but he is not bound at his peril in every case to keep them from injuring his neighbor's person.

The objections to such a decision as the one supposed in the case of the auctioneer do not rest on the general theory of liability. They spring entirely from the special needs of commerce. It does not become unjust to hold a person liable for unauthorized meddling with another's property until there arises the practical necessity for rapid dealing. But where that necessity exists, it is not surprising to find—and we do find—a different tendency in the law. The absolute protection of property, however natural to a primitive community more occupied with production than with exchange, is hardly consistent with the requirements of modern business. Even when the rules we have been considering were established, the traffic of the public markets was governed by more liberal principles. On the continent of Europe it was decided long ago that the policy of protecting titles must yield to the policy of protecting trade. Casaregis held that the general principle, nemo plus juris in alium transferre potest quam ipse habet—no one can transfer to another a greater right than he has himself—must give way in mercantile dealings to the rule possession vaut titre, possession is as good as title. In later times, as markets overt (the open public markets where a buyer in good faith got a good title) have lost their importance, the Factors' Acts and their successive amendments, which protect those who deal in good faith with agents entrusted with goods, have moved more and more in the direction of adopting the Continental doctrine.

I must preface the argument from precedent by recalling what was said in the first Lecture about early forms of liability, and especially about the appeals. It was shown there that the appeals de pace et plagis—of breach of the peace and wounding—and the appeals of mayhem became the action of trespass, and that those appeals and the early actions of trespass were always, so far as appears, for intentional wrongs.

The words contra pacem, "against the peace," in the writ of trespass were no doubt put there to lay a foundation for the king's jurisdiction over the case. But there seems to be no reason to give a similar purpose to vi et armis, "with force and arms," or cum vi sua, "with his force," as it was often put. Glanvill says that wounds are within the sheriff's jurisdiction unless the accuser adds a charge of breach of the king's peace. Yet the wounds are given with force and arms just as much in the one case as in the other. Bracton says that the lesser wrongs he describes belong to the king's jurisdiction "because they are sometimes against the peace of our lord the king"—while, as has been observed, they were supposed always to be committed intentionally. It might even be inferred that the allegation "against the peace" was originally material, and it will be remembered that trespasses once carried the liability to pay a fine to the king.

If it is true that trespass was originally confined to intentional wrongs, there is hardly any need to consider the argument drawn from the scope of the general issue. In form, the general issue was a softening of the strict word-for-word denial (de verbo in verbum) of the ancient procedure, to which the inquest granted by the king's writ was unknown. The strict form seems to have lasted in England for some time after trial of the issue by recognition—by a sworn body of neighbors—was introduced. When a recognition was granted, the inquest was, of course, competent only to speak to the facts, as has been said above. And when the general issue was introduced, trespass was still confined to intentional wrongs.

We may now take up the authorities. It will be remembered that the earlier precedents date from a time when the assize and the jurata had not yet given way to the modern jury. Those bodies spoke from their own knowledge, either to an issue defined by the writ or to certain familiar questions of fact arising in the trial of a case. They did not hear the whole case upon evidence brought before them. Their function was narrower than the one the jury has since gained, and so it naturally happened that, once they had declared what the defendant had done, the judges laid down the standard by which those acts were to be measured without their help. Hence the question in the Year Books is not a loose, general inquiry of the jury whether they think the alleged trespasser was negligent on whatever facts they may find. It is a well-defined issue of law, to be decided by the court: whether certain acts set out upon the record are a ground of liability. It is possible that the judges dealt pretty strictly with defendants. And it is quite easy to pass from the premise that defendants have been held trespassers for a variety of acts, with no mention of neglect, to the conclusion that any act by which another was damaged will make the actor liable. But a more exact scrutiny of the early books will show that liability in general, then as later, was founded on the tribunal's opinion that the defendant ought to have acted otherwise—in other words, that he was to blame.

Returning first to the case of the thorns in the Year Book, it will be seen that the falling of the thorns into the plaintiff's close, although a result the defendant did not wish, was in no other sense against his will. When he cut the thorns, he did an act that obviously and necessarily would have that consequence, and he must be taken to have foreseen it and not to have prevented it. Chief Justice Choke says: "As to what was said about their falling in, ipso invito, that is no plea, but he ought to show that he could not do it in any other way, or that he did all in his power to keep them out." And both judges treat the unlawfulness of the entry upon the plaintiff's land as following from the unlawfulness of dropping the thorns there. Choke admits that if the thorns or a tree had been blown over onto the plaintiff's land, the defendant might have entered to get them. Chief Justice Crew says of this case, in Millen v. Fawdry, that the opinion was that "trespass lies, because he did not plead that he did his best endeavor to hinder their falling there; yet this was a hard case." The statements of law made by counsel in argument may be left to one side, although Brian, who was one of them, is quoted and mistaken for one of the judges by Sir William Blackstone in Scott v. Shepherd.

The principal authorities are the shooting cases. Since shooting is an extra-hazardous act, it would not be surprising if men were held to do it at their peril in public places. The liability has been put on the general ground of fault, however, wherever the line of necessary precaution may be drawn. In Weaver v. Ward, the defendant pleaded that he and the plaintiff were skirmishing in a trainband—a company of citizen militia at drill—and that in discharging his musket he wounded the plaintiff by accident and misfortune, and against his own will. On demurrer, the court said that "no man shall be excused of a trespass,... except it may be judged utterly without his fault. As if a man by force take my hand and strike you, or if here the defendant had said, that the plaintiff ran cross his piece when it was discharging, or had set forth the case with the circumstances so as it had appeared to the court that it had been inevitable, and that the defendant had committed no negligence to give occasion to the hurt." The later cases simply follow Weaver v. Ward.

The passages quoted above in favor of the strict doctrine—from Sir Thomas Raymond in Bessey v. Olliot, and from Sir William Blackstone in Scott v. Shepherd—are both taken from dissenting opinions. In the latter case it is pretty clear that the majority of the court thought it was not a trespass to ward off personal danger by instantly tossing away a squib, a lighted firework, that someone else had thrown onto one's market stall, even though this gave the squib a new motion and the plaintiff's eye was put out as a result. The last case cited above, in stating the arguments for absolute responsibility, was Leame v. Bray. The question there was whether the action, for running down the plaintiff, should have been case rather than trespass. The defendant objected to trespass on the ground that the injury happened through his neglect but was not done willfully. So there was no question before the court of absolute responsibility for one's acts, since negligence was admitted; and the language used is all aimed simply at the proposition that the damage need not have been done intentionally.

In Wakeman v. Robinson, another runaway case, there was evidence that the defendant pulled the wrong rein, and that he ought to have kept a straight course. The jury were instructed that if the injury was caused by an immediate act of the defendant, it did not matter whether the act was willful or accidental. On a motion for a new trial, Chief Justice Dallas said: "If the accident happened entirely without default on the part of the defendant, or blame imputable to him, the action does not lie.... The accident was clearly occasioned by the default of the defendant. The weight of evidence was all that way. I am now called upon to grant a new trial, contrary to the justice of the case, upon the ground, that the jury were not called on to consider whether the accident was unavoidable, or occasioned by the fault of the defendant. There can be no doubt that the learned judge who presided would have taken the opinion of the jury on that ground, if he had been requested so to do." This language may not have fitted the defendant's plea, which was the general issue; but the pleadings were not referred to, and the doctrine is believed to be sound.

In America there have been several decisions on the point. In Brown v. Kendall, Chief Justice Shaw settled the question for Massachusetts. That was an action of trespass for assault and battery. It appeared that the defendant, while trying to separate two fighting dogs, had raised his stick over his shoulder in the act of striking, and had accidentally hit the plaintiff in the eye, injuring him severely. The case was stronger for the plaintiff than if the defendant had been acting in self-defense. But the court held that although the defendant was under no duty to separate the dogs, still, if he was doing a lawful act, he was not liable unless he fell short of the care that men of ordinary prudence would use under the circumstances; and that the burden was on the plaintiff to prove the lack of such care.

In such a matter no authority deserves more respect than that of Chief Justice Shaw, for the strength of that great judge lay in an accurate appreciation of the needs of the community whose officer he was. Some, indeed many, English judges could be named who have surpassed him in exact technical knowledge. But few have lived who were his equals in understanding the grounds of public policy to which all laws must ultimately be referred. It was this which made him, in the words of the late Judge Curtis, the greatest magistrate this country has produced.

Brown v. Kendall has been followed in Connecticut, in a case where a man fired a pistol—in lawful self-defense, as he claimed—and hit a bystander. The court was strongly of the opinion that the defendant was not answerable on the general principles of trespass unless he had failed to use such care as was practicable under the circumstances. The foundation of liability in trespass, as well as in case, was said to be negligence. The Supreme Court of the United States has given its approval to the same doctrine. The language of Harvey v. Dunlop, Chief Justice Nelson's case, has been quoted already, and there is a case in Vermont that tends in the same direction.

Supposing it now conceded that the general idea on which liability to an action is founded is fault or blameworthiness in some sense, the question arises whether it is so in the sense of personal moral shortcoming—which is what Austin's teaching would come to in practice. The words of Justice Rede, quoted earlier from the Year Book, give a sufficient answer: "In trespass the intent" (we may say, more broadly, the defendant's state of mind) "cannot be construed." Suppose a defendant were allowed to testify that, before acting, he had considered carefully what a prudent man would do under the circumstances, and, having formed the best judgment he could, had acted on it. If his story were believed, it would be conclusive against his negligence, judged by a moral standard that took his personal characteristics into account. But supposing any such evidence had got before the jury, it is very clear that the court would say: Gentlemen, the question is not whether the defendant thought his conduct was that of a prudent man, but whether you think it was.

Some middle point must be found between the horns of this dilemma.

The standards of the law are standards of general application. The law takes no account of the infinite varieties of temperament, intellect, and education which make the inner character of a given act so different in different men. It does not attempt to see men as God sees them, for more than one sufficient reason. In the first place, the impossibility of nicely measuring a man's powers and limitations is far clearer than the impossibility of finding out his knowledge of law—and that has been thought to account for what is called the presumption that every man knows the law. But a more satisfactory explanation is that, when men live in society, a certain average of conduct, a sacrifice of individual peculiarities going beyond a certain point, is necessary to the general welfare. If, for instance, a man is born hasty and awkward, and is always having accidents and hurting himself or his neighbors, no doubt his inborn defects will be allowed for in the courts of Heaven. But his slips are no less troublesome to his neighbors than if they sprang from guilty neglect. His neighbors accordingly require him, at his proper peril, to come up to their standard, and the courts which they establish decline to take his personal equation into account.

The rule that the law does, in general, determine liability by blameworthiness is subject to the limitation that small differences of character are not allowed for. The law considers, in other words, what would be blameworthy in the average man, the man of ordinary intelligence and prudence, and determines liability by that. If we fall below that level in those gifts, it is our misfortune; so much we must have at our peril, for the reasons just given. But he who is intelligent and prudent does not act at his peril, in theory of law. On the contrary, it is only when he fails to use the foresight of which he is capable, or uses it with evil intent, that he is answerable for the consequences.

There are exceptions to the principle that every man is presumed to have ordinary capacity to avoid harming his neighbors, and they illustrate both the rule and the moral basis of liability in general. When a man has a distinct defect of such a kind that everyone can recognize it as making certain precautions impossible, he will not be held answerable for not taking them. A blind man is not required to see at his peril. No doubt he is bound to consider his infirmity in governing his actions. But if he is properly in a certain situation, his failure to take precautions that require eyesight would not prevent his recovering for an injury to himself, and, it may be presumed, would not make him liable for injuring another. So it is held that, where he is the plaintiff, a very young child is bound only to take the precautions of which a child is capable; and the same principle may be cautiously applied where he is the defendant. Insanity is a harder matter to deal with, and no general rule can be laid down about it. There is no doubt that in many cases a man may be insane and yet perfectly capable of taking the precautions, and of being moved by the motives, that the circumstances demand. But if insanity of a pronounced kind exists, plainly making the sufferer incapable of complying with the rule he has broken, good sense would require it to be admitted as an excuse.

Putting together the qualification just established and the general proposition laid down before it, it will now be assumed as follows. On the one hand, the law presumes or requires a man to have ordinary capacity to avoid harming his neighbors, unless a clear and manifest incapacity is shown. On the other hand, it does not in general hold him liable for unintentional injury unless, having that capacity, he might and ought to have foreseen the danger—in other words, unless a man of ordinary intelligence and forethought would have been to blame for acting as he did. The next question is whether this vague test is all the law has to say on the matter; and, which is the same question in another form, by whom the test is to be applied.

Although the grounds of legal liability are moral to the extent just explained, it must be borne in mind that law works only within the sphere of the senses. If the external phenomena—the visible acts and omissions—are such as the law requires, it is wholly indifferent to the internal phenomena of conscience. A man may have as bad a heart as he chooses, if his conduct is within the rules. In other words, the standards of the law are external standards. However much the law may take moral considerations into account, it does so only for the purpose of drawing a line between the bodily motions and rests it permits and those it does not. What the law really forbids, and the only thing it forbids, is the act on the wrong side of the line, be that act blameworthy or otherwise.

Again, any legal standard must in theory be one that would apply to all men, not specially excepted, under the same circumstances. It is not intended that the public force should fall upon an individual by accident, or at the whim of any body of men. The standard, that is, must be fixed. In practice, no doubt, one man may have to pay and another may escape, according to the different feelings of different juries. But this merely shows that the law does not perfectly accomplish its ends. The theory or intention of the law is not that the approval or blame which a particular twelve men may feel should be the test. They are supposed to leave their own peculiarities to one side and to represent the feeling of the community. The ideal average prudent man—whose equivalent the jury is taken to be in many cases, and whose guilt or innocence is the supposed test—is a constant, and his conduct under given circumstances is in theory always the same.

Finally, any legal standard must in theory be capable of being known. When a man has to pay damages, he is supposed to have broken the law, and he is further supposed to have known what the law was.

If, then, the ordinary liabilities in tort arise from failure to comply with fixed and uniform standards of external conduct, which every man is presumed and required to know, it is obvious that it ought to be possible, sooner or later, to put these standards into words, at least to some extent—and that doing so must in the end be the business of the court. It is equally clear that the featureless generality, that the defendant was bound to use such care as a prudent man would use under the circumstances, ought to be continually giving way to the specific rule that he was bound to use this or that precaution under these or those circumstances. The standard the defendant was bound to meet was a standard of specific acts or omissions, with reference to the specific circumstances in which he found himself. If, in the whole field of unintentional wrongs, the courts never got further than the question of negligence, and left every case to the jury without rudder or compass, they would simply be confessing that they could not state a very large part of the law which they required the defendant to know. And they would be asserting, by implication, that nothing could be learned by experience. But neither courts nor legislatures have ever stopped at that point.

From the time of Alfred to the present day, statutes and decisions have busied themselves with defining the precautions to be taken in certain familiar cases—that is, with substituting for the vague test of the care used by a prudent man a precise test of specific acts or omissions. The fundamental thought is still the same: the way prescribed is the way in which prudent men are in the habit of acting, or else it is a way laid down for cases where prudent men might otherwise be in doubt.

It will be observed that the external tests of liability I am about to mention illustrate the tendency of the law of tort to become more and more concrete, by judicial decision and by statute; but their existence does not interfere with the general doctrine I have maintained as to the grounds of liability. The argument of this Lecture, although opposed to the doctrine that a man acts or exerts force at his peril, is by no means opposed to the doctrine that he does certain particular acts at his peril. It is the coarseness, not the nature, of the standard that is objected to. If, when the question of the defendant's negligence is left to a jury, negligence does not mean the actual state of the defendant's mind but a failure to act as a prudent man of average intelligence would have acted, then he is required to conform to an objective standard at his peril, even in that case. When a more exact and specific rule has been reached, he must obey that rule at his peril to the same extent. But, further, if the law is wholly a standard of external conduct, a man must always comply with that standard at his peril.
