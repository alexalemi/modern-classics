Lecture X: Successions after Death
(Part 1 of 2)

In the Lecture on Possession, I tried to show that the idea of possessing a right as such was absurd in itself. All rights are consequences attached to filling some situation of fact. A right that can be acquired by possession differs from others only in this: the situation it is attached to is of a kind that can be filled by different persons one after another, or by anyone at all without regard to whether he fills it lawfully—as is the case where the situation consists in having a tangible object within one's power.

When the law recognizes a right of this sort, there is no difficulty in transferring it. Or, more accurately, there is no difficulty in different persons enjoying similar rights over the same thing one after another. If A, who possesses a horse or a field, gives up the possession to B, the rights B acquires stand on the same ground as A's did before. The facts from which A's rights sprang have ceased to be true of A, and are now true of B. The consequences the law attaches to those facts now exist for B, as they did for A before. The situation of fact from which the rights spring is a continuing one, and anyone who occupies it, no matter how, has the rights attached to it. But there is no possession possible of a contract. The fact that yesterday A gave B a consideration and received a promise in return cannot be laid hold of by X and transferred from A to himself. The only thing that can be transferred is the benefit or the burden of the promise—and how can these be separated from the facts that gave rise to them? How, in short, can a man sue or be sued on a promise in which he had no part?

Up to this point, in dealing with any particular right or obligation, it has been assumed that the facts from which it sprang were true of the person entitled or bound. But it often happens, especially in modern law, that a person acquires a special right and is allowed to enforce it, although the facts that give rise to it are not true of him, or are true of him only in part. One of the chief problems of the law is to explain the machinery by which this result has been brought about.

It will be observed that the problem does not cover the whole field of rights. Some rights cannot be transferred by any device or contrivance—for instance, a man's right to bodily safety or to his reputation. Others, again, are incidents of possession, and within the limits of that idea no other is needed. As Savigny said, "Succession does not apply to possession by itself."

But the idea of possession will carry us only a very little way toward understanding the modern theory of transfer. That theory depends very largely on the idea of succession, to use the word just quoted from Savigny, and so successions will be the subject of this Lecture and the next. I shall begin by explaining the theory of succession to persons who have died. When that is done, I shall pass to the theory of transfer between living people, and shall consider whether any relation can be established between the two.

The first is easily shown to rest on a fictitious identification between the dead man and his successor. As a first step toward the further discussion, and also for its own sake, I shall briefly state the evidence concerning the executor (the person named in a will to settle the dead man's affairs), the heir (the person on whom land descends when its owner dies), and the devisee (the person to whom land is left by will). In order to understand the theory of our law about the first of these, at least, scholars agree that it is necessary to consider the structure and position of the Roman family as it was in the infancy of Roman society.

Continental jurists have long been collecting the evidence that in the earlier periods of Roman and German law alike, the unit of society was the family. The Twelve Tables still recognize the interest of the lower members of the family in the family property. Heirs are called sui heredes—that is, heirs of themselves, or of their own property—as Gaius explains. The Roman jurist Paulus says that they are regarded as owners in a certain sense even in their father's lifetime, and that after his death they do not so much receive an inheritance as obtain the full power of dealing with their own property.

Starting from this point, it is easy to understand how heirs succeeded to a dead paterfamilias in the Roman system. If the family owned the property that the paterfamilias administered, its rights were unaffected by the death of its temporary head. The family went on, although the head died. And when, probably by a gradual change, the paterfamilias came to be regarded as the owner instead of a mere manager of the family's rights, the nature and continuity of those rights did not change along with the title to them. The familia, the household and all that belonged to it, passed on to the heirs as the ancestor had left it. The heir succeeded not to the ownership of this or that thing separately, but to the whole hereditas, the headship of the family, with certain rights of property attached to it. And of course he took this headship, this right of representing the family's interests, subject to whatever changes the last manager had made.

The whole bundle of the ancestor's rights and duties—or, to use the technical phrase, the total persona he sustained—was easily separated from his natural personality. For this persona was only the sum of what had once been family rights and duties, and originally any individual sustained it only as head of the family. Hence it was said to be carried on by the inheritance, and when the heir took it up, he could bring an action for injuries committed before he did.

So the Roman heir came to be treated as identified with his ancestor for the purposes of the law. And so it is clear how, in that instance, the impossible transfers I am trying to explain were accomplished. Rights to which B, as B, could show no title, he could readily maintain under the fiction that he was the same person as A, whose title nobody denied.

It is not necessary at this point to study family rights in the German tribes. For no one disputes that the modern executor gets his characteristics from the Roman heir. Wills, too, were borrowed from Rome; the Germans whom Tacitus describes did not know them. Administrators—persons appointed to settle an estate where there is no executor—were a later imitation of executors, introduced by statute for cases where there was no will, or where for any other reason there were no executors.

The executor has the legal title to the whole of the testator's personal property, and, generally speaking, the power to dispose of it. Formerly he was entitled to the residue—whatever was left after the bequests had been paid out—if the will did not dispose of it. He took it, one may fairly guess, not as a legatee of those particular chattels, but because he represented the person of the testator, and therefore had all the rights the testator would have had after the distribution if he had been alive. Nowadays the residue is generally left by the will itself, but even now it is not regarded as a specific gift of the chattels that remain undisposed of. I cannot help thinking that this doctrine echoes the one under which the executor took the residue in former times.

No such rule has governed residuary devises of land—gifts by will of "all the rest" of a man's real estate. In England these have always been held to be specific, down to the present day. So if a devise of land should fail, that land would not pass under the residuary clause, but would descend to the heir as if there had been no will.

Again, the appointment of an executor relates back to the date of the testator's death: it is treated as if it had taken effect at that moment. The continuity of the person is preserved by this fiction, as in Rome it was preserved by personifying the inheritance ad interim, in the meantime.

Enough has been said to show the likeness between our executor and the Roman heir. And bearing in mind what was said about the heres, the Roman heir, it will easily be seen how it came to be said, as it often was in the old books, that the executor "represents the person of his testator." The meaning of this feigned identity has been found in history, but we must also appreciate the help it gave in overcoming a technical difficulty. If the executor represents the person of the testator, there is no longer any trouble about allowing him to sue or be sued on his testator's contracts. In the reign of Edward III, when an action of covenant was brought against executors, Persay objected: "I never heard that one should have a writ of covenant against executors, nor against other person but the very one who made the covenant, for a man cannot oblige another person to a covenant by his deed except him who was party to the covenant." But it is useless to object that the promise sued on was made by A, the testator, and not by B, the executor, when the law says that for this purpose B is A. Here, then, is one class of cases in which a transfer is accomplished with the help of a fiction—a fiction that reflects, as fictions so often do, the facts of an early stage of society, and that could hardly have been invented if those facts had been otherwise.

Executors and administrators are the chief, if not the only, example of universal succession in English law—succession to a dead man's affairs as a whole, rather than to this or that thing. But although they succeed per universitatem, to the whole, in the way just explained, they do not succeed to every kind of property. The personal property goes to them, but land takes another course. All real estate not disposed of by will goes to the heir, and the rules of inheritance are quite distinct from those that govern the distribution of chattels. So the question arises whether the English heir, the successor to real estate, shows the same likeness to the Roman heres as the executor does.

The English heir is not a universal successor. Each and every parcel of land descends as a separate and specific thing. Nevertheless, within his narrower sphere he unquestionably represents the person of his ancestor. Different opinions have been held about whether the same was true in early German law. Dr. Laband says that it was; Sohm takes the opposite view. It is commonly supposed that among the German tribes ownership by the family, at least of land, came before ownership by individuals, and it has been shown how naturally representation followed from a similar state of things at Rome. But there is no need to consider whether our law on this subject is of German or Roman origin, because the principle of identification has clearly prevailed from the time of Glanvill to the present day. If the Germans did not know it, it is plainly accounted for by the influence of the Roman law. If there was anything of the sort in the Salic Law, it was no doubt due to natural causes like those that gave rise to the principle at Rome. But either way, I cannot doubt that the modern doctrine has taken a good deal of its form, and perhaps some of its substance, from the mature system of the civilians, in whose language it was expressed for so long. For the same reasons, there is also no need to weigh the evidence of the Anglo-Saxon sources, although several passages in their laws make it fairly clear that there was some identification.

As late as Bracton, two centuries after the Norman Conquest, the heir was not the successor to lands alone, but represented his ancestor in a much more general sense, as will be seen directly. The Anglo-Saxons did not know the office of executor in the sense of an heir, and even in Bracton's time it does not seem to have been what it has since become. So there is no need to go back further than the early Norman period, after the appointment of executors had become common and the heir was more nearly what he is now.

When Glanvill wrote, a little more than a century after the Conquest, the heir was bound to warrant the reasonable gifts his ancestor had made—that is, to guarantee them to the grantees and their heirs. And if the ancestor's goods were not enough to pay his debts, the heir was bound to make up the shortfall out of his own property. Neither Glanvill nor his Scottish imitator, the Regiam Majestatem, limits this liability to the amount of property the heir inherited from the same source. That makes the identification of heir and ancestor as complete as it was in the Roman law before Justinian introduced such a limit. On the other hand, a century later, Bracton makes it plain that the heir was bound only so far as property had descended to him, and the same limit appears in the early sources of the Continent, Norman and others. The heir's liabilities were probably shrinking. Britton and Fleta, the imitators of Bracton, and perhaps Bracton himself, say that an heir is not bound to pay his ancestor's debt unless the ancestor's deed specially binds him to it. The later law required that the heir be named in the deed if he was to be held.

But at any rate, in Bracton's time the identification of heir and ancestor still came close to a universal succession, as another statement of his shows. He asks whether a testator can leave his rights of action by will, and answers no, as far as concerns debts that were not proved and recovered in the testator's lifetime. Actions of that sort belong to the heirs, and must be sued in the secular court. For until such debts are recovered in the proper court, the executor cannot proceed for them in the Church's court.

This shows that the identification worked both ways. The heir was liable for the debts his ancestor owed, and he could recover the debts owed to his ancestor, until the executor took his place in the King's Courts as well as in those of the Church. Within the limits just explained, the heir was also bound to warrant property his ancestor had sold, to the buyer and the buyer's heirs. After this evidence that the modern heir began by representing his ancestor generally, it is not necessary to hunt for expressions in later books, since his position has since been narrowed. But just as we have seen that the executor is still said to represent the person of his testator, so in the time of Edward I the heir was said to represent the person of his ancestor. And at a much later date it was said that "the heir is in representation in point of taking by inheritance eadam persona cum antecessore"—the same persona as his ancestor.

A great judge who died only a few years ago repeats language that would have been just as familiar to the lawyers of Edward or of James. Baron Parke lays down that in general a party is not required to make profert of an instrument—to produce it in court—when he is not entitled to possess it. Then he says that there is an exception "in the cases of heir and executor, who may plead a release to the ancestor or testator whom they respectively represent; so also with respect to several tortfeasors, for in all these cases there is a privity between the parties which constitutes an identity of person." (Tortfeasors are wrongdoers; privity is a legal relation between persons that the law treats as binding one by the acts of the other.)

But this is not all. The identity of person was carried further still. If a man died owning land in fee—that is, as an estate of inheritance—and left sons, the land went to the eldest son alone. But if he left only daughters, it descended to them all equally. In that case several individuals together carried on their ancestor's persona. Yet it was always laid down that they were only one heir. To work out this result, not only was one person identified with another, but several persons were reduced to one, so that they might sustain a single persona.

What was the persona? It was not the sum of all the ancestor's rights and duties. We have seen that for many centuries his general status—the sum of all his rights and duties except those connected with real property—has been taken up by the executor or administrator. The persona carried on by the heir was confined from an early day to real estate in its technical sense: that is, to property governed by feudal principles, as distinguished from chattels, which, as Blackstone tells us, include whatever was not a feud, a feudal holding.

But the heir's persona was not even the sum of all the ancestor's rights and duties connected with real estate. It has been said already that every fee descends as a specific thing, and not as part of some larger universitas, some whole. This appears not so much from the fact that different parcels might be governed by different rules of descent, so that the same person would not be heir to both, as from the very nature of feudal property. Under the feudal system in its full strength, holding land was only one incident of a complex personal relation. The land was forfeited for failure to render the services for which it was granted; the tenant could renounce the service if the lord broke the duties he owed in return. It rather seems that at the beginning of the feudal period, under Charlemagne, a man could hold land of only one lord. Even when it had become common to hold of more than one, the strict personal relation was modified only enough to save the tenant from having to perform services that conflicted. Glanvill and Bracton tell us that a tenant who held of several lords was to do homage—the ceremony by which a tenant became his lord's man—for each fee, but to reserve his allegiance for the lord of whom he held his chief estate. But if the different lords should make war on each other, and the chief lord should command the tenant to serve him in person, the tenant ought to obey, saving the service due to the other lord for the fee he held of him.

We see, then, that the tenant had a distinct persona, or status, in respect of each of the fees he held. The rights and duties attached to one of them had no relation to the rights and duties attached to another. A succession to one had no connection with the succession to another. Each succession was the taking up of a distinct personal relation, in which the successor was to be determined by the terms of that relation.

The persona we are trying to define is the estate. Every fee is a distinct persona, a distinct hereditas, or inheritance, as it has been called since the time of Bracton. We have already seen that it may be sustained by more than one person where there are several heirs, as well as by one, just as a corporation may have more members or fewer. But it may not only be divided lengthwise, so to speak, among persons interested in the same way at the same time. It may also be cut across into successive interests, to be enjoyed one after another. In technical language, it may be divided into a particular estate—a lesser interest such as an estate for life—and remainders, the interests that follow it. But they are all parts of the same fee, and the same fiction still governs them. We read in an old case that "he in reversion and particular tenant are but one tenant." (The one in reversion is the person to whom the land comes back when the lesser estate ends.) This is only a statement of counsel, to be sure. But it was made to account for a doctrine that seems to need the explanation: that after the death of the tenant for life, the one in reversion might bring error or attaint—a proceeding to overturn a false verdict by a jury—against an erroneous judgment or false verdict given against the tenant for life.

To sum up the results so far: the heir of modern English law gets his characteristic features from the law as it stood soon after the Conquest. At that time he was a universal successor in a very broad sense. Many of his functions as such were soon transferred to the executor. The heir's rights came to be confined to real estate, and his liabilities to those connected with real estate and to obligations of his ancestor that expressly bound him. The succession to each fee, or feudal inheritance, is distinct; it is not part of the sum of all the ancestor's rights regarded as one whole. But to this day the executor in his sphere, and the heir in his, represent the person of the dead man, and are treated as if they were one with him for the purpose of settling their rights and obligations.

The bearing this has on the contracts of the dead man has been pointed out. But its influence is not confined to contract; it runs through everything. The most striking instance, however, is the acquisition of rights by prescription. Take the case of a right of way. A right of way over a neighbor's land can be acquired only by grant, or by using it adversely—as of right, without the owner's leave—for twenty years. A man uses a way for ten years and dies. Then his heir uses it for ten years. Has any right been acquired? If common sense alone is consulted, the answer must be no. The ancestor did not get any right, because he did not use the way long enough. And just as little did the heir. How can it improve the heir's title that another man trespassed before him? Clearly, if four strangers to each other used the way for five years each, the last would acquire no right. But here the fiction that has been so carefully explained comes in. From the point of view of the law, it is not two persons who have each used the way for ten years, but one who has used it for twenty. The heir has the advantage of sustaining his ancestor's persona, and the right is acquired.

Successions Inter Vivos

I now come to the most difficult and obscure part of the subject. It remains to be discovered whether the fiction of identity was extended to anyone besides the heir and the executor. And if we find, as we do, that in express terms it went only a little further, the question will still arise whether the way of thinking, and the ideas made possible by the doctrine of inheritance, have not silently changed the law of dealings between the living. It seems to me demonstrable that their influence has been profound, and that without understanding the theory of inheritance it is impossible to understand the theory of transfer inter vivos—between living persons.

The difficulty in dealing with the subject is to convince the sceptic that there is anything to explain. Nowadays the idea that a right is valuable is almost the same as the idea that it can be turned into money by selling it. But it was not always so. Before you can sell a right, you must be able to make a sale thinkable in legal terms. At the beginning of this Lecture I put the case of transferring a contract. I have just mentioned the case of gaining a right by prescription when neither party has met the requirement of twenty years' adverse use. In that instance there is not even a right at the time of the transfer, but only the bare fact of ten years' past trespassing. A way, until it becomes a right of way, is just as little capable of being held by a possessory title as a contract is. If, then, a contract can be sold, and if a buyer can add the time of his seller's adverse use to his own, what is the machinery by which the law works out the result?

The most superficial acquaintance with any system of law in its earlier stages will show with what difficulty, and by what slow degrees, such machinery has been provided, and how the lack of it has narrowed the range of things that could be alienated, or transferred. It is a great mistake to assume that it is a mere matter of common sense that the buyer steps into the shoes of the seller, to use our telling metaphor. Suppose that sales and other civil transfers had kept the form of capture in war that they seem to have had in the infancy of Roman law—a form that was partly kept in at least one instance, the acquiring of wives, after the transaction had in fact taken the more civilized shape of a purchase. The idea that the buyer came in as the seller's adversary would probably have gone along with the fiction of a hostile taking, and the buyer would have stood on his own position as founding a new title. Without the help of ideas drawn from some other source, it would have been hard to work out a legal transfer of objects that did not admit of possession.

A possible source of such other ideas was to be found in family law. The principles of inheritance supplied a fiction and a way of thinking that might at least have been extended into other fields. To prove that they were in fact so extended, it will be necessary to examine the law of Rome once more, as well as what remains of German and Anglo-Saxon customs.

I will take up first the German and Anglo-Saxon laws, which are the ancestors of our own on one side of the house. For although what we get from those sources is not in the direct line of the argument, it lays a foundation for it by showing the course of development in different fields.
