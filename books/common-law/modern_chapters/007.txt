Lecture IV: Fraud, Malice, and Intent—The Theory of Torts
(Part 1 of 2)

The next subjects to be considered are fraud, malice, and intent. In discussing unintentional wrongs, the greatest difficulty to be overcome was the doctrine that a man always acts at his peril. In what follows, the difficulty runs the other way. It will be to prove that actual wickedness—the kind these three words describe—is not an element in the civil wrongs to which they are applied.

It was shown, in dealing with the criminal law, that when we call an act malicious in everyday speech, we mean that harm to another person was intended to come of it, and that the harm was desired for its own sake, as an end in itself. For the purposes of the criminal law, however, intent alone was found to matter, and to have the same consequences as intent with ill will added on top. Carrying the analysis further, intent was found to be made up of two things: foresight of the harm as a consequence, and a desire to bring it about, the desire being thought of as the motive for the act. Of these two, again, only foresight seemed to matter. As a last step, foresight was reduced to its lowest terms. The conclusion was that, subject to certain exceptions which were explained, the general basis of criminal liability was knowledge, at the time of acting, of facts from which common experience showed that certain harmful results were likely to follow.

It remains to be seen whether a similar reduction is possible on the civil side of the law, and whether fraudulent, malicious, intentional, and negligent wrongs can thus be brought into a single, philosophically continuous series.

A word of preliminary explanation will be useful. It was shown in the Lecture just referred to that an act, although it always implies intent, is in itself indifferent to the law. An act is a willed, and therefore an intended, coordination of muscular contractions. But the intent that the act necessarily implies stops there. And all muscular motions, or combinations of them, are harmless apart from the circumstances that accompany them, and the act itself does not necessarily imply that those circumstances are present. To strike out with the fist is the same act whether it is done in a desert or in a crowd.

The same considerations which were urged to show that an act alone, by itself, does not and ought not to impose either civil or criminal liability, apply, at least often, to a series of acts—that is, to conduct—even though the series shows a further coordination and a further intent. For instance, to utter a sentence falsely stating that a certain barrel contains No. 1 Mackerel is the same series of acts whether the sentence is spoken in the privacy of one's own room or to another man in the course of a bargain. In either case, to be sure, there is a further intent, beyond the coordination of muscles for a single sound: the intent to assert that a certain barrel has certain contents, which the ordering of the words necessarily shows. But both the series of acts and the intent are in themselves indifferent. They are innocent when spoken in solitude, and they become a ground of liability only when certain accompanying circumstances are shown.

The intent that is meant when we speak of it as an element of legal liability is an intent directed toward the harm complained of, or at least toward harm. It is not necessary in every case to carry the analysis all the way back to the simple muscular contractions that make up a course of conduct. On the same principle that requires something more than an act followed by damage to make a man liable, we are constantly free to treat a coordinated series of acts as a practically simple element, indifferent in itself, when we ask what further circumstances or facts must be present before the conduct in question is at the actor's peril. It will save confusion, and the need for repetition, if this is kept in mind in the discussion that follows.

The chief forms of liability in which fraud, malice, and intent are said to be necessary elements are deceit, slander and libel, malicious prosecution, and conspiracy; to these, perhaps, trover may be added.

Deceit is a notion drawn from the moral world, and in its popular sense it plainly implies wickedness. The common-law doctrine about it is generally stated in terms that fit only actual guilt and actual guilty intent. It is said that a man is liable to an action for deceit if he makes a false representation to another, knowing it to be false, and intending that the other should believe it and act on it—provided the person addressed does believe it, and is thereby persuaded to act to his own harm. This is no doubt the typical case, and it is a case of intentional moral wrong. Now, what is the party's conduct here? It consists in uttering certain words, arranged so that uttering them implies a knowledge of the meaning they would convey if heard. But that conduct, with only that knowledge, is neither moral nor immoral. Go one step further and add the knowledge that another person is within hearing; still the act has no definite character. The elements that make it immoral are the knowledge that the statement is false, and the intent that it shall be acted on.

The principal question, then, is whether this intent can be reduced to the same terms as it has been in other cases. The answer is not difficult. It is perfectly clear that the intent that a false representation should be acted on would be conclusively established by proof that the defendant knew the other party intended to act on it. If the defendant foresaw the consequence of his acts, he is chargeable, whether his motive was a desire to get the other party to act, or simply an unwillingness, for private reasons, to tell the truth. If the defendant knew a present fact—the other party's intent—which, according to common experience, made it likely that his act would have the harmful consequence, he is chargeable, whether or not he in fact foresaw the consequence.

In this matter the general conclusion follows from a single instance. For the moment it is admitted that in one case knowledge of a present fact, such as the other party's intent to act on the false statement, makes proof of an intent to induce him to act on it unnecessary, it is admitted that the lesser element is all that is needed in the larger compound. Intent includes knowledge enough for foresight, as has been shown. So when you prove intent you prove knowledge, and intent may often be the easier of the two to prove. But when you prove knowledge you do not prove intent.

It may be said, however, that in such a case as the one supposed, intent is implied or presumed. But this is only propping up a false theory with a fiction. It is very much like saying that consideration—what is given in return for a promise—is presumed for an instrument under seal, a formal document sealed by the party bound. That is merely a way of reconciling the formal theory that every contract must have a consideration with the obvious fact that sealed instruments do not need one. Whenever it is said that a certain thing is essential to liability, but that it is conclusively presumed from something else, there is always reason to suspect that the essential element is to be found in that something else, and not in what is said to be presumed from it.

As to the intent required for deceit, we need not stop at the single instance just given. The law goes no further than to require proof either of the intent, or that the other party was justified in inferring such an intention. So the whole meaning of the requirement is this: the natural and manifest tendency of the representation, under the known circumstances, must have been to create the belief that it was made with a view to action, and so to lead to action on the faith of it. What is called the standard of intent is thus really an external standard of conduct under the known circumstances, and the analysis of the criminal law holds good here too.

Nor is this all. The law, pursuing the course of specification explained in the last Lecture, decides what the tendency of representations is in certain cases—for instance, a statement at the time of a sale that a horse is sound; or, in general, any statement of fact which it is known the other party intends to rely on. Beyond these specific rules lies the vague realm of the jury.

The other moral element in deceit is knowledge that the statement was false. Strictly, I am not concerned with this, because everything necessary is done once the elements of risk are reduced to action and knowledge. But it will help the general object of showing that the tendency of the law everywhere is to pass beyond moral standards and reach external ones, if this knowledge of falsehood can be turned into a formula that does not necessarily imply guilt, although of course in fact it is generally accompanied by guilt. The moment we look at it critically, we find the moral side shade away.

The question is, what known circumstances are enough to throw the risk of a statement on the man who makes it, if it leads another man to act and turns out to be untrue? Now, a man may plainly take the risk of his statement by express agreement, or by an implied one which the law reads into his bargain. In legal language, he may warrant its truth; and if it is not true, the law treats it as a fraud just as much when he makes it believing it fully as when he knows it to be untrue and means to deceive. If, in selling a horse, the seller warranted him to be only five years old, and in fact he was thirteen, the seller could be sued for deceit at common law, although he thought the horse was only five. The common-law liability for the truth of statements is therefore wider than the sphere of actual moral fraud. But, again, it is generally enough if a representation is made recklessly, without knowing whether it is true or false. Now what does "recklessly" mean? It does not mean actual personal indifference to the truth of the statement. It means only that the grounds for the statement were so far insufficient that a prudent man could not have made it without leading to the inference that he was indifferent. That is to say—repeating an analysis we have gone through before—it means that the law, applying a general objective standard, decides that if a man makes his statement on those grounds he is liable, whatever the state of his mind, and even though he personally may have been perfectly free from wickedness in making it.

Hence the same reasoning already applied to intent may be applied to knowledge of falsity. Actual knowledge may often be easier to prove than that the evidence was not enough to warrant the statement, and when it is proved, it contains the lesser element. But as soon as the lesser element is shown to be enough, it is shown that the law is ready to apply an external or objective standard here also.

Courts of equity—the courts that grew up beside the common-law courts to give relief where the strict common law gave none—have laid down the doctrine in terms so completely detached from the defendant's actual moral condition that they go to the opposite extreme. It is said that "when a representation in a matter of business is made by one man to another calculated to induce him to adapt his conduct to it, it is perfectly immaterial whether the representation is made knowing it to be untrue, or whether it is made believing it to be true, if, in fact, it was untrue."

Perhaps the actual decisions could be reconciled on a narrower principle. But the rule just stated goes so far as to say that in business matters a man makes every statement likely to be acted on at his peril. This seems hard to justify as policy. The moral starting point of liability in general should never be forgotten, and the law cannot, without disregarding it, hold a man answerable for statements based on facts that would have convinced a wise and prudent man of their truth. The public advantage and necessity of freedom in giving information is strong enough to protect even the slander of a third person. It ought all the more, it seems to me, to protect statements made at the request of the very party who complains of them.

The common law, at any rate, keeps the reference to morality by making fraud the ground on which it proceeds. It does not hold that a man always speaks at his peril. But starting from the moral ground, it works out an external standard of what would be fraudulent in the average prudent member of the community, and requires every member, at his peril, to avoid that. As in other cases, it is gradually accumulating precedents which decide that certain statements, under certain circumstances, are at the peril of the party who makes them.

The elements of deceit which throw the risk of his conduct on a party, then, are these. First, making a statement of facts that purports to be serious. Second, the known presence of another person within hearing. Third, known facts sufficient to warrant the expectation, or suggest the probability, that the other party will act on the statement. (What facts are sufficient has been specifically decided by the courts in some instances; in others the question would no doubt go to the jury on the principles already explained.) Fourth, the falsehood of the statement. This must be known; or else the known evidence about the matter stated must be such as would not warrant belief according to the ordinary course of human experience. (On this point, too, the court may be found to lay down specific rules in some cases.)

I next take up the law of slander. It has often been said that malice is one of the elements of liability, and the doctrine is commonly stated like this. Malice must exist, but the law presumes it from the mere speaking of the words. You may rebut this presumption of malice by showing that the words were spoken under circumstances that made the communication privileged—that is, protected—as when a lawyer speaks in the necessary course of his argument, or a person answers in good faith an inquiry about the character of a former servant. And then, it is said, the plaintiff may in some cases meet this defense by showing that the words were spoken with actual malice.

All this sounds as if actual intent to cause the damage complained of, if not ill will, were at the bottom of this class of wrongs. Yet it is not so. The word "malice" points, as usual, to an original moral standard. But the rule that malice is presumed on proof of speaking certain words amounts to saying that the outward conduct of speaking those words may be actionable whether or not the damage to the plaintiff was intended. And this falls in with the general theory, because the manifest tendency of slanderous words is to harm the person they are spoken of. Again, the real substance of the defense is not that the damage was not intended—that would be no defense at all. It is that, whether it was intended or not—that is, even if the defendant foresaw it, and foresaw it with pleasure—the manifest facts and circumstances under which he spoke were such that the law considered the damage to the plaintiff less important than the benefit of free speech.

It is harder to apply the same analysis to the last stage of the process, but perhaps not impossible. It is said that the plaintiff may meet a case of privilege made out by the defendant by proving actual malice, that is, an actual intent to cause the damage complained of. But how is this actual malice made out? By showing that the defendant knew his statement was false, or that his untrue statements went grossly beyond what the occasion required. Now is it not very plain that the law is looking at something entirely different from the defendant's intent? The fact that the defendant foresaw the damage to the plaintiff, and foresaw it with pleasure, matters no more here than it would where the communication was privileged. The question, again, is entirely one of knowledge, or of some other external standard. And what makes even knowledge important? It is that the reason for which a man is allowed, in the other instances, to make false charges against his neighbors is missing. It is in the public interest that people should be free, in certain circumstances, to give the best information they can without fear. But there is no public benefit in having lies told at any time. When a charge is known to be false, or goes beyond what the occasion requires, it is not necessary to make it in order to speak freely. So it falls under the ordinary rule, that certain charges are made at the party's peril in case they turn out to be false, whether evil consequences were intended or not. The defendant is liable, not because his intent was evil, but because he made false charges without excuse.

It will be seen that the peril of conduct begins further back here than with deceit, since slander is more universally harmful in its tendency. There must be some accompanying circumstances. At the least, there must be a human being in existence whom the statement points to. There must be another human being within hearing who understands the statement, and the statement must be false. But it is arguable that the presence of a hearer need not be known—just as the falsity of the charge certainly need not be—and that a man must take the risk of even an idle statement being overheard, unless he made it under known circumstances of privilege. It would be no great curtailment of freedom to deny a man immunity for attaching a charge of crime to his neighbor's name, even when he supposes himself alone. But it is not clear that the law would go quite that far.

The next form of liability is comparatively unimportant. I mean the action for malicious prosecution. A man may recover damages against another who, maliciously and without probable cause, has started a criminal prosecution against him on a false charge—or, in some cases, a civil one. The want of probable cause refers, of course, only to the state of the defendant's knowledge, not to his intent. It means that the facts known to the defendant when he began the suit did not amount to probable cause. But the standard applied to what the defendant knew is external to him. The question is not whether he thought the facts amounted to probable cause, but whether the court thinks they did.

Then as to malice. The defendant's conduct consists in starting proceedings on a charge which is in fact false, and which has failed. That is the root of the whole matter. If the charge was true, or if the plaintiff was convicted—even though he may now be able to prove that he was wrongly convicted—the defendant is safe, however great his malice, and however little ground he had for his charge.

Suppose, however, that the charge is false and fails. It may readily be admitted that malice did originally mean an evil motive, an actual intent to harm the plaintiff by making a false charge. Here again the legal remedy started from the moral basis. The occasion for it was no doubt similar to the one that gave rise to the old law of conspiracy: a man's enemies would sometimes try to destroy him by setting the criminal law in motion against him. Since it was punishable for several to combine for such a purpose, it was concluded, with some hesitation, that when a single individual wickedly attempted the same thing, he should be liable on similar grounds. I must fully admit that there is weighty authority for the view that malice, in its ordinary sense, is to this day a distinct fact, to be proved and to be found by the jury.

But this view cannot be accepted without hesitation. It is admitted, on the one side, that the existence of probable cause, believed in, is a justification in spite of malice. It is admitted, on the other, that "it is not enough to show that the case appeared sufficient to this particular party, but it must be sufficient to induce a sober, sensible and discreet person to act upon it, or it must fail as a justification for the proceeding upon general grounds." On the one side, malice alone will not make a man liable for starting a groundless prosecution; on the other, his justification depends not on his opinion of the facts but on the court's. When his actual moral condition is disregarded to this extent, it is a little hard to believe that the existence of an improper motive should matter. Yet that is what malice must mean in this case, if it means anything; for the evil effects of a successful indictment are of course intended by anyone who gets another indicted. I cannot help thinking that a jury would be told that knowledge or belief that the charge was false, at the time it was made, was conclusive evidence of malice. And if so, for reasons that need not be repeated, malice is not the important thing, but the facts known to the defendant.

Nevertheless, it is obviously treading on delicate ground to make it actionable to set the regular processes of the law in motion. So it is, of course, entirely possible to say that the action shall be limited to cases where the charge was brought from improper motives, at least if the defendant thought there was probable cause. Such a limitation would stand almost alone in the law of civil liability. But the nature of the wrong is peculiar; and, moreover, it is quite consistent with the theory of liability advanced here that liability should, in a given instance, be confined to actual wrongdoing in the moral sense.

The only other cause of action in which the moral state of the defendant's mind might seem important is conspiracy. The old action of that name was much like malicious prosecution, and no doubt it was originally confined to cases where several persons had conspired to get another indicted from evil motives. But in the modern action on the case in which conspiracy is charged, the allegation as a rule means only that two or more persons were cooperating so closely in their acts that the act of any one was the act of all. Generally speaking, the liability depends not on the cooperating or conspiring, but on the character of the acts done, taking them all as if done by one man, or without asking whether they were done by one person or by several. There may be cases, to be sure, in which the result could not be accomplished, or the offense could not ordinarily be proved, without a combination of several people—for instance, the removal of a teacher by a school board. The conspiracy would not affect such a case except in a practical way. But the question would be raised whether, notwithstanding the board's right to remove the teacher, proof that its members were moved by ill will would make the removal actionable. Policy, it might be said, forbids going behind their judgment. But actual evil motives, together with the absence of grounds, take away this protection; because policy, although it does not require them to take the risk of being right, does require that they judge honestly on the merits.

Other isolated instances like the last might perhaps be found in different parts of the law, in which actual ill will would affect a man's liability for his conduct. Again, in trover for the conversion of another's chattel, where the control exercised over it was slight and ambiguous, it has been said that the taking must be "with the intent of exercising an ownership over the chattel inconsistent with the real owner's right of possession." But this seems to be no more than a faint shadow of the doctrine explained in connection with larceny, and it needs no further or special discussion. Trover is commonly understood to rest, like larceny, on the plaintiff's being deprived of his property; although in practice every possessor has the action, and, generally speaking, the briefest wrongful withholding of possession is a conversion.

Whether the exceptions are more or fewer, the general purpose of the law of torts is to secure a man compensation for certain forms of harm to his person, reputation, or property at the hands of his neighbors—not because those harms are wrong, but because they are harms. The true explanation of why liability refers to a moral standard, in the sense that has been explained, is not that the law aims at improving men's hearts. It is that the law means to give a man a fair chance to avoid doing the harm before he is held responsible for it. It is meant to reconcile the policy of letting accidents lie where they fall, and the reasonable freedom of others, with the protection of the individual from injury.

But the law does not even try to protect a man from all harms. An unrestricted enjoyment of all his possibilities would interfere with other, equally important enjoyments on the part of his neighbors. There are certain things the law allows a man to do even though he foresees that harm to another will follow. He may charge a man with a crime if the charge is true. He may set himself up in business where he foresees that the effect of his competition will be to reduce another shopkeeper's trade, perhaps to ruin him. He may put up a building that cuts off another's beautiful view, or he may drain the water under his own ground and thereby drain another man's well; and many other cases might be given.

Since any of these things may be done with foresight of their evil consequences, it would seem that they might be done with intent, and even with malevolent intent, to produce them. The whole argument of this Lecture and the one before tends to this conclusion. If the aim of liability is simply to prevent harm, or to compensate for it, so far as is consistent with avoiding the extreme of making a man answer for accident, then when the law permits the harm to be knowingly inflicted, it would be a strong thing if the presence of malice made any difference to its decisions. That might happen, to be sure, without affecting the general views maintained here; but it is not to be expected, and the weight of authority is against it.

As the law, at one extreme, allows certain harms to be inflicted regardless of the moral condition of the person who inflicts them, so, at the other extreme, it may on grounds of policy throw the absolute risk of certain undertakings on the person who engages in them, regardless of blame in any sense. Instances of this kind were mentioned in the last Lecture, and will be referred to again.

Most liabilities in tort lie between these two extremes. They are founded on the infliction of harm which the defendant had a reasonable opportunity to avoid at the time of the acts or omissions that were its proximate cause. But as fast as specific rules are worked out in place of the vague reference to the conduct of the average man, they line up alongside other specific rules based on public policy, and the grounds they grew from cease to be visible. So, as will be seen shortly, rules that seem to lie outside blameworthiness in any sense have sometimes been traced to remote fault, while others that started from the general notion of negligence may just as easily be traced to some outside ground of policy.

Apart from the two extremes just mentioned, it is now easy to see how the law generally fixes the point at which a man's conduct begins to be at his own peril. When we understand the principle by which the law of torts fixes that point, we have a common ground of classification, and a key to the whole subject, so far as tradition has not turned the law aside from a consistent theory. It has been made fairly clear from what came before that I find that ground in knowledge of the circumstances accompanying an act or course of conduct which, but for those circumstances, would be indifferent.

But before that test is discussed, it is worth remarking that a possible common ground is reached one step earlier in the descent from malice through intent and foresight. Foresight is a possible common denominator of the wrongs at the two extremes, malice and negligence. The purpose of the law is to prevent harm to a man at the hands of his neighbors, or to secure him compensation for it, so far as is consistent with the other considerations that have been mentioned—excepting, of course, such harm as the law permits to be intentionally inflicted. When a man foresees that harm will result from his conduct, the principle that excuses him for accident no longer applies, and he is liable. But, as has been shown, he is bound to foresee whatever a prudent and intelligent man would have foreseen; and therefore he is liable for conduct from which such a man would have foreseen that harm was likely to follow.
