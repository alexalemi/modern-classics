Lecture V: The Bailee at Common Law
(Part 3 of 3)

We now come to the great case of Morse v. Slue, decided in 1671 and 1672. It was an action against the master of a ship lying in the river Thames, for the loss of goods entrusted to him. The goods had been carried off by robbers, and it was found that the ship had the usual guard aboard at the time. There seem to have been two counts. The first was on the law and custom of England, under which masters of ships were bound "carefully to govern, preserve, and defend goods shipped, so long as said ship should remain in the river Thames"; or, in another report, to keep safely goods shipped to be carried from London beyond sea, "without loss or subtraction," so that for want of such keeping they should not come to any damage; or, in a third, "to keep safely goods delivered to them to carry, dangers of the sea excepted." (The exception last stated was perhaps drawn by the reporter from the usual form of bills of lading, which were referred to in argument.) The second count, which is usually overlooked, was a special count "in case on delivery and being stolen by his neglect."

The case was argued twice, and all the reports agree, as far as they go, in their account of the points pressed.

Holt, for the plaintiff, maintained, first, that the master receives goods generally, without special terms, citing Southcote's Case; and that "only guardian in socage who hath the custody by law, and factor who is servant at the master's dispose, and so cannot take care, are exempt." (A guardian in socage held the land of an heir under age by force of law; a factor was an agent who bought and sold goods for a merchant.) Second, that the master is paid for his keeping, and is therefore a proper person to be sued. Third, that the master has a remedy over against the wrongdoers, citing the case of the Marshal of the King's Bench. Further, that the mischief would be great if the master were not liable, since merchants put their trust in him and could never show any particular default, as the bill of lading made plain; and, finally, that neglect in fact appeared.

On the other side, it was urged that no neglect had been found, and that the master was only a servant, so that if anyone was liable, it was the owners of the ship. It was also suggested that, since there would have been no liability had the goods been taken at sea—for then the case would have fallen under the admiralty law—it was absurd that one rule should govern the beginning of the voyage and another the rest of it.

Footnote: This last is the main point mentioned by two of the reporters.

On the second argument, it was again maintained for the plaintiff that the defendant was liable "at the common law on the general bailment," citing Southcote's Case, and also that he was liable under the Roman and maritime law as a public carrier and master of a ship.

The opinion of the court was delivered by Chief Justice Hale. It was held that, since the ship lay within the body of a county, the admiralty law did not apply; or, as one report puts it, "the master could not avail himself of the rules of the civil law, by which masters are not chargeable pro damno fatali"—that is, for loss by unavoidable accident. The master was liable to an action because he took a reward, and "he might have made a caution for himself, which he omitting and taking in the goods generally, he shall answer for what happens." The case of Kenrig v. Eggleston seems also to have been referred to. It was further said that the master was an officer rather than a servant, and in effect received his wages from the merchant who paid the freight. Finally, on the question of negligence, the court said it was not enough to have the usual number of men guarding the ship; it was neglect not to have enough to guard the goods, unless against the common enemies of the realm. For this it cited the case of the Marshal, which, it will be remembered, was merely the principle of Southcote's Case and the common law of bailment in another form.

It will be observed that this case did not turn on any special custom, either as to common carriers or as to shipmasters. All the arguments, and the opinion of the court, assumed that if the case was governed by the common law, and not by the milder rules of the civil law relied on for the defence; and if the defendant could be regarded as a bailee, and not merely a servant of the owners; then the general law of bailment would apply, and the defendant would be charged, as in Southcote's Case, "by his general acceptance."

It can hardly be supposed, however, that so enlightened a judge as Sir Matthew Hale would not have broken away from the Year Books if a case had come before him in which property had been received as a pure favor to the plaintiff, without consideration or reward, and had then been taken from the defendant by robbery. Such a case was tried before Chief Justice Pemberton in 1681, and he very sensibly ruled that no action lay, declining to follow the law of Lord Coke's time to such extreme results.

About the same time, the defendant's common calling began to take on a new importance. The other alternative allegation, the assumpsit, was in the end the more important of the two: it led to the doctrine, not objectionable in itself, that all duties arising from a bailment are founded on contract. But the assumpsit had by now given rise to a special action of its own, and so was not much used where the action was in tort, while the other averment, the common calling, occurs more and more often. The notion was plainly gaining ground that the liability of common carriers for loss of goods, whatever the cause of the loss, came from a special principle peculiar to them and not applicable to bailees in general. The confusion of independent duties which has been explained, and of which the first trace was seen in Rich v. Kneeland, was soon to become complete.

It will be seen directly that Lord Holt took a different view. Note, too, the way the Marshal's case was dealt with in one of the later reports. Modern illustrations of the doctrine will be found in Fleming v. Manchester, Sheffield, and Lincolnshire Railway Company, and the cases cited there.

Holt became Chief Justice. Three of the cases of this period were rulings of his.

Footnote: I have in mind Lovett v. Hobbs, Chamberlain v. Cooke, Boson v. Sandford (which cites Southcote's Case), Upshare v. Aidee, and Middleton v. Fowler.

In Lane v. Cotton, in 1701, he showed his disapproval of Southcote's Case, and his impression that the common law of bailment had been borrowed from Rome. The overthrow of Southcote's Case and of the old common law may be said to date from Coggs v. Bernard, in 1703. Lord Holt's famous opinion in that case quotes largely from the Roman law, as it filtered down to him through Bracton. But whatever influence that may have had on his general views, the point decided, and the distinctions he drew about common carriers, were of English growth.

The action did not sound in contract. The complaint was of damage to the goods, and the plaintiff sued in tort, laying an assumpsit as inducement—the introductory allegation leading up to the charge—to a charge of negligence, just as in the days of Henry VI. The plea was not guilty. But after a verdict for the plaintiff, the defendant moved in arrest of judgment—"for that it was not alleged in the declaration that the defendant was a common porter, nor averred that he had anything for his pains." Consideration was never alleged or thought of in the primitive assumpsit, but in the modern action of contract in that form it was required. Hence it was inferred that wherever an assumpsit was laid, even in an action of tort for damage to property, it was the allegation of a contract, and that a consideration must be shown for the undertaking—although the contrary had been decided in the reign of Queen Elizabeth.

But the motion did not prevail, and judgment was given for the plaintiff. Lord Holt was well aware that the use of an assumpsit was not confined to contract. It is true that he said, "The owner's trusting [the defendant] with the goods is a sufficient consideration to oblige him to a careful management," or to return them. But this means a consideration as distinguished from one sufficient to oblige him to carry them, which Holt thought the defendant would not have been bound to do. He then says expressly, "This is a different case, for assumpsit does not only signify a future agreement, but, in such cases as this, it signifies an actual entry upon the thing and taking the trust upon himself"; and in this he followed the earlier cases in the Year Books.

Footnote: How little Lord Holt meant to adopt the modern view, that delivery, being a detriment to the owner, was a consideration, may be further seen by examining the cases he put and agreed to from the Year Books. The subject is taken up in Lecture VII.

That was enough to decide the case, and the rule in Southcote's Case had nothing to do with it. But the duty of common carriers by reason of their calling was now supposed to extend to all kinds of losses, and the doctrine of Southcote's Case was probably supposed to extend to many kinds of damage. So in a general discussion it became necessary to reconcile the two principles, or to choose between them.

The Chief Justice therefore went on to distinguish between bailees for reward exercising a public employment—common carriers, common hoymen, masters of ships, and so on—and all other bailees. He denied the rule in Southcote's Case as to the second class. He said that the principle of strict responsibility was confined to the first class, and was applied to them on grounds of public policy. And he said that factors were exonerated, not because they were mere servants, as had always been laid down (among others, by Holt himself in arguing Morse v. Slue), but because they were not within the reason of the rule.

The reader who has followed the argument so far will hardly need convincing that this did not mean the adoption of the Praetor's Edict. There is further evidence at hand if it is wanted.

In the first place, as we have seen, there was a century of precedents, ending with Morse v. Slue, argued by Holt himself, in which the liability of masters of ships, hoymen, carriers, and the like had been decided. Morse v. Slue is cited and relied on, and there is no hint of dissatisfaction with the other cases. On the contrary, they furnished the examples of bailees for reward exercising a public calling. The distinction between bailees for reward and others is Chief Justice Popham's. The further qualification, exercising a public calling, was also English, as has partly appeared already and as will be explained further on.

In the next place, the strict rule is not confined to nautae, caupones, and stabularii—the shipmasters, innkeepers, and stable-keepers of the Edict—nor even to common carriers. It is applied to all bailees for reward exercising a public calling.

In the next place, the degree of responsibility is precisely that of bailees in general, as worked out by the earlier decisions; and it is quite unlike, and much more severe than, the responsibility imposed by the Roman law, as others have observed.

And finally, the exemption from liability for acts of God or the public enemy is characteristically English, as will be proved further on.

But it has been partly shown in this Lecture that the law of today has made the carrier's burden heavier than it was in the time of the Year Books. Southcote's Case, and the earlier authorities that have been cited, all concern a loss by robbery, theft, or trespass, and hold the bailee liable where, in theory at least, he has a remedy over against the wrongdoer. It was with such cases in view, as we have seen, that the rule arose, although it is not improbable that it would also have been applied to a loss nobody could explain; the writ against innkeepers reads absque subtractione seu amissione custodire, "to keep without removal or loss." In later times the principle may have been extended from loss by theft to loss by destruction. In Symons v. Darknoll, in the reign of Charles I, already cited as decided on the authority of Southcote's Case, the goods were spoiled, not stolen, and probably had not even perished in specie—that is, ceased to exist as the things they were. By then the old rule had become an arbitrary precedent, followed according to its form with little thought of its true purpose.

The language of Coggs v. Bernard is that "the law charges the person thus intrusted to carry goods as against all events but acts of God and the enemies of the king." This was adopted by solemn decision in Lord Mansfield's time, and it is now settled that the common carrier "is liable for all losses which do not fall within the excepted cases." That is to say, he has become an insurer to that extent: not only against the disappearance or destruction of the goods, but against every form of damage to them, except as excepted above.

The process by which this came about has been traced above, but a few words may be added here. The Year Books, even when dealing with the destruction (as distinguished from the conversion) of chattels in the hands of a bailee, always state his liability as based on his fault—though it must be admitted that the language is used alio intuitu, with a different question in view. A jettison in a tempest—throwing the goods overboard to save the ship—seems to have been a good plea for a factor in the time of Edward III; but that cannot be relied on as an analogy. The argument from the Marshal's case is stronger. There it seems to have been thought that the burning of the prison was as good an excuse for an escape as a release by alien enemies. This must mean an accidental fire, and would seem to imply that the Marshal was not liable in that event if he was not at fault. The writs in the Register against bailees to keep or carry goods all carry the general allegation of negligence, and so do the older precedents of declarations, so far as I have observed, whether they state the custom of the realm or not. But a bailee was answerable for goods wrongfully taken from him, as an innkeeper was for goods stolen from his inn, whether he was negligent or not.

It is true that the Marshal's case speaks of his negligent keeping when the prisoners were released by rebels (although that was far less likely to come from negligence, one would think, than a fire in the prison), and that after Lord Coke's time negligence was alleged even though the goods had been lost by a wrongful taking. So, too, the writ against innkeepers is pro defectu hujusmodi hospitatorum, "for the default of such innkeepers." In these instances neglect only means a failure in fact to keep safely. As was said at a much later date, "everything is a negligence in a carrier or hoyman that the law does not excuse." The allegation is simply the usual allegation of actions on the case, and seems to have spread from the earlier declarations for damage when case supplanted detinue and the use of case became universal. It can hardly have been immaterial to the kind of case for which it was first introduced. But the short reason for disbelieving that the old law gave any warrant for making the carrier an insurer against damage is that there seem to be no early cases in which bailees were held to such a responsibility, and that it was not within the principle on which they were made answerable for a loss by theft.

Having traced the process by which a common carrier has been made an insurer, it only remains to say a word on the origin of the admitted exceptions from the risk he assumes. We have already seen how loss by the public enemy came to be mentioned by Chief Justice Holt. It is the old distinction taken in the Marshal's case: against the public enemy the bailee has no remedy over.

As for the act of God, it was a general principle, not peculiar to carriers or to bailees, that a duty was discharged if an act of God made it impossible to perform. Lord Coke mentions the case of a jettison from a Gravesend barge, and another of a party bound to keep up sea-walls against flooding, as subject to the same limitation; and a similar statement about contracts in general will be found in the Year Books. It is another form of the principle which has been laboriously reargued in our own day: that parties are excused from performing a contract which has become impossible before breach, whether because the thing has perished or because of a change in circumstances whose continued existence was the foundation of the contract, provided there was no warranty and no fault on the part of the contractor. Whether the act of God has now acquired a special meaning with regard to common carriers may be left for others to consider.

It appears from the foregoing evidence that we cannot determine which classes of bailees are subject to the strict responsibility imposed on common carriers by turning to the Praetor's Edict and then consulting the dictionaries under nautae, caupones, or stabularii. The question of precedent is simply how far the old common law of bailment still survives. We can answer it only by listing the decisions in which the old law is applied, and we shall find it hard to bring them together under a general principle. The rule in Southcote's Case has been done away with for bailees in general: that is clear. But it is equally clear that the rule has not held its ground even within the limits of the public policy invented by Chief Justice Holt. It is not true today that all bailees for reward exercising a public calling are insurers. No such doctrine is applied to grain elevators or safe-deposit vaults.

How Lord Holt came to distinguish between bailees for reward and others has been shown above. It is more to the point here to notice that his further qualification, exercising a public calling, was part of a protective system which has passed away. One inclined to be hostile might say that it was one of many signs that the law was administered in the interest of the upper classes. It has been shown above that if a man was a common farrier, he could be charged for negligence without an assumpsit. The same judge who threw out that suggestion established in another case that the farrier could be sued if he refused to shoe a horse on reasonable request. Common carriers and common innkeepers were liable in the same way, and Lord Holt stated the principle: "If a man takes upon him a public employment, he is bound to serve the public as far as the employment extends, and for refusal an action lies." An attempt to apply this doctrine generally at the present day would be thought monstrous. But it formed part of a consistent scheme for holding those who followed useful callings up to the mark. Another part was the liability of persons exercising a public employment for loss or damage, heightened in cases of bailment by what remained of the rule in Southcote's Case. The scheme has given way to more liberal notions; but the disjecta membra—the scattered limbs—still move.

Lord Mansfield stated his views of public policy in terms not unlike those Chief Justice Holt used in Coggs v. Bernard, but he distinctly confines their application to common carriers. "But there is a further degree of responsibility by the custom of the realm, that is, by the common law; a carrier is in the nature of an insurer.... To prevent litigation, collusion, and the necessity of going into circumstances impossible to be unravelled, the law presumes against the carrier, unless," etc.

At the present day it is assumed that the principle is confined in this way, and the argument is shifted to the question of who are common carriers. It is thus conceded, by implication, that Lord Holt's rule has been abandoned. But the trouble is that with it disappear not only the general system which we have seen Lord Holt held, but also the special reasons repeated by Lord Mansfield. Those reasons apply to other bailees as well as to common carriers. Besides, hoymen and masters of ships were not originally held liable because they were common carriers; all three were treated as co-ordinate species, even in Coggs v. Bernard, where they were mentioned only as so many instances of bailees exercising a public calling. We do not get a new and single principle simply by giving a single name to all the cases to be accounted for. If there is a sound rule of public policy which ought to impose a special responsibility on common carriers, as those words are now understood, and on no others, it has never yet been stated. If, on the other hand, there are considerations that apply to a particular class among those so called—for instance, to railroads, which may have a private individual at their mercy, or may wield a power too vast for the common welfare—we do not prove that the reasoning extends to a general ship or a public cab by calling all three common carriers.

If there is no common rule of policy, and common carriers remain a merely empirical exception to general doctrine, courts may well hesitate to stretch the meaning of those words. Furthermore, notions of public policy that would not leave parties free to make their own bargains are somewhat discredited in most departments of the law. Hence it may perhaps be concluded that, if any new case should arise, the degree of responsibility, and the validity and interpretation of any contract of bailment there may be, should stand open to argument on general principles, and that the matter has been set at large so far as early precedent is concerned.

I have treated the law of carriers at greater length than its share of the subject deserves, because it seems to me an interesting example of the way in which the common law has grown up, and especially because it is an excellent illustration of the principles laid down at the end of the first Lecture.
