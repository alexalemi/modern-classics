Lecture V: The Bailee at Common Law
(Part 2 of 3)

A few years later the famous Littleton states the law in the same way. He says that if goods are delivered to a man, he shall have an action of trespass if they are carried off, because he is chargeable over. That is, he is bound to make the loss good to the person who trusted him with them.

Footnote: It is proper to add that in the second of these cases Littleton does not seem to distinguish between servants and bailees.

In the ninth year of Edward IV, Danby says that if a bailee received goods to keep as his own goods, then robbery shall excuse him; otherwise not. Again, in a later case, robbery is said to be no excuse at all. There may have been some hesitation about robbery when the robber was unknown, so that the bailee had no remedy over. There may even have been hesitation about robbery generally, on the ground that, because the robbery was a felony, the bailee could not go against either the robber's body or his estate: the one was hanged and the other forfeited. But there is not a shadow of doubt that the bailee was not excused by an ordinary wrongful taking. "If the goods are taken by a trespasser, of whom the bailee has conusance"—that is, whom he knows—"he shall be chargeable to his bailor, and shall have his action over against his trespasser." The same point was touched in other passages of the Year Books, and the rule of law is clearly implied by the reason given for the bailee's right to sue in the cases cited above.

The principle was decided directly, in accordance with the ancient law, in the famous case of Southcote v. Bennet. This was an action of detinue for goods delivered to the defendant to keep safely. The defendant admitted the delivery, and answered that he had been robbed of the goods by one J.S. "And, after argument at the bar, Gawdy and Clench, ceteris absentibus"—the other judges being absent—"held that the plaintiff ought to recover, because it was not a special bailment; that the defendant accepted them to keep as his proper goods, and not otherwise; but it is a delivery, which chargeth him to keep them at his peril. And it is not any plea in a detinue to say that he was robbed by one such; for he hath his remedy over by trespass, or appeal, to have them again." This passage from Croke's report implies what Lord Coke says outright in his own report of the case, that "to be kept, and to be kept safe, is all one." And both reports agree that the obligation was founded on the delivery alone. Croke's report also confirms the caution that Lord Coke adds at the end of his: "Note, reader, it is good policy for him who takes any goods to keep, to take them in special manner, scil. to keep them as he keeps his own goods,... or if they happen to be stolen or purloined, that he shall not be answerable for them; for he who accepted them ought to take them in such or the like manner, or otherwise he may be charged by his general acceptance." In other words, the careful bailee should say at the start that he takes the goods only on those terms.

Down to this time, at least, the law was clear. If a person accepted possession of goods to keep for another, even as a favor, and lost them by a wrongful taking wholly without his own fault, he was bound to make good the loss—unless, when he took possession, he had expressly stipulated against such a responsibility. Lord Holt in Coggs v. Bernard, and Sir William Jones in his book on Bailments, tried to show that Southcote v. Bennet was not supported by authority. The attempts were futile, as any one who will study the Year Books for himself may see. Chief Baron Peryam had laid down the same principle seven years before, in Drake v. Royman, and Southcote's Case was followed without question as a leading precedent for a hundred years.

Thus the circle of analogies between the English and the early German law is complete. In both there is the same procedure for lost property, turning on the single question whether the plaintiff had lost possession against his will. There is the same principle that, if the person trusted with the property parted with it to another, the owner could not recover it from that other, but had to get his indemnity from his own bailee. There is the same inverted explanation—that the bailee could sue because he was answerable over—and yet the substance of the true doctrine survives in the rule that when he had no remedy he was not answerable. And, finally, there is the same absolute responsibility for loss, even when the loss happened without any fault in the person trusted. The last and most important of these principles is seen in force as late as the reign of Queen Elizabeth. We now have to follow its later fortunes.

A common carrier—one who makes a public business of carrying goods for anybody who will pay—is liable for goods that are stolen from him, or otherwise lost from his charge, except by the act of God or the public enemy: that is, by natural forces no man could resist, or by a nation at war with his own. Two notions have been held about the source of this rule. One is that it was borrowed from the Roman law. The other is that it was introduced by custom, as an exception to the general law of bailment, in the reigns of Elizabeth and James I.

I shall try to show that both notions are wrong. This strict responsibility is a fragmentary survival from the general law of bailment which I have just explained. The changes the old law has undergone were due to three things: in part to a confusion of ideas which came in when the action on the case displaced detinue; in part to ideas of public policy which Lord Holt read into the precedents; and in part to still later ideas of policy which later judges have read into the reasoning of Lord Holt.

Southcote's Case was decided in the forty-third year of Queen Elizabeth, A.D. 1601. I think the first mention of a carrier that bears on the question occurs in Woodlife's Case, decided four or five years earlier, in 1596 or 1597. It was an action of account—the old action against one who had handled another's property and owed him a reckoning—for merchandise delivered to the defendant, apparently as a factor, or agent to buy and sell ("pur merchandizer"), and clearly not as a carrier. The defendant's plea was that the goods had been taken by robbers at sea along with his own. Gawdy, one of the judges who later decided Southcote's Case, thought the plea bad. But Chief Justice Popham said that, although it would not be a good plea for a carrier, because a carrier is paid for his carriage, there was a difference on this point between carriers and other servants and factors.

This is repeated in Southcote's Case, and it seems to involve a double distinction: first, between paid and unpaid bailees; and next, between bailees and servants. If the defendant was a servant without control over the goods, he might not fall within the law of bailment at all, and in the early law factors were treated on the footing of servants.

The other distinction, between paid and unpaid, marked the entrance of the doctrine of consideration into the law of bailment. Consideration originally meant quid pro quo—something given in exchange—as will be explained in Lecture VII. That is how it was treated in Doctor and Student, the sixteenth-century dialogue between a doctor of divinity and a student of the common law, written when the principle was still young. Chief Justice Popham probably borrowed his distinction between paid and unpaid bailees from that work, where common carriers are given as an example of the paid class. A little earlier, reward had made no difference.

But in Woodlife's Case, in reply to what the Chief Justice had said, Gawdy cited the case of the Marshal of the King's Bench, stated above. At that Popham fell back on the old distinction: the jailer had a remedy over against the rebels who broke his prison, but in the case before the court there was no remedy over.

The other cases relied on were some of those on bailment in general collected above—the same authorities, in short, on which Southcote's Case was founded. The principle adopted was the same as in Southcote's Case, and the only question was whether the defendant fell within it. Nothing was said of any custom of the realm, nor had anything ever been said of one in any reported case before this time. I believe this is the first instance in which carriers are distinguished in any way from any other class of persons trusted with goods. There is no hint in the old books of any special obligation peculiar to them; and it is certainly not true that this case introduced one. It will be noticed, with reference to what follows, that Popham does not speak of common carriers, but simply of carriers.

Next came Southcote's Case itself, in 1601, which presented the old law pure and simple, without regard to reward or any modern innovation. In this case, and in the earlier instances of loss by theft, the action was detinue, and the plaintiff counted—that is, stated his claim—simply, we may presume, on a delivery and a wrongful detaining of the goods.

But about this time important changes took place in the procedure usually adopted, and they must be explained. If the chattel could be returned in specie—the thing itself—detinue gave no satisfaction for any damage it might have suffered through the bailee's neglect. The natural remedy for such damage was the action on the case. But before case could be made entirely satisfactory, certain difficulties had to be overcome. The neglect that caused the damage might be a mere omission. And what was there in a nonfeasance—a failure to act—that resembled trespass, to support the analogy on which trespass on the case was founded? Moreover, to charge a man for not acting, you must show that it was his duty to act. As pleadings were construed in those days, it would not have been enough to allege that the plaintiff's goods were damaged by the defendant's negligence. These troubles had been got over by the well-known words super se assumpsit, which will be explained later. For a long time assumpsit did not become an independent action of contract. The allegation was simply the inducement—the introductory statement of circumstances—to an action of tort. The ground of liability was that the defendant had begun upon the undertaking, so that his negligent omission, which let in the damage, could be connected with his acts as part of his dealing with the thing. We shall find Lord Holt recognizing this original meaning of assumpsit when we come to Coggs v. Bernard. Of course it was not confined to cases of bailment.

But there was another way, besides this, by which the defendant could be charged with a duty and made liable in case. Although it is less familiar to lawyers, it has a special bearing on the law of carriers in later times. If damage had been done or brought about by the act or omission of the defendant in the pursuit of one of the more common callings—that of a farrier, for instance, who shoes horses—it seems that the action could be maintained without laying an assumpsit, on the allegation that he was a "common" farrier. This principle, too, was wholly independent of bailment. It expressed the general obligation of those who carried on a public or "common" business to practise their art on demand, and to show skill in it. "For," as Fitzherbert says, "it is the duty of every artificer to exercise his art rightly and truly as he ought."

Once it had been established in this way that case would lie for damage occasioned by the defendant's omission as well as for damage caused by his act, there was no reason to deny it even when the negligent custody had resulted in the destruction of the property. From there it was only a step to extend the same form of action to every case of loss by a bailee, and so to take away the defendant's right to wage his law—the ancient privilege of clearing himself by his own oath, backed by the oaths of neighbors who swore to his credit. Detinue, the primitive remedy, kept that mark of primitive procedure. The last extension was made at about the time of Southcote's Case. But when one form of action came to be used alike for damage or destruction by the bailee's neglect and for loss by a wrong-doer against whom the bailee had a remedy over, a source of confusion was opened about the foundation and nature of the defendant's duty.

Footnote: The process may be traced by reading the cases in order, from the reign of Henry VII, through the early years of Henry VIII and the later years of Elizabeth, down to the eleventh year of James I.

In truth there were two sets of duties. One was not peculiar to bailees, and arose from the defendant's assumpsit or his public calling, as just explained. The other was the ancient obligation peculiar to bailees as such, of which Southcote's Case was an example. But by Lord Coke's time assumpsit had been taken over by contract and the doctrine of consideration had been developed. After that, any obligation of a bailee might be thought of as part of a contract of bailment, and it seemed unnecessary to distinguish nicely between the two sets of duties, provided a consideration and a special promise could be alleged. Furthermore, since the defendant's public calling had formerly had the same effect as an assumpsit for the purpose of charging him in tort, it now seems to have been thought an equally good substitute for a special promise in order to charge him in assumpsit. In Rogers v. Head the argument was that to charge a man in assumpsit you must show either his public calling at the time of the delivery, or a special promise on sufficient consideration. This argument assumes that a bailee who received goods in the course of a public employment—as a common carrier, for instance—could be charged in this form of action for a breach of either set of duties, by alleging either his public calling or his reward and a special promise. It seems to have been admitted, as was repeatedly decided both before and after that case, that one who was not a common carrier could have been charged for failing to deliver in a special action—that is, in case, as distinguished from assumpsit.

Footnote: There is an obscurely reported case of the late years of Elizabeth, Mosley v. Fosset, which seems to have been assumpsit against an agistor—a man paid to pasture another's cattle—for a horse stolen while in his charge. It asserts in passing that "without such special assumpsit the action does not lie." This must refer to the form of the action, since the judges who decided Southcote's Case took part in the decision.

Suppose, next, that the plaintiff sued in case, for a tort. As before, the breach of duty complained of might be damage to property of the kind that had always been sued for in that form of action. Or it might be a loss by theft, for which detinue would formerly have been brought, and which fell on the bailee only because of the bailment. If the goods had been stolen, the bailee's liability rested neither on his common calling nor on his assumpsit and his neglect. It arose from the naked facts that he had accepted a delivery and that the goods were gone, and in such cases it ought to have been enough to allege those facts in the declaration. But it was very natural that the time-honored foundations of the action on the case, in its narrower use, should still be laid in the pleadings even after the scope of the action had been enlarged. We shall have to ask later whether the principles of Southcote's Case were not also stretched in the opposite direction, to cases that did not fall within them. The reasons for the rule it laid down had lost their meaning centuries before Gawdy and Clench were born, when owners acquired the right to sue for the wrongful taking of property in the hands of a bailee. The rule itself was a dry precedent, likely to be followed according to the letter because the spirit had departed. It had begun to totter when the reporter warned bailees to accept goods on terms that would get rid of it.

Footnote: The second count in Morse v. Slue, below, shows that the averment of negligence was mere form.

Accordingly, although that decision was the main authority relied on, whenever a peculiar responsibility was placed on bailees, for the hundred years between it and Coggs v. Bernard, we find that the pleaders did various things. Sometimes an assumpsit was laid, as in the early precedents. More often the bailee was alleged to be a common bargeman, or common carrier, or the like, without much reference to the special nature of the tort in question. And the true bearing of that allegation was sometimes lost sight of. At first, however, there were only slight signs of confusion in the language of one or two cases; and if the duty was understood to fall within the principle of Southcote's Case, pleaders did not always allege the common or public calling, which was held to be unnecessary. But they also borrowed other devices from the precedents in case, or used them to strengthen an obligation they did not well understand. Chief Justice Popham had approved a distinction between paid and unpaid bailees, so it was thought prudent to lay a reward. Negligence was of course alleged. And finally it became common to allege an obligation "by the law and custom of the realm." This last deserves a little more attention.

There is no writ in the Register—the official collection of the forms of writ—that alleges any special obligation of common carriers by the custom of the realm. But the writ against innkeepers did lay a duty "by the law and custom of England," and it was easy to adopt the phrase. The allegation did not so much imply the existence of a special principle as state a proposition of law in the form then usual. Other writs of trespass allege a common-law duty in the same way, and still others set out a duty imposed by statute. So, too, "the judges were sworn to execute justice according to law and the custom of England."

The duties of a common carrier, so far as the earlier evidence goes, were simply those of bailees in general, together with the liabilities generally attached to the exercise of a public calling. The word "common" spoke only to the second point, as has been shown above. This is further illustrated by the fact that, when the duty was set out in this way, it was not alleged as an obligation peculiar to common carriers as such. It was laid as the custom or law of common hoymen, or lightermen—the men who worked the small coasting boats and the barges of a harbor—or whatever the business of the party happened to be. It will be noticed that Chief Justice Holt, in Coggs v. Bernard, states the liability as applying to all bailees for reward who exercise a public employment, and mentions common hoymen and masters of ships alongside common carriers, not as included under them. It will also be noticed that in the cases before that time there is no settled formula for the obligation in question. In each case it is set out that the defendant was answerable for what he was said to have done or failed to do in that particular instance.

Returning now to the cases in their order, Rich v. Kneeland comes next, in the eleventh year of James I, A.D. 1613. It was an action on the case, that is, in tort, against a common hoyman. In Croke's report nothing is said of custom. But the declaration alleges that the defendant was a common bargeman; that the plaintiff delivered him a portmanteau and other things to carry, and paid him for it; and that the defendant tam negligenter custodivit—kept it so negligently—that it was taken from him by persons unknown. This is like the second count in Morse v. Slue, below. The defendant's plea was demurred to, and judgment was given for the plaintiff. A writ of error was then brought, to have the judgment reviewed, and the error assigned was that "this action lies not against a common bargeman without special promise. But all the Justices and Barons held, that it well lies as against a common carrier upon the land." If we follow this report, it seems at first glance that importance was attached to the common calling. But the loss was clearly within the principle of Southcote's Case, which required neither a special promise nor a common calling for its application, and which remained unquestioned law for three quarters of a century afterwards. So the court must have been referring to the form of action used (case), and not to whether the defendant was liable in some form of action (detinue). The objection was that "this action lies not" without special promise—not that the defendant was not liable. Even narrowed in this way, the decision rather encourages the notion that allegations which were needed to charge a man for damage happening through his neglect, in the older and narrower use of this action, were also needed in its new extension to a different class of wrongs. Since it was by now fairly clear that case would lie for a nonfeasance, the notion was mistaken, and we shall see that later decisions denied it.

Footnote: The use which has been made of this case in later times shows how extremely difficult it is, in the older books, to distinguish between principles of substantive law and rules relating only to procedure.

According to Hobart's report of the same case, it was alleged that the defendant was a common hoyman, carrying goods by water for hire, and that by the custom of England such carriers ought to keep the goods so that they should not be lost by the default of themselves or their servants. "And it was resolved that, though it was laid as a custom of the realm, yet indeed it is common law." This last resolution may mean only that the custom of the realm and the common law are the same thing, as had been said about innkeepers long before. But the law as to innkeepers, which the writ called the custom of the realm, had something of the air of a special principle reaching beyond the law of bailment, since an innkeeper's liability extended to goods within the inn of which he did not have the custody. The court may have meant to set such a special principle against the common law, the general law of bailment, which governed the case before it.

Whatever doubts some of Croke's language might raise, standing alone, one fact remains beyond dispute. For nearly a century from Woodlife's Case, the liability of carriers for loss of goods—whether the custom of the realm or the defendant's common calling was alleged or not—rested on the authority of Southcote's Case, and was meant to be decided on its principle.

Symons v. Darknoll, in 1628, is precisely in point. The declaration was that by the common law every lighterman ought so to manage his lighter that the goods carried in it should not perish. "And although no promise laid, it seemed to the court that the plaintiff should recover; and not alleging that defendant was common lighterman was no harm. Hyde, C. J., delivery makes the contract." This did not mean that delivery was a good consideration for a promise. It meant, as Southcote's Case had laid down, that delivery, without a special acceptance to keep the goods only as one's own, bound the bailee to keep them safely, and therefore made it unnecessary to allege either an assumpsit or the defendant's common calling. Justice Whitlock pointed out that the action was in tort, not contract. "Et en cest case... Southcote's Case fuit cite"—and in this case Southcote's Case was cited.

The same year, Sergeant Maynard, arguing in Williams v. Hide, stated the same rule for bailments in general, again citing Southcote's Case.

In Kenrig v. Eggleston, in 1648, which was "case against a country carrier for not delivering a box," and so on, of which he had been robbed, nothing was said about custom, nor about the defendant's being a common carrier, unless the words just quoted imply that he was. But it was laid down, as in Southcote's Case, that "it must come on the carrier's part to make special acceptance" if he wanted to lessen his liability as a bailee.

Nichols v. Moore, in 1661, was case against a "water carrier" between Hull and London, alleging a delivery to him at York. It was moved in arrest of judgment—a motion that judgment not be entered because the record is defective—that the defendant had not undertaken to carry the goods from York to Hull. "But notwithstanding this per totam curiam"—by the whole court—"the defendant shall be charged on his general receipt at York, according to Southcote's Case."

It is fair to mention that in Matthews v. Hopkins, in the seventeenth year of Charles II, the declaration was on the custom of the realm against a common carrier, and there was a motion in arrest of judgment on three grounds: that the custom of the realm was misrecited; that the defendant was not alleged to have been a carrier at the time he received the goods; and that counts in trover and in case on the custom had been joined. Judgment was arrested, apparently on the last ground. But the court went on: "And, although the declaration may be good without recital of the custom of the realm, as Hobart says, still it is the better way to recite it."
