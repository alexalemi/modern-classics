Lecture II: The Criminal Law
(Part 1 of 2)

At the beginning of the first Lecture it was shown that the appeals of the early law were aimed only at intentional wrongs. The appeal was a far older form of procedure than the indictment, the formal accusation of crime brought in the name of the state, and it may be said to have had a criminal as well as a civil side. It had a double object: to satisfy the private party for his loss, and to satisfy the king for the breach of his peace. On its civil side it was rooted in vengeance. It was a proceeding to recover those compositions, at first optional and afterwards compulsory, by which a wrong-doer bought the spear from his side. Whether, so far as the king was concerned, it had the same object of vengeance, or was aimed more particularly at revenue, does not matter, since the king's claim did not enlarge the scope of the action.

It seems a fair inference that indictable offences were originally limited in the same way as those which gave rise to an appeal. For whether the indictment arose by a splitting up of the appeal, or in some other way, the two were closely connected.

An acquittal of the appellee, the accused, on the merits was a bar to an indictment. And on the other hand, once an appeal was fairly started, the case might still be carried on in the king's behalf, even though the appellor, the accuser, failed to prosecute, or was defeated by a plea.

The presentment is the other parent of our criminal procedure: the accusation laid before the court by a body of neighbors sworn to report the crimes of their district. It had an origin distinct from the appeal. If, as has been thought, it was merely the successor of fresh suit, the hot pursuit of a wrong-doer by those he had wronged, and of lynch law, then this too is the child of vengeance, even more clearly than the other.

The desire for vengeance implies an opinion that its object is actually and personally to blame. It takes an internal standard, not an objective or external one, and condemns its victim by that. The question is whether such a standard is still accepted, either in this primitive form or in some more refined development. That is commonly supposed, and it seems not impossible, considering how slowly the criminal law has improved.

It certainly may be argued, with some force, that it has never ceased to be one object of punishment to satisfy the desire for vengeance. The argument becomes plain if we consider the cases in which, for one reason or another, compensation for a wrong is out of the question.

Thus an act may be of a kind that makes indemnity impossible, by putting an end to the principal sufferer, as in murder or manslaughter.

Again, these and other crimes, like forgery, although directed against one individual, tend to make others feel unsafe, and this general insecurity cannot be paid for.

Again, there are cases where there is no means of enforcing indemnity. In Macaulay's draft of the Indian Penal Code, breaches of contract for carrying passengers were made criminal. The palanquin-bearers of India, who carried travelers in covered litters on poles, were too poor to pay damages. Yet they had to be trusted to carry unprotected women and children through wild and desolate country, where their desertion would have left those in their charge in great danger.

In all these cases punishment remains as an alternative. A pain can be inflicted on the wrong-doer that does not restore the injured party to his former situation, or to another equally good, but is inflicted for the very purpose of causing pain. And so far as this punishment takes the place of compensation—whether because the person wronged is dead, or because the persons affected are countless, or because the suffering cannot be priced in money, or because the criminal is poor—it may be said that one of its objects is to gratify the desire for vengeance. The prisoner pays with his body.

The statement may be made stronger still. It may be said not only that the law does make the gratification of revenge an object, but that it ought to. This at any rate is the opinion of two authorities as great, and as opposed in their other views, as Bishop Butler and Jeremy Bentham. Sir James Stephen says, "The criminal law stands to the passion of revenge in much the same relation as marriage to the sexual appetite."

The first requirement of a sound body of law is, that it should correspond with the actual feelings and demands of the community, whether right or wrong. If people would gratify the passion of revenge outside the law when the law did not help them, the law has no choice but to satisfy the craving itself, and so avoid the greater evil of private retribution. At the same time, this passion is not one that we encourage, either as private individuals or as lawmakers. Moreover, it does not cover the whole ground. There are crimes that do not excite it, and we should naturally expect the most important purposes of punishment to reach as far as punishment itself reaches. It remains to be discovered whether such a general purpose exists, and if so, what it is. Different theories still divide opinion on the subject.

It has been thought that the purpose of punishment is to reform the criminal; that it is to deter the criminal and others from committing similar crimes; and that it is retribution. Few would now maintain that the first of these was the only purpose. If it were, every prisoner should be released as soon as it is clear that he will never repeat his offence, and if he is incurable he should not be punished at all. And of course it would be hard to reconcile the death penalty with this doctrine.

The main struggle lies between the other two. On one side is the notion that there is a mystic bond between wrong and punishment; on the other, that the infliction of pain is only a means to an end. Hegel, one of the great expounders of the first view, puts it in his almost mathematical form: wrong is the negation of right, and punishment is the negation of that negation, or retribution. So the punishment must be equal to the crime, in the sense of proportionate to it, because its only function is to destroy it. Others, without this logical apparatus, are content to rely on a felt necessity that suffering should follow wrong-doing.

It is objected that the preventive theory is immoral. It overlooks the ill-desert of wrong-doing, the objection runs, and it gives no measure of how much punishment is due, except the lawgiver's own opinion of how much suffering will be enough to prevent the crime. In the language of Kant, it treats man as a thing, not as a person; as a means, not as an end in himself. It is said to conflict with the sense of justice, and to violate the fundamental principle of all free communities, that their members have equal rights to life, liberty, and personal security.

In spite of all this, probably most English-speaking lawyers would accept the preventive theory without hesitation. As to the charge that it violates equal rights, it may be replied that the dogma of equality makes an equation between individuals only, not between an individual and the community. No society has ever admitted that it could not sacrifice individual welfare to its own existence. If it needs conscripts for its army, it seizes them, and marches them, with bayonets in their rear, to death. It runs highways and railroads through old family places in spite of the owner's protest. It pays the market value in that case, to be sure, because no civilized government sacrifices the citizen more than it can help; but it still sacrifices his will and his welfare to those of the rest.

Footnote: Even the law recognizes that this is a sacrifice.

If it were necessary to encroach further on the field of morals, it might be suggested that the dogma of equality, even between individuals, applies only within the limits of ordinary dealings in the common run of affairs. You cannot argue with your neighbor except on the admission, for the moment, that he is as wise as you, although you may by no means believe it. In the same way, you cannot deal with him, where both are free to choose, except on the footing of equal treatment and the same rules for both. The ever-growing value set on peace and on the social relations tends to make the law of social life look like the law of all life. But it seems to me clear that the ultima ratio—the last argument, not only of kings but of private persons—is force, and that at the bottom of all private relations, however tempered by sympathy and all the social feelings, is a justifiable self-preference. If a man is on a plank in the deep sea which will only float one, and a stranger lays hold of it, he will thrust him off if he can. When the state finds itself in a similar position, it does the same thing.

The considerations that answer the argument of equal rights also answer the objection that the theory treats man as a thing, and the like. If a man lives in society, he is liable to find himself so treated. The degree of civilization a people has reached is no doubt marked by their anxiety to do as they would be done by. It may be the destiny of man that the social instincts will grow to control his actions absolutely, even in anti-social situations. But they have not yet done so. And since the rules of law are, or should be, based on a morality that is generally accepted, no rule founded on a theory of absolute unselfishness can be laid down without a breach between law and working beliefs.

If it is true, as I shall presently try to show, that the general principles of criminal and civil liability are the same, it will follow from that alone that theory and fact agree in frequently punishing people who have been guilty of no moral wrong—people who could not be condemned by any standard that did not openly disregard the personal peculiarities of the individuals concerned. If punishment stood on the moral grounds proposed for it, the first thing to be considered would be the limits on the capacity for choosing rightly that arise from abnormal instincts, want of education, lack of intelligence, and all the other defects most marked in the criminal classes. I do not say that they should not be considered, or at least I do not need to say so for my argument. I do not say that the criminal law does more good than harm. I only say that it is not enacted or administered on that theory.

There remains the affirmative argument for the theory of retribution: that the fitness of punishment following wrong-doing is axiomatic, and is instinctively recognized by unperverted minds. I think it will be seen, on self-inspection, that this feeling of fitness is absolute and unconditional only in the case of our neighbors. Suppose a man has satisfied himself that an act of his was wrong, and that he will never do it again. It does not seem to me that he would feel the least need or propriety, as between himself and an earthly punishing power alone, in his being made to suffer for what he had done—although, once third persons came in, he might admit as a philosopher the necessity of hurting him to frighten others. But when our neighbors do wrong, we sometimes feel the fitness of making them smart for it, whether they have repented or not. The feeling of fitness seems to me to be only vengeance in disguise, and I have already admitted that vengeance was an element, though not the chief element, of punishment.

But again, the supposed intuition of fitness does not seem to me to reach as far as the thing it is meant to account for. The lesser punishments are just as fit for the lesser crimes as the greater for the greater. The demand that crime be followed by its punishment should therefore be equal and absolute in both. Again, a malum prohibitum, an act wrong only because the law forbids it, is just as much a crime as a malum in se, an act wrong in itself. If there is any general ground for punishment, it must apply to the one as much as to the other. But suppose the wrong in the case just imagined was a breach of the revenue laws, and the government had been repaid its loss. It will hardly be said that we should feel any inner necessity that a man who had thoroughly repented should be punished for it, except on the ground that his act was known to others. If it was known, the law would have to make good its threats, so that others might believe and tremble. But if the fact was a secret between the sovereign and the subject, the sovereign, if wholly free from passion, would undoubtedly see that punishment in such a case was wholly without justification.

On the other hand, there can be no case in which the lawmaker makes certain conduct criminal without thereby showing a wish and purpose to prevent that conduct. Prevention, then, would seem to be the chief and only universal purpose of punishment. The law threatens certain pains if you do certain things, intending thereby to give you a new motive for not doing them. If you persist in doing them, it has to inflict the pains in order that its threats may continue to be believed.

If this is a true account of the law as it stands, the law does undoubtedly treat the individual as a means to an end, and uses him as a tool to increase the general welfare at his own expense. It has been suggested above that this course is perfectly proper; but even if it is wrong, our criminal law follows it, and the theory of our criminal law must be shaped accordingly.

Further evidence that our law goes beyond the limits of retribution, and puts the public well-being ahead of the individual, will be found in some doctrines that cannot be satisfactorily explained on any other ground.

The first of these is that even the deliberate taking of life will not be punished when it is the only way of saving one's own. This principle is not so clearly established as the one I shall mention next, but it has the support of very great authority. If it is the law, it must rest on one of two grounds. Either self-preference is proper in the case supposed; or, even if it is improper, the law cannot prevent it by punishment, because a threat of death at some future time can never be a strong enough motive to make a man choose death now in order to avoid the threat. If the first ground is adopted, it admits that a single person may sacrifice another to himself, and a fortiori—all the more—that a people may. If the second is taken, then by abandoning punishment when punishment can no longer be expected to prevent the act, the law abandons the retributive theory and adopts the preventive one.

The next doctrine leads to still clearer conclusions. Ignorance of the law is no excuse for breaking it. This principle of substance is sometimes put in the form of a rule of evidence, that every one is presumed to know the law, and so it has been defended by Austin and others on the ground that ignorance is hard to prove. But if justice requires the fact to be found out, the difficulty of doing so is no ground for refusing to try. And every one must feel that ignorance of the law could never be admitted as an excuse, even if the fact could be proved by sight and hearing in every case. Furthermore, now that the parties to a case can testify, it may be doubted whether a man's knowledge of the law is any harder to investigate than many questions that the courts do go into. Whatever difficulty there is would be met by putting the burden of proving ignorance on the lawbreaker.

Nor can the principle be explained by saying that we are commanded not only to abstain from certain acts, but also to find out that we are so commanded. For if there were such a second command, it is very clear that the guilt of failing to obey it would bear no proportion to the guilt of knowingly disobeying the principal command. Yet the failure to know would receive the same punishment as the failure to obey the principal law.

The true explanation of the rule is the same as the one that accounts for the law's indifference to a man's particular temperament, faculties, and so forth. Public policy sacrifices the individual to the general good. It is desirable that the burden should be equal for all, but it is still more desirable to put an end to robbery and murder. No doubt there are many cases in which the criminal could not have known that he was breaking the law. But to admit the excuse at all would be to encourage ignorance where the lawmaker has determined to make men know and obey, and justice to the individual is rightly outweighed by the larger interests on the other side of the scales.

If these arguments are sound, it is already plain that liability to punishment cannot be finally and absolutely settled by considering the actual personal unworthiness of the criminal alone. That consideration will govern only so far as the public welfare permits or demands. And if we take into account the general result the criminal law is meant to bring about, we shall see that the actual state of mind accompanying a criminal act plays a different part from what is commonly supposed.

For the most part, the purpose of the criminal law is only to induce external conformity to rule. All law is directed to conditions of things manifest to the senses. It may bring those conditions about directly, by the use of force—as when it protects a house from a mob with soldiers, or takes private property for public use, or hangs a man under a judicial sentence. Or it may bring them about indirectly, through men's fears. Either way, its object is equally an external result. In directing itself against robbery or murder, for instance, its purpose is to put a stop to the actual physical taking and keeping of other men's goods, or the actual poisoning, shooting, stabbing, and otherwise putting to death of other men. If those things are not done, the law forbidding them is equally satisfied, whatever the motive.

Put this purely external purpose of the law together with the fact that the law is ready to sacrifice the individual so far as necessary to accomplish it, and we can see more readily than before that the actual degree of personal guilt in any particular offence cannot be the only element, if it is an element at all, in the liability incurred. It is often assumed that the condition of a man's heart or conscience ought to count for more in deciding criminal liability than civil. So far from that being true, it might almost be said to be the very opposite of the truth. For civil liability, in its immediate working, is simply a redistribution of an existing loss between two individuals; and it will be argued in the next Lecture that sound policy lets losses lie where they fall, except where a special reason can be shown for interfering. The most frequent of such reasons is that the party charged has been to blame.

I do not mean to deny that criminal liability, like civil, is founded on blameworthiness. Such a denial would shock the moral sense of any civilized community; or, to put it another way, a law that punished conduct which would not be blameworthy in the average member of the community would be too severe for that community to bear. I mean only to point out this: when we are dealing with the part of the law that aims more directly than any other at setting standards of conduct, we should expect to find there, more than anywhere else, that the tests of liability are external, and independent of the degree of evil in the particular person's motives or intentions. The conclusion follows directly from the nature of the standards to which conformity is required. These are not only external, as was shown above, but of general application. They do not merely require every man to get as near as he can to the best conduct possible for him. They require him at his own peril to come up to a certain height. They take no account of incapacities, unless the weakness is so marked as to fall within well-known exceptions, such as infancy or madness. They assume that every man is as able as every other to behave as they command. If they fall on any one class harder than on another, it is on the weakest. For it is precisely to those most likely to go wrong through temperament, ignorance, or folly that the threats of the law are most dangerous.

How the doctrine that liability is founded on blameworthiness is to be reconciled with liability where the party is not to blame will be worked out more fully in the next Lecture. The answer lies in the conception of the average man, the man of ordinary intelligence and reasonable prudence. Liability is said to arise out of such conduct as would be blameworthy in him. But he is an ideal being, represented by the jury when a question is put to them, and his conduct is an external or objective standard when applied to any given individual. That individual may be morally without stain, because he has less than ordinary intelligence or prudence. But he is required to have those qualities at his peril. If he has them, he will not, as a general rule, incur liability without blameworthiness.

The next step is to take up some crimes in detail, and to discover what analysis will teach us about them.

I will begin with murder. Sir James Stephen, in his Digest of Criminal Law, defines murder as unlawful homicide with malice aforethought. In his earlier work he had explained that malice meant wickedness, and that the law had settled which states of mind were wicked in the necessary degree. Without that preliminary, he goes on in his Digest as follows:—

"Malice aforethought means any one or more of the following states of mind..... (a.) An intention to cause the death of, or grievous bodily harm to, any person, whether such person is the person actually killed or not; (b.) Knowledge that the act which causes death will probably cause the death of, or grievous bodily harm to, some person, whether such person is the person actually killed or not, although such knowledge is accompanied by indifference whether death or grievous bodily harm is caused or not, or by a wish that it may not be caused; (c.) An intent to commit any felony whatever; (d.) An intent to oppose by force any officer of justice on his way to, in, or returning from the execution of the duty of arresting, keeping in custody, or imprisoning any person whom he is lawfully entitled to arrest, keep in custody, or imprison, or the duty of keeping the peace or dispersing an unlawful assembly, provided that the offender has notice that the person killed is such an officer so employed."

Malice, as used in common speech, includes intent, and something more. When an act is said to be done with intent to do harm, it is meant that a wish for the harm is the motive of the act. Intent, however, is perfectly consistent with regretting the harm as such, and wishing for it only as a means to something else. But when an act is said to be done maliciously, it is meant not only that a wish for the harmful effect is the motive, but also that the harm is wished for its own sake—or, as Austin would say more accurately, for the sake of the pleasure that knowing about the suffering would give. Now it is apparent from Sir James Stephen's list that, of these two elements of malice, only the intent matters in murder. It is just as much murder to shoot a sentry in order to free a friend as to shoot him because you hate him. Malice in the definition of murder does not have the same meaning as in common speech, and, for the reasons just given, it has been thought to mean criminal intention.

But intent in turn will be found to break down into two things: foresight that certain consequences will follow from an act, and the wish for those consequences, working as a motive that brings about the act. The question then is whether intent, in its turn, cannot be reduced to a lower term. Sir James Stephen's statement shows that it can be: knowledge that the act will probably cause death—that is, foresight of the consequences of the act—is enough in murder, as it is in tort.

For instance, a newborn child is laid naked out of doors, where it must perish as a matter of course. This is none the less murder because the guilty party would have been very glad to have a stranger find the child and save it.

But again, what is foresight of consequences? It is a picture of a future state of things, called up by knowledge of the present state of things, the future being seen as standing to the present in the relation of effect to cause. Again we must look for a reduction to lower terms. If the known present state of things is such that the act will very certainly cause death, and the probability is a matter of common knowledge, then one who does the act, knowing the present state of things, is guilty of murder, and the law will not inquire whether he actually foresaw the consequences or not. The test of foresight is not what this very criminal foresaw, but what a man of reasonable prudence would have foreseen.

On the other hand, there must be actual present knowledge of the present facts that make an act dangerous. The act is not enough by itself. An act, it is true, implies intention in a certain sense. It is a muscular contraction, and something more. A spasm is not an act. The contraction of the muscles must be willed. And since an adult who is master of himself foresees with mysterious accuracy the outward movement that will follow his inward effort, that movement may be said to be intended. But the intent that necessarily goes with the act ends there. Nothing would follow from the act except for its surroundings. All acts, taken apart from their surrounding circumstances, are indifferent to the law. To crook the forefinger with a certain force, for instance, is the same act whether the trigger of a pistol is next to it or not. It is only the surrounding circumstances—a pistol loaded and cocked, and a human being so placed as to be manifestly likely to be hit—that make the act a wrong. Hence it is no sufficient foundation for liability, on any sound principle, that the proximate cause of a loss, the cause nearest to it, was an act.

The reason for requiring an act is that an act implies a choice, and it is felt to be unwise and unjust to make a man answer for harm unless he might have chosen otherwise. But the choice must be made with a chance of contemplating the consequence complained of, or else it has no bearing on responsibility for that consequence. If this were not so, a man might be held answerable for everything that would not have happened but for some choice of his in the past. He might be held answerable, for instance, for falling on a man in a fit, which he would not have done had he not chosen to come to the city where he was taken ill.

All foresight of the future, all choice with regard to any possible consequence of action, depends on what is known at the moment of choosing. An act cannot be wrong, even when done in circumstances in which it will be hurtful, unless those circumstances are known or ought to be. A fear of punishment for causing harm cannot work as a motive unless the possibility of harm can be foreseen. So far, then, as criminal liability is founded on wrong-doing in any sense, and so far as the threats and punishments of the law are meant to deter men from bringing about various harmful results, they must be confined to cases where the circumstances making the conduct dangerous were known.

Still, in a more limited way, the same principle applies to knowledge as to foresight. It is enough that such circumstances were actually known as would have led a man of common understanding to infer from them the rest of the facts making up the present state of things. For instance, if a workman on a rooftop at midday knows that the space below him is a street in a great city, he knows facts from which a man of common understanding would infer that there were people passing below. He is therefore bound to draw that inference; in other words, he is charged with knowledge of that fact too, whether he draws the inference or not. If, then, he throws a heavy beam down into the street, he does an act which a person of ordinary prudence would foresee is likely to cause death or grievous bodily harm, and he is dealt with as if he foresaw it, whether in fact he did or not. If a death is caused by the act, he is guilty of murder. But if the workman has reasonable cause to believe that the space below is a private yard from which every one is shut out, and which is used as a rubbish heap, his act is not blameworthy, and the homicide is a mere misadventure—an accident for which no one is to blame.

To make an act that causes death murder, then, the actor ought, on principle, to know, or have notice of, the facts that make the act dangerous. There are certain exceptions to this principle, which will be stated presently, but they apply less to murder than to some lesser crimes created by statute. For the most part the general rule prevails in murder.

But furthermore, on the same principle, the danger that in fact exists under the known circumstances ought to be of a kind that a man of reasonable prudence could foresee. Ignorance of a fact and inability to foresee a consequence have the same effect on blameworthiness. If a consequence cannot be foreseen, it cannot be avoided. But there is this practical difference. In most cases the question of knowledge is a question about the actual condition of the defendant's mind, whereas the question of what he might have foreseen is decided by the standard of the prudent man—that is, by general experience. For it must be remembered that the object of the law is to prevent human life from being endangered or taken. The law considers blameworthiness in punishing so far as not to hold a man responsible for consequences that no one, or only some exceptional specialist, could have foreseen. But the reason for this limit is simply to make a rule that is not too hard for the average member of the community. Since the purpose is to compel men to abstain from dangerous conduct, and not merely to restrain them from evil inclinations, the law requires them at their peril to know the teachings of common experience, just as it requires them to know the law. Subject to these explanations, it may be said that the test of murder is the degree of danger to life attending the act under the known circumstances of the case.

It needs no further explanation to show that when the particular defendant does, for any reason, foresee what an ordinary man of reasonable prudence would not have foreseen, the ground of exemption no longer applies. A harmful act is excused only on the ground that the party neither did foresee harm nor could, with proper care, have foreseen it.

At first sight it would seem that this analysis ought to exhaust the whole subject of murder. But it does not, without some further explanation. If a man forcibly resists an officer who is lawfully making an arrest, and kills him, knowing him to be an officer, it may be murder, although no act is done which, but for the officer's official function, would be criminal at all. So, too, if a man does an act with intent to commit a felony, one of the graver crimes, and thereby accidentally kills another: if, for instance, he fires at chickens, meaning to steal them, and accidentally kills the owner, whom he does not see. A case like this last one seems hardly reconcilable with the general principles laid down above. It has been argued somewhat as follows. The only blameworthy act is firing at the chickens, knowing them to belong to another. It is neither more nor less blameworthy because an accident happens afterwards; and hitting a man whose presence could not have been suspected is an accident. The fact that the shooting is felonious does not make it any more likely to kill people. If the object of the rule is to prevent such accidents, it should make accidental killing with firearms murder, not accidental killing in the effort to steal; while if its object is to prevent stealing, it would do better to hang one thief in every thousand by lot.
