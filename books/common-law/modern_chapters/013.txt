Lecture VI: Possession
(Part 2 of 3)

Since that is how the law directly works in the case of possession, one would think that the intent we are looking for is the intent that runs most nearly parallel to it. If what the law does is to keep others from interfering with the object, it would seem that the intent the law should require is an intent to exclude others. I believe that such an intent is all the common law thinks necessary, and that on principle no more should be required.

It may be asked whether this is not simply the animus domini seen from the other side. Even if it were, it would still be better to look at the front of the shield than at the back. But it is not the same thing, if we give the animus domini the meaning the Germans give it—the meaning that denies possession to bailees in general. The intent to appropriate a thing, or to deal with it as owner, can hardly exist without an intent to exclude others, and something more besides; but the intent to exclude may very well exist where there is no intent to hold as owner. A tenant for years means to exclude everyone, the owner included, until his term runs out; yet he has not the animus domini in the sense just explained. Still less has a bailee with a lien, who does not even mean to use the thing, but only to keep it until he is paid. Further, the common law protects a bailee against strangers when it would not protect him against the owner, as in the case of a deposit or any other bailment that the owner can end at pleasure. We may therefore say that even the intent to exclude need not be as wide as the animus domini would imply. If a bailee intends to exclude strangers to the title, that is enough for possession under our law, although he is perfectly ready to hand the thing back to its owner at any moment. It is of the essence of the German view, by contrast, that the intent must not be relative: it must be an absolute, self-regarding intent to take the benefit of the thing. Again, even if the motives, wishes, and intentions most present to a possessor's mind were all self-regarding, it would not follow that his intent toward others was not the important thing in analyzing the law. But, as we have seen, a depositary is a true possessor under the common-law theory, although his intent is not self-regarding at all, and he holds solely for the owner's benefit.

Besides the cases of bailees and tenants, there is another class of cases which will probably—though not necessarily—be decided one way or the other according to whether we adopt the test of an intent to exclude or the test of the animus domini. Bridges v. Hawkesworth will serve as a starting point. There a customer dropped a pocketbook on the floor of a shop, and another customer picked it up before the shopkeeper knew anything about it. Common-law judges and civilians would agree that the finder got possession first, and so could keep it as against the shopkeeper. For the shopkeeper, not knowing of the thing, could not have intended to appropriate it; and, having invited the public into his shop, he could not have intended to exclude them from it. But suppose the pocketbook had been dropped in a private room. How should the case be decided then? There can be no animus domini unless the thing is known of. But an intent to exclude others from it may be contained in the larger intent to exclude others from the place where it is, without any knowledge that the object exists.

In McAvoy v. Medina, a pocketbook had been left on a barber's table, and it was held that the barber had a better right than the finder. The opinion is rather obscure. It draws a distinction between things deliberately put down on a table and things dropped on the floor. It may possibly rest on the ground that, when an owner leaves a thing in that way, there is an implied request to the shopkeeper to look after it, which gives him a better right than someone who actually finds it before he does. This is rather strained, however, and perhaps the court thought that the barber had possession as soon as the customer left the shop. A little later the same court heard a suit for a reward offered to whoever found a pocketbook. The plaintiff had discovered it where the owner had left it, on a desk provided for customers in a bank, outside the teller's counter. The court said that this was not the finding of a lost article, and that "the occupants of the banking house, and not the plaintiff, were the proper depositaries of an article so left." This language might seem to imply that the plaintiff was not the first person to get possession after the owner lost it; and that, although the floor of a shop may be likened to a street, the public are to be treated as excluded from a shop's desks, counters, and tables except for the particular use allowed them. Perhaps, however, the case decides only that the pocketbook was not lost within the terms of the offer.

I should not have thought it safe to draw any conclusion from the English cases on wreck, which are tangled up with questions of prescription and other rights. But the exact point seems to have been decided here in Massachusetts. For it has been held that, if a stick of timber washes ashore on a man's land, he thereby acquires a "right of possession" as against an actual finder who comes onto the land to carry it off. A right of possession is said to be enough to support trespass; but the court seems to have meant possession itself by the phrase. Chief Justice Shaw states the question as which of the parties had "the preferable claim, by mere naked possession, without other title," and there does not seem to have been any right of possession in the case unless there was actual possession.

In a criminal case, where a stranger took iron from the bottom of a canal, the indictment was held rightly to lay the property in the canal company, although it does not appear that the company knew the iron was there, or had any lien on it.

The only intent concerning the thing that can be found in such cases is the general intent which the occupier of land has to exclude the public from the land, and so, as a consequence, from whatever is on it.

The Roman lawyers would probably have decided all these cases the other way, although they cannot be supposed to have worked out the refined theories that have since been built on what remains of their writings.

I may return here to the case of goods delivered in a locked chest, or in a bale, and the like. It is a rule of the criminal law that if the bailee of such a chest or bale wrongfully sells the whole chest or bale, he does not commit larceny; but if he breaks bulk, he does, because in the first case he does not commit a trespass and in the second he does. The reason sometimes given is that by breaking bulk the bailee ends the bailment, so that the goods at once go back into the possession of the bailor. This is perhaps an unnecessary fiction, as well as an inadequate one. The rule comes from the Year Books, and the theory of the Year Books was that, although the chest was delivered to the bailee, the goods inside it were not; and this theory was applied to civil as well as criminal cases. The bailor has the power and the intent to exclude the bailee from the goods, and may therefore be said to be in possession of them as against the bailee.

Footnote: It may not be necessary to go quite so far, however, and I do not rely on these cases as establishing the theory.

On the other hand, a case in Rhode Island goes against the view taken here. A man bought a safe and then, wanting to sell it again, sent it to the defendant, giving him leave to keep his books in it until it was sold. The defendant found some banknotes stuck in a crack in the safe. When this came to the plaintiff's ears, he demanded the safe and the money. The defendant sent back the safe but refused to give up the money, and the court upheld his refusal. I venture to think this decision wrong. Nor would my opinion change if we assumed—what the report does not make perfectly clear—that the defendant received the safe as a bailee, and not as a servant or agent, and that his permission to use it was general. The court's argument turns on the plaintiff's not being a finder. The question is whether he needed to be one. It is hard to believe that, if the defendant had stolen the notes out of the safe while it was in its owner's hands, the property could not have been laid in the owner of the safe, or that the owner could not have maintained trover for them had they been converted in those circumstances. Sir James Stephen seems to have drawn a similar conclusion from Cartwright v. Green and Merry v. Green; but I believe there is no warrant for it in the cases, and still less for the reason he suggests.

Footnote: Stephen says it is "because [the owner of the safe] cannot be presumed to intend to act as the owner of it when he discovers it"—a reason drawn from Savigny, and not fitted to the English law, as has been shown.

It will be understood, however, that Durfee v. Jones, the Rhode Island case, is perfectly consistent with the view maintained here of the general nature of the intent required. It touches only the subordinate question whether the intent to exclude must be aimed at the specific thing, or may be included, even unconsciously, in a larger intent—as I am inclined to believe it may.

So far nothing has been said about the custody of servants. It is a well-known doctrine of the criminal law that a servant who criminally converts his master's property, entrusted to him and in his custody as a servant, is guilty of theft, because he is regarded as having taken the property out of his master's possession. That is the same as saying that a servant who has custody of his master's property as a servant does not have possession of it, and the Year Books say exactly that.

There is an odd distinction by which, if the servant receives the thing from some third person for his master, the servant has the possession, and so cannot commit theft. The old cases make it more rational. For the distinction they draw is that while the servant is in the house or with his master, the master keeps possession; but if the master hands his horse to his servant to ride to market, or gives him a bag to carry to London, then the thing is out of the master's possession and in the servant's. In this more intelligible form the rule would not prevail today. But one half of it—that a guest at a tavern does not have possession of the plate he is served on—is no doubt still law, for guests in general are likened to servants in their legal position.

Footnote: It may be doubted whether the old law would have sanctioned the rule in the modern form.

Footnote: Guests have been said to be a part of the family pro hac vice—for the occasion.

Outside the criminal law there are few English decisions on whether a servant has possession. But the Year Books suggest no difference between civil and criminal cases, and there is an almost unbroken tradition of courts and approved writers that he has not, in any case. A master has maintained trespass against a servant for converting cloth the servant was employed to sell, and the American cases go the full length of the old doctrine. It has often been remarked that a servant must be distinguished from a bailee.

Footnote: Some of the American cases have been denied, on the ground that the custodian was not a servant.

But it may be asked how denying possession to servants can be squared with the test proposed here; and it will be said, truly, that a servant has as much intent to exclude the world at large as a borrower does. The law of servants is unquestionably at odds with that test. And there can be no doubt that those who have built their theories on the Roman law have been led by this fact, together with the Roman doctrine about bailees in general, to look for their formula of reconciliation where they have. But in truth the exception for servants stands on purely historical grounds. A servant is denied possession not because of anything peculiar in his intent toward the things in his custody—either toward his master or toward other people—that sets him apart from a depositary, but simply as one of the incidents of his status. It is familiar that the status of a servant keeps many marks of the time when he was a slave. The master's liability for his servant's torts is one instance. The present rule is another. A slave's possession was his owner's possession, on the practical ground of the owner's power over him, and because the slave had no standing before the law. The notion that his personality was merged in that of the head of his family survived the era of emancipation.

I have shown in the first Lecture that agency grew out of this earlier relation in the Roman law, by extending to a freeman, pro hac vice, ideas drawn from the law of slaves. The same is true, I think, of our own law, whose later development seems to have been largely under Roman influence. As late as Blackstone, agents appear under the general heading of servants, and the first precedents cited for the special law of agents were cases of master and servant. Blackstone's language is worth quoting: "There is yet a fourth species of servants, if they may be so called, being rather in a superior, a ministerial capacity; such as stewards, factors, and bailiffs: whom, however, the law considers as servants pro tempore, with regard to such of their acts as affect their master's or employer's property."

Footnote: Factors are always called servants in the old books, and bailiffs are spoken of in the same way.

It is quite true that in modern times many of the effects of either relation—master and servant, or principal and agent—can be explained as the result of acts done by the master himself. If a man tells another to make a contract in his name, or orders him to commit a tort, no special idea is needed to explain why he is held liable; although even in such cases, where the go-between was a freeman, the law did not reach that conclusion until it had become fairly mature. But if Agency deserves to stand as a title of the law at all, it must be because some peculiar consequences are attached to the fact of the relation. If the mere power to bind a principal to a contract he authorized were all, we might as well have a chapter on ink and paper as on agents. But it is not all. Even in the field of contract we find the striking doctrine that an undisclosed principal—one whose existence the other party did not know of—has the rights as well as the obligations of a known contractor: he can be sued, and, more remarkable still, he can sue on his agent's contract. The first precedent cited for the rule that a promise made to an agent may be pleaded as a promise to the principal is a case of master and servant.

Footnote: These rules seem to be somewhat modern even as to servants. In the earlier Year Books a master's liability for debts his servant contracted is very narrowly limited.

Since my present object is only to show what the doctrine of identification—the treating of servant and master as one person—means for the theory of possession, it would be out of place to consider at any length how far that doctrine must be called on to explain a principal's liability for his agent's torts, or whether some more reasonable rule governs other cases than the one applied where the actor has a fairly definite status as a servant. I allow myself a few words, because I shall not be able to come back to the subject.

If the courts had so far treated a master's liability for his servant's torts as the decaying remnant of an obsolete institution, it would not be surprising to find it confined to the cases settled by ancient precedent. But that has not happened. It has been extended by analogy to new relations. It exists where the principal does not stand toward the actual wrongdoer in the position of paterfamilias, the Roman head of the household. A man may be held liable for another where the relation was so passing as to rule out any idea of status—as for the negligence of someone else's servant acting for the defendant for the moment, or of a neighbor helping him as a volunteer. And, so far as is known, no principal has ever escaped on the ground of the dignity of his agent's employment. The courts habitually speak as if the same rules applied to brokers and other agents as to servants properly so called. Indeed, it has been laid down in so many words that the liability of employers is not confined to servants, although the usual cases are, of course, those of menial servants and the like, who could not pay a large verdict.

Footnote: I am inclined to think that this extension has been largely due to the influence of the Roman law. See Lecture I, and notice the part that the precedents about fire have played in shaping the modern doctrine of master and servant. Lord Holt's examples in one of the leading cases come from the Roman law; and in another, Doctor and Student is referred to for the general principles of liability—and Doctor and Student states Roman law.

On the other hand, if the peculiar doctrines of agency are anomalous, and form, as I believe, the vanishing point of the servile status, it may well happen that common sense will refuse to carry them out to their furthest applications. We may see such conflicts between tradition and the instinct of justice on the question of identifying a principal who knows the truth with an agent who makes a false statement, so as to make out a fraud, as in Cornfoot v. Fowke; or on the question, argued in many English cases, of a principal's liability for his agent's frauds. But so long as the fiction at the root of a master's liability is left alive, it is as hopeless to reconcile the differences by logic as to square the circle.

Footnote: One judge has commented on Cornfoot v. Fowke in the same way as the text. The other grounds for that decision do not matter here.

Footnote: The objections which Baron Bramwell raises to holding one man liable for the frauds of another are really objections to the peculiar consequences attached to the relation of master and servant in general, and the same learned judge has urged them in that more general form.

In an article in the American Law Review I referred to a phrase of Godefroi's about agents: eadem est persona domini et procuratoris—"the master and the agent are the same person." This idea of a fictitious unity of person has been called a darkening of counsel in a recent useful book. But it has the approval of Sir Henry Maine, and I believe it must stand as expressing an important aspect of the law, if, as I have tried to show, there is no adequate and complete explanation of the modern law except that rules survived in practice which lost their true meaning when the people they governed ceased to be slaves. There is no difficulty in understanding what is meant by saying that a slave has no legal standing, but is absorbed in the family which his master represents before the law. The meaning seems just as clear when we say that a free servant, in his relations as a servant, is in many respects likened by the law to a slave—not, of course, to his own harm as a freeman. The next step is simply that others who are not servants in a general sense may be treated as if they were servants in a particular connection. That is the progress of ideas as history shows it to us. And that is what is meant by saying that the characteristic feature which justifies agency as a title of the law is the absorption, pro hac vice, of the agent's legal individuality in that of his principal.

If this were carried out logically, it would follow that an agent appointed to hold possession in his principal's name would not be treated as having the legal possession, or as entitled to bring trespass. But after what has been said, no opinion can be given on whether the law would go that far unless precedent shows it. Notice the nature of the case. It is that of an agent appointed for the very point and purpose of possession. A bailee may be an agent for some other purpose. A free servant may be made a bailee. But the bailee holds in his own name, as we say, following the Roman idiom, and the servant or agent holding as such does not.

Footnote: Compare the Roman rule with the passage from Blackstone quoted above: "He possesses in whose name the thing is possessed; the agent lends his services to another's possession."
