A TANGLED TALE — TRANSLATION STRATEGY
Lewis Carroll (Charles Dodgson), serialised 1880–1885, collected 1885
Source: Project Gutenberg #29042 (the illustrated HTML edition)


1. WHAT THIS IS

Ten comic stories, each with a mathematical puzzle buried in it, written
month by month for The Monthly Packet — a magazine for young women. A
month after each story Carroll printed his own review of the answers his
readers had sent in: he sorts them into a CLASS LIST by pseudonym, praises
the good ones, and takes the wrong ones apart with enormous relish.

The book is therefore two books, and the second is the better one. The
Knots are about 12,000 words; the Answers are about 15,000. TRANSLATE THE
ANSWERS WHOLE. They are not an appendix in the sense of being optional —
they are the half of the book where Carroll is actually present, arguing
with named correspondents, being witty and occasionally unfair. Same rule
as soap-bubbles/, where "Practical Hints" is a third of the book and is
the reason it exists.

Carroll is not the Alice of these pages. He is a working Oxford
mathematician being funny at his own subject's expense, and the register
is donnish, quick, and affectionate. Where Alice's nonsense is
uncontrolled on purpose, this is nonsense in the service of arithmetic.


2. WHAT NEEDS MODERNIZING, AND WHAT DOES NOT

The prose is Victorian but not archaic. There is no thee/thou, no -eth,
almost nothing to unstack. What actually blocks a reader is narrower and
more specific than usual:

  - THE MONEY. Shillings, pence and guineas throughout, and the
    arithmetic depends on them.
  - THE UNITS. Miles per hour is fine; but weights, distances and the
    occasional "score" need to read naturally.
  - THE SOCIAL FURNITURE. A lodging-house keeper's terms, a cab fare,
    a governess's position, the etiquette of who takes whom in to
    dinner. Where the puzzle depends on the arrangement, the
    arrangement has to be legible to a reader who has never met it.
  - THE PSEUDONYMS. His correspondents sign themselves Balbus, Simple
    Susan, Sea Breeze, A Marlborough Boy, Galanthus Nivalis Major. LEAVE
    THEM ALONE. They are the joke, and half the wit of the Answers is
    Carroll punning on them.
  - THE LATIN. "De Omnibus Rebus" is a pun (all things / omnibuses) and
    the Knot is about an omnibus. Keep the title and let the story do
    the work; do not translate it and do not explain it.


3. THE MONEY — DECIDED IN ADVANCE, DO NOT RE-DECIDE

KEEP the pounds, shillings and pence. Do NOT decimalise.

Several answers work out the way they do BECAUSE there are twelve pence
in a shilling and twenty shillings in a pound. Convert to decimal and the
puzzle stops being the puzzle. It is Carroll's arithmetic in Carroll's
units — the same rule that keeps Ball's Martian canals and Fleming's
aether.

The currency gets ONE gloss, in the preface, in a bracketed note: twelve
pence to the shilling, twenty shillings to the pound, a guinea is
twenty-one shillings. After that, nothing. A reader who has the ratios
can follow every sum in the book, and a reader who is reminded of them
every chapter will stop reading.


4. THE ARITHMETIC — THE ONE THING THAT MATTERS MOST

CHECK EVERY ANSWER THAT CLAIMS TO BE COMPUTED. Work the sums.

This is the fleming/ lesson and it applies here more sharply than
anywhere else in the project, because in this book the arithmetic IS the
content. Fleming printed 495x3 as 1475 and gave one refractive index two
different values; nobody noticed for a century. Carroll has known
disputed and ambiguous solutions — he argues with correspondents about
some of them in the text — and a wrong number that looks right passes
the word ratio, the figure parity and must_contain alike.

So: every fraction, every total, every intermediate step. Where Carroll
is right, keep him. Where the source is a transcription slip, correct it
silently. Where CARROLL HIMSELF is wrong or his problem is genuinely
ambiguous — which happens, and which his correspondents tell him — say
so in a short bracketed note. Do not quietly fix his mathematics; that
is his argument with his readers and it is part of the book.

Run the numeric-token check from fleming/running_notes.txt on every
file, and expect it to be noisy here in a way it is not elsewhere:
digits spelled as words are correct in prose but every figure must still
be traceable.


5. THE PLATES

Nine, and they fall into three kinds.

SEVEN are Arthur B. Frost illustrations of the stories, and Carroll
captioned them himself with lines quoted out of the text ("why do they
say 'Bamboo!' so often?"). Those captions ride through and are
MODERNISED, not replaced — the ball/ rule. Keep his phrasing as the
first clause and extend with a short descriptive one; the caption is
also the epub's alt text.

ONE is the Chelsea-bun street cry set as actual sheet music (Figure 8).
It is a picture of a tune. Caption it as what it is.

TWO are diagrams in the Answers (Figures 9 and 10 — the family tree of
the dinner party, and the four houses set round a square). These have no
caption in the original and need one that says what the diagram is FOR,
because the prose refers to them by their letters and numbers. LOOK AT
THE PLATE BEFORE CAPTIONING IT.


6. VOICE

Fast, dry, and pleased with itself. Carroll's narrators are amused by
their own characters. Mad Mathesis drags her niece round London; Balbus
writes essays to his pupils; Her Radiancy holds court; the Governor of
Kgovjni arranges a dinner party of impossible relations. Play it as
comedy, because it is comedy.

In the Answers, the register shifts and must shift with it: this is a
tutor marking work, delighted when it is good and merciless when it is
lazy. He calls a wrong answer "a piece of arithmetical impertinence" and
he means it kindly. Keep the edge. Where he is teasing a named
correspondent, the tease is the point.

Reading level: eighth grade to high school, as always — but the
mathematics is not to be simplified. Make the PROSE easy and let the
problems be exactly as hard as Carroll made them. The whole appeal of
the book is that it takes a young reader seriously.


7. RATIO

Expect close to 1:1, as with the other English-to-English volumes.
Verify with --min-ratio 0.85 --max-ratio 1.3. Unpacking a compressed
piece of Victorian social shorthand expands; cutting throat-clearing
contracts; the sums themselves are the same length in any century.
