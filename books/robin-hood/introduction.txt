Howard Pyle was born in Wilmington, Delaware, in 1853 and became one of the best-known American illustrators of his day, drawing for Harper's magazines and later teaching a generation of artists, among them N. C. Wyeth. The Merry Adventures of Robin Hood, published by Charles Scribner's Sons in New York in 1883, was the first long book he both wrote and illustrated. Every picture in it is his, down to the lettering and the borders, and the forty-four he drew for the story are reproduced here from the first edition. He went on to write Otto of the Silver Hand, Men of Iron and four books of King Arthur, and he died in Florence in 1911.

Pyle did not invent Robin Hood. Rhymes about him were already being sung in the 1370s, when the poem Piers Plowman mentions them, and the oldest surviving ballads date from the fifteenth century. Over the next three hundred years the outlaw picked up Maid Marian, Friar Tuck, a home in the reign of Richard the Lionheart and a title, Earl of Huntington. Pyle took a dozen of these ballads, among them Robin Hood and the Tinker, the Curtal Friar, Allan a Dale, Guy of Gisbourne and the long Gest of Robin Hood, and joined them into one story with a beginning and an end. Nearly every later film and retelling borrows from it: the fight with Little John on the log bridge, the golden arrow at Nottingham, the King in disguise in Sherwood.

He wrote it for children, and he made it a comedy. Almost every chapter is a trick, a fight with staffs, a feast and a song, and the loser usually laughs and joins the band. The narrator talks to the reader as if they were sitting together. There is real death in it too, at the beginning and at the end, and Pyle tells both plainly.

Pyle also dressed the whole book in an invented old-fashioned English, full of "thou" and "quoth" and "marry," which was meant to sound like the time of the ballads but was never the speech of any century. This edition, aimed at readers of about ten to fourteen, takes that costume off and tells every episode and every conversation in today's English. The songs stay songs, rhymed. Old measures are given in yards and miles. Three things are kept as Pyle printed them: the thieves' slang that baffles Robin in chapter 16, the old inscription on Robin's grave, and the titles Pyle lettered into his pictures.

The Gutenberg text follows a later printing that dropped Pyle's division into a Prologue, eight Parts and an Epilogue, and his short summary at the head of each Part. Both are restored from the first edition. The titles given to the Parts are this edition's.
