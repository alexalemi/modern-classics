TEXT ANALYSIS — Howard Pyle, The Merry Adventures of Robin Hood of Great
Renown in Nottinghamshire (New York: Scribner, 1883)

THE BOOK
Pyle (1853-1911), an illustrator from Wilmington, Delaware, wrote and drew
this as his first book. He took the medieval and early-modern Robin Hood
ballads (Robin Hood and the Tinker, the Curtal Friar, Allan a Dale, Guy of
Gisbourne, the Gest of Robyn Hode's sorrowful knight and the death at
Kirklees), "snipped and clipped and tied together again in a score of
knots" as his Preface says, and turned them into a single sequence of comic
adventures for young readers: a Prologue, eight Parts of two or three
chapters, and an Epilogue. It made the modern Robin Hood. Almost every
later film borrows its shape: the quarterstaff fight on the log bridge, the
golden-arrow shooting match, the Sheriff fooled at his own table, the
disguised King in Sherwood.

STYLE, TONE, THEMES
- Comic and warm. Every episode is a trick, a bout, a feast, a song, and a
  forgiveness. Fights end with both men laughing and the loser joining the
  band. The Sheriff is greedy and pompous, never truly frightening, until
  the Epilogue.
- A storyteller's voice that talks to its listener: "as I have told", "you
  and I were not bidden", "But I leave it with you to say", "dear friend",
  "sweet Reader". Pyle's narrator is in the room. KEEP EVERY ASIDE.
- Lavish nature writing in long cumulative sentences (the Maytime catalog
  in the Prologue, the Epilogue's sunsets). Keep every image; unstack the
  sentence.
- Proverbs. Robin and friends quote "old Gaffer Swanthold", a sage Pyle
  invented, and folk sayings ("the egg cracked cannot be cured"). They are
  jokes and character: keep each one, in plain modern words, still sounding
  like a proverb.
- Themes: fair play, generosity, the poor robbed by the rich and the
  church, loyalty, laughter as the best weapon, and in the Epilogue the
  end of good things.

WHOSE ARCHAISM IS IT? (memory: whose-archaism-is-it)
All of it is Pyle's. He wrote in 1883 in an invented mock-Elizabethan
costume ("lithe and listen", "quoth", "thou", "marry", "wot", "ween",
"eke", "an thou darest", "prythee", "good den") laid over a twelfth-
century story. It is not the English of Henry II's time (that would be
Anglo-Norman French and early Middle English) nor of the ballads. screen.py
scores the book 29.8 archaic tokens per thousand, higher than Burton's
Nights. So the costume comes off completely: no thou/thee/thy, no quoth,
no -eth/-est verbs, no "marry", "wot", "ween", "trow", "eke", "anon",
"fain", "prythee", "an" for "if". What stays is the STORY WORLD: lads and
lasses, yeomen, the greenwood, Lincoln green, quarterstaffs, marks and
groats, saints' oaths, Gaffer Swanthold.

THREE THINGS THAT ARE NOT COSTUME, AND STAY AS PRINTED
1. The Kirklees epitaph at the very end. Pyle presents it explicitly as an
   old inscription "in the ancient English in which it was written". Keep
   it letter for letter, then fold a plain meaning into the next sentence.
2. The beggars' thieves' cant in chapter 16 ("fibbed a chouse quarrons in
   the Rome pad for the loure in his bung"). It is gibberish to Robin in
   the story; that is the joke. Keep it, and fold Pyle's footnote gloss
   into the narration.
3. The titles lettered into the plates ("Robin Hood meeteth the tall
   Stranger on the Bridge"). They are part of the picture. The caption
   quotes them exactly as printed, then describes the picture in modern
   words.

AUDIENCE AND READING LEVEL
Pyle wrote for children; this edition is aimed at readers about 10 to 14
(and the adults reading aloud to them). The house test, made stricter:
every sentence one a ten-year-old could follow read aloud, and one a
fourteen-year-old would not find babyish. Lively, warm, funny, vivid.
Short and medium sentences; dialogue that sounds like people talking
(contractions welcome) without modern slang (no "okay", "guys", "cool",
"kid" as address). Plain words over fancy ones. COMPLETE: every episode,
every exchange, every song, every aside; nothing summarized. The ratio
check guards this; expect 0.95-1.10 (removing "thou dost" shortens,
unstacking lengthens).

CHALLENGES
- Oaths and interjections on almost every line (see running_notes lock).
- Old measures and numbers: "threescore rods", "an ell around the waist",
  "fivescore", "sevenscore", "a furlong". Convert to modern units and plain
  numbers (memory: modern-units-in-retellings). Keep "seven feet tall".
- Money: pounds, marks, shillings, pence, groats, farthings, angels. Keep
  the coins (they are the texture of every bargain) and gloss once each,
  the first time they appear, inside the sentence.
- False friends for a modern child: "gay" (bright, merry), "lusty"
  (strong, hearty), "queer" (odd), "crown" (top of the head), "worshipful"
  (Pyle uses it with irony for the Sheriff), "presently" (soon), "stout"
  (strong, brave — NOT fat), "jolly" (merry; fine), "humor" (mood).
- Songs. About twenty ballads and catches are sung in the book. They are
  part of the fun and stay as VERSE: modernize the words, keep the rhyme
  scheme and roughly the meter, keep every stanza and refrain. See the
  running_notes for the mechanics.
- The Gutenberg transcription carries a few scanning slips ("shine" for
  "thine", "I wet" for "I wot", "I bow" for "I trow", "without new" for
  "without flaw"); render by sense.

STRUCTURE
Preface; Prologue; Part One to Part Eight (chapters numbered 1-20
continuously; Pyle numbered them I, II, III within each Part); Epilogue.
Pyle's italic Part summaries ("In which it is told how...") open each
Part's first chapter, in italics. The Part divider titles ("Part One: The
Sheriff Tries Three Times") are this edition's, not Pyle's: he printed only
"Part First", and the titles exist so a young reader can find an episode in
the contents. Five long chapters are split into two files each.

SENSITIVE CONTENT — guidance
- Death and violence are few but real, and Pyle does not hide them: the
  forester Robin kills at eighteen, Guy of Gisbourne slain and stripped
  "all bloody", the Sheriff killed in the Epilogue battle, Robin bled to
  death by the Prioress. Render them plainly, at Pyle's own measured
  pace: neither softened (the reader must not lose the fact) nor lingered
  over. Robin's grief after the first killing is the moral of the
  Prologue; keep it whole.
- Ale, wine and sack are drunk constantly. This is the period and the
  merriment; keep it, without comment.
- Clergy are mocked (the Bishop of Hereford, the Prior of Emmet, the
  friars' greed). That is the ballad tradition and Pyle's satire; render
  it as his.
- Women: Pyle's courtesy is of its time ("sniveling like a girl of
  fourteen", lasses carrying eggs). Keep what characters say as theirs.
- Little John's threat to burn Kirklees and Robin forbidding him: keep
  both; Robin's mercy is the point.

FAMOUS PASSAGES (also in must_contain.txt)
- The opening: "In merry England in the time of old, when good King Henry
  the Second ruled the land".
- The Preface's last words: "Give me your hand."
- Little John's christening: "Little John you shall be called".
- Robin's last request: where the arrow lands, there let his grave be dug.
- The epitaph, letter for letter.
- "Lincoln green" and "Gaffer Swanthold" survive throughout.
