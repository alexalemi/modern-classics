Percy Williams Bridgman (1882–1961) spent his whole working life at Harvard, where he made his name squeezing things. He built presses that reached pressures no one had reached before and measured what happened to metals, liquids and crystals inside them, and in 1946 he was given the Nobel Prize in Physics for that work. This short book comes from a different side of him. In the spring of 1920 he gave five lectures on dimensional analysis to the physics graduate students at Harvard, and Yale University Press published them in 1922.

Dimensional analysis is the trick of finding out how a result must depend on its causes by keeping track of units alone. A pendulum's period can only be built out of its length, its mass, the pull of gravity and the size of its swing, and there is essentially one way to combine those into something measured in seconds. Every physics student used the method, and Bridgman thought hardly anyone understood why it worked or when it failed. His preface says there was nowhere a systematic account of its principles, and that the misconceptions about it had spread into published speculation. The book is that account. Its centre is the Π theorem, which Edgar Buckingham had set out in 1914, and it works through dozens of problems, from pendulums and drops of liquid to the scale models on which airplane design was then being tested, and the constants of radiation.

Two things are worth knowing before starting. First, Bridgman keeps asking what the numbers in a formula are the numbers of, and he answers in terms of the "rules of operation" by which each quantity is measured. Five years later he made that idea the basis of The Logic of Modern Physics, and this is one of the places it began. Second, the physics is that of 1920: units are centimetres, grams and seconds; the radiation constants come from Planck's formula as it then stood; radiation still travels through "the ether", though Bridgman has no patience for speculation about its "true" dimensions. The small raised numbers in the text point to the reference lists at the end of each chapter, which cite journals in the abbreviations of the day (Phys. Rev., Phil. Mag., Nat.).

This is a restored edition. Bridgman's words are unchanged. No transcription of the book existed, so every page of the 1922 printing was read from the scan, and the words were then checked against two other copies. The formulas, about eleven hundred of them, are typeset as mathematics rather than pictured, and each was read twice from the page. A few slips in the printing, a misspelt word and an exponent or two that do not match the working, are kept as printed. The index, which was page numbers only, is left out.
