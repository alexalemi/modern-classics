However, let us by actual example carry through this problem, including the dielectric constant of empty space explicitly as a new kind of fundamental quantity which cannot be expressed in terms of mass, length, and time. Call the dielectric constant of empty space \(k\), and use the same letter to stand for the quantity itself, and its dimension. Then the unit of electrostatic charge is now defined by the relation, \(\text{force} = e^2/k r^2\). Field strength is to be defined as before as \(e E = \text{Force}\). If we formulate the problem in terms of these fundamentals, the electrostatic field equations will now contain \(k\) explicitly, so that the dimensional constant \(k\) appears in the list of variables. The formulation of the problem is now as follows:

	*Name of Quantity.* | *Symbol.* | *Dimensional Formula.*
	Charge, | \(e\) | \(M^{\frac{1}{2}} L^{\frac{3}{2}} T^{-1} k^{\frac{1}{2}}\)
	Field strength, | \(E\) | \(M^{\frac{1}{2}} L^{-\frac{1}{2}} T^{-1} k^{-\frac{1}{2}}\)
	Energy density, | \(u\) | \(M L^{-1} T^{-2}\)
	Dielectric constant of empty space, | \(k\) | \(k\)

We again look for a dimensionless product in which the terms are \(E\), \(u\), and \(k\), and find the result to be \[ u = \text{Const } k E^2. \] This reduces to that previously found on putting \(k\) equal to unity, which was the value of \(k\) in the previous formulation of the problem. The form above appears somewhat more general than the form previously obtained in virtue of the factor \(k\), but this factor does not tell us anything more about nature, but merely shows how the formal expression of the result will change when we change the formulation of the definitions at the basis of our system of equations. The inclusion of the factor \(k\) in the result and in the definitions is therefore of no advantage to us, and never can be of advantage, if our considerations are correct.
