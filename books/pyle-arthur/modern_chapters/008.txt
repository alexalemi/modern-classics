Chapter 2: How King Arthur Fought the Sable Knight and Was Badly Wounded, and How Merlin Carried Him Safely Away

[Figure ao: The battle with the Sable Knight — Below a castle on a rock, two knights fight on foot with swords on the grass, while a figure in a long black robe watches.]

So King Arthur and Merlin rode on together through the forest for a good long while, until they could tell that they must be drawing near to the place where the Sable Knight lived, the knight the King was seeking so eagerly. For the forest, which until then had been a wilderness, very deep and mossy, began to grow thinner and more open, as though some dwelling place of men were close at hand.

And after a little while they saw before them a rushing, violent stream of water that tore through a dark and gloomy glen. They saw, too, that a bridge of stone crossed this stream, and that on the far side of the bridge there lay a smooth and level lawn of green grass, where knights could joust very well. And beyond this lawn they saw a tall and forbidding castle, with smooth walls and a straight tower; and the castle was built upon the rocks in such a way that it seemed to be all one piece with the stone. So they knew that this must be the castle the page and Sir Griflet had told of.

*King Arthur comes to the castle of the Sable Knight*.

For halfway across the bridge they saw a black shield hanging, and a brass mallet beside it, exactly as the page and Sir Griflet had said. And on the far side of the stream there stood an apple tree, and among its leaves hung a great many shields bearing all sorts of different designs, exactly as those two had reported. Some of those shields were clean and bright, and some were dirty and stained with blood; some were smooth and whole, and some were split, as though by fighting between knight and knight. And every one of those shields had belonged to some knight whom the Sable Knight, who lived in the castle, had overthrown in combat with his own hand.

"By the splendor of Paradise!" said King Arthur. "That must be a truly valiant knight, who by his own strength alone has overthrown and cast down so many other knights. Why, Merlin, there must be a hundred shields hanging in that tree!"

To this Merlin answered, "And you, Lord, may count yourself very lucky if your own shield is not hanging there too before the sun goes down this evening."

"That," said King Arthur, with a very steady face, "will be as God wills. For truly, I want more than ever to try my strength against that knight. Think of it: what special honor would come to me if I were to overcome so valiant a warrior as this Sable Champion seems to be, since he has been victorious over so many other good knights!"

Then, having spoken his mind, King Arthur at once pushed his horse forward and rode onto the bridge, and there he plainly read the challenge written in red letters beneath the shield:

*Whoever strikes this shield does so at his peril*.

*King Arthur challenges the Sable Knight*.

When he had read these words, the King seized the brass mallet and struck the shield such a violent blow that the sound of it echoed back from the smooth walls of the castle, and from the rocks it stood on, and from the edges of the forest all around, as though a dozen other shields had been struck in all those different places.

And in answer to that sound, the portcullis of the castle (the great iron grating that guarded its gate) was at once let down, and out rode a knight, very huge of frame and dressed all in black armor. All his clothing, too, and all the trappings of his horse were entirely black, so that he looked very grim and forbidding. And this Sable Knight came across that level meadow of smooth grass at a very stately and dignified pace, neither in haste nor slowly, but with great pride and a haughty bearing, as befitted a champion who, perhaps, had never yet been beaten in battle. So, reaching the head of the bridge, he reined in his horse and saluted King Arthur with great dignity, and very haughtily as well. "Ha, Sir Knight!" he said. "Why, after reading the words written there, did you strike my shield? Now I tell you that for your discourtesy I will soon take your shield away from you, and hang it up on that apple tree, where you see all those other shields hanging. So either hand your shield over to me without any more fuss, or else get ready to defend it with your body, in which case you will certainly suffer great pain and harm."

"Thank you for the choice you give me," said King Arthur. "But as for taking my shield away, I believe that will be as Heaven wills, and not as you will. Know this, you unkind knight: I have come here for no other purpose than to do battle with you, and so to try to win back, with my own body, all those shields that hang there on that apple tree. So make ready at once, so that I can fight with you, perhaps to your great disadvantage."

"That I will," replied the Sable Knight. And with that he turned his horse's head and rode back a certain distance across the level lawn, and took his place wherever seemed best to him. And King Arthur rode out onto the lawn as well, and took his place wherever seemed best to him.

Then each knight set his spear and his shield for the charge, and, being ready for the attack, each shouted to his war horse and drove his spurs deep into its sides.

*King Arthur contends with the Sable Knight*.

Then those two noble horses rushed forward like lightning, racing across the ground with such tremendous speed that the earth trembled and shook beneath them as if in an earthquake. And the two knights met squarely in the very middle of the field, crashing together like a thunderbolt. So violently did they strike against each other that both spears burst into splinters, right down to the hand guard and the stump of the shaft, and the horses staggered back from the shock. Only the extraordinary skill of their riders kept them from falling in that collision.

But with great spirit each of those two knights lifted up his horse with his own courage, and so finished his course in safety.

And indeed King Arthur was greatly amazed that he had not overthrown his opponent, for at that time, as I have said, he was held to be the very best knight in all of Britain, the one most proven in deeds of arms. So he marveled at the strength and skill of the knight he had charged against, that he had not been overthrown by the force of the blow that had struck his defenses. So when they met again in the middle of the field, King Arthur greeted that knight and spoke to him with great courtesy, saying: "Sir Knight, I do not know who you are, but I give you my knightly word that you are the mightiest knight I have ever met in all my life. Now I ask you to get down from your horse at once, and let the two of us fight this battle on foot and with swords, for it would be a pity to let it end this way."

"Not so," said the Sable Knight. "Not so. Until one of us has been overthrown, I will not fight this battle on foot." And with that he shouted "Ho! Ho!" in a very loud voice, and at once the gateway of the castle opened, and out came running two tall squires dressed all in black, trimmed with crimson. And each of these squires carried in his hand a great spear of ash wood, new and well seasoned, and never yet strained in battle.

So King Arthur chose one of these spears and the Sable Knight took the other, and then each went back to the place from which he had made his first charge.

Then once again each knight rushed his horse to the attack, and once again each struck the other so squarely in the middle of his shield that the spears were splintered, until only the guard and the stump of the shaft were left in each knight's grip.

*The knights break lances a second time*.

Then, as before, King Arthur would have fought the battle out on foot and with swords, but again the Sable Knight would not have it so. Instead he called aloud to those inside the castle, and at once two more squires came out with fresh new spears of ash wood. So each knight took a spear once more, and, armed with it, each chose his place on that fair, level lawn of grass.

*King Arthur is overthrown*.

And now, for the third time, having made themselves ready, those two excellent knights hurled themselves together in a furious charge. And now, as twice before, King Arthur struck the Sable Knight so squarely in the center of his shield that the spear he held burst into splinters. But this time the Sable Knight's spear did not break that way, but held; and so violent was the blow he struck on King Arthur's shield that it pierced right through the center of it. Then, under that great and powerful blow, the girths of the King's saddle burst apart, and both he and his horse were thrown violently backward. And King Arthur would have been hurled to the ground if he had not leaped from his saddle with extraordinary skill and knightly quickness. So, though his horse was overthrown, he himself kept his footing and did not fall into the dust. Even so, the blow he had received was so violent that for a little while he was completely stunned, and everything whirled around before his eyes.

But when his sight came back to him, he was filled with an anger so fierce that it seemed to him as though all the blood in his heart had rushed into his brain, so that he saw nothing before his eyes but red, the color of blood. And when this too had passed, he saw the Sable Knight sitting on his horse not far away. At once King Arthur ran to him, caught the bridle rein of his horse, and cried out to the Sable Knight with great fury, "Come down, you black knight, and fight me on foot and with your sword!"

"That I will not do," said the Sable Knight, "for look, I have overthrown you. So hand over your shield to me, so that I may hang it on that apple tree, and go on your way as others have done before you."

"That I will not!" cried King Arthur in a great passion. "I will neither yield nor go away from here until one of us has completely conquered the other." And with that he shoved the Sable Knight's horse backward by the bridle rein so hard that the knight was forced to leap from his saddle to save himself from being thrown to the ground.

*The knights fight on foot with swords*.

And now each knight was as furious as the other. So each drew his sword and set his shield, and then they rushed together like two wild bulls in battle. They thrust, they struck, they circled, they parried, they struck again and again, and the sound of their blows, crashing and clashing one upon the other, filled the whole space around them with a tremendous uproar. Nobody could ever fully imagine the fury of that fight. For the blows that each gave the other were so violent that whole pieces of armor were hacked from their bodies, and many deep and grievous wounds were given and received, so that the armor of each of them was all stained red with the blood that flowed down over it.

At last King Arthur, growing almost wild with fury, struck so fierce a blow that no armor could have withstood it if it had landed squarely. But with that stroke it happened that his sword broke off at the hilt, and the blade flew up into the air in three pieces. Yet the stroke was so wonderfully fierce that the Sable Knight groaned and staggered and ran around in a circle, as though he had gone blind and did not know where to turn his steps.

But soon he recovered himself, and seeing King Arthur standing close by, and not knowing that his enemy now had no sword to defend himself with, he threw aside his shield, took his own sword in both hands, and struck with it so terrible a stroke that he split through King Arthur's shield and through his helmet and right down to the bone of his skull.

*King Arthur is badly wounded*.

Then King Arthur thought that he had received his death wound, for his brain swam like water, his thighs shook terribly, and he sank down to his knees, while the blood and sweat, mixed together in the darkness of his helmet, ran down into his eyes in a lather and blinded him. And seeing him so grievously hurt, the Sable Knight called on him very fiercely to yield and to surrender his shield, since he was now too badly wounded to fight any more.

But King Arthur would not yield. Instead, catching the other by his sword belt, he pulled himself up to his feet. Then, having somewhat recovered from his daze, he seized the other knight in both arms, and, putting his knee behind the Sable Knight's thigh, he threw him backward onto the ground so violently that the sound of his fall was astounding to hear. And with that fall the Sable Knight was knocked senseless for a while. Then King Arthur at once unlaced the Sable Knight's helmet and saw his face, and he knew him in spite of the blood that was still running down his own face in great streams. He knew that this knight was King Pellinore, who has been named before in this history, and who had twice made war against King Arthur. (It has already been told how King Arthur had driven that other king away from the homes of men and into the forests, so that now he lived in this poor, gloomy castle, from which he made war on all the knights who came to that place.)

Now when King Arthur saw who it was he had been fighting, he cried out aloud, "Ha! Pellinore, is it you, then? Now yield to me, for you are entirely at my mercy." And with that he drew his misericord (the thin dagger a knight carried to give the finishing stroke) and set its point at King Pellinore's throat.

But by now King Pellinore had largely recovered from his fall, and, seeing that blood was pouring down out of his enemy's helmet, he knew that the other must have been very badly wounded by the blow he had just received. So he caught King Arthur's wrist in his hand and turned the point of the dagger away from his own throat, so that it no longer threatened him with any great danger.

And indeed, what with his terrible wound and the loss of blood, King Arthur had now grown so sick and faint that it seemed to him he was near death. And so, without very much trouble, King Pellinore suddenly heaved himself up from the ground and overthrew his enemy, so that now King Arthur lay beneath his knees.

*King Pellinore moves to kill King Arthur*.

And by this time King Pellinore was wild with the fury of the hard battle he had fought. He was so enraged that his eyes were all bloodshot, like those of a wild boar, and a froth, like the foam on a champing boar's jaws, stood in the beard around his lips. So he wrenched the dagger out of his enemy's hand, and at once began to unlace his helmet, meaning to kill him where he lay. But at that moment Merlin came hurrying up, crying out, "Stop! Stop, Sir Pellinore! What are you about to do? Hold back your wicked hand! For the man who lies beneath you is none other than Arthur, King of all this realm!"

At this King Pellinore was astonished beyond measure. For a little while he was silent, and then he cried out in a very loud voice, "Is that so, old man? Then truly your words have doomed this man to death. For no one in all this world has ever suffered such harm and such wrongs as I have suffered at his hands. He has taken from me my power, and my kingship, and my honors, and my lands, and has left me only this gloomy, dismal castle in the forest to live in. So, since he is now in my power, he will die right here, if for no other reason than this: if I let him go free now, he will certainly take his revenge once he has recovered from all the harm he has suffered at my hands."

*Merlin lays a spell upon King Pellinore*.

Then Merlin said, "Not so! He will not die at your hands, for I myself will save him." And with that he raised his staff and struck King Pellinore across the shoulders. At once King Pellinore fell down and lay on the ground on his face, like a man who had suddenly died.

At this, King Arthur raised himself on his elbow and saw his enemy lying there as though dead, and he cried out, "Ha, Merlin! What is this you have done? I am very sorry for it, for I can see that by your magic arts you have killed one of the best knights in all the world."

"Not so, my lord King!" said Merlin. "For truly, I tell you that you are far nearer to your death than he is. He is only asleep and will soon wake up; but you are in such a state that it would take only a very little to make you die."

And indeed King Arthur was terribly sick, sick to the very heart, from the grievous wound he had received, so that it was only with great difficulty that Merlin could help him up onto his horse. When he had done this, and had hung the King's shield on the horn of his saddle, Merlin at once led the wounded man away across the bridge, and, leading the horse by the bridle, took him off into the forest.

Now I must tell you that in that part of the forest there lived a certain hermit who was so holy that the wild birds of the woods would come and rest on his hand while he read his prayer book; and he was so saintly in his gentleness that the wild does would come right up to the door of his hermitage and stand there while he milked them for his own refreshment. And this hermit lived so far away from the homes of men that when he rang the bell for morning prayers or for evening prayers, there was hardly ever anyone to hear the sound of it except the wild creatures that lived nearby. Yet even so, royal people and others of high rank would sometimes come to this remote and lonely place, as though on a pilgrimage, because of the hermit's great holiness.

*Merlin brings King Arthur to the cell of a lonely hermit*.

So Merlin brought King Arthur to this holy refuge, and when they had reached it, he and the hermit lifted the wounded man down from his saddle, the hermit speaking many words of pity and sorrow, and together they carried him into the holy man's cell. There they laid him on a bed of moss, unlaced his armor, examined his wounds, bathed them with pure water, and dressed his hurts, for that hermit was a very skillful healer. So for all that day and part of the next, King Arthur lay on the hermit's bed like a man about to die. He saw everything around him as though through a thin layer of water, and his breath hung on his lips and fluttered there, and he could not even lift his head from the bed because of the weakness that lay upon him.

*The Lady Guinevere consults with the priest*.

Now on the afternoon of the second day there arose a great noise and commotion in that part of the forest. For it happened that the Lady Guinevere of Cameliard had come on a pilgrimage to that holy man, together with her whole court of ladies and knights, because the fame of his saintliness had reached even the place where she lived. That lady had a favorite page who was very sick with a fever, and she hoped that the holy man might give her some charm or amulet by whose power the boy might perhaps be cured. So she had come to that place with her entire court, until all that part of the forest was made bright with fine clothes, and its silence was made merry with the sound of talk and laughter, the singing of songs, the chatter of many voices, and the neighing of horses. And the Lady Guinevere rode in the midst of her damsels and her court, and her beauty outshone the beauty of her damsels as the splendor of the morning star outshines all the lesser stars around it. For then and ever afterward she was held by all the courts of chivalry to be the most beautiful lady in the world.

Now when the Lady Guinevere came to that place, she saw King Arthur's milk-white war horse standing and cropping the green grass of the open glade near the hermitage. And she saw Merlin, too, standing beside the door of the cell. So she asked him whose noble war horse that was, grazing on the grass in that lonely place, and who it was that lay inside the cell. And Merlin answered her, "Lady, the one who lies inside is a knight, very badly wounded, so that he is sick almost to death!"

"Heaven have pity!" cried the Lady Guinevere. "What a sad thing this is that you tell me! Now I beg you to take me to that knight at once, so that I may see him. For I have in my court a very skillful physician, who is well practiced at curing the kinds of wounds that knights receive in battle."

*The Lady Guinevere bids her physician heal King Arthur*.

So Merlin brought the lady into the cell, and there she saw King Arthur where he lay stretched out on the bed. And she did not know who he was. Yet it seemed to her that never in all her life had she seen so noble-looking a knight as the one who lay so badly wounded in that lonely place. And King Arthur lifted his eyes to where she stood beside his bed of pain, surrounded by her maidens, and in the great weakness that lay upon him he did not know whether the one he saw was a mortal lady, or whether she was not rather some tall, straight angel who had come down from one of the lordly courts of Paradise to visit him in his pain and distress. And the Lady Guinevere was filled with great pity at seeing King Arthur in so sorrowful a state. So she called to her the skillful physician who traveled with her court, and she told him to bring a certain alabaster box of very precious balm. And she commanded him to examine that knight's wounds and to anoint them with the balm, so that he might be healed of his hurts as quickly as possible.

So that wise and skillful physician did as the Lady Guinevere commanded, and at once King Arthur felt all his aches ease away, and a great contentment of spirit came over him. And when the lady and her court had gone, he found his heart greatly lifted, and three days later he was completely healed, and as well and strong and vigorous as he had ever been in all his life.

And this was the first time that King Arthur ever saw that beautiful lady, the Lady Guinevere of Cameliard, and from that time on he never forgot her; she was almost always present in his thoughts. And so, when he had recovered, he said to himself: "I will forget that I am a king, and I will cherish the thought of this lady, and I will serve her faithfully, as a good knight may serve the lady he has chosen."

And so he did, as you will hear later in this book.
