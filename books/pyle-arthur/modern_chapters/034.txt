Chapter 3: How King Arthur Overcame the Knight-Enchanter, and How Sir Gawain Showed the True Nobility of His Knighthood

[Figure by: Sir Gawain finds the beautiful lady — Gawain, one arm raised, stands in a dark chamber before a tall, beautiful lady in a long gown.]

*King Arthur returns to the castle of the evil knight*.

Now when King Arthur came to the castle, its gate was opened to him at once and he went in. And when he had gone in, several attendants came and led him into the hall where he had been before. There he saw the knight of that castle and a great many people who had come to watch the end of the adventure. And when the knight saw King Arthur, he said to him, "Sir, have you come to keep your pledge?" "Yes," said King Arthur, "for that is what I vowed to you." Then the knight of the castle said, "Sir, have you guessed the riddle?" And King Arthur said, "I believe I have." The knight of the castle said, "Then let me hear your answer. But if you make any mistake, or if you do not guess rightly, then your life is forfeit." "Very well," said King Arthur, "let it be so. Now this is the answer to your riddle: What a woman wants most is to have her own way."

Now when the lord of the castle heard King Arthur guess rightly, he did not know what to say or where to look; and everyone who was there also saw that the King had guessed rightly.

Then King Arthur came up very close to that knight, with a very stern face, and he said, "Now, you traitor knight! A year ago you asked me to join in your sport with you, so now it is my turn to ask you to have some sport with me. And this is the sport I will have: you shall give me that chain and locket that hang around your neck, and I shall give you the collar that hangs around mine."

*King Arthur kills the knight of the castle*.

At this the face of that knight went all pale, like ashes, and he let out a sound like the cry a hare makes when the hound seizes it. Then King Arthur caught him very violently by the arm, and he seized the locket and tore it away from the knight's neck. At that the knight shrieked very loudly and fell on his knees and begged the King for mercy, and there was a great uproar in that place. Then King Arthur opened the locket, and there inside was a ball like crystal, very clear and shining. And King Arthur said, "I will have no mercy." And with that he flung the ball down violently on the stone floor, so that it broke with a loud noise. And in that same instant the knight-conjurer gave a piercing, bitter cry and fell down on the ground; and when they ran to lift him up, they found that he was entirely dead.

Now when the people of that castle saw their knight suddenly dead like this, and saw King Arthur standing there in the fury of his kingly majesty, they were so afraid that they shrank away from the King where he stood. Then the King turned and walked out of that castle, and no one stopped him. And he mounted his horse and rode away, and no one hindered him or stood in his way as he went.

Now when the King had left the castle in this way, he went straight to the hut where the old woman lived, and he said to her, "You have helped me a very great deal in my hour of need, so now I will keep the pledge I made to you. I will take you to my Court, and you shall choose one of my knights for your husband. For I do not think there is a single knight in all my Court who would not be very glad to do anything in his power to reward one who has saved me as you have done this day."

*King Arthur takes the old woman away with him*.

With that he took the old woman and lifted her up onto his horse's back behind the saddle; then he mounted his horse himself, and so they rode away from that place. And the King behaved toward that aged woman in every way with the greatest consideration, as though she had been a beautiful lady of the highest rank in the land. He showed her such respect that, if she had been a lady of royal blood, he could not have shown her more.

So in due time they reached the Court, which was then at Caerleon. And they arrived there at about midday.

Now at about that time it happened that the Queen and a number of the lords and ladies of the Court were out in the fields enjoying the pleasant Maytime weather. For no one in all the world except the squire Boisenard knew anything of the danger that hung over King Arthur, and so they were all very glad of the pleasant season. Now as King Arthur came near that place, they looked up and saw him coming, and they were astonished beyond all measure to see King Arthur riding toward them across that field with that old woman behind him on the saddle. So they stood still to wait until King Arthur reached them.

But when King Arthur had come up to them, he did not get down from his horse. He sat there and looked at them all very steadily. And Queen Guinevere said, "Sir, what is this? Do you mean to play some merry joke today, that you have brought that old woman here?"

"Lady," said King Arthur, "if it were not for this old woman, it would likely have been a very sorry joke for you and for me. For if she had not helped me, I would now be a dead man, and in a few days you would all no doubt have been in a great passion of grief."

Then everyone who was there marveled greatly at the King's words. And the Queen said, "Sir, what is it that has happened to you?"

Then King Arthur told them everything that had happened to him, from the very beginning, when he and Boisenard had left the castle of Tintagalon. And when he had finished his story, they were greatly amazed.

Now there were seventeen lords of the Court there. So when King Arthur had finished his story, he said to them, "Sirs, I have given my pledge to this aged woman that whichever one of you she may choose shall take her as his wife, and shall treat her with all the honor that he possibly can; for that was the condition she laid upon me. Now tell me, did I do right in pledging to her that I would fulfill what she desired?" And all who were there said, "Yes, lord, you did right. For we would do anything in the world to save you from such danger as the one you have escaped."

*The old woman chooses Sir Gawain*.

Then King Arthur said to that old woman, "Lady, is there any one of these knights here whom you would choose to be your husband?" At this the old woman pointed her very long, bony finger at Sir Gawain, and said, "Yes, I would marry that lord. For I can see by the chain around his neck, and by the golden circlet on his hair, and by the proud nobility of his looks, that he must be the son of a king."

Then King Arthur said to Sir Gawain, "Sir, are you willing to fulfill my pledge to this old woman?" And Sir Gawain said, "Yes, lord. Whatever you ask of me, that I will do." So Sir Gawain came to the old woman, and took her hand in his and raised it to his lips; and not one of all those present so much as smiled. Then they all turned and went back to the King's castle. And they were very silent and downcast, for this was a heavy trouble that had come upon that Court.

*Sir Gawain takes the old woman for his wife*.

Now after they had returned to the Court, they gave that old woman certain rooms in the castle, and they dressed her in rich clothes such as a queen might wear, and they gave her a Court of attendants such as was fit for a queen. And it seemed to the whole Court that, in the rich robes she wore, she was ten times uglier than she had been before. So when eleven days had passed, Sir Gawain was married to that old woman in the chapel of the King's Court, with great ceremony and pomp and splendor. And everyone who was there was as sad and sorrowful as though Sir Gawain had been called upon to suffer death.

After they were married, Sir Gawain and the old woman went to Sir Gawain's house. And there Sir Gawain shut himself away from all the world and would let no one come near him. For he was proud beyond all measure, and in this great humiliation he suffered so much that words cannot tell how great that humiliation was. That is why he shut himself away from the world, so that no one might see his grief and his shame.

*Sir Gawain is in great sorrow*.

And all the rest of that day he walked up and down his room without stopping, for he was in such utter despair that it came into his mind that it would be well if he took his own life; for it seemed to him impossible to bear such shame as had come upon him. Then after a while the darkness of early night fell, and with it a certain strength came to Sir Gawain, and he said, "It is shameful of me to behave like this. For since I have married that lady, she is my true wedded wife, and I am not treating her with the honor she has a right to." So he went out of that place and looked for the rooms of the old woman who was his wife; and by that time it was completely dark. But when Sir Gawain came into the place where she was, the old woman scolded him, crying out at him, "So, Sir! You have treated me very badly on this, our wedding day. You have stayed away from me all afternoon, and now you only come to me when it is dark night." And Sir Gawain said, "Lady, I could not help it, for I was very heavily weighed down with many cares. But if I have neglected you today, I beg your forgiveness for it, and I will be willing to do everything in my power to make up to you for any neglect I have shown you." Then the lady said, "Sir, it is very dark in here. Let us have a light." "It shall be as you wish," said Sir Gawain, "and I myself will go and fetch a light for you."

So Sir Gawain went out of that place, and he brought back two wax tapers, one in each hand, set in candlesticks of gold; for he meant to show every respect to that old woman. And when he came into the room he saw that she was at the far end of it, and he went toward her; and she rose and stood before him as he came near.

*Of the beautiful lady who appears to Sir Gawain*.

But when the circle of light fell upon that old woman, and Sir Gawain saw who it was that stood before him, he cried out aloud in a very great voice at the great marvel and wonder of what he saw. For instead of the old woman he had left, he saw a lady of extraordinary beauty, in the very flower of her youth. He saw that her hair was long and glossy and very black, and that her eyes were black as well, like black jewels, and that her lips were like coral and her teeth like pearls. So for a while Sir Gawain could not speak, and then he cried out, "Lady! Lady! Who are you?"

Then that lady smiled at Sir Gawain with such loving kindness that he did not know what to think, except that this was an angel who had come down to that place out of Paradise. So he stood before her for a long time and could find no more words to say, and she went on smiling at him very kindly in that way. Then by and by Sir Gawain said to her, "Lady, where is the woman who is my wife?" And the lady said, "Sir Gawain, I am she." "That is not possible," cried Sir Gawain, "for she was old and extraordinarily ugly, but I believe you are more beautiful than any lady I have ever seen." And the lady said, "Nevertheless, I am she. And because you have taken me for your wife of your own free will and with great courtesy, part of the enchantment that lay upon me has been lifted from me. For now I shall be able to appear before you in my own true shape. Where a little while ago I was as ugly and foul as you saw me, now for half of each day I am to be as you see me, and for the other half I must be ugly as I was before."

Then Sir Gawain was filled with a joy too great for words. And along with that joy there came an overwhelming passion of love for that lady. So he cried out aloud several times, "This is surely the most wonderful thing that ever happened to any man in all the world." With that he fell down on his knees, and took the lady's hands in his own, and kissed her hands with great fervor; and all the while she smiled at him as she had at first.

Then the lady spoke again and said, "Come, sit down beside me, and let us decide for which part of the day I shall have the one form and for which part the other. For I may have the one appearance all day and the other appearance all night."

Then Sir Gawain said, "I would have you in this form during the night, for then we are together in our own home; and since you are truly as I see you now, I do not care in the least how the world may look on you."

At this the lady spoke with great spirit, saying, "No, sir, I would not have it that way. For every woman loves the good opinion of the world, and I would gladly enjoy what beauty I have in the eyes of the world, and not have to bear the scorn and contempt of men and women."

To this Sir Gawain said, "Lady, I would have it the other way."

And she said, "No, I would have it my way."

*Sir Gawain gives the lady her own way*.

Then Sir Gawain said, "So be it. For since I have taken you for my wife, I must show you respect in all things. So you shall have your own way in this and in everything else."

Then the lady burst out laughing beyond all measure, and she said, "Sir, I only put this to you as a last test. For as I am now, so I shall always be."

At this Sir Gawain was so filled with joy that he did not know how to contain himself.

So they sat together for a long time, hand in hand. Then after a while Sir Gawain said, "Lady, who are you?" And she answered, "I am one of the Ladies of the Lake. But for your sake I have become mortal like other women, and have left that very beautiful home where I once lived. I have kept you in my heart for a long while now. For I was not very far away at that time when you said farewell to Sir Pelleas beside the lake, and there I saw how you wept and grieved when Sir Pelleas left you, and my heart went out to you in great pity. So after a while I left that lake and became mortal for your sake. Then, when I learned of the trouble King Arthur had fallen into, I took that chance to have him bring me to you, so that I might test the whole nobility of your knighthood. And I have found it to be everything I believed it could be. For though I seemed to you so old, so ugly, and so foul, you have treated me with such kindness and regard that I do not believe you could have behaved more courteously to me if I had been the daughter of a king. And so it now gives me such pleasure to have you for my knight and my true lord that I cannot well tell you how great my joy is."

*Sir Gawain orders great rejoicing*.

Then Sir Gawain said, "Lady, I do not think it can be as great as my joy in having you." And with that he came to her, and laid his hand on her shoulder, and kissed her on the lips.

Then after that he went out and called in a great voice all through the house, and the people of the house came running from everywhere. And he ordered them to bring lights and refreshments. They brought the lights, and when they had brought them and saw that beautiful lady in place of the aged woman, they were filled with great wonder and joy. They cried out aloud and clapped their hands and made a great noise of rejoicing. And they set out a great feast for Sir Gawain and his lady, and in place of the sorrow and darkness that had been there before, there was joy and light, and music and singing. And those of the King's Court, seeing this from a distance, said, "It is very strange that Sir Gawain should take so much joy in having married that old hag."

But when the next morning came, the lady dressed herself in clothes of yellow silk, and hung many strands of precious stones of different colors about herself, and set a golden crown upon her head. And Sir Gawain had his horse brought, and he had a snow-white palfrey brought for the lady, and so they rode out from that place and came into the Court of the King. But when the King and the Queen and their Courts saw that lady, they were so astonished that they did not know what to say for pure wonder. And when they heard all that had happened, they rejoiced greatly and cheered aloud, so that all their mourning was changed into rejoicing. And indeed, there was not one knight in all that Court who would not have given half his life to have been as fortunate in that matter as Sir Gawain, the son of King Lot of Orkney.

Such is the story of Sir Gawain, and this is the meaning I draw from it. Just as that poor, ugly old woman appeared to the eyes of Sir Gawain, so a man's duty sometimes appears to him ugly and very unwelcome to his wishes. But when he has wedded himself to that duty, so that he has made it one with himself as a bridegroom makes himself one with his bride, then that duty suddenly becomes very beautiful, both to him and to others.

So may it be with you: may you take up your duty, no matter how little you may like to do so. For truly, a man will hardly have any real pleasure in his life unless his wishes become wedded to his duty and hold fast to it, as a husband holds fast to his wife. For when wishes are wedded to duty like this, the soul takes great joy to itself, as though a wedding had taken place between a bridegroom and a bride within the temple of the heart.

And in the same way, when you have become entirely wedded to your duty, you will become just as worthy as that good knight and gentleman Sir Gawain. For a man does not need to wear armor to be a true knight; he needs only to do his very best, with all patience and humility, as it has been set out for him to do. So when your own time comes to show your knighthood by taking up your duty, I pray that you, too, may prove yourself as worthy as Sir Gawain proved himself in this story that I have told you here.

Conclusion

So ends this volume, in which the history of those Three Worthies who were of the Court of King Arthur has been told, with every detail of the story.

And now, if God will give me the grace to do so, I will some day, not very long from now, write the further history of a number of other knights and worthies of whom I have not yet spoken.

And among the first of these shall be Sir Lancelot, whom all the world knows to have been the greatest knight in feats of arms who ever lived, except Sir Galahad, who was his son. And I shall tell you the story of Sir Uwain and Sir Geraint, and of Sir Percival, and of a number of others.

But more of this another time. For now, with great regret, I bid you farewell and bring this history to a close.

So may God grant that we come together again at another time, with such happiness and prosperity that you may have a free and untroubled heart to enjoy the history of those excellent men that I shall then set before you. Amen.
