Chapter 1: How Sir Kay Fought in a Great Tournament in London and Broke His Sword, and How Arthur Found Him a New One

*Of Sir Ector, the Trustworthy Knight*.

Now among the great people who were called to London Town by the Archbishop's summons, as I have told you, there was a certain knight, very honorable and of high rank, named Sir Ector of Bonmaison. He was called the Trustworthy Knight, because he faithfully kept the secrets of those who confided in him, and because he always did for every man, whether of high or low degree, exactly what he had promised to do, without falling short in the least. So this noble and excellent knight was held in great regard by everyone who knew him. For not only was he honorable in his conduct, but he was also of very high rank and great wealth. He owned seven castles in Wales and in the country to the north of it, and also certain rich farmlands with villages belonging to them, and several forests of great size, both in the north country and in the west. This very noble knight had two sons. The elder was Sir Kay, a young knight of great courage and promise, already well known in the tournaments because of several very honorable deeds of arms he had done. The other was a young lad eighteen years old, named Arthur, who at that time was serving, and serving well, as Sir Kay's squire.

Now when Sir Ector of Bonmaison received the Archbishop's summons by messenger, he immediately called these two sons to him and told them to get ready at once to go with him to London Town, and they did so. In the same way he told a great number of his retainers and squires and pages to make themselves ready, and they did so too. And so, with a very fine company of armed men and with great show and ceremony, Sir Ector of Bonmaison made his way to London Town, in obedience to the Archbishop's command.

When he arrived there, he took up his lodging in a certain field where many other noble knights and mighty lords had already settled themselves. There he set up a very handsome pavilion of green silk, and raised his banner bearing the emblem of his house, which was a black gryphon on a field of green.

All over this field stood a great crowd of other pavilions of many different colors, and above each pavilion flew the pennant and the banner of the mighty lord it belonged to. So many pennants and banners were there that in places the sky was almost hidden by the bright colors of the fluttering flags.

Among the great lords who had come there in answer to the Archbishop's summons were many very famous kings and queens and noblemen of high degree. There was King Lot of Orkney, who had married a stepdaughter of Uther Pendragon, and there was King Uriens of Gore, who had married another stepdaughter of that great king. There was King Ban, and King Bors, and King Rience, and King Leodegrance, and many others of the same rank. In all there were no fewer than twelve kings and seven dukes; so that, what with all their courts of lords and ladies and squires and pages waiting on them, the town of London had hardly ever seen the like before that day.

*The Archbishop declares a tournament*.

Now the Archbishop of Canterbury, bearing in mind how extraordinary an occasion it was that had brought so many kings and dukes and high lords to the adventure of the sword and the anvil, had commanded that a very stately and noble tournament should be proclaimed: a great contest of arms, in which knights fought one another for honor and glory before all the people. He also commanded that this contest should be held in a certain field near the great cathedral, three days before the test of the sword and the anvil was to be made (which, as I told you, was to happen on Christmas Day). To this tournament every knight was invited who was of good enough birth and rank to be fit to take part in it. And so a great many high-born knights applied to be admitted, and in such numbers that three heralds were kept very busy looking into their claims to the right to fight. For these heralds examined the coats of arms and the family records of everyone who applied, with great care and caution.

*Sir Kay asks permission to attend the tournament*.

Now when Sir Kay heard the news of this tournament, he went to his father, and when he stood before him he said: "Sir, I am your son, and I have inherited from you a very high rank, both in birth and in fortune. And I find that I have a tremendous desire to risk my body in this tournament. So, if I can prove my standing as a knight before this council of heralds, it may bring great honor and credit to you and to our house if I take part in this adventure. And so I ask your permission to do as I wish."

To this Sir Ector replied: "My son, you have my permission to enter this honorable contest. And I hope that God will give you a great deal of strength, and also such grace of spirit that you may win honor for yourself and credit for all of us who are of your blood."

So Sir Kay went away full of joy, and he went at once to the gathering of heralds and laid his claims before them. And after they had duly examined his right to knighthood, they entered his name as one of the knights who would fight, just as he wished; and at this Sir Kay was filled with great contentment and joy of heart.

When his name had been written on the list of those who would fight, Sir Kay chose his young brother Arthur to be his squire, and to carry his spear and his pennant before him onto the field of battle. And Arthur was very glad as well, because of the honor that had come to him and to his brother.

Now, when the day of the tournament came, an enormous crowd of people gathered to watch that noble and courtly contest of arms. For at that time, as I have said, London was extraordinarily full of nobles and knights, so that it was reckoned that no fewer than twenty thousand lords and ladies (besides those twelve kings with their courts and the seven dukes with theirs) were gathered in the stands all around the lists, which were the fenced grounds of the battle, to watch the deeds of those chosen knights. And those noble people sat so close together, and so filled the seats and benches set aside for them, that it looked as though a solid wall of human beings surrounded the meadow where the battle was to be fought. And indeed any knight might well be stirred to do his very best on such a great occasion, with the eyes of so many beautiful ladies and noble lords watching what he did. And so the hearts of all the knights there swelled with eagerness to outdo one another and to tumble their opponents into the dust.

In the center of this wonderful court of lords and ladies stood the box and the throne of the Lord Archbishop himself. Above the throne was a canopy of purple cloth, decorated with silver lilies, and the throne itself was hung all around with purple velvet, embroidered with two designs, turn and turn about: the figure of Saint George in gold, and silver crosses of Saint George circled with golden halos. There the Lord Archbishop sat in great state and splendor, surrounded by a very lofty court of high-ranking churchmen and of honorable knights, so that the whole center of the field glittered with the splendor of gold and silver embroidery, and was made beautiful by every color of rich clothing, and bright with fine armor of excellent workmanship. And indeed, so stately was the whole scene that very few of those who were there had ever seen so noble a preparation for battle as the one they saw that day.

Now, when all that great assembly had taken their places and everything had been made ready in proper order, a herald came and stood before the Archbishop's throne and blew a very strong, loud blast on a trumpet. At that signal the gates of the lists were opened at once, and two parties of knights rode in, one party at the northern end of the battle meadow and the other at the southern end. Then all at once that empty field was aglitter with the bright splendor of sunlight on polished armor and trappings. And so the two parties took up their positions, each at the place that had been assigned to it, the one to the north and the other to the south.

*Sir Kay takes part in the lists*.

Now the party that Sir Kay had joined was at the north end of the field, and it numbered ninety-three knights. The other party stood at the south end of the field, and it numbered ninety-six. But though Sir Kay's party was three fewer than the other, it was nonetheless the stronger by a good deal, because there were a number of knights of great strength and fame in its company. Indeed, I may mention here that two of those knights later became highly respected companions of the Round Table: Sir Mador de la Porte, and Sir Bedevere, who was the last man to see King Arthur alive on this earth.

So, when everything was prepared according to the rules of the tournament, and when the knights had made themselves ready in every way that was needed, and had set their spears and shields as knights do who are about to enter serious battle, the herald put his trumpet to his lips a second time and blew on it with all his might. Then, having sounded this blast, he waited a while, and then he blew the trumpet again.

And at that blast, each party of knights left its place and rushed against the other in a great uproar, with such noise and fury that the whole earth groaned beneath the hoofs of the war-horses, and trembled and shook as if in an earthquake.

So those two companies met, one against the other, in the middle of the field, and the roar of breaking lances was so terrible that everyone who heard it was astonished and appalled by the sound. Several fair ladies fainted away in terror of the noise, and others shrieked aloud. For not only was there that great uproar, but the air was entirely filled with flying splinters of ash wood.

In that famous charge seventy very noble and honorable knights were thrown from their horses, and many of them were trampled under the horses' hoofs. And so, when the two companies drew back, each to its own place, the ground could be seen to be covered all over with broken pieces of lances and fragments of armor, and many knights could be seen lying sorrowfully in the middle of all that wreckage. Some of these champions tried to get up and could not, while others lay perfectly still, as though they were dead. Squires and pages ran out to them in great numbers, and lifted up the fallen men and carried them away to places of safety. And attendants likewise ran out and gathered up the pieces of armor and the broken spears and carried them off to the barriers, so that after a while the field was entirely clear once more.

Then everyone who was looking down on that meadow cheered loudly, with great joy of heart, for such a noble and glorious contest of arms in friendly trial had hardly ever been seen in all that realm before.

*Sir Kay does well in the encounter*.

Now let us turn to Sir Kay. For in this charge he had carried himself so well that no knight there had done better than he, and perhaps no one had done as well. Though two opponents had aimed their spears at him at the same time, he had held firm against them both. And he struck one of those two so violently, right in the middle of his defenses, that he lifted the man clean over the back of his horse and flung him down half a spear's length behind it, so that the fallen knight rolled over three times in the dust before he stopped.

And when the knights of Sir Kay's party who were near him saw what he had done, they cheered him loudly and with such enthusiasm that Sir Kay was wonderfully satisfied and pleased at heart.

And indeed it must be said that at that time there was hardly any knight in all the world who was so excellent at deeds of arms as Sir Kay. And though there came knights later of much greater fame and of more glorious deeds (as will be told in this book in good time), yet at that time Sir Kay was reckoned by many to be one of the most wonderfully mighty knights in all that realm, whether riding alone in search of adventure or fighting in battle.

So that round of the contest was run, to the great pleasure and satisfaction of everyone who saw it, and especially of Sir Kay and his friends. And after it was finished, the two parties returned in good order, each to its own place once more.

And when they got there, each knight handed his spear to his squire. For the next charge was to be made with swords, and so all lances and other weapons were to be put aside; such was the rule of that courteous and well-bred contest of arms.

*Of the contest with swords*.

So when the herald blew his trumpet again, every knight drew his sword, all of them so eager for battle that there was a great splendor of blades flashing in the air at once. And when the herald blew a second time, each party pushed forward into the contest with great nobility of heart and eagerness of spirit, every knight meaning to meet his opponent with all the strength that was in him.

Then at once there began a battle so fierce that if those knights had been bitter enemies of long standing instead of friendly contestants, the blows they dealt each other could not have been more violent, or more astonishing to watch.

And in this round too Sir Kay proved himself so extraordinary a champion that his equal was nowhere to be seen on all that field. For he struck down five knights violently, one after another, before anyone could check his advance.

So, seeing him do such work as this, several knights of the other party tried to get at him so as to meet him as he came on.

Among these was a certain knight named Sir Balamorgineas, who was so huge in body that he rode head and shoulders above every other knight. And he had such extraordinary strength that people believed he could hold his own against three ordinary knights at once. So when this knight saw the work Sir Kay was doing, he called out to him, "Ho! Ho! Sir Knight of the black gryphon, turn this way and do battle with me!"

*Sir Kay fights with Sir Balamorgineas*.

Now when Sir Kay saw that Sir Balamorgineas meant to come against him like that, very threateningly and set on fighting him, he turned toward his enemy with great cheerfulness of spirit. For at that time Sir Kay was very full of youthful fire, and thought nothing of attacking any enemy who might challenge him to battle.

(So it was at that time. But afterward, when he became Seneschal, the officer who runs the king's household, and when other and mightier knights appeared at the King's court, it happened that he would sometimes avoid a fight with such a knight as Sir Lancelot, or Sir Pelleas, or Sir Marhaus, or Sir Gawain, if he could do so without loss to his honor.)

So, being very full of the spirit of youth, he turned with great vigor of heart, all on fire with the eagerness and fury of battle. And he cried out in a great voice, "Very well, I will do battle with you, and I will cast you down like your fellows!" And with that he struck at Sir Balamorgineas with wonderful fierceness, with all his might. Sir Balamorgineas took the stroke on his helmet and was completely dazed by the fury of it, for he had never felt anything like it before. His head swam so lightly that he had to hold on to the horn of his saddle to keep himself from falling.

*Sir Kay breaks his sword*.

But it was a great pity for Sir Kay that, with the fierceness of the blow, his sword blade snapped off short at the hilt and flew so high into the air that it seemed to rise above the towers of the cathedral as it went. Yet that is what happened, and so Sir Kay was left without any weapon. And yet people thought that with that stroke he had Sir Balamorgineas entirely at his mercy, and that if he could have struck one more blow with his sword he might easily have overcome him.

But as it was, Sir Balamorgineas soon recovered enough to see that his enemy was now entirely at his mercy. And so, filled beyond measure with rage at the blow he had received, he pressed toward Sir Kay, meaning to strike him down in a violent attack.

At that point it might have gone very badly for Sir Kay, had not three of his companions in arms seen the terrible danger he was in. They pushed in between him and Sir Balamorgineas, meaning to take that knight's attack on themselves and so save Sir Kay from being overthrown. They did this so successfully that Sir Kay was able to push his way out of the crush and escape to the barriers without suffering any more harm at the hands of his enemies.

*Sir Kay tells Arthur to get him a sword*.

Now when he reached the barrier, his squire, young Arthur, came running to him with a goblet of spiced wine. Sir Kay opened the visor of his helmet to drink, for he was thirsty beyond measure. And his face was all covered with blood and sweat, and he was so parched from the battle that his tongue stuck to the roof of his mouth and he could not speak. But when he had drunk what Arthur gave him, his tongue was loosened, and he shouted at the young man in a loud and violent voice: "Ho! Ho! Brother, get me another sword to fight with, for I am certainly winning a great deal of glory for our house today!" And Arthur said, "Where shall I get you a sword?" And Kay said, "Hurry to our father's pavilion and fetch me another sword from there, for this one I have is broken." And Arthur said, "I will do it as fast as I can." And with that he put his hand on the barrier and vaulted over it into the lane beyond. He ran down the lane as fast as he could go, meaning to do the task his brother had given him; and just as fast he ran on to the pavilion his father had set up in the meadows.

But when he reached Sir Ector's pavilion, he found no one there, for all the attendants had gone off to the tournament. Nor could he find any sword fit for his brother to use, and so he was at a loss to know what to do.

*Arthur draws the sword from the anvil*.

In this difficulty he thought of the sword that stood thrust into the anvil in front of the cathedral, and it seemed to him that a sword like that would suit his brother's purpose very well. So he said to himself, "I will go there and get that sword if I can, for it will certainly do very well for my brother to finish his battle with." And at that he ran as fast as he could to the cathedral. When he got there he found that no one was standing guard at the block of marble, as there always had been until then, for all the guards had gone off to the contest of arms that was going on. And the anvil and the sword stood where he could reach them. So, with no one there to stop young Arthur, he leaped up onto the block of marble and set his hands on the hilt of the sword. And he bent his body and pulled very strongly on the sword, and it came out of the anvil with wonderful smoothness and ease. And he held the sword in his hand, and it was his.

When he had gotten the sword in this way, he wrapped it in his cloak so that no one might see it (for it shone with an extraordinary brightness and splendor), and he leaped down from the marble block and hurried with it to the field of battle.

Now when Arthur came back into the meadow, he found Sir Kay waiting for him with great impatience. And when Sir Kay saw him, he cried out very fiercely, "Have you got a sword?" And Arthur said, "Yes, I have one here." With that he opened his cloak and showed Sir Kay the sword he had brought.

Now when Sir Kay saw the sword, he knew it at once, and he did not know what to think or what to say. So for a while he stood there, like a man turned to stone, staring at that sword. Then after a while he said, in a very strange voice, "Where did you get that sword?" And Arthur looked at his brother and saw that his face was deeply troubled and had gone as white as wax. And he said, "Brother, what is wrong with you, that you look so strange? I will tell you the whole truth. I could find no sword in our father's pavilion, so I thought of the sword that stood in the anvil on the marble block in front of the cathedral. So I went there and tried to draw it out, and it came out with wonderful ease. And when I had drawn it out, I wrapped it in my cloak and brought it here to you, as you see."

*Sir Kay keeps the sword for his own*.

Then Sir Kay turned his thoughts inward and said to himself, "Why, my brother Arthur is still hardly more than a child. And besides, he is extremely innocent. So he does not know what he has done, or what the doing of it means. Now, since he has won this weapon, why should I not claim the deed for myself, and so gain the glory that comes with it?" At that he roused himself at once and said to Arthur, "Give me the sword and the cloak," and Arthur did as his brother told him. And when he had done so, Sir Kay said to him, "Tell no one about this, but keep it secret in your own heart. Meanwhile, go to our father where he sits by the lists, and ask him to come at once to the pavilion where we are lodging."

And Arthur did as Sir Kay told him, full of wonder that his brother should seem so troubled in spirit. For he did not know what he had done in drawing that sword out of the anvil, nor what great things were to come of that small deed. For that is how it is in this world: a man may sometimes prove himself worthy of a great trust such as that, and yet, in the humility of his spirit, be entirely unaware that he is worthy of it. And so it was with young Arthur at that time.
