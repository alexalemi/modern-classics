Chapter 3: How Many Kings and Great Dukes Tried to Draw the Sword from the Anvil and Failed, and How Arthur Tried and Succeeded

[Figure ak: How Arthur drew out the sword — Arthur, a young squire in a hooded cloak, grips the hilt of the sword set in the anvil on top of the marble block, while bearded onlookers watch.]

So when the morning of Christmas Day had come, many thousands of people of every rank, both high-born and humble, gathered in front of the cathedral to watch the test of that sword.

Now a canopy of embroidered cloth of many colors had been spread above the sword and the anvil, and a platform had been built all around the block of marble. And near that place a throne had been set up for the Archbishop, for the Archbishop was to oversee the test and to see that every part of it was carried out with fairness and great care.

So, when the morning was half gone, the Archbishop himself came with great pomp and ceremony and took his seat on the high throne that had been placed for him. And all his court of clergy and knights gathered around him, so that he made a very proud and splendid show of courtly state.

Now nineteen kings and sixteen dukes had gathered for that test, and each of them was of such noble and lofty rank that he had high hopes he would be proved before the world that day to be the rightful king and High King of all Britain. So after the Archbishop had settled himself on his throne, several of them came and demanded that he put the matter to the test at once. So the Archbishop ordered his herald to sound a trumpet and to call all who had the right to try the sword to come forward to that adventure, and the herald did as the Archbishop ordered.

*King Lot of Orkney tries the sword and fails*.

And as soon as the herald had sounded his trumpet, the first of those kings appeared to make trial of the sword, and the one who came was King Lot of Orkney and the Isles. With King Lot came eleven knights and five squires, so that he made a very noble show before the eyes of everyone. And when King Lot had arrived at that place, he climbed onto the platform. First he bowed to the Archbishop, and then he laid his hands on the pommel of the sword in the sight of all. He bent his body and pulled on the sword with great strength, but he could not move the blade in the anvil even by the width of a hair, for it stood as fast as the iron in which it was planted. After that first attempt he tried three times more, but still he could not move the blade in the iron at all. Then, having tried four times, he gave up and came down from that place. And he was filled with great anger and indignation that he had not succeeded.

*Many others try and fail*.

After King Lot came his brother-in-law, King Uriens of Gore, and he too tried the sword in the same way King Lot had done. But he did no better than the other king. After King Uriens came King Fion of Scotland, and after King Fion came King Mark of Cornwall, and after King Mark came King Rience of North Wales, and after King Rience came King Leodegrance of Cameliard. And after him came all those other kings and dukes I have spoken of, and not one of them all was able to move the blade. Some of these high and mighty lords were filled with anger and indignation that they had not succeeded, and others were ashamed that they had failed at that task before the eyes of all who watched them. But whether they were angry or ashamed, it did their cause no good at all.

Now when all the kings and dukes had failed in that adventure, the people who were there were very much astonished, and they said to one another, "How can this be? If all those kings and dukes of such lofty rank have failed at this adventure, who can hope to succeed? Here were all the men most worthy of that high honor, and all of them have tried to draw that sword, and all of them have failed. Who is left to come after them who can hope to succeed?"

*The kings and dukes are discontented*.

And the kings and dukes talked among themselves in the same way. By and by six of the worthiest of them came forward, namely King Lot, King Uriens, King Pellinore, King Ban, King Rience, and Duke Clarence of Northumberland. They stood before the Archbishop's throne and spoke to him like this: "Sir, all the kings and dukes of this realm have striven before you to draw out that sword, and look: not one of all who have attempted it has succeeded. What are we to understand, then, except that the enchanter Merlin has set up this adventure to bring shame and disgrace on all of us who are here, and on you, who are the head of the Church in this realm? For who in all the world can hope to draw a sword blade out of a bed of solid iron? Surely it is beyond the power of any man. Is it not plain, then, that Merlin has made a mockery of us all? So, rather than have this whole great assembly called here for nothing, we beg you in your wisdom to choose at once, from among the kings gathered here, the one best fitted to be High King of this realm. And when you have chosen him, we promise to obey him in everything he may command. Truly, such a choice will be far more worthwhile than wasting time on this foolish business of trying to draw a sword out of an anvil that no man in all the world can draw."

Then the Archbishop was much troubled in spirit, for he said to himself, "Can it be true that Merlin has deceived me, and has made a mockery of me and of all these kings and lords? Surely this cannot be. For Merlin is exceedingly wise, and he would not make a mockery of the whole realm for the sake of such a sorry joke as this would be. Surely he has some purpose in this that we know nothing of, since we are less wise than he is. So I will be patient a while longer." And so, having reasoned this out within himself, he spoke aloud to those seven great lords. "Sirs," he said, "I still have faith that Merlin has not deceived us, so I ask you to be patient just a little while longer. If, in the time it takes a man to count to five hundred twice over, no one comes forward to perform this task, then at your request I will choose one from among you and proclaim him King and High King of all." For the Archbishop had faith that Merlin was about to declare a king before them all at any moment.

Now let us leave these lords and turn to Arthur and his father and brother.

For Merlin had told those three to stay in their pavilion until he judged the time was right for them to come out. And now that time had come, Merlin and Sir Ulfius went to Sir Ector's pavilion, and Merlin said, "Arthur, get up and come out, for the hour has come for you to perform before the whole world the miracle you performed in private not long ago." So Arthur did as Merlin told him, and he came out of the pavilion with his father and his brother, and he was like someone walking in a dream.

*Merlin brings Arthur to the test*.

So the five of them went down from there toward the cathedral and the place of the test. And when they came to the crowd gathered there, the people made way for them, marveling greatly and saying to one another, "Who are these with Merlin the Enchanter and Sir Ulfius, and where have they come from?" For the whole world knew Merlin and Sir Ulfius, and the people could tell that something very extraordinary was about to happen. And Arthur was dressed all in flame-colored clothes embroidered with silver thread, so that others in the crowd said, "Surely that young man is very handsome to look at. Now who can he be?"

But Merlin said not a word to anyone. He brought Arthur through the crowd to the place where the Archbishop sat, and the crowd made way for him so that nothing held him back. And when the Archbishop saw Merlin coming with those others, he stood up and said, "Merlin, who are these you bring to us, and what is their business here?" And Merlin said, "My lord, here is one who has come to try that sword." And the Archbishop said, "Which one is he?" And Merlin said, "This is he," and he laid his hand on Arthur.

*Merlin proclaims that Arthur is royal*.

Then the Archbishop looked at Arthur and saw that the youth had a very handsome face, and his heart went out to Arthur and he loved him a great deal. And the Archbishop said, "Merlin, by what right does this young man come here?" And Merlin answered, "My lord, he comes here by the best right there is in the world. For the young man who stands before you dressed in red is the true son of Uther Pendragon and of his lawful wife, Queen Igraine."

Then the Archbishop cried out in great amazement, and those who stood near and heard what Merlin said were so astonished that they did not know what to think. And the Archbishop said, "Merlin, what is this you are telling me? For who in all the world has ever heard, until now, that Uther Pendragon had a son?"

To this Merlin answered: "No one has ever known of it until now, except a very few. For this is how it happened. When this child was born, the spirit of prophecy was upon me, and I foresaw that Uther Pendragon would die before very long. So I feared that the King's enemies would lay violent hands on the young child for the sake of his inheritance. And so, at the King's command, another man and I took the young child from his mother and gave him to a third, and that man took the royal child and has raised him ever since as his own son. And there are others here who can testify to the truth of these things. For the man who was with me when the young child was taken from his mother was Sir Ulfius, and the man to whom he was entrusted was Sir Ector of Bonmaison. These two witnesses, who are beyond all reproach, will swear to the truth of what I have said, for here they stand before you to confirm it."

And Sir Ulfius and Sir Ector said, "Everything Merlin has spoken is true, and to that we pledge our most faithful and sacred word of honor."

Then the Archbishop said, "Who could doubt the word of such honorable witnesses?" And he looked at Arthur and smiled at him.

Then Arthur said, "Do I have your permission, my lord, to take hold of that sword?" And the Archbishop said, "You have my permission, and may the grace of God go with you in your attempt."

*Arthur draws out the sword*.

At that Arthur went to the block of marble and laid his hands on the hilt of the sword that was thrust into the anvil. He bent his body and pulled very hard, and the sword came out with great ease and very smoothly. And when he had the sword in his hands, he swung it around his head so that it flashed like lightning. After he had swung it three times around his head, he set its point against the face of the anvil and pressed down on it very hard. And the sword slid very smoothly back into the place where it had stood before; and when it was there, halfway deep, it stood fast. And so Arthur accomplished that marvelous miracle of the sword in the eyes of all the world.

Now when the people gathered there saw this miracle performed before their very faces, they raised their voices all together and shouted so loudly, with such a huge uproar, that it seemed as though the whole earth rocked and trembled with the sound.

And while they were shouting, Arthur took hold of the sword again, drew it out, swung it again, and once more drove it back into the anvil. And when he had done that, he drew it out a third time and did the same thing as before. So everyone who was there saw that miracle performed three times over.

*Several of the kings and dukes are angry*.

And all the kings and dukes who were there were filled with great amazement. They did not know what to think or say when they saw someone who was little more than a boy perform the task at which the best of them had failed. Some of them, having seen that miracle, were willing to accept Arthur because of it, but others would not accept him. These drew back and stood apart, and as they stood there they said among themselves, "What is this? Who can believe such a thing, that a beardless boy should be set over us all and made King and High King of this great realm to rule us? No! No! We will have none of him for our King." And others said, "Isn't it plain that Merlin and Sir Ulfius are raising up this unknown boy so they can raise themselves up along with him?" So these discontented kings talked among themselves, and the most bitter of them all were King Lot and King Uriens, who were Arthur's brothers by marriage.

Now when the Archbishop saw how discontented these kings and dukes were, he said to them, "What now, sirs! Are you not satisfied?" And they said, "We are not satisfied." And the Archbishop said, "What would you have?" And they said, "We would have some other sort of king for Britain than a beardless boy whom nobody knows, and whose birthright only three men can vouch for." And the Archbishop said, "What of that? Has he not performed the miracle that you yourselves tried and failed to perform?"

But these high and mighty lords would not be satisfied. With angry faces turned away, they left that place, filled with rage and indignation.

*Certain kings and dukes accept Arthur*.

But others of the kings and dukes came and bowed to Arthur and paid their respects to him, wishing him joy of what he had achieved; and the chief of those who came to him in friendship was King Leodegrance of Cameliard. And the whole multitude acknowledged him and crowded around that place, shouting so that it sounded like thunder.

Now all this while Sir Ector and Sir Kay had stood to one side, and they were heavy with sorrow. For it seemed to them that Arthur had suddenly been raised so far above their station that they could never hope to come near him again. For now he was of royal rank, and they were only ordinary knights. But after a while Arthur saw them standing there with downcast faces, and he went straight to them and took first one and then the other by the hand and kissed each of them on the cheek. At that they were glad again, at being raised up to him like this.

And when Arthur left that place, great crowds of people followed him, so that the streets were completely packed with the press of people. And the multitude kept loudly hailing him as the chosen King of England, and those nearest to him tried to touch the hem of his clothes. So Arthur's heart was lifted up with great joy and gladness, until his soul took wing and flew like a bird into the sky.

So Arthur achieved the adventure of the sword that day and came into his royal birthright. And may God grant His grace to all of you, so that you too may succeed in your undertakings. For any man may be a king in whatever life he is placed in, if only he can draw out the sword of success from the iron of circumstance. So when your own time of testing comes, I do hope it may go with you as it went with Arthur that day, and that you too may win success, to your own entire satisfaction, and to your great glory and perfect happiness. Amen.

Conclusion

*Arthur makes several other trials of the sword and succeeds every time*.

Now after these things had happened there was a great deal of talk among men, and great confusion and uproar. For while some of the kings and nearly all the common people said, "Look! Here is a king come to us, as it were, straight out of Heaven to bring peace to our troubled land," other kings (and there were more of them) said, "Who is this beardless boy who comes claiming to be High King of Britain? Who ever heard of him before? We will have none of him without further trial and stronger proof." So, for the sake of peace, the Archbishop ordered that another test of the sword should be made at Candlemas, the feast at the start of February. And again, all those who tried to draw out the sword failed, but Arthur drew it out several times, very easily, in the sight of all. After that a third trial was made at Easter, and after that a fourth trial at Pentecost. And at every one of these trials Arthur drew the sword out of the anvil again and again, and no one but he could draw it out.

*Arthur is crowned King of Britain*.

And after that fourth trial, several of the kings and many of the lesser barons and knights and all of the common people cried out that these were trials enough, and that Arthur had certainly proved himself to be the rightful King. So they demanded that he should be made King in fact, so that he could rule over them. For by now it had come about that wherever Arthur went, great crowds followed him, hailing him as the true son of Uther Pendragon and the rightful High King of Britain. So the Archbishop, seeing how the people loved Arthur and how much they wanted him for their King, ordered that he should be anointed and crowned with royal rank; and so it was done in the great Cathedral. Some say that the Cathedral was St. Paul's, and some say that it was not.

*Arthur overcomes his enemies*.

But when Arthur had been crowned, all those who were against his kingship withdrew in great anger and at once began to prepare for war against him. But the people were on Arthur's side and joined with him, and so did several kings and many of the lesser barons and knights. And on Merlin's advice, Arthur made friends and allies of a number of other kings, and together they fought two great wars against his enemies and won both of them. In the second war a very famous battle was fought near the Forest of Bedegraine (and so it was called the Battle of Bedegraine), and in that battle Arthur defeated his enemies so completely that they could never again hope to join together in war against him.

*Arthur brings his nephews to court*.

And from King Lot, his brother-in-law, King Arthur brought two of his sons to court to live there and to serve as hostages for the peace from then on. These two were Gawain and Gaheris, and in time they became very famous and accomplished knights. And from King Uriens, his other brother-in-law, Arthur brought to court his one son, Uwain, to hold as a hostage for the peace; and he too became in time a very famous and accomplished knight. And because of these hostages there was peace between those three royal brothers-in-law for all time afterward. And a certain very famous king and knight named King Pellinore (who was one of his enemies) Arthur drove out of his lands, away from the homes of men and into the forest. And King Rience (who was another of his enemies) he drove into the mountains of North Wales. And other kings who were his enemies he brought under his rule, so that the whole land was more at peace than it had been since the days of Uther Pendragon.

And King Arthur made Sir Kay his Seneschal, as he had promised to do. And he made Sir Ulfius his Chamberlain, and Merlin his Counsellor, and Sir Baudwin of Britain his Constable. And these men were all of such a kind that they greatly added to the glory and fame of his reign and set him securely on his throne.

Now when King Arthur's reign had become firmly established in this way, and the fame of his greatness began to spread through the world, many men of noble soul and generous spirit and high knightly skill (knights who wanted above all things to win glory at arms in the courts of chivalry) saw that great honor and advancement were likely to be won under such a king. And so it came about that from all parts, little by little, there began to gather around King Arthur a court of noble, honorable knights such as men had never seen before that time, and perhaps will never see again.

For even to this day the story of these good knights is known to most of mankind. Yes, the names of many kings and emperors have passed away and been forgotten, but the names of Sir Galahad, and of Sir Lancelot of the Lake, and of Sir Tristram of Lyonesse, and of Sir Percival of Wales, and of Sir Gawain, and of Sir Uwain, and of Sir Bors de Ganis, and of many others of that noble household and worthy brotherhood, are still remembered by men. And so I think it very likely that as long as words are written down, the deeds of these heroes will be remembered.

So in the story that is still to come, I have made it my task to tell whoever reads this book about many of these adventures, and to tell him besides certain things that I do not believe everybody knows. And by and by, when I tell of the founding of the Round Table, I will set down a list of a number of the heroes who gathered at Arthur's court at this time, as the men chosen to found that order of the Round Table, and who for that reason were given the title "The Ancient and Honorable Companions of the Round Table."

For though this whole story is chiefly about King Arthur, the glory of these great and honorable knights was his glory, and his glory was theirs. And so one cannot tell of the glory of King Arthur without also telling of the glory of those noble gentlemen.
