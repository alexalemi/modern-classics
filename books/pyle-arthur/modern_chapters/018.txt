Prologue

[Figure be: The Enchanter Merlin — The old white-bearded enchanter in a dark cap sits reading a great book, a crystal ball on the table before him.]

[Figure bf: Headpiece to the Prologue: an enchantress in heavy necklaces holds out a slender wand, under a scroll lettered Sorcery.]

One day King Arthur sat in the Royal Hall of Camelot with the Queen, and all of his Court and all of her Court were there with them. And there was great joy and merriment in that place.

*A messenger comes to the King at Camelot*.

While they sat there, an armed knight suddenly came into the Hall. His armor was all covered with blood and dust, and he had a great many wounds upon his body. Everyone there was astonished and frightened at the sight of that knight, for the look of him promised no good news for King Arthur. The knight-messenger came to where the King was, so weak from his many wounds that he was nearly fainting. And he brought those who were there the news that five kings, enemies of King Arthur, had suddenly come into that land and were burning and plundering the country on every side.

And the knight-messenger said that these five kings were the King of Denmark, the King of Ireland, the King of Soleyse, the King of the Vale, and the King of Longtinaise. They had brought a great army with them and were laying waste the land all around, so that the whole realm was in bitter hardship and sorrow because of the ruin they made.

At this news King Arthur struck his palms together with great force and cried out, "Alas! Who would be a king! Will the time never come when these wars and troubles will end, and we shall have perfect peace in this land?" With that he rose up in great agitation and went out of that place, and everyone who was there was deeply troubled.

So King Arthur at once sent messengers to the two friendly kings who were nearest to him, that is, to King Pellinore and to King Uriens of Gore, and he asked them to come to his aid without losing any time. Meanwhile he himself gathered together a large army, meaning to go out and meet his enemies without delay.

*King Arthur asks for the help of King Uriens and King Pellinore*.

So he went out, and on the third day he came with his army to the forest of Tintagalon. There he stopped, meaning to rest for a little while until King Pellinore and King Uriens should join him.

But the five kings, his enemies, had news that King Arthur was in that place. At once they made a forced march through North Wales, meaning to strike him before those other two kings could come to his aid. So they came by night to where King Arthur was, and they fell upon him so unexpectedly that his army was in great danger of being put to flight by that attack.

But King Arthur drew his army together by his own courage and greatness of heart, and so they defended themselves with great spirit until King Pellinore appeared with his army and joined in the battle.

*King Arthur is victorious*.

So in the end King Arthur won a great victory over his enemies, for they were put to flight and scattered in every direction. And through that war, and because these five kings submitted to him, King Arthur won back all of the realm that had once been his father's, and more besides.

*Eight knights of the Round Table are killed in battle*.

Now in that war eight of the knights of the Round Table lost their lives, and King Arthur mourned their loss with great sorrow; for these were the first knights of the Round Table to lose their lives in battle in his defense.

While King Arthur was grieving very bitterly for these eight knights, Merlin came to him and said, "Do not be downcast, lord. For look! You still have many excellent knights around you, and you certainly cannot have very much trouble in filling the eight places that death has left empty. Now if you follow my advice, you will choose some very worthy adviser from among the knights-companion of your Round Table, and you will consult with him in this matter (for the counsel of two is better than the counsel of one), and between you, you can fill the places that the war has left empty."

This advice seemed very good to King Arthur, so he did as Merlin advised. That very morning he called King Pellinore into his private chamber and laid the matter before him, and the two of them talked it over together. In that consultation King Pellinore gave King Arthur this advice: that four old and worthy knights should be chosen to fill four of those empty seats, and four young and eager knights should be chosen to fill the other four, and in that way all eight seats would be filled.

*King Arthur and King Pellinore choose eight old and worthy knights for the Round Table*.

Now that advice seemed good to King Arthur, and so he said, "Let it be done that way." So first the two of them chose the four old knights, and these were they: King Uriens of Gore, and King Lac, and Sir Hervise de Reuel, and Sir Galliar of Rouge. And from the younger knights of the Court they chose Sir Marvaise of Leisle, and Sir Lionel, the son of King Ban of Benwick, and Sir Cadar of Cornwall. So there was still one place left to be filled.

*They choose three young knights for the Round Table*.

Now it was a very hard thing to decide who should fill that place, for there were at that time two very honorable young knights at the Court. One of these was Sir Baudemagus, a young knight, the brother of Sir Uwain and the son of King Uriens of Gore and Queen Morgan le Fay (and that lady was King Arthur's half-sister, as has been told before). The other young knight was Sir Tor, who, though he had only lately come to the Court, had already carried out several very famous adventures. Sir Tor was a son of King Pellinore (though not by his Queen), and King Pellinore loved him a very great deal.

Then King Pellinore said to King Arthur, "Lord, there are certainly only two knights in all your Court to choose from to fill this eighth seat at the Round Table. One of these is your sister's son, Sir Baudemagus, and the other is my son, Sir Tor. Now I cannot advise you in this matter, so you, Lord, must choose one or the other of these young knights to fill that place. But this much I may say: it will please me very greatly if your favor should fall on Sir Baudemagus. For then all the world will believe that I have been above reproach in my dealings in this affair, whereas if Sir Tor is chosen, all men will say that I favored my own son."

*King Arthur chooses Sir Tor for the Round Table*.

Then King Arthur thought about this matter for a long while, and at last he spoke and said, "Sir, I have weighed this whole affair, and it is my belief that Sir Tor is the better knight of the two. For he has carried out several very excellent adventures, while Sir Baudemagus, though he is a worthy knight, has not yet shown any very great achievement in the fields of knighthood. So, in God's name, let Sir Tor be seated as a companion of the Round Table."

Then King Pellinore said, "So be it," and with that they both rose and went out of that place.

And at that very moment, wonderful to tell, the names of the eight worthies they had chosen appeared, each upon the back of the seat at the Round Table that belonged to him. And so the decision of those two knights was confirmed in that way in the sight of all the world.

*Queen Morgan le Fay is offended with King Arthur*.

Now when word of all this reached the ears of Queen Morgan le Fay, she was greatly offended that Sir Baudemagus, her son, had been passed over and another chosen in his place. So she cried out against King Arthur in the hearing of several people, saying: "Ha! What is this? Are blood and kinship worth nothing in the eyes of this King, that he passes over so worthy a knight as his own nephew and chooses in his place one who was not born in wedlock? My husband's house has suffered many grievous wrongs at the hands of King Arthur. Why, he has taken away our royal power and made us all little better than captives in his own Court! That in itself is as great an insult as if we were his bitter enemies instead of his near kin. But what he has now done to my son, in passing him over like this, is a greater insult than that other."

And Queen Morgan le Fay spoke like this not only to King Uriens, who was her husband, but to Sir Uwain and to Sir Baudemagus, who were her sons. But King Uriens of Gore rebuked her for her words, for he had grown to love King Arthur very much because of the high nobility of his nature. And Sir Uwain rebuked her too, saying that he would listen to nothing evil said of King Arthur. For not only did he love King Arthur better than anyone else in all the world, but the King was at once the mirror of all knighthood and the very fountainhead of honor.

So these two spoke. But Sir Baudemagus listened to what his mother, Queen Morgan, said, for he was very angry with King Arthur because the King had passed him over. So he left the Court without asking King Arthur's leave and went riding as a knight-errant in search of adventure, and at this King Arthur was very sorry.

Now, as I have said, Queen Morgan le Fay spoke of her indignation to several other people of the Court, so that word of it came at last to the ears of King Arthur and grieved him a very great deal. So when Queen Morgan came to him one day and begged his leave to depart from the Court, he spoke to her with great sadness of spirit, saying, "My sister, I am very sorry that you are not pleased with what I have done in this matter, for God knows that I have tried to do the best I could. And though I would very much rather that Sir Baudemagus were a fellow of the Round Table, yet it was my most honest belief that, for several reasons, Sir Tor had the better right to a seat at that Table. Now if I had chosen otherwise than by my true judgment, what virtue would the Round Table have, when I should have shown favor to a man because he is my kinsman?"

Then Queen Morgan le Fay said with great heat, "Sir, all that you say only adds to the insult our house has suffered at your hands. For now you not only deny my son that seat, but you belittle him by comparing him unfavorably with this low-born knight you have chosen. Now the only pleasure I can have in talking with you is to beg you to let me go away from this place."

Then King Arthur, speaking with great dignity, said, "Lady, it shall be as you wish, and you shall go wherever it pleases you. For God forbid that I should stand in the way of your wishes. What is more, I shall see to it that you do not leave this place without such a Court for company as may well befit one who is the wife of one king and the sister of another."

*Queen Morgan le Fay leaves the Court of King Arthur*.

And he did just as he said he would, for he sent Queen Morgan le Fay away from his Court with great honor and in high state. But the more patient King Arthur was with her and the more favor he showed her, the angrier Queen Morgan le Fay grew with him and the more she hated him.

So she made her way to a broad mouth of a river where it met the sea. There she sent away those whom the King had sent with her, and she took ship with her own Court in several vessels and sailed away to that enchanted island called Avalon, which was her home.

This island of Avalon was a very strange and wonderful land, such as was not to be seen anywhere else in all the world. For it was like a Paradise for beauty. It was covered all over with gardens of every kind of flower, mingled with groves of lovely trees, some bearing fruit and others all in bloom with blossoms. And besides these there were many terraces of lawn, and smooth slopes of grass lying all around the edges of the island and looking out over the sea from tall white walls of pure marble. And in the midst of these gardens and orchards and groves and lawns and terraces there stood a multitude of castles and towers, built up one above another, some as white as snow and others very gay with many colors.

And the greatest marvel of that wonderful island was this: in the midst of all those castles and towers stood a single tower built entirely of lodestone, the magnet-stone. And in that lay the great mystery of the place.

For the island floated on the surface of the water, and that tower of lodestone had such power that Avalon would float from place to place at the will of Queen Morgan le Fay. So sometimes it would be here and sometimes it would be there, just as that royal lady willed it.

Nor were there very many people who had seen that island. For at times it would be covered all over with a silvery mist of enchantment, so that no eyes could see it unless they were fairy eyes. But sometimes it had been seen, like a vision of Paradise. And then whoever saw it would hear happy voices sounding from its lawns and groves, very thin and clear because of the great distance (for no one ever came near Avalon unless Queen Morgan le Fay allowed it), and he would hear music so sweet that his very soul might grow faint with it. Then Avalon would suddenly vanish in a most marvelous way, and whoever had seen it would know that he was not likely ever to see it again.

Such was the island of Avalon. If you would like to read more about it, you will find a great deal in a certain book written in French and called "Ogier le Danois," which is to say, Ogier the Dane.

Queen Morgan le Fay loved this island a very great deal. And many people say that King Arthur is still alive in that place, lying there very peacefully and quietly while he waits for the appointed time when he will return to the world to set right all that is wrong in it. That is why I have told you about it here, with all these particulars.
