Chapter 2: How Sir Pelleas Defeated a Red Knight Named Sir Adresack and Freed Twenty-Two Prisoners from His Castle

[Figure bp: Sir Pelleas meets the sorrowful lady in Arroy — In a forest Pelleas, seen from behind, reaches up to a sorrowful lady seated on her horse, her squire beside her.]

Now, after that wonderful happening, they traveled on without stopping for a long while. They did not pause anywhere until, about an hour after the first hours of the morning, they came to a part of the forest where charcoal-burners were at work at their trade. Here Sir Pelleas ordered them to draw rein and rest for a while, and so they dismounted to rest and refresh themselves, just as he had ordered.

*A sorrowful lady and a squire come into the forest*.

Now as they sat there refreshing themselves with food and drink, there came suddenly out of the forest a sound of great weeping and loud crying, and almost at once a lady in a pitiful state rode out of the thickets into that open place, mounted on a spotted palfrey. Behind her rode a young squire dressed in the colors green and white and seated on a sorrel horse. He too seemed to be full of great sorrow, for he was all in disorder and very downcast in his face. The lady's face was all swollen and red with weeping, and her hair hung down on her shoulders with neither net nor band to hold it in place, and her clothes were badly torn by the brambles and much stained from traveling through the forest. And the young squire who rode behind her came with his head drooping and his clothes in the same sad disorder, his cloak dragging behind him, fastened to his shoulder by only a single lace.

Now when Sir Pelleas saw the lady and the squire in such a sorry state, he got up at once from where he sat, went straight to the lady, took her horse by the bridle, and stopped it where it was. The lady looked at him, but she did not see him, for she was altogether blinded by her grief and distress. Then Sir Pelleas said to her, "Lady, what is wrong, that you are grieving so terribly?" And she answered him, "Sir, it does not matter, for you cannot help me." "How do you know that?" said Sir Pelleas. "I fully intend to help you, if I can possibly do so."

Then the lady looked more closely at Sir Pelleas, and she made him out as though through a mist of sorrow. She saw that he was not dressed in armor, but only in holiday clothes of fine crimson cloth. At that she began weeping all over again, and very bitterly, for she thought that here was someone who could give her no help in her trouble. So she said, "Sir, you mean kindly, but how can you hope to help me, when you have neither weapons nor armor to aid you in taking up such a quarrel?" But Sir Pelleas said, "Lady, I cannot tell how I may help you until you tell me what your sorrow is. Yet I have good hope that I may serve you once I know what it is that has troubled your mind so." Then, still holding her horse by the bridle, he led the lady forward to the place where Parcenet still sat beside the cloth spread with the food they had been eating. When he had come there, he very gently persuaded the lady to get down from her horse. Then, just as gently, he made her sit down on the grass and eat some of the food. When she had done so, and had drunk some of the wine, she found herself greatly refreshed, and she began to take heart again. Then, seeing her so far recovered, Sir Pelleas asked her once more what her trouble was, and begged her to open her heart to him.

So, encouraged by his cheerful words, she told Sir Pelleas about the trouble that had brought her to such a state.

*The sorrowful lady tells her story*.

"Sir Knight," she said, "the place where I live is a long way from here. I came from there this morning with a very good knight named Sir Brandemere, who is my husband. We have been married only a little more than four weeks, so that until this morning our happiness was still entirely new to us. Now this morning Sir Brandemere wanted to take me out hunting at the break of day, and so we went out with a little hunting hound that my knight was wonderfully fond of. When we came to a certain place in the forest, a doe suddenly sprang up in front of us, and the hound chased after it at once, barking and baying furiously. Then my lord and I and this squire followed after it, with great high spirits and delight in the chase. Now when we had followed the doe and the hound for a long distance (the hound chasing the doe with a great passion of eagerness), we came to a place where we saw ahead of us a rushing stream of water, crossed by a long, narrow bridge. And we saw that on the other side of the stream there stood a strong castle with seven towers, and that the castle was built up on the rocks in such a way that the rocks and the castle looked altogether like one single rock.

"Now, as we came near that bridge, all of a sudden the portcullis of the castle was raised and the drawbridge was let down very suddenly and with a great noise, and out of the castle at once rode a knight dressed entirely in red. All the trappings and gear of his horse were red as well, and the spear he carried in his hand was of ash wood painted red. He came out in a very terrible way, and rode forward until in a moment he stood at the far end of that narrow bridge. Then he shouted aloud to Sir Brandemere, my husband, 'Where do you think you are going, Sir Knight?' And Sir Brandemere answered him, 'Sir, I want to cross this bridge, for my hound, which I love dearly, has crossed here chasing a doe.' Then that Red Knight cried out in a loud voice, 'Sir Knight, you set foot on this bridge only at your peril. For this bridge belongs to me, and whoever wants to cross it must first overthrow me, or else he may not cross.'

"Now my husband, Sir Brandemere, was dressed at that time only in light clothing, such as a man might wear for hunting or for hawking, except that on his head he wore a light steel cap wrapped with a scarf I had given him. Even so, he was so great of heart that he would not let any such challenge from that Red Knight go unanswered. So, telling me and this squire (whose name is Ponteferet) to stay on the near side of the bridge, he drew his sword and rode forward to the middle of the bridge, meaning to force his way across if he could. And when that Red Knight, who was dressed in full armor, saw what my husband meant to do, he threw aside his spear, drew his sword, and rode forward to meet my knight. So they met in the middle of the bridge, and when they met, that Red Knight raised himself in his stirrups and struck my husband, Sir Brandemere, on the crown of his steel cap with his sword. And I saw the blade of the Red Knight's sword cut through Sir Brandemere's cap and deep into his skull, so that the blood ran down over the knight's face in a great flood. Then Sir Brandemere fell straight down from his horse and lay there as though he were dead.

"Having overthrown him in this way, that Red Knight got down from his horse and lifted Sir Brandemere back onto the horse he had fallen from, so that he lay across the saddle. Then, taking both horses by the bridles, the Red Knight led them straight back across the bridge and into his castle. As soon as he had gone into the castle, the portcullis was closed behind him at once and the drawbridge was raised. He paid no attention at all either to me or to Ponteferet the squire, but went away leaving us without one word of comfort. And now I do not know whether my husband, Sir Brandemere, is alive or dead, or what has happened to him."

And as the lady spoke these words, the tears poured down her face again.

*Sir Pelleas takes up an adventure to help the sorrowful lady*.

Then Sir Pelleas was deeply moved with pity, and he said, "Lady, your case is truly a very sorrowful one, and I am greatly grieved for you. Indeed, I would gladly help you as far as it lies in my power. So, if you will lead me to this bridge and to that grim castle you speak of, I make you my vow that I will try my very best to find out where your good knight is, and what has happened to him."

"Sir," said the lady, "I am very grateful to you for your goodwill. Yet you cannot hope to succeed if you venture to take on so dangerous an adventure as that without either weapons or armor to defend yourself. For think how cruelly that Red Knight treated my husband, Sir Brandemere, with no regard at all for his lack of weapons or armor. So it is not likely that he will treat you any more courteously." And Parcenet too raised her voice loudly in support of the lady's words, begging Sir Pelleas not to be so unwise as to do what he had in mind. And Ponteferet the squire called out to Sir Pelleas in the same way, telling him he should not do this thing, or that at the very least he should arm himself before he set out on this adventure.

But to everything they said Sir Pelleas answered, "Do not hold me back from what I mean to do. For I tell you all that I have more than once taken on adventures even more dangerous than this, and yet I have escaped without any great harm to myself." And he would not listen to anything the lady or the damsel might say, but got up from that place and helped the lady and the damsel onto their palfreys. Then he mounted his own horse, and when the squire and the pages had mounted theirs, the whole party set off from that place at once.

*They come to the castle of the Red Knight*.

So they traveled a long way through the forest, with Ponteferet the squire telling them which way to go so as to bring them at last to the castle of the Red Knight. At last they came to a more open place in that wilderness, where a steep, bare hill rose in front of them. When they had climbed to the top of that hill, they saw below them a river, very wild and rushing. They also saw that the river was spanned by a bridge, very straight and narrow, and that on the far side of the bridge and the river there stood a very strong castle with seven tall towers. What is more, the castle and its towers were built up on the rocks, very lofty and high, so that it was hard to tell where the rocks ended and the walls began, and the towers and the walls looked altogether like one rock of stone.

Then Ponteferet the squire pointed with his finger and said, "Sir Knight, that is the castle of the Red Knight over there, and he carried Sir Brandemere into it after he had wounded him so terribly." Then Sir Pelleas said to the lady, "Lady, I will go at once and ask after your husband's welfare."

With that he set spurs to his horse and rode down the hill toward the bridge very boldly. And when he had come nearer to the bridge, suddenly the portcullis of the castle was raised and the drawbridge was let down with a great noise and clatter, and at once out of the castle rode a knight dressed all in red armor and red gear, and this knight came forward at great speed toward the head of the bridge. When Sir Pelleas saw him coming on so threateningly, he said to those who had followed him down the hill, "Stay where you are, and I will go forward and speak with this knight, and ask him about the injury he has done to Sir Brandemere." At this Ponteferet the squire said to him, "Wait, Sir Knight, you will be hurt." But Sir Pelleas said, "No, I will not be hurt."

So he went out onto the bridge very boldly, and when the Red Knight saw him coming, he said, "Ha! Who are you, who dare to come onto my bridge like this?"

Sir Pelleas answered him, "It does not matter who I am. But you are to know, you discourteous knight, that I have come to ask you where you have put that good knight Sir Brandemere, and to ask you why you treated him so cruelly a little while ago."

At this the Red Knight grew very full of rage. "Ha! Ha!" he cried fiercely, "you will soon find that out, to your great sorrow! For as I have treated him, so will I quickly treat you, and in a little while I will bring you to him. Then you may ask him whatever you please. But since you are unarmed and without armor, I would not want to do you any bodily harm. So I demand that you surrender yourself to me at once. Otherwise it will be very much to your pain and sorrow, if you force me to use violence to make you surrender."

Then Sir Pelleas said, "What! What! Would you really attack a knight who is entirely without weapons or armor, as I am?" And the Red Knight said, "Certainly I will, if you do not yield yourself to me at once."

"Then," said Pelleas, "you are not fit to be dealt with as a proven knight should be dealt with. So, if I fight you, your overthrow must be of a kind that would shame any belted knight who wears golden spurs."

*Sir Pelleas overthrows the Red Knight with a great stone*.

With that he looked around him for some weapon to suit his purpose, and he saw that a certain huge stone lay loose on the top of the bridge's wall. This stone was so big that five men of ordinary strength could hardly have lifted it. But Sir Pelleas lifted it out of its place with great ease, and, raising it in both hands, he ran quickly toward the Red Knight and flung the rock at him with tremendous force. The stone struck the Red Knight in the middle of his shield and drove the shield back against his chest with great violence. And the force of the blow drove the knight backward out of his saddle, so that he fell from his horse to the ground with a terrible crash and lay on the bridge as though he were altogether dead.

And when the people inside the castle who were looking out saw that blow, and when they saw the Red Knight overthrown, they raised their voices in loud wailing, so that the outcry was terrible to hear.

But Sir Pelleas ran as fast as he could to the fallen knight and set his knee on his chest. He unlaced the knight's helmet and lifted it off. And he saw that the knight's face was strong and handsome, and that he was not altogether dead.

So when Sir Pelleas saw that the Red Knight was not dead, and saw that he was about to get his breath back after the blow he had taken, he drew that knight's misericord from its sheath and set the point at his throat. So when the Red Knight came to from his swoon, he saw death before him, in the face of Sir Pelleas and in the point of the dagger.

When the Red Knight saw how near death was, he begged Sir Pelleas for mercy, saying, "Spare my life!" And Sir Pelleas said, "Who are you?" And the knight said, "My name is Sir Adresack, and men call me Adresack of the Seven Towers." Then Sir Pelleas said to him, "What have you done with Sir Brandemere, and how is that good knight faring?" And the Red Knight answered, "He is not so badly wounded as you think."

Now when Sir Brandemere's lady heard these words, she was filled with such joy that she clapped her hands together, crying out loud in thanksgiving.

But Sir Pelleas said, "Now tell me, Sir Adresack, do you have other prisoners at your castle besides that knight, Sir Brandemere?" Sir Adresack answered, "Sir Knight, I will tell you truly. There are twenty-one other prisoners in my castle besides him: that is, eighteen knights and squires of rank, and three ladies. For I have guarded this bridge for a long time, and everyone who has tried to cross it I have taken prisoner and held for ransom. And in that way I have gathered great wealth and won great estates."

*Sir Pelleas lays his commands on the Red Knight*.

Then Sir Pelleas said, "You are truly a wicked and discourteous knight, to treat travelers who come your way like this, and I would do well to kill you where you lie. But since you have begged me for mercy, I will grant it to you, though only with great shame to your knighthood. What is more, if I spare your life, there are several things you must do. First, you must go to Queen Guinevere at Camelot, and there you must tell her that the knight who left her unarmed has taken your armor from you and has armed himself with it to defend her honor. Second, you must confess your wrongs to King Arthur, just as you have confessed them to me, and you must beg his pardon for them, asking him in his mercy to spare your life. These are the things you must do."

To this Sir Adresack said, "Very well. I promise to do these things, if you will spare my life."

*Sir Pelleas puts on the armor of Sir Adresack*.

Then Sir Pelleas let him get up, and he came and stood before Sir Pelleas. And Sir Pelleas called Ponteferet the squire to him and said, "Take this knight's armor off his body and put it on mine, as you know how to do." And Ponteferet did as Sir Pelleas told him. He unarmed Sir Adresack and dressed Sir Pelleas in Sir Adresack's armor, while Sir Adresack stood ashamed before them all. Then Sir Pelleas said to him, "Now take me into your castle, so that I may free the prisoners you so wickedly hold there." And Sir Adresack said, "It shall be done as you command."

So they all went together to the castle and into it, and it was an exceedingly stately place. There they saw a great many servants and attendants, and these came at Sir Adresack's command and bowed down before Sir Pelleas. Then Sir Pelleas told Sir Adresack to send for the keeper of the dungeon, and Sir Adresack did so. And Sir Pelleas ordered the keeper to lead them to the dungeon, and the keeper bowed down before him in obedience.

Now when they came to that dungeon, they saw that it was a very lofty place and exceedingly strong. And there they found Sir Brandemere and the others Sir Adresack had spoken of.

*Sir Pelleas frees the prisoners*.

And when that sorrowful lady saw Sir Brandemere, she ran to him with loud cries of joy, and threw her arms around him and wept over him. And he held her and wept, and forgot his wound entirely in the joy of seeing her again.

In the various rooms of that part of the castle there were, in all, eighteen knights and squires and three ladies, besides Sir Brandemere. Among those knights were two from King Arthur's court: Sir Brandiles and Sir Mador de la Porte. And when these two saw that it was Sir Pelleas who had set them free, they came to him and embraced him with great joy and kissed him on both cheeks.

And all those who had been set free rejoiced greatly, and gave Sir Pelleas such praise and acclaim that he was well pleased with it.

Then, when Sir Pelleas saw all the prisoners who had been in that dungeon, he grew very angry with Sir Adresack, and he turned to him and said, "Go, Sir Knight, and do the penance I have laid on you. For I am very much displeased with you, and I am afraid I may come to regret the mercy I have shown you."

At that Sir Adresack turned away and left that place at once. He called his squire to him, took him along, and rode off to Camelot to do the penance he had promised Sir Pelleas to do.

After he had gone, Sir Pelleas and the prisoners he had freed went through the different parts of the castle. And there they found thirteen chests of gold and silver money and four caskets of jewels, very fine and very brilliant. All this treasure had been paid as ransom by prisoners who had been held there by force in times past.

Sir Pelleas ordered all those chests and caskets to be opened, and when the people there looked inside them, all their hearts leapt with joy at the sight of that great treasure.

*Sir Pelleas divides the treasures of Sir Adresack among the prisoners*.

Then Sir Pelleas ordered all that treasure of gold and silver to be divided into nineteen equal parts. When it had been divided, he said, "Now let each of you who has been held prisoner in this place take one part of this treasure for his own, to repay him for the sorrows he has suffered." What is more, to each of the ladies who had been held prisoner there he gave a casket of jewels, saying to her, "Take this casket of jewels to repay you for the sorrow you have suffered." And to Sir Brandemere's lady he gave a casket of the jewels for what she had suffered.

But when the people there saw that Sir Pelleas had kept no part of that great treasure for himself, they all cried out to him, "Sir Knight! Sir Knight! What is this? Look, you have set aside no part of this treasure for yourself."

Then Sir Pelleas answered, "You are right, I have not. For there is no need for me to take any of this gold and silver, or any of these jewels, for myself. You see, you have suffered much at the hands of Sir Adresack, and so you ought to be repaid for it. But I have suffered nothing at his hands, and so I need no such repayment."

Then they were all amazed at his generosity, and praised him highly for his greatness of heart. And all those knights swore to be faithful to him until death.

*They stay at the castle of Sir Brandemere*.

Then, when all these things were done, Sir Brandemere begged everyone there to come with him to his castle, so that they might refresh themselves with a time of merriment and good cheer. They all said they would go with him, and they did go. And at Sir Brandemere's castle there was great rejoicing, with feasting and jousting, for three days.

And everyone there loved Sir Pelleas with an astonishing love, because of that collar of emeralds and opals and gold. Yet no one knew of the magic power of that collar, and Sir Pelleas did not know of it either.

So Sir Pelleas stayed at that place for three days. And when the fourth day came, he got up early in the morning and ordered his horse to be saddled, and the palfrey of the damsel Parcenet, and the horses of their pages.

Then, when everyone there saw that he meant to leave, they begged him not to go. But Sir Pelleas said, "Do not hold me back, for I must go."

Then those two knights of Arthur's court, Sir Brandiles and Sir Mador de la Porte, came to him and begged him to let them go with him on that adventure. At first Sir Pelleas refused them, but they begged him all the more, so that at last he was glad to say, "You shall go with me."

So he left that place with his company, and all those who stayed behind were very sorry that he had gone away.
