Chapter 2: How King Rience Came to Cameliard, and How King Arthur Fought the Duke of North Umber

[Figure at: Two knights fight before Cameliard — Below the towers and walls of a hilltop town, two mounted knights charge at each other on a meadow.]

*King Rience and Duke Mordaunt come to Cameliard*.

Now one day about this time a messenger came to the Court of King Leodegrance with news that King Rience of North Wales and Duke Mordaunt of North Umber were on their way there, and that they were bringing with them a very noble and very large Court of knights and lords. King Leodegrance was much troubled in spirit at this news. He did not know what such a visit might mean, and yet he greatly feared that it meant trouble for him. So on the day that King Rience and the Duke of North Umber appeared before the castle, King Leodegrance went out to greet them, and the three of them met in the meadows that lie beneath the castle walls of Cameliard.

There King Leodegrance welcomed them in a fitting manner, and asked them to come into the castle with him so that he might entertain them as their rank deserved.

*The King and the Duke send a challenge to King Leodegrance*.

But King Rience did not trouble to give any pleasant answer to this courtesy of King Leodegrance's. "No," he said, "we will not go with you into your castle, King Leodegrance, until we learn whether you are our friend or our enemy. For right now, truly, we are not such good friends of yours that we care to sit down at your table and eat your salt. Nor can we be anything but your enemies until you have first satisfied our demands: that is, that you give me the lands I have demanded of you, and that you give my cousin, Duke Mordaunt of North Umber, the Lady Guinevere to be his wife. In these matters it is in your power to make us either your friends or your enemies. So we will stay here, outside your castle, for five days. In that time you may frame your answer, and so we shall know whether we are to be friends or enemies."

"And in the meantime," said Duke Mordaunt of North Umber, "I hold myself ready to fight for my right to the hand of the Lady Guinevere against any knight of your Court who cares to deny my just claim to her. And if there is no knight in all your Court who can stand against me in a bout of arms, then you yourself can hardly hope to defend yourself against the great army of knights that King Rience has gathered to bring against you, if you refuse us what we ask."

Then King Leodegrance was terribly downcast, for he feared those proud lords and did not know what to say in answer to them. So he turned and walked back into his castle, weighed down with great anxiety and sorrow. And King Rience and Duke Mordaunt and their Court of lords and knights pitched their pavilions in the meadows facing the castle, until the whole plain was covered with them. There they made their lodging with great rejoicing, with the sound of feasting and singing and merrymaking, for it was a very noble Court that King Rience had gathered around him.

*Duke Mordaunt rides before the castle*.

When the next morning came, Duke Mordaunt of North Umber rode out clad all in armor of proven strength. He rode up and down the field before the castle and shouted a great challenge to those within, daring any knight to come out and meet him in knightly combat. "Ho!" he cried. "What now, you Knights of Cameliard! Is there no one who will come out to meet me? How can you hope to stand against the Knights of North Wales, if you are afraid to meet one single knight from North Umber?" So he jeered at them in his pride, and no one dared to come out of Cameliard against him. For the Duke of North Umber was one of the most famous knights of his day, of enormous strength and great success in arms, and now, in these times of peace, there was no one in King Leodegrance's Court who was anywhere near able to face a warrior of such proven skill and valor. So no one took up the challenge that the Duke of North Umber gave to the Court of Cameliard. Meanwhile many people gathered on the walls of Cameliard and gazed down at that proud and haughty duke, all decked out in his splendid armor, and they were all grieved and ashamed that there was no one in that peaceful town to go out against him. And all the lords and knights of King Rience's Court came and stood in front of the King's pavilion, and laughed and clapped their hands and cheered Duke Mordaunt as he rode up and down before them. And the merrier they grew, the more shamefaced the people of Cameliard became. "Ho! Ho!" cried that proud Duke. "What now! Will no one come out to meet me? How then can you of Cameliard hope to face the King of North Wales and all his knightly army, when I am only one man of it?" And the people of Cameliard, gathered on the walls, listened to him in shame and sorrow.

Now all this while King Arthur was digging in the garden. Even so, he knew very well everything that was happening, and how the Duke of North Umber was riding so proudly up and down before the castle walls. And suddenly it came to him that he could not bear it any longer. So he laid aside his spade and slipped out secretly by a back way, and went up into the town.

Now there was in Cameliard a very rich merchant named Ralph of Cardiff, and the fame of his wealth and his high position had reached even King Arthur's ears at Caerleon. So it was to his house that King Arthur made his way.

*King Arthur seeks armor to do battle*.

And while he was in a narrow lane not far from the merchant's house, he took off his magic cap of disguise and took on something of his noble appearance once more, for now he meant to let those who looked at him see that he was a knight. So when he stood before the rich merchant in his private room, and the merchant looked up into his face, the merchant did not know what to think at seeing so noble a lord dressed all in frieze, the coarse woolen cloth of working people. For though King Arthur was a stranger to the good man, so that he did not know his face, the merchant could tell that he was no ordinary knight, but surely someone of high rank and authority, even though he was dressed in frieze.

Then King Arthur opened the front of his jerkin and showed the merchant the gold collar that hung around his neck. He also showed him that under the rough frieze coat he wore an undergarment of fine purple silk embroidered with gold. And then he showed the good man his own signet ring, and when the merchant saw it, he knew it for the ring of the King of Britain. So, seeing these signs of high and lordly authority, the merchant rose and stood before the King and took off his cap.

"Sir Merchant," said the King, "you should know that I am a stranger knight here in disguise. Even so, I can tell you that I am a very good friend to King Leodegrance and wish him very well. You surely know how the Duke of North Umber rides up and down continually before the King's castle, and challenges anyone inside to come out and fight him for the Lady Guinevere. Now I have a mind to try that combat myself, and I very much hope that I will succeed in upholding the honor of Cameliard and in bringing shame on its enemies.

"Sir Merchant, I know very well that you have several suits of noble armor in your treasury, for their fame has reached my ears even though I live a good distance from here. So I want you to equip me as well as you are able, so that I can go at once and try a bout of arms with that Duke of North Umber. And I pledge you my knightly word that you shall be fully paid for the best suit of armor you can let me have, and that within a very little while."

"My Lord," said Master Ralph, "I can see that you are no ordinary wandering knight, but someone of very high rank; so it is a great pleasure to carry out all your wishes. But even if you were other than you are, I would be entirely willing to equip you with armor, since you have a mind to ride out against that duke over there."

At this he rang a little silver bell that stood near him, and at its sound several attendants appeared at once. Into their hands he gave the King, telling them to do him extraordinary honor. So some of those attendants prepared a bath of warm water for the King, perfumed with ambergris and very pleasant to the body. And after he had bathed in it and been dried with soft linen towels, other attendants led him to a hall all hung with tapestries and embroideries, and there a noble feast had been spread ready for his refreshment. Here the lordly merchant himself waited on the King, serving him with meats of several kinds, all very delicate, and with fine white bread as well. And he poured him wines from many countries, some as red as rubies and others as yellow as gold. Indeed, the King had hardly ever enjoyed a better feast than the one that the merchant Ralph of Cardiff had spread for him.

*King Arthur is armed by Ralph of Cardiff*.

And after he had refreshed himself fully with eating, six pages came, richly dressed in fine azure silk, and they took the King to a very grand room and there dressed him in a suit of Spanish armor, most skillfully made and all inlaid with gold. Armor like it could hardly be found anywhere in the land. The jupon, the cloth tunic worn over the armor, and all the other trappings of the armor were of satin as white as milk. And the shield was white, with no coat of arms or emblem of any sort upon it. Then these attendants led the King into the courtyard, and there stood a noble war-horse, as white as milk. All the trappings of the horse were of milk-white cloth, with no emblem or ornament of any kind, and the bridle and the reins were studded all over with knobs of silver.

Then, after the attendants had helped King Arthur mount this horse, the lordly merchant came forward and gave him many words of good cheer, and the King said farewell to him and rode away, all shining in white and glittering in his fine armor, so that he looked like the full moon at harvest time.

And as he rode down the stony streets of the town, the people turned and gazed after him, for he made a very noble sight as he passed along the narrow way between the houses.

So King Arthur made his way to the postern gate of the castle, and when he reached it he dismounted and tied up his horse. Then he went straight into the garden, and finding an attendant there, he asked to speak with the Lady Guinevere at once. The attendant, amazed at his lordly bearing, went and delivered the message. And after a while the Lady Guinevere came, wondering greatly, and walked along a gallery with several of her damsels until she stood above the place where King Arthur was. And when King Arthur looked up and saw her above him, he loved her with all his heart. And he said to her: "Lady, I have a great wish to do you whatever honor I can. For I am going out now to fight that Duke of North Umber who rides up and down before this castle. And I hope, and truly believe, that I shall bring about his downfall. So I beg of you some token, such as a lady may give a knight to wear when he rides out to do her honor."

*The Lady Guinevere accepts King Arthur as her champion*.

Then the Lady Guinevere said: "Truly, Sir Knight, I wish I knew who you are. Yet, though I do not know, I am entirely willing to take you for my champion, as you offer. So, as for the token you speak of, if you will tell me what it is you want, I will gladly give it to you."

"If that is so, Lady," said King Arthur, "I would very much like to have the necklace you wear around your throat. For it seems to me that if I had it tied around my arm, I would find my courage greatly increased by it."

"Indeed, Sir Knight," said the Lady, "what you ask of me you shall certainly have." And as she spoke, she took from her long, smooth neck the necklace of pearls she was wearing, and dropped it down to King Arthur where he stood.

And King Arthur took the necklace and tied it around his arm, and thanked her warmly for it. Then he saluted the Lady Guinevere with very knightly grace, and she saluted him in return. And then he went straight away from that place, swelling with joy that the Lady Guinevere had shown him such favor.

Now word had gone around Cameliard that a knight was going out to fight the Duke of North Umber. So great crowds gathered on the walls, and King Leodegrance and the Lady Guinevere and the whole Court of the King came to the part of the castle walls that overlooked the meadow where the Duke of North Umber held the field. So huge a crowd was soon gathered that any knight might be encouraged to do his very best before such a multitude as looked down on that field.

Then suddenly the portcullis of the castle was raised and the drawbridge let down, and the White Champion rode out to the combat he had undertaken. And as he rode across that narrow bridge, the hoofs of his war-horse struck the planks with a noise like thunder. And when he came out into the sunlight, his armor suddenly blazed like lightning, and when the people saw him they shouted aloud.

Then, when the Duke of North Umber saw a knight all clad in white, he rode straight to him and spoke to him with words of knightly greeting. "Sir," he said, "I see that you wear no crest on your helmet and have no emblem of any sort on your shield, so I do not know who you are. Even so, I believe that you are a knight of good rank and proven courage, or else you would not have come here like this."

"Truly, Sir Knight," said King Arthur, "I am of a rank equal to your own. And as for my courage, I believe it has been proven in as many combats as yours has."

"Sir Knight," said the Duke of North Umber, "you speak with a very large spirit. Even so, you had better say whatever prayers you can, for I am about to throw you down from your saddle so hard that you will never get up again. That is how I have served better men than you can ever hope to be."

To this King Arthur answered very calmly: "That will be as Heaven wills, Sir Knight, and not as you will."

*King Arthur overthrows Duke Mordaunt*.

So each knight saluted the other and rode to his appointed place, and there each set his spear and his shield and made himself ready for the charge. Then a silence fell over everyone, so deep that a man could hear his own heart beating in the stillness. For a little while each knight sat like a statue made of iron. Then, suddenly, each shouted to his war-horse and drove his spurs into its flanks and launched himself forward from his place. And so they met in the middle of the course with a noise like a violent clap of thunder. And the spear of the Duke of North Umber burst into splinters right down to the handle. But King Arthur's spear did not break; it held, so that the Duke was hurled out of his saddle like a windmill, whirling through the air and striking the earth so hard that the ground shuddered beneath him. Indeed, he rolled over and over a full three times before he stopped falling.

Then all the people on the wall shouted with all their might, so that the noise of it was altogether astonishing; for they had hardly dared to hope that their champion would prove so extraordinarily strong and skillful.

Meanwhile, the men of King Rience's Court ran at once to the Duke of North Umber where he lay on the ground, and they quickly unlaced his helmet to give him air. At first they thought he was dead, and then they thought he was likely to die, for he lay without any life or motion at all. Nor did he come out of the faint in which he lay for a full two hours and more.

Now while the attendants were busy around Duke Mordaunt of North Umber, King Arthur sat on his horse very quietly, watching everything they did. Then, seeing that his enemy was not dead, he turned around and rode away from that place.

He did not go back into Cameliard at that time. For he judged that he was not yet entirely finished with these enemies to the peace of his realm, and so he meant not to return the horse and the armor to the merchant just yet, but to keep them a while for another occasion.

So he remembered how, on his way to Cameliard, he had passed through an arm of the forest where some woodcutters were at work felling trees. And, remembering that place, he thought he would go there and leave his horse and armor in the care of those simple country folk until he needed them again. So now he rode away into the countryside, leaving behind him the town and the castle and all the noise of shouting and rejoicing. And he did not once so much as turn his head to look back at the place where he had so violently overthrown his enemy.

And now you will soon hear of some pleasant and very joyous adventures that happened to him before he had accomplished all his purposes. For when a man is a king among men, as King Arthur was, he is of so calm and even a temper that neither victory nor defeat can make him either puffed up in his own opinion or so troubled in spirit that he is cast down into despair. So if you want to become like King Arthur, you will take all your triumphs as he took this victory. You will not be turned aside from your final purpose by the great applause that many people may give you, but you will first finish the work you have set yourself to do before you let yourself sit down at ease and enjoy the fruits of your victory.

Yes, he who is a true king of men will not say to himself, "Look! I am worthy to be crowned with laurels." Instead he will say to himself, "What more is there that I can do to make the world better because of my efforts?"
