Chapter 5: How Sir Gawain Met Sir Pelleas and Promised to Help Him with the Lady Ettard

[Figure bs: Sir Gawain dines with the Lady Ettard — A hooded figure in a black friar's robe stands in a doorway, watching Gawain and Ettard share a cup at a table.]

Now, after that wonderful lady had vanished from their sight in that way, the three knights stood for a little while utterly astonished, for they did not know whether to believe what their eyes had seen. Then, by and by, Sir Gawain spoke. "Truly," he said, "that was a very wonderful thing that happened to us, for never in all my life have I known so strange a miracle to take place. Now, it is very plain that some excellent adventure lies in what we have seen. So let us go down into that valley, for there we shall surely discover the meaning of what we have just now witnessed. For I swear that I have hardly ever seen so terribly powerful a knight as the one who just fought that battle down there, and I cannot in any way understand why, when he had so nearly won the victory over his enemies, he should have surrendered himself to them as he did."

And Sir Uwain and Sir Marhaus agreed that it would be well to go down and find out the meaning of what they had seen.

So the three of them and their attendants rode down into the valley.

And they rode on until they came to a certain grove of trees, and there they saw three handsome pavilions standing: the first pavilion of white cloth, the second of green cloth, and the third of scarlet cloth.

*The three knights meet the two*.

Now, as the three companions drew near to the pavilions, two knights came out to meet them. And when Sir Gawain and Sir Uwain saw the shields of the two, they knew at once that they were Sir Brandiles and Sir Mador de la Porte. And in the same way Sir Brandiles and Sir Mador de la Porte knew Sir Gawain and Sir Uwain, and each party was very much astonished to meet the other in so strange a place. So when they came together they greeted one another very joyfully and clasped hands with strong love and good fellowship.

Then Sir Gawain introduced Sir Marhaus to Sir Brandiles and Sir Mador de la Porte, and after that the five knights all went together into those three pavilions, talking all the while in great friendship and pleasure. And when they came into the pavilion of Sir Brandiles, they found a good meal laid out there of white bread and wine of excellent flavor.

Then after a while Sir Gawain said to Sir Brandiles and Sir Mador de la Porte, "Sirs, a little while ago we saw a very strange thing. As we stood together at the top of that hill and looked down into this plain, we saw a single knight dressed all in red armor fighting against ten knights. And that one knight in red armor fought the ten with such fury that he drove them all before him, though they were so many and he was only one. And truly I swear that I have hardly ever seen a knight show such great skill and strength in arms as he did. Yet when he had overcome all but two of those knights, and was well on the way to winning a clear victory, he suddenly gave himself up to the two of them and let them seize him and tie him up and drive him from the field with great indignity. Now, I beg you, tell me the meaning of what we saw, and who that knight was who fought so great a battle and yet surrendered so shamefully."

At this Sir Brandiles and Sir Mador de la Porte made no answer but looked away, for they did not know what to say. And when Sir Gawain saw that they were embarrassed, he began to wonder more than ever what it all meant. So he said, "What is this? Why don't you answer me? I order you to tell me what your looks mean, and who that red knight is!"

Then after a while Sir Mador de la Porte said, "I will not tell you, but you may come and see."

Then Sir Gawain began to think that perhaps there was something here that it would be better not to make public, and that he had better look further into the matter alone. So he said to the other knights, "Wait here a little while, sirs, and I will go with Sir Mador de la Porte."

*Sir Mador de la Porte brings Sir Gawain to Sir Pelleas*.

So Sir Gawain went with Sir Mador de la Porte, and Sir Mador led him to the white pavilion. And when they came there, Sir Mador drew aside the curtains of the pavilion and said, "Go in!" And Sir Gawain went in.

Now, when he came into the pavilion, he saw that a man sat on a couch of rushes covered with a blue cloth, and after a moment he saw that the man was Sir Pelleas. But Sir Pelleas did not see him at once. He sat with his head bowed, like someone completely overwhelmed by a great despair.

And when Sir Gawain saw who it was that sat on the couch, he was greatly amazed and cried out, "Ha! Is it you, Sir Pelleas? Is it you?"

But when Sir Pelleas heard Sir Gawain's voice, and saw who it was that spoke to him, he let out a terribly bitter cry. And he sprang to his feet and ran as far away as the walls of the pavilion would let him, and turned his face to the wall.

*Sir Gawain rebukes Sir Pelleas*.

Then, after a while, Sir Gawain spoke very sternly to Sir Pelleas. "Sir," he said, "I am astonished and deeply ashamed that a knight of King Arthur's royal court and of his Round Table should behave in so dishonorable a way as I saw you behave today. For it is hardly to be believed that a knight of your fame and nobility would let himself be captured and tied up by two unknown knights, as you let yourself be today. How could you bring yourself to submit to such indignity and insult? Now I demand that you explain this to me."

But Sir Pelleas was silent and would not answer. Then Sir Gawain cried out very fiercely, "Ha! Will you not answer me?" And Sir Pelleas shook his head.

Then Sir Gawain spoke again, still very fiercely. "Sir!" he said. "You shall answer me one way or another! Either you will tell me the meaning of your shameful conduct, or else you will fight me to the uttermost. For I will not allow you to bring such shame on King Arthur and his Round Table without defending the honor and good name of the King and of the Table myself. Once you and I were dear friends, but unless you clear yourself at once, I shall hold you in contempt and regard you as an enemy."

At this Sir Pelleas spoke like a man nearly out of his mind. "I will tell you everything," he said. Then he confessed it all to Sir Gawain, telling him everything that had happened since the time he left Queen Guinevere's May Court to set out on this adventure, and Sir Gawain listened to him in great amazement. And when Sir Pelleas had finished telling all that had happened to him, Sir Gawain said, "Truly, this is very strange. Indeed, I cannot understand how you came to be so tangled in the charms of this lady, unless she has bewitched you with some powerful enchantment."

To this Sir Pelleas said, "Yes, I believe I have been bewitched, for I am completely beside myself in this matter, and I cannot control my passion at all."

Then Sir Gawain thought for a long while, considering the matter very seriously, and by and by he said, "I have a plan, and it is this. I will go to the Lady Ettard myself and look carefully into this affair. And if I find that anyone has tangled you in enchantments, I will do my very utmost to punish that person with great suffering. For I will not have another enchanter deceive you, as one has already deceived Merlin the Wise."

Then Sir Pelleas said to Sir Gawain, "How will you manage this, so as to get into the presence of the Lady Ettard?"

*Sir Gawain makes a plan with Sir Pelleas*.

Sir Gawain replied, "That I will tell you. We two shall exchange armor, and I will go to the castle in your armor. When I get there, I shall say that I have beaten you in a fight and taken your armor away from you. Then perhaps they will let me into the castle to hear my story, and I shall have a chance to speak with her."

Then Sir Pelleas said, "Very well. It shall be as you decide."

So Sir Pelleas called for a squire, and Sir Gawain called for his squire, and the two of them took the armor off Sir Pelleas and dressed Sir Gawain in it. When they had done that, Sir Gawain mounted Sir Pelleas's horse and rode openly into the field where Sir Pelleas had paraded before.

Now it happened that the Lady Ettard was walking at that time on a terrace inside the castle walls, from which she could look down into that meadow. So when she saw a red knight parading in the meadow, she thought it was Sir Pelleas come back again, and at that she was annoyed and offended beyond all measure. So she said to those near her, "That knight annoys me so terribly that I am afraid I shall fall ill from vexation if he comes here many more times. I wish I knew how to rid myself of him. For only an hour ago I sent ten good knights against him, and he beat them all with great speed, to their great dishonor and mine."

So she beckoned to the Red Knight, and when he had come close to the castle walls, she said to him, "Sir Knight, why do you come here to torment me and insult me like this? Can't you understand that the more often you come to pester me this way, the more I hate you?"

Then Sir Gawain opened the visor of his helmet and showed his face, and the Lady Ettard saw that the Red Knight was not Sir Pelleas. And Sir Gawain said, "Lady, I am not the man you take me for, but someone else. For look: I have your enemy's armor on my body, and so you can see that I have beaten him. You may well suppose that I could hardly be wearing his armor unless I had taken it from him by force of arms. So you need not trouble yourself about him any longer."

Then the Lady Ettard could not help believing that this knight, whom she did not know, had really overthrown Sir Pelleas in a fight and taken his armor away from him. And indeed she was greatly astonished that such a thing could have happened, for it seemed to her that Sir Pelleas was one of the greatest knights in the world, and she wondered who this knight could be who had overthrown him in battle. So she commanded several of her attendants to go out and bring that red knight into the castle and to pay him great honor, since he must surely be one of the very greatest champions in the world.

*Sir Gawain enters Grantmesnle*.

So Sir Gawain came into the castle and was brought before the Lady Ettard, where she stood in a wonderfully large and noble hall. That hall was lit by seven tall windows of colored glass, and it was hung all around with tapestries and hangings, very rich and of the finest quality, so that Sir Gawain was greatly astonished at the splendor of everything he saw there.

Now, Sir Gawain had taken the helmet off his head, and he carried it under his arm against his hip. His head was bare, so that everyone there could see his face very plainly. And so they all saw that he was extremely handsome: his eyes were as blue as steel, his nose high and curved, and his hair and beard very dark and rich in color. What is more, he carried himself so steadily and so proudly that those who looked at him were awed by how very knightly he appeared.

Then the Lady Ettard came to Sir Gawain and gave him her hand, and he knelt down and raised it to his lips. And the lady spoke to him very graciously. "Sir Knight," she said, "it would give me a great deal of pleasure if you would tell us your name and let us know your rank and station."

To this Sir Gawain replied, "Lady, I cannot tell you these things at present, for I am bound by a vow to keep them secret just now. So I ask you to be patient with me for a little while."

Then the Lady Ettard said, "Sir Knight, it is a great pity that we may not know your name and rank. All the same, though we are still ignorant of who you are, I hope that you will give us the pleasure of your company for a while, and that you will be gracious enough to stay in this poor place of ours for two or three days, while we offer you whatever refreshment we can."

*The magical necklace enchants Sir Gawain*.

Now at this point a very unlucky thing happened, and it was this. The Lady Ettard had come to love that necklace of emeralds and opals and gold that she had borrowed from Sir Pelleas, and she loved it so much that she never let it out of her keeping, by day or by night. So at that very moment she was wearing it hung about her neck and throat. And as she talked to Sir Gawain, he looked at that necklace, and its enchantment began to take a very strong hold on him. For soon he began to feel as though his heart were being drawn with a tremendous longing out of his breast and toward the Lady Ettard, so much so that in a little while he could not take his eyes off her at all. And the more he looked at the necklace and at the lady, the more the enchantment of the jewel took hold of his spirits. And so, when the Lady Ettard spoke so graciously to him, he was very glad to accept her kindness. Gazing at her very ardently all the while, he said, "Lady, you are exceedingly kind to show me such great courtesy, and I shall be glad beyond measure to stay with you for a short while."

At these words the Lady Ettard was very greatly pleased, for she said to herself, "Surely this knight, though I do not know who he may be, must be a champion of extraordinary strength and of great deeds. Now if I can persuade him to stay in this castle as my champion, I shall certainly win great credit by it, for I shall have someone to defend my rights who must surely be the greatest knight in all the world." So she put forth every charm and every grace of manner to please Sir Gawain, and Sir Gawain was altogether delighted by the kindness of her ways.

Now Sir Engamore was there at the time, and he was very deeply troubled in spirit. For in the same degree that Sir Gawain received courtesy from the Lady Ettard, in that same degree Sir Engamore was cast down into great sorrow and distress, so much so that it was pitiful to see him. For Sir Engamore said to himself, "Before these foreign knights came here, the Lady Ettard was very kind to me, and was willing to take me for her champion and her lord. But first Sir Pelleas came and overthrew me, and now this strange knight comes and overthrows him. So, beside such a great champion as this one, I have become as nothing in her eyes." So Sir Engamore left that place and went to his own small private room, where he sat down alone in great sorrow.

Now the Lady Ettard had commanded that a very noble and splendid feast be prepared for Sir Gawain and herself, and while it was being made ready, she and Sir Gawain walked together in the pleasure garden of the castle. For there was very pleasant shade in that place, and flowers grew there in great abundance, and many birds sang very sweetly among the blossoms of the trees. And as Sir Gawain and the lady walked together in this way, the attendants stood a little distance off and watched them. And they said to one another, "Surely it would be a very good thing if the Lady Ettard would take this knight for her champion, and if he would stay here in Grantmesnle forever."

*Sir Gawain and the Lady Ettard feast together*.

So Sir Gawain and the lady walked together, talking very cheerfully, until sunset. By then the supper was ready, and they went in and sat down to it. And as they ate, a number of pages with very fair faces played on harps before them, and several damsels sang very sweetly along with the music, so that Sir Gawain's heart swelled with joy. And he said to himself, "Why should I ever leave this place? After all, I have been banished from King Arthur's court. Why then should I not set up a court of my own here, which might in time come to equal his in glory?" And the Lady Ettard was so beautiful in his eyes that this seemed to him a wonderfully pleasant thought.

Now let us turn to Sir Pelleas.

*Sir Pelleas is in doubt*.

For after Sir Gawain had left him, the heart of Sir Pelleas began to fear that he had not acted wisely, and at last he said to himself, "Suppose Sir Gawain should forget his duty to me when he meets the Lady Ettard? For it seems that perhaps she has some powerful charm that might easily draw Sir Gawain's heart toward her. And if Sir Gawain should come within the circle of an enchantment like that, he may forget his duty to me and may sin against the honor of his knighthood."

And the more Sir Pelleas thought about this, the more troubled he grew in his mind. So at last, when evening had fallen, he called a squire to him and said, "Go and bring me the robe of a black friar, for I want to go to the castle of Grantmesnle in disguise." So the squire went as he was commanded and brought him such a robe, the black hooded gown of the wandering preachers, and Sir Pelleas dressed himself in it.

By that time darkness had come down entirely over the face of the earth, so that no one could have known Sir Pelleas even if they had seen his face. So he went to the castle, and the people there, thinking that he was a black friar, as he appeared to be, let him into the castle by the postern gate.

*Sir Pelleas comes to the castle in disguise*.

As soon as Sir Pelleas was inside the castle, he began to ask very carefully where he might find the knight who had come there that afternoon. And the people of the castle, still thinking him a friar of the black order, said to him, "What do you want with that knight?" Sir Pelleas answered, "I have a message for him." The people of the castle said, "You cannot get to that knight just now, for he is at supper with the Lady Ettard, and he is keeping her company in pleasant conversation."

At this Sir Pelleas began to grow very angry, for he greatly disliked the thought that Sir Gawain should at that moment be making merry with the Lady Ettard. So he said, speaking very sternly, "I must speak with that knight at once, and so I order you to take me to him without delay." Then the people of the castle said, "Wait, and we will see whether that knight is willing to have you come to him."

So one of the attendants went to the place where Sir Gawain sat at supper with the Lady Ettard, and said, "Sir Knight, a black friar has come here who demands to speak with you at once. He will not be refused, but keeps on making that demand."

At this Sir Gawain was greatly troubled in his conscience, for he knew that he was not dealing honorably with Sir Pelleas, and he wondered whether this black friar might be a messenger from his friend. And yet he could not see how he could refuse to speak with such a messenger. So, after thinking for a while, he said, "Bring the black friar here, and let him deliver his message to me."

So Sir Pelleas, dressed as a black friar, was brought by the attendants into the outer room of the place where Sir Gawain sat at supper with the lady. But for a little while Sir Pelleas did not go into the room. He stood behind the curtain of the anteroom and watched them, for he wanted to make sure whether or not Sir Gawain was true to him.

Now everything in that room where the knight and the lady sat was decked out with extraordinary splendor, and it was lit by several score of wax candles that gave off a most delightful perfume as they burned. And as Sir Pelleas stood behind the curtains, he watched Sir Gawain and the Lady Ettard as they sat together at the table, and he saw that they took great pleasure in each other's company. And he saw that Sir Gawain and the lady drank wine out of the same goblet, and that the cup was of gold. And as he watched the two of them making merry together, he was filled with great anger and indignation, for now he saw that Sir Gawain had betrayed him.

So by and by he could hold himself back no longer, and he took five steps into the room and stood before Sir Gawain and the Lady Ettard. And when they looked up at him in great surprise, he threw back the hood from his face, and they knew him. Then the Lady Ettard shrieked loudly, crying out, "I have been betrayed!" And Sir Gawain sat altogether silent, for he had not a single word to say either to the lady or to Sir Pelleas.

*Sir Pelleas insults Sir Gawain*.

Then Sir Pelleas came close to the Lady Ettard with such a fierce face that she could not move for fear. And when he had come near her, he seized that necklace of emeralds and opals and gold so violently that he broke its clasp, and so he tore it from her neck. Then he said, "This is mine, and you have no right to it!" And with that he thrust it inside his tunic. Then he turned on Sir Gawain where he sat, and said, "You are false both to your knighthood and to your friendship, for you have betrayed me completely." And with that he raised his arm and struck Sir Gawain across the face with the back of his hand, so hard that the marks of his fingers were left in red all across Sir Gawain's cheek.

Then Sir Gawain turned as pale as ashes, and he cried out, "Sir, I have truly betrayed you, but you have insulted me so deeply that our injuries are equal." To this Sir Pelleas replied, "Not so. For the injury I gave you is only on your cheek, but the injury you gave me is on my heart. All the same, I will answer to you for the insult I have given you. But you also shall answer to me for the wrong you have done me in betraying me."

Then Sir Gawain said, "I am willing to answer to you in full measure." And Sir Pelleas said, "You shall indeed do so." With that he turned and left the place, and he did not so much as look again at either Sir Gawain or the Lady Ettard.

But now that the Lady Ettard no longer had the magic necklace about her neck, Sir Gawain felt nothing of the great enchantment that had drawn him to her so powerfully before. And so he now felt a dislike for her as strong as the liking that had drawn him to her before. And he said to himself, "How could I possibly have betrayed my knighthood like this for this lady, and done so much harm to my friend!" So he pushed back his chair very violently and rose from the table, meaning to leave her.

*Sir Gawain and the Lady Ettard speak bitterly together*.

But when the Lady Ettard saw what he meant to do, she spoke to him in very great anger, for she was deeply offended that he had deceived her when he said he had beaten Sir Pelleas. So she said hotly, "You may go, and I am very willing to have you do so, for you lied when you told me that you had beaten Sir Pelleas. For now I see that he is both a stronger and a nobler knight than you are. He struck you as though you were his servant, and you still carry the marks of his fingers on your cheek."

At this Sir Gawain was furiously angry, and filled through and through with shame at what had happened to him. So he said, "Lady, I think you have bewitched me, to bring me to such a pitch of dishonor. As for Sir Pelleas, look out into that meadow tomorrow, and see if I do not put a deeper mark on him than he has ever put on me." With that he left the room and went down into the courtyard, and called on the attendants there to bring him his horse. So they did as he commanded, and he rode out at once into the night.

And he was very glad of the darkness of the night, for it seemed to him that it was easier to bear his shame in the dark. So when he came to the grove of trees, he would not go into the pavilion where his friends were. And when Sir Uwain and Sir Marhaus came out to him and asked him to come in, he would not do it but stayed outside in the darkness. For he said to himself, "If I go in where there is a light, they may see the mark of Sir Pelleas's hand upon my face."

So he stayed outside in the darkness and told them to go away and leave him alone.

But when they had gone, he called his squire to him and said, "Take this red armor off me and carry it into the pavilion of Sir Pelleas, for I hate it." So the squire did as Sir Gawain commanded, and Sir Gawain walked up and down the whole night through, greatly troubled in spirit and in heart.
