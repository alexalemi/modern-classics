Chapter 3: How Sir Pelleas Fought Sir Engamore, the Knight of the Green Sleeves, and What Happened to the Lady Ettard

[Figure bq: Parcenet covers Sir Pelleas with a cloak — Below the castle wall the damsel Parcenet wraps a long cloak around Pelleas, who stands in his shirt.]

Now Sir Pelleas and his party, and the damsel Parcenet and hers, traveled on until some time in the afternoon they came to the farthest edge of the forest. There the woodlands ended altogether, and many fields and meadows lay spread out beneath the sky, with farms and small crofts and orchards of trees all in bloom with tender leaves and sweet-smelling blossoms.

And Sir Pelleas said, "This is truly a very beautiful land we have come into." At that the damsel Parcenet was very well pleased, and she said, "Sir, I am very glad that what you see pleases you, for all this country belongs to the Lady Ettard, and it is my home. What is more, from the top of that hill over there you can see the castle of Grantmesnle, which lies in the valley below." Then Sir Pelleas said, "Let us hurry! For I am wonderfully eager to see that place."

*They reach Grantmesnle*.

So they set spurs to their horses and rode up that hill at an easy gallop. And when they had reached the top of it, there below them lay the Castle of Grantmesnle, as plain to see as if it were held in the palm of a hand. Sir Pelleas saw that it was an exceedingly beautiful castle, built entirely of red stone on the outside, with many buildings of red brick inside the wall. Behind the walls lay a little town, and from where they stood they could see its streets, and the people coming and going about their business. So Sir Pelleas, seeing how fine that castle was, said, "Truly, maiden, that is a very fair estate over there."

"Yes," said Parcenet, "those of us who live there think it a very excellent estate."

Then Sir Pelleas said to Parcenet, "Maiden, that grove of young trees near the castle looks like a very pleasant spot. So my companions in arms and I will make our camp there. There, too, we will have three pavilions set up to shelter us by day and by night. In the meantime, I beg you to go to the lady your mistress and tell her that a knight has come to this place who, although he does not know her, holds that the Lady Guinevere of Camelot is the most beautiful lady in all the world. And I beg you to tell the lady that I am here to maintain that claim against all comers at the risk of my body. So if the lady has any champion who will fight on her behalf, I will meet him in that field over there tomorrow at midday, a little before I eat my midday meal. For at that time I mean to ride into that field and parade up and down there until my friends call me in to my dinner; and I will take my stand in that place in honor of the Lady Guinevere of Camelot."

"Sir Pelleas," said the damsel, "I will do just as you ask of me. And though I cannot wish that you will be the victor in that fight, yet truly I am sorry to leave you. For you are both a very brave and a very courteous knight, and I find that I feel a great friendship for you."

Then Sir Pelleas laughed and said, "Parcenet, you are bent on giving me praise far beyond what I deserve." And Parcenet said, "No, sir, for you deserve everything I can say to your credit."

Then the two of them took leave of one another with great goodwill and much kindness, and the maiden and the three pages went one way, while Sir Pelleas and his two companions and the attendants they had brought with them went into the grove of young trees, as Sir Pelleas had ordered.

*Sir Pelleas and his fellow knights make their camp in a grove of trees*.

There they set up three pavilions in the shade of the trees: one pavilion of fine white cloth, the second of green cloth, and the third of scarlet cloth. And over each pavilion they set a banner bearing the emblem of the knight to whom that pavilion belonged. Above the white pavilion was the emblem of Sir Pelleas, which was three swans with spread wings on a silver field. Above the red pavilion, which was Sir Brandiles's, was a red banner bearing his emblem, which was a hand in armor gripping a hammer. Above the green pavilion, which was Sir Mador de la Porte's, was a green banner bearing his emblem, which was a carrion crow holding a white lily in one claw and a sword in the other.

*Sir Pelleas challenges Sir Engamore*.

So when the next day came, and midday was near, Sir Pelleas rode out into that field in front of the castle as he had promised to do. He was dressed from head to foot in the red armor he had taken from the body of Sir Adresack, so that in that armor he looked very terrible. He rode up and down before the castle walls for a long while, crying in a loud voice, "Hear me! Hear me! Here stands a knight of King Arthur's court and of his Round Table, who declares, and is ready to prove it with his body, that the Lady Guinevere, King Arthur's Queen of Camelot, is the most beautiful lady in all the world, not excepting anyone at all. So if any knight holds otherwise, let him come out at once and defend his opinion with his body."

Now after Sir Pelleas had appeared in that meadow, a great stir arose inside the castle, and many people came up onto the walls and gazed down at Sir Pelleas as he paraded up and down that field. After some time had passed, the drawbridge of the castle was let down, and out rode a knight who was very huge of frame and exceedingly proud in his bearing. This knight was dressed from head to foot in green armor, and on each arm he wore a green sleeve, which was why he was sometimes called the Knight of the Green Sleeves.

So that Green Knight rode toward Sir Pelleas, and Sir Pelleas rode toward the Green Knight, and when they came together they saluted each other with a great deal of civility and knightly courtesy. Then the Green Knight said to Sir Pelleas, "Sir Knight, will you grant me the great favor of knowing your name?"

And Sir Pelleas answered, "That I will. I am Sir Pelleas, a knight of King Arthur's court and of his Round Table."

Then the Green Knight said, "Ha, Sir Pelleas, it is a great honor for me to deal with so famous a knight, for who is there in the courts of chivalry who has not heard of you? Now, if I have the good fortune to overthrow you, then all your honor will become my honor. And in return for your courtesy in telling me your name, I give you my name and title, which is Sir Engamore of Malverat, also known as the Knight of the Green Sleeves. And I may tell you further that I am the champion of the Lady Ettard of Grantmesnle, and that I have defended her claim to be the most beautiful lady without equal for eleven months, against all comers. If I defend it successfully for one month longer, then I am to win her hand and become lord of all this fair estate. So I am ready to do the very utmost in my power in her honor."

Then Sir Pelleas said, "Sir Knight, I thank you for your words of greeting, and I too will do my utmost in this encounter." At that the two knights saluted each other with their lances, and each rode to his appointed place.

*Sir Pelleas overthrows Sir Engamore*.

Now a great crowd of people had come down to the lower walls of the castle and of the town to watch the contest of arms that was about to take place, so it would be hard to imagine a worthier occasion for knights to meet in a glorious contest of friendly jousting. So each knight made himself ready in every way, and set his spear and lance with great care and caution. When everything had been made ready for that encounter, a herald, who had come out from the castle into the field, gave the signal for the charge. At once, in an instant, each knight drove his spurs into his horse, and they rushed at each other with such terrible speed that the ground shook and trembled under the pounding of their horses' hooves. They met exactly in the center of the field, each knight striking the other in the middle of his shield with a violence that was very terrible to see. And the spear of Sir Engamore burst into as many as thirty pieces, but the spear of Sir Pelleas held, so that the Green Knight was hurled out of his saddle so violently that he struck the ground more than a spear's length behind his horse's tail.

Now when the people standing on the walls saw how completely the Green Knight had been overthrown in that encounter, they raised a great outcry, for there was no other knight like Sir Engamore in all that country. And the Lady Ettard cried out loudest of all, for Sir Engamore was very dear to her, and when she saw him flung down so violently on the ground, she thought that perhaps he had been killed.

Then three squires ran to Sir Engamore, lifted him up, and unlaced his helmet to give him air. And they saw that he was not dead, but only in a deep swoon. After a while he opened his eyes, and at that Sir Pelleas was very glad, for it would have grieved him to have killed that knight. Now when Sir Engamore came back to his senses, he demanded very fiercely that he be allowed to go on with the fight against Sir Pelleas on foot and with swords. But Sir Pelleas would not have it. "No, Sir Engamore," he said, "I will not fight you over so serious a quarrel as that, for I have no such ill will against you." And at this refusal Sir Engamore burst into tears out of pure vexation and shame at being so completely overthrown.

Then Sir Brandiles and Sir Mador de la Porte came up and praised Sir Pelleas highly for the excellent way he had borne himself in the encounter, and at the same time they spoke kindly to Sir Engamore and tried to comfort him for the misfortune that had happened to him. But Sir Engamore took very little comfort from their words.

Now while they were all standing there together, the Lady Ettard came out from the castle with a very gay and handsome company of squires and ladies, and they came across the meadow toward where Sir Pelleas and the others stood.

When Sir Pelleas saw that lady coming, he drew his misericord and cut the laces of his helmet, and took the helmet off his head, and went forward bareheaded to meet her.

*Sir Pelleas greets the Lady Ettard courteously*.

But when he came near her, he saw that she was many times more beautiful than the picture of her painted on the ivory panel he had seen before, and his heart went out to her with a very great strength of liking. So he knelt down on the grass before her, and put his hands together palm to palm, and said, "Lady, I very humbly beg your forgiveness for having fought against your claim like this. For if I had not undertaken that fight for my Queen, I would rather have been your champion than the champion of any lady I have ever seen."

Now at that time Sir Pelleas wore around his neck the collar of emeralds and opals and gold that the Lady of the Lake had given him. So when the Lady Ettard looked at him, that necklace drew her heart to him with a very powerful enchantment. She smiled at Sir Pelleas very warmly, gave him her hand, and made him rise from where he knelt. And she said to him, "Sir Knight, you are a very famous warrior, for I do not suppose there is anyone who knows anything of chivalry who has not heard of the fame of Sir Pelleas, the Gentle Knight. So although my champion, Sir Engamore of Malverat, has overthrown all comers until now, he need not feel very much ashamed to have been overthrown by so tremendously strong a knight."

*Sir Pelleas and his fellow knights go to the castle of Grantmesnle*.

Sir Pelleas was very glad of the kind words the Lady Ettard spoke to him, and he presented Sir Brandiles and Sir Mador de la Porte to her. The Lady Ettard spoke very graciously to these knights as well, moved to it by the extraordinary liking she felt for Sir Pelleas. She begged those knights to come into the castle and refresh themselves with good cheer, and the knights said they would do so very soon. So each knight went back to his pavilion, and there each dressed himself in fine clothes and ornaments of gold and silver, in such a way that he was noble company for any court. Then those three knights went to the castle of Grantmesnle, and when they arrived there, everyone was astonished at how noble they looked.

But Sir Engamore, who had by now recovered from his fall, was very downcast, for he said to himself, "Who am I, beside these noble lords?" So he stood off to one side, very low in heart and heavy in spirit.

Then the Lady Ettard set out a very fine feast, and Sir Pelleas and Sir Brandiles and Sir Mador de la Porte were very glad of it. She seated Sir Pelleas at her right hand and Sir Engamore at her left. And Sir Engamore was even more downcast, for until now he had always sat at the Lady Ettard's right hand.

Now because Sir Pelleas wore that wonderful collar the Lady of the Lake had given him, the Lady Ettard could not keep her liking from him. So after they had refreshed themselves and had gone out into the castle's pleasure garden to walk in the warm sunshine, the lady wanted Sir Pelleas beside her all the time. And when it was time for those visiting knights to leave the castle, she begged Sir Pelleas to stay a while longer. Sir Pelleas was very glad to do so, for he was pleased beyond measure with the graciousness and the beauty of the Lady Ettard.

So after a while Sir Brandiles and Sir Mador de la Porte went back to their pavilions, and Sir Pelleas stayed on in the castle of Grantmesnle for a while longer.

*Sir Pelleas and the Lady Ettard feast together*.

That night the Lady Ettard had a supper prepared for herself and Sir Pelleas, and at that supper only she and Sir Pelleas sat at the table, while the damsel Parcenet waited on the lady. While they ate, some young pages and squires played very sweetly on harps, and some maidens who attended the lady's court sang so sweetly that it made the listener's heart swell to hear them. And Sir Pelleas was so enchanted by the sweetness of the music and by the beauty of the Lady Ettard that he did not know whether he was really on earth or in Paradise. So, out of his great happiness, he said to the Lady Ettard, "Lady, I wish I could do something for you, to show you how high is the regard and the honor in which I hold you."

Now all the while Sir Pelleas sat beside her, the Lady Ettard had kept her eyes on that wonderful collar of gold and emeralds and opals that hung around his neck, and she wanted that collar very badly. So now she said to Sir Pelleas, "Sir Knight, you can indeed do me a great favor, if you have a mind to." "What favor can I do you, Lady?" said Sir Pelleas. "Sir," said the Lady Ettard, "you can give me that collar that hangs around your neck."

At this Sir Pelleas's face fell, and he said, "Lady, I cannot do that, for that collar came to me in such an extraordinary way that I cannot part with it."

Then the Lady Ettard said, "Why can you not part with it, Sir Pelleas?"

So Sir Pelleas told her all about that extraordinary adventure with the Lady of the Lake, and how that fairy lady had given him the collar.

At this the Lady Ettard was greatly astonished, and she said, "Sir Pelleas, that is a very wonderful story. Even so, though you cannot give me that collar, you can at least let me wear it for a little while. For truly I am charmed by the beauty of that collar beyond anything I can tell, and so I beg you to let me wear it for a little."

*Sir Pelleas lets the Lady Ettard wear the collar*.

Then Sir Pelleas could refuse her no longer, and he said, "Lady, you shall have it to wear for a while." So he took the collar from around his neck and hung it around the neck of the Lady Ettard.

Then, after a little while, the magic power of that jewel left Sir Pelleas and passed into the Lady Ettard, and the Lady Ettard looked at Sir Pelleas with altogether different eyes from those she had looked at him with before. She said to herself, "Ha! What was wrong with me, that I was so enchanted with that knight, to the disgrace of my champion who has served me so faithfully? Has this knight not done me a grievous insult? Did he not come here for no other reason than that? Did he not overthrow my own true knight in scorn of me? Then what was wrong with me, that I showed him such favor as I have shown him?" But although she thought all this, she gave no sign of it to Sir Pelleas, but seemed to laugh and talk very cheerfully. All the same, she began at once to turn over in her mind some way to take revenge on Sir Pelleas. For she said to herself, "Look! Is he not my enemy, and is my enemy not now in my power? So why should I not take full revenge on him for everything he has done to us of Grantmesnle?"

*The Lady Ettard lays plans against Sir Pelleas*.

So after a while she made an excuse, got up, and left Sir Pelleas. She took Parcenet aside and said to the damsel Parcenet, "Go and bring me a powerful sleeping draught, right away." Then Parcenet said, "Lady, what are you going to do?" And the Lady Ettard said, "Never mind." And Parcenet said, "Would you give a sleeping draught to that noble knight?" And the lady said, "I would." Then Parcenet said, "Lady, that would surely be a wicked thing to do to someone who sits in peace at your table and eats your salt as your guest." To which the Lady Ettard said, "Don't you trouble yourself about that, girl, but go at once and do as I tell you."

Then Parcenet saw that it would not be wise for her to disobey the lady. So she went at once and did as she was told. She brought the sleeping draught to the lady in a cup of pure wine, and the Lady Ettard took the cup and said to Sir Pelleas, "Take this cup of wine, Sir Knight, and drink it to me, as deeply as the goodwill you bear me." Now Parcenet stood behind her lady's chair, and when Sir Pelleas took the cup, she frowned and shook her head at him. But Sir Pelleas did not see it, for he was intoxicated with the beauty of the Lady Ettard, and with the enchantment of the collar of emeralds and opals and gold that she now wore. So he said to her, "Lady, even if there were poison in that cup, I would still drink the wine in it at your command."

At that the Lady Ettard burst out laughing beyond all measure, and she said, "Sir Knight, there is no poison in that cup."

So Sir Pelleas took the cup and drank the wine, and he said, "Lady, what is this? The wine is bitter." And the Lady Ettard answered, "Sir, that cannot be."

*Sir Pelleas sleeps*.

Then in a little while Sir Pelleas's head grew extremely heavy, as though it were made of lead, so that he bowed his head down on the table where he sat. All the while the Lady Ettard sat watching him very strangely, and after a while she said, "Sir Knight, are you asleep?" Sir Pelleas made no answer, for the fumes of the sleeping draught had risen into his brain, and he slept.

Then the Lady Ettard got up laughing, and she clapped her hands and called her attendants. And she said to them, "Take this knight away and carry him into an inner room. When you have brought him there, strip him of his fine clothes and his ornaments, so that nothing is left on him but his undergarments. When you have done that, lay him on a cot and carry him out of the castle into the meadow below the walls where he overthrew Sir Engamore, so that when morning comes he will be a mockery and a joke to everyone who sees him. In this way we will humiliate him in the very field where he overthrew Sir Engamore, and his humiliation will be greater than Sir Engamore's has been."

Now when the damsel Parcenet heard this, she was deeply distressed, so that she went off by herself and wept for Sir Pelleas. But the others took Sir Pelleas and did to him just as the Lady Ettard had commanded.

When the next morning came, Sir Pelleas woke with the sun shining in his face. He had no idea at all where he was, for his brain was still fogged by the sleeping draught he had taken. So he said to himself, "Am I dreaming, or am I awake? For surely the last thing I remember is that I sat at supper with the Lady Ettard, and yet here I am in an open field, with the sun shining down on me."

So he raised himself on his elbow, and he saw that he was lying under the castle walls, near the postern gate. And above him, on the top of the wall, was a great crowd of people, and when they saw that he was awake, they laughed at him and mocked him. The Lady Ettard too gazed down at him from a window, and he saw that she was laughing at him and making merry. And then he saw that he lay there dressed only in his linen undershirt, and that his feet were bare, as though he were ready for bed at night. So he sat on the cot, saying to himself, "Surely this must be some shameful dream that is weighing on me." And he could not shake off his bewilderment at all.

*The damsel Parcenet brings help to Sir Pelleas*.

Now, as he sat there, the postern gate suddenly opened, and the damsel Parcenet came out of it. Her face was all wet with tears, and she carried in her hand a flame-colored cloak. She ran straight to Sir Pelleas and said, "You good and gentle knight, take this and wrap yourself in it."

At this Sir Pelleas knew that this was no dream, but a shameful truth, and he was seized with such an agony of shame that he began to tremble, and his teeth chattered as though he had a fever. Then he said to Parcenet, "Maiden, I thank you." And he could find no more words to say. So he took the cloak and wrapped himself in it.

Now when the people on the walls saw what Parcenet had done, they hooted at her and abused her with many hard words. So the maiden ran back into the castle, but Sir Pelleas got up and made his way toward his pavilion, wrapped in that cloak. And as he went, he staggered and tottered like a drunken man, for a great burden of shame lay upon him, almost more than he could carry.

*Sir Pelleas grieves greatly over his shame*.

So when Sir Pelleas reached his pavilion, he went in and threw himself face down on his couch and lay there without saying a word. After a while Sir Brandiles and Mador de la Porte heard of the plight Sir Pelleas had fallen into, and they hurried to where he lay and grieved greatly over him. They were also extremely angry at the shame that had been put upon him, and they said, "We will get help from Camelot, and we will break open that castle and bring the Lady Ettard here to beg your pardon for this insult. This we will do even if we have to drag her here by the hair of her head."

But Sir Pelleas did not lift his head; he only groaned and said, "Let it be, Sirs, for under no circumstances shall you do that, since she is a woman. As things are, I would defend her honor even if I died defending it. For I do not know whether I am bewitched or what it is that is wrong with me, but I love her with a very great passion, and I cannot tear my heart away from her."

At this Sir Brandiles and Sir Mador de la Porte were greatly astonished, and they said to each other, "Surely that lady has laid some powerful spell on him."

Then after a while Sir Pelleas told them to go away and leave him, and they did so, though not very willingly.

So Sir Pelleas lay there all that day until the afternoon came. Then he roused himself and told his squire to bring him his armor. When Sir Brandiles and Sir Mador de la Porte heard of this, they went to where he was and said, "Sir, what do you mean to do?" Sir Pelleas said, "I am going to try to make my way into the Lady Ettard's presence." Then they said, "What madness is this?" "I do not know," said Sir Pelleas, "but it seems to me that if I do not see the Lady Ettard and talk with her, I shall surely die of longing to see her." And they said, "Surely this is madness." And he answered, "I do not know whether it is madness, or whether I am caught in some enchantment."

So the squire brought Sir Pelleas his armor as he had ordered, and he dressed Sir Pelleas in it so that he was fully armed from head to foot. Then at once Sir Pelleas mounted his horse and rode out toward the castle of Grantmesnle.

Now when the Lady Ettard saw Sir Pelleas parading up and down the meadow below the castle again, she called six of her best knights to her and said to them, "Look, Sirs, there is that knight who brought so much shame on us yesterday. Now I order you to go out against him and punish him as he deserves."

So those six knights went and armed themselves, and when they had done so, they rode straight out against Sir Pelleas.

*Sir Pelleas overcomes six knights*.

Now when Sir Pelleas saw them coming, his heart overflowed with fury, and he shouted in a great voice and charged at them. For a while they stood up to him, but he could not be withstood, for he fought with a fury beyond anything, so that soon they broke away before him and fled. He chased them furiously all around that field and struck four of them down from their horses. Then, when only two of those knights were left, Sir Pelleas suddenly stopped fighting and called out to those two knights, "Sirs, I surrender myself to you."

*Sir Pelleas yields himself prisoner*.

At that those two knights were greatly astonished, for they were completely filled with fear of his strength, and they did not know why he should yield to them. All the same, they came and took hold of him and led him toward the castle. At this Sir Pelleas said to himself, "Now they will bring me to the Lady Ettard, and I shall speak with her." For it was for this reason that he had let himself be taken by those two knights.

But it was not to happen as Sir Pelleas wished. For when they had brought him close under the castle, the Lady Ettard called down to them from a window in the wall. She said, "What are you doing with that knight?" They said, "We are bringing him to you, Lady." At this she cried out very fiercely, "Do not bring him to me! Take him and tie his hands behind his back and tie his feet under his horse's belly, and send him back to his companions."

Then Sir Pelleas lifted his eyes to that window and cried out in a great passion of despair, "Lady, it was to you I surrendered, and not to these unworthy knights."

But the Lady Ettard cried out all the more fiercely, "Drive him away, for I hate the sight of him."

*The Lady Ettard puts Sir Pelleas to shame*.

So those two knights did as the Lady Ettard said: they took Sir Pelleas and bound him hand and foot upon his horse. And when they had done that, they let his horse carry him back to his companions in that state.

Now when Sir Brandiles and Sir Mador de la Porte saw Sir Pelleas coming back to them with his hands tied behind his back and his feet tied under his horse's belly, they were utterly filled with grief and despair. So they loosed the cords from his hands and feet, and they cried out to Sir Pelleas, "Sir Knight, Sir Knight, are you not ashamed to put up with such disgrace as this?" And Sir Pelleas shook and trembled as though with a fever, and he cried out in great despair, "I do not care what happens to me!" They said, "Not to yourself, Sir Knight, but think what shame you bring on King Arthur and his Round Table!" At this Sir Pelleas cried aloud in a great and terrible voice, "I do not care about them, either."

All this happened because of the powerful enchantment of the collar of emeralds and opals and gold that Sir Pelleas had given to the Lady Ettard, and that she wore all the time. For it was beyond the power of any man to resist the enchantment of that collar. And so it was that Sir Pelleas was bewitched and brought to that great depth of shame.
