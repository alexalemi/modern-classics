Chapter 1: How Queen Guinevere Went Maying, and How Sir Pelleas Took On a Quest for Her

[Figure bn: Sir Pelleas, the Gentle Knight — The young knight stands in a coat patterned with swans, holding his helmet under his arm, beside a swan crest with spread wings.]

[Figure bo: Headpiece to the Story of Sir Pelleas: a knight in armor sits his horse on a rocky shore, looking out over still water toward a distant island.]

*Queen Guinevere goes Maying*.

Now it happened one pleasant day in the springtime that Queen Guinevere went Maying, out into the fields to welcome the spring, with a fine company of the knights and ladies of her Court. Among those knights were Sir Pelleas, and Sir Geraint, and Sir Dinadan, and Sir Aglovale, and Sir Agravain, and Sir Constantine of Cornwall, and various others, so that hardly such another Court was to be found in all the world, then or before or since.

The day was wonderfully pleasant. The sunlight was all yellow, like gold, and the breeze was soft and gentle. The little birds sang with very great joy, and everywhere there bloomed so many flowers of every kind that the meadows were all carpeted with their tender green. So it seemed to Queen Guinevere that it was very good to be out in the fields and under the open sky at such a season.

*A damsel comes to the May party*.

Now as the Queen and her Court walked joyfully among the blossoms, one of the damsels who waited on the Lady Guinevere suddenly cried out, "Look! Look! Who is that coming over there?" At that Queen Guinevere raised her eyes, and she saw a damsel riding across the meadows on a milk-white palfrey, with three pages dressed in sky-blue. The damsel too was dressed entirely in blue. She wore a finely made chain of gold around her neck and a band of gold around her forehead, and her hair, which was as yellow as gold, was wrapped all about with ribbons of blue embroidered with gold. And one of the pages who followed the damsel carried a square frame, not very large, which was wrapped and covered with a curtain of crimson satin.

When the Queen saw that fine company approaching, she told one of the knights who attended her to go out and meet the damsel. And the knight who went out at her command was Sir Pelleas.

*Sir Pelleas talks with the damsel*.

So when Sir Pelleas met the damsel and her three pages, he spoke to her like this: "Fair damsel, I am commanded by the lady over there to greet you and to ask you the favor of your name and your errand."

"Sir Knight," said the damsel, "I can see from your face and your bearing that you are some lord of very high rank and great nobility, so I will gladly tell you that my name is Parcenet. I am a damsel belonging to the Court of a certain very great lady who lives a long way from here, and who is called the Lady Ettard of Grantmesnle. I have come here hoping to be brought before Queen Guinevere. So if you can tell me where I may find that noble lady, I shall certainly be very much in your debt."

"Ha, Lady!" said Sir Pelleas. "You will not have very far to go to find Queen Guinevere, for there she walks, over there, surrounded by her Court of lords and ladies." Then the damsel said, "Please, take me to her."

So Sir Pelleas brought Parcenet to the Queen, and Queen Guinevere received her very graciously, saying, "Damsel, what is it you want of us?"

*The damsel tells Queen Guinevere about the Lady Ettard*.

"Lady," said the damsel, "I will tell you that very readily. The Lady Ettard, my mistress, is thought by everyone in the country where she lives to be the most beautiful lady in the world. Now, lately, such reports have come to us of your great beauty that the Lady Ettard has seen fit to send me here to find out with my own eyes whether what is said of you is really true. And indeed, Lady, now that I stand before you, I must say that you are the fairest lady my eyes have ever seen, unless it is the Lady Ettard herself."

At that Queen Guinevere laughed with great merriment. And she said, "It seems to me a very funny business that you should have traveled so far for so small a matter." Then she said, "Tell me, damsel, what is it that your page carries so carefully wrapped up in that curtain of crimson satin?"

"Lady," said the damsel, "it is a true and perfect likeness of the Lady Ettard, my mistress."

Then Queen Guinevere said, "Show it to me."

*The damsel shows the Lady Ettard's picture to the Queen and her Court*.

At this the page who carried the picture got down from his palfrey, and, coming to Queen Guinevere, he knelt on one knee and uncovered the picture so that the Queen and her Court could look at it. Then they all saw that the picture was very skillfully painted on a panel of ivory, framed in gold and set with many jewels of different colors. And they saw that it was the picture of a lady of such extraordinary beauty that everyone who looked at it marveled. "Why, damsel!" said Queen Guinevere, "your lady is indeed blessed with wonderful beauty. If she truly looks like that picture, then I believe her equal in loveliness is not to be found anywhere in the world."

At this Sir Pelleas spoke up and said, "Not so, Lady. For I declare, and I am ready to defend my words at the risk of my life, that you yourself are far more beautiful than that picture."

"Well, well, Sir Knight!" said the damsel Parcenet. "It is a good thing you say so this far away from Grantmesnle. For at that place there is a certain knight named Sir Engamore of Malverat, who is a very strong knight indeed, and who upholds the opposite of what you say, in favor of the Lady Ettard, against all comers who dare to fight him."

*Sir Pelleas takes on the adventure in honor of the Queen*.

Then Sir Pelleas knelt down before Queen Guinevere and put his palms together. "Lady," he said, "I beg you of your grace to honor me so far as to accept me as your true knight in this matter. For I would gladly attempt an adventure on your behalf, if I have your permission to do so. So, if you give me leave, I will go out at once to meet this knight the damsel speaks of, and I very much hope that when I find him I shall overthrow him, to the increase of your glory and honor."

Then Queen Guinevere laughed again out of pure merriment. "Sir," she said, "it pleases me beyond measure that you should take so small a quarrel as this on yourself for my sake. For if you take up so small a quarrel, how much more readily would you take up a serious quarrel of mine! So I accept you very joyfully as my champion in this affair. Now go at once and arm yourself in whatever way is fitting for this adventure."

"Lady," said Sir Pelleas, "if I have your leave, I will go into this affair dressed just as I am. For I hope to win armor and equipment for myself along the way, and in that case this adventure will be still more to your credit than it would otherwise be."

The Queen was very much pleased at this, that her knight should undertake so serious an adventure dressed only in his holiday clothes, and so she said, "Let it be as you wish." Then she told her page, Florian, to go and fetch the best horse he could find for Sir Pelleas. And Florian, running with all speed, soon came back with a noble steed, so black that I believe there was not a single white hair on him.

Then Sir Pelleas said farewell to Queen Guinevere and her merry May Court, and they bade him farewell with great cheering. And then he mounted his horse and rode away with the damsel Parcenet and the three pages dressed in blue.

When they had gone some distance, the damsel Parcenet said, "Sir, I do not know your name or your rank, or who you are."

To this Sir Pelleas said, "Damsel, my name is Pelleas, and I am a knight of King Arthur's Round Table."

*Sir Pelleas and Parcenet talk together*.

At that Parcenet was very much astonished, for Sir Pelleas was thought by many to be the best knight-at-arms alive, except only King Arthur and King Pellinore. So she cried out, "Sir, it will certainly be a very great honor for Sir Engamore to fight with so famous a knight as you." To this Sir Pelleas said, "Damsel, I think there are several knights of King Arthur's Round Table who are better knights than I am." But Parcenet said, "I cannot believe that is so."

Then after a while Parcenet said to Sir Pelleas, "Sir, how will you get yourself armor to fight Sir Engamore?" "Maiden," said Sir Pelleas, "I do not know just now where I shall find armor. But before the time comes for me to fight Sir Engamore, I have faith that I shall find armor fit for my purpose. For you must understand that it is not always the armor a man wears on his body that brings him success. More often it is the spirit that lifts him up to his undertakings."

Then Parcenet said, "Sir Pelleas, I do not believe it often happens that a lady has so good a knight as you to fight on her behalf." To which Sir Pelleas said very cheerfully, "Damsel, when your time comes, I hope you will have a much better knight than I am to serve you." "Sir," said Parcenet, "nothing like that is likely to happen to me." At this Sir Pelleas laughed with a very light heart. Then Parcenet said, "Heigh-ho! I wish I had a good knight to serve me."

To this Sir Pelleas answered very gravely, "Maiden, the first one I catch I will give to you for your very own. Now, would you like him fair or dark, short or tall? For if you would rather have him short and fair, I will let the tall dark one go; but if you would rather have him tall and dark, I will let the other sort go."

Then Parcenet looked very steadily at Sir Pelleas, and she said, "I would like him about as tall as you are, with the same color of hair and eyes, and with a straight nose like yours, and with a good wit such as you have."

"Alas!" said Sir Pelleas. "I wish you had told me this before we came so far from Camelot, for I could easily have found you such a knight there. They have them in such plenty at that place that they keep them in wicker cages and sell them two for a penny." At this Parcenet laughed very cheerfully and said, "Then Camelot must be a very wonderful place, Sir Pelleas."

So, talking merrily, they traveled on their way with great joy and contentment, taking much pleasure in the springtime and in the pleasant meadows they rode through, without a care of any sort, their hearts full of cheerfulness and goodwill.

That night they stayed at a very quaint and pleasant inn that stood at the edge of the Forest of Usk. And the next morning they set out early, in the freshness of the new day, leaving that place and riding into the shadows of the forest.

*Sir Pelleas and Parcenet come to the Forest of Adventure*.

Now, after they had traveled a good distance in that forest, the damsel Parcenet said to Sir Pelleas, "Sir, do you know what part of the woods this is?" "No," said Sir Pelleas. "Well," said Parcenet, "this part of the woodland is sometimes called Arroy, and sometimes the Forest of Adventure. For I must tell you it is a very wonderful place, full of magic of many kinds. It is said that no knight may enter this forest without some adventure happening to him."

"Damsel," said Sir Pelleas, "what you tell me is very good news. For if we should happen on some adventure here, perhaps I may be able to get armor suitable for my purpose."

So they went straight into the Forest of Adventure, and they traveled through it for a long way, marveling greatly at the look of the place they had come into. For the forest was very dark and silent and wonderfully strange, and altogether different from any other place they had ever seen. So it seemed to them that it would not be at all surprising if some extraordinary adventure should happen to them.

*They find an old woman beside the stream*.

After they had traveled like this for a good while, they suddenly came out of the thicker part of the woods into a wide open clearing. And there in front of them they saw a violent stream of water, which rushed along very wildly with a great roar of many noises. And they saw that beside the stream there was a thorn tree, and under the thorn tree a bank of green moss, and on the bank of moss there sat an aged woman who looked very miserable. For that old woman was extraordinarily withered with age. Her eyes were all red, as though they watered with endless weeping, many bristles grew on her cheeks and chin, and her face was covered with so many wrinkles that there was no part of it free of them.

When the old woman saw Sir Pelleas and Parcenet and the three pages coming toward the place where she sat, she called out in a loud voice, "Sir, will you not carry me over this water on your horse? For you see, I am very old and feeble, and I cannot cross this river by myself."

Then Parcenet scolded the old woman, saying, "Hush, be quiet! Who are you, to ask this noble knight to do you such a service as that?"

*Sir Pelleas carries the old woman across the water*.

But Sir Pelleas was not pleased with Parcenet, and he said, "Damsel, you do not speak rightly in this matter. What becomes a true knight is to give help to anyone at all who needs it. For King Arthur is the perfect mirror of knighthood, and he has taught his knights to help all who ask them for help, whatever their rank may be." With these words Sir Pelleas got down from his horse and lifted the old woman up onto the saddle. Then he mounted again himself and rode straight into the ford of the river, and so brought the old woman safely across the torrent to the other side. And Parcenet followed him, marveling greatly at his knightliness, and the three pages followed her.

*Of the wonderful Lady of the Lake*.

When they had reached the other side of the water, Sir Pelleas got down, meaning to help the old woman off the horse. But she did not wait for his help. Instead she leaped down very lightly at once from where she sat. And suddenly Sir Pelleas saw that she whom he had taken for an aged and withered old crone was really a very strange and wonderful lady of extraordinary beauty. And, marveling greatly, he saw that she was dressed in clothing of a kind that neither he nor anyone else there had ever seen before. Because of the way she looked he knew that she was no ordinary mortal, but surely a creature of enchantment. For he saw that her face was wonderfully clear and as white as ivory, and that her eyes were very black and extraordinarily bright, like two jewels set in ivory. He saw that she was dressed all in green from head to foot, and that her hair was long and perfectly black, as soft and glossy as fine silk. And he saw that she wore around her neck a collar of opals and emeralds set in gold, and that around her wrists were bracelets of finely worked gold set with opals and emeralds. And from all these things he knew that she must be one of the fairy folk.

(For this was how the Lady Nimue of the Lake looked; this was how she had appeared to King Arthur, and this was how she appeared now to Sir Pelleas and those who were with him.)

So, seeing the wonderful magic of that lady, Sir Pelleas knelt down before her and put his hands together, palm to palm. But the Lady of the Lake said, "Sir, why do you kneel to me?" "Lady," said Sir Pelleas, "because you are so wonderfully strange and beautiful." "Sir," said the Lady of the Lake, "you have done me a very good service, and you are certainly a most excellent knight. So rise, and kneel no longer!" So Sir Pelleas got up from his knees and stood before her, and he said, "Lady, who are you?" And she answered, "I am one who feels a very great kindness toward King Arthur and all his knights. My name is Nimue, and I am the chief of those Ladies of the Lake you may have heard tell of. I took on the form of a wretched old woman to test your knightliness, and, as you see, I have not found you lacking in true service." Then Sir Pelleas said, "Lady, you have certainly done me a great favor in this." At that the Lady of the Lake smiled at Sir Pelleas very kindly, and she said, "Sir, I have a mind to do you a greater favor than that."

*The Lady of the Lake gives Sir Pelleas the collar of gold and jewels*.

And with these words she at once took from around her neck that collar of opals and emeralds and gold, and hung it around the shoulders of Sir Pelleas, so that it lay on his breast in a wonderful glory of shimmering colors.

"Keep this," she said, "for it has very powerful magic."

And with that she vanished instantly from the sight of everyone there, leaving them astonished and amazed beyond measure at what had happened.

And Sir Pelleas was like a man in a dream, for he did not know whether what he had seen was a vision, or whether he had seen it with his waking eyes. So he mounted his horse in complete silence, as though he did not know what he was doing, and in complete silence he led the way from that place. Nor did any of the others speak just then. Only after they had gone a good distance did Parcenet say, in a frightened voice, "Sir, that was a very wonderful thing that happened to us." And Sir Pelleas said, "Yes, maiden."

Now the necklace that the Lady of the Lake had hung around Sir Pelleas's neck had this power: whoever wore it was loved by everyone who looked at him. For the collar was enchanted with that particular power. But Sir Pelleas knew nothing at all about this, and so he simply took joy in the rare beauty of the jewel the Lady of the Lake had given him.
