Prologue

[Figure ad: The ornamented title of The Book of King Arthur, in an arched frame hung with ribbons, garlands and carved figures.]

[Figure ae: Uther Pendragon — The old High King sits grim-faced in a helmet with a crown, holding a scepter, his cloak marked with a dragon.]

[Figure af: Headpiece to the Prologue: a king in a fleur-de-lis mantle, a lady, and a man with a staff stand before a castle and an embroidered hanging.]

Long ago there lived a very noble king named Uther Pendragon, and he became High King over all of Britain. Two men helped him greatly to win the high kingship of the realm, and they stood by him in everything he did. The first of these was a very powerful enchanter, who was sometimes a prophet as well, known to men as Merlin the Wise; and he gave Uther Pendragon very good advice. The second was an excellent and famous knight named Ulfius, whom many people thought the greatest leader in war of any man then alive; and he gave Uther Pendragon help and counsel in battle. So, with the help of Merlin and Sir Ulfius, Uther Pendragon was able to overcome all his enemies and become king of the whole realm.

*Uther Pendragon takes the Lady Igraine for his wife*.

After Uther Pendragon had ruled his kingdom for a number of years, he married a beautiful and gentle lady named Igraine. This noble lady was the widow of Gerlois, the Duke of Tintagil, and by that prince she had two daughters, one named Margaise and the other Morgan le Fay. And Morgan le Fay was a famous sorceress. The Queen brought these daughters with her to the court of Uther Pendragon after she married that mighty king, and there Margaise was married to King Uriens of Gore, and Morgan le Fay was married to King Lot of Orkney.

*Of the birth of the young child, and the dangers around him*.

Now after a while Uther Pendragon and Queen Igraine had a son, and he was a very beautiful baby, large and strong of bone. And while the child still lay wrapped in his swaddling clothes, in a cradle of gold and deep sea-blue, Merlin came to Uther Pendragon with the spirit of prophecy strong upon him (as it often was), and, speaking in that spirit, he said: "Lord, it has been given to me to see that you will soon fall sick of a fever, and that you may die of a violent sweat that will follow it. Now, if such a grievous thing should happen to us all, this young child, who is truly the hope of this whole realm, will be in great danger of his life. For many enemies will surely rise up and try to seize him for the sake of his inheritance, and either he will be killed or he will be held prisoner, with little hope of ever escaping. So I beg you, Lord, to let Sir Ulfius and me carry the child away at once to some safe place of refuge, where he can be hidden in secret until he grows to manhood and can defend himself against whatever dangers may threaten him."

When Merlin had finished speaking, Uther Pendragon answered him with a very steady face: "Merlin, as far as my death is concerned, when my time comes to die I believe God will give me the grace to meet my end with a cheerful heart. For truly, my lot in that is no different from that of any other man ever born of woman. But as for this young child, if your prophecy is true, then his danger is very great, and it would be well to carry him away to some safe shelter, as you advise. So I ask you to do as you think best in this matter, and to keep in your heart that this child is the most precious thing I shall leave behind me to this land."

*King Uther dies, as Merlin foretold*.

All this, as I have said, Uther Pendragon spoke with great calm and steadiness of spirit. And Merlin did as he had advised: he and Sir Ulfius carried the child away by night, and no one but the two of them knew where the baby had been taken. And soon afterward Uther Pendragon was seized with the sickness Merlin had foretold, and he died exactly as Merlin had feared he would. So it was a very good thing that the child had been taken to a place of safety.

And after Uther Pendragon had departed this life, things went just as Merlin had feared, for the whole realm fell into great disorder. Every lesser king fought against his neighbor to be high king, and wicked knights and barons prowled the highways as they pleased and cruelly forced helpless travelers to pay them toll. Some travelers they took prisoner and held for ransom, and others they killed because they had no ransom to pay. So it was a very common sight to see a dead man lying by the roadside, if you ventured to make a journey on some business or other. And so, after a while, that whole sorrowful land groaned under the trouble that lay upon it.

*The Archbishop of Canterbury takes counsel with Merlin*.

Nearly eighteen years passed in this great misery, and then one day the Archbishop of Canterbury sent for Merlin and spoke to him like this: "Merlin, men say that you are the wisest man in all the world. Can you not find some way to heal the troubles of this unhappy realm? Turn your wisdom to this matter, and choose a king who will be a fit high king for us, so that we may live in happiness once more, as we did in the days of Uther Pendragon."

Then Merlin raised his face to the Archbishop and said: "My lord, the spirit of prophecy that sometimes comes upon me moves me now to tell you that I can see this country will soon have a king who will be wiser and greater and more worthy of praise than even Uther Pendragon was. And he will bring order and peace where there is now disorder and war. What is more, I can tell you that this king will be of Uther Pendragon's own royal blood."

To this the Archbishop said: "What you tell me, Merlin, is a wonderfully strange thing. But in this spirit of prophecy can you not foretell when this king is to come? And can you tell us how we shall know him when he appears among us? For there are many lesser kings who would gladly be high king of this land, and many of them think themselves fit to rule over all the others. How then are we to tell the real king from those who may proclaim themselves the rightful king?"

"My lord Archbishop," said Merlin, "if I have your permission to use my magic, I will set up a test, and if any man achieves it, the whole world will know at once that he is the rightful King and High King of this realm." And the Archbishop said to this, "Merlin, I bid you do whatever seems right to you in this matter." And Merlin said, "I will do so."

*Merlin prepares a test of kingship*.

So Merlin, by his magic, made a huge square block of marble suddenly appear in an open place in front of the cathedral door. And on this block of marble he made an anvil stand, and into the anvil he made a great naked sword be thrust, halfway down its blade. And this sword was the most wonderful that anyone had ever seen, for the blade was of blue steel and extraordinarily bright and gleaming. And the hilt was of gold, chased and carved with marvelous skill and set with a great many precious stones, so that it shone with wonderful brightness in the sunlight. And around the sword these words were written in letters of gold:

*Whoever pulls this sword out of the anvil is the rightful King of England, born to the crown*.

So a great many people came and gazed at that sword and marveled at it exceedingly, for nothing like it had ever been seen on earth before.

Then, when Merlin had worked this miracle, he told the Archbishop to call together all the chief people of the land at Christmastime, and he told the Archbishop to command that every man should try to draw out the sword, because whoever succeeded in drawing it out of the anvil would be the rightful King of Britain.

So the Archbishop did as Merlin said. And this was the marvel of the marble stone and the anvil, which anyone can easily read about for himself in the book that Robert de Boron wrote a very long time ago, called The Romance of Merlin.

Now when the Lord Archbishop's summons went out, calling all the chief people of the land to try that miracle (for it truly would be a miracle to draw a sword blade out of an anvil of solid iron), the whole realm was thrown at once into a great stir, so that every man asked his neighbor, "Who will draw out that sword, and who will be our King?" Some thought it would be King Lot, and others thought it would be King Uriens of Gore (these two being Uther Pendragon's sons-in-law). Some thought it would be King Leodegrance of Cameliard, and others that it would be King Rience of North Wales. Some thought it would be this king and others that king, for the whole world was divided into parties that thought whatever they liked best.

Then, as Christmas drew near, it soon looked as though the entire world was making its way to London Town. The highways and byways filled up with travelers, kings and lords and knights and ladies and squires and pages and men-at-arms, all heading to the place where the test of the sword and the anvil was to be made. Every inn and castle was so packed with travelers that it was a marvel how so many people could fit inside them, and everywhere along the roads there were tents and pavilions (great tents of silk and canvas) pitched to shelter those who could find no room indoors.

But when the Archbishop saw the crowds that were gathering, he said to Merlin, "Truly, Merlin, it would be a very strange thing if among all these great kings and noble, honorable lords we did not find someone worthy to be King of this realm."

At this Merlin smiled and said, "Do not be surprised, my lord, if among all those who seem so extraordinarily worthy, not one is found worthy; and do not be surprised if, among all those who are unknown, one should rise up who proves himself entirely worthy."

And the Archbishop thought deeply about Merlin's words. And so this story begins.
