Chapter 3: How King Arthur Met Four Knights, and What Came of It

[Figure av: The White Champion meets two knights at the mill — A knight on a horse, seen from behind, faces two armed knights at the door of a mill below its turning sails.]

Now the day was wonderfully sweet and pleasant to someone as strong of body and as light of heart as good King Arthur. For the bright clouds sailed smoothly across the blue sky in enormous heaps of vapor, and the wind blew across the long grass of the meadowlands and across the fields of growing wheat, so that a multitude of waves rolled over the hills and valleys as if over a whole sea of green. One moment the whole earth would be darkened by the wide shadows of those clouds, and the next, everything would suddenly burst out again into a wonderful radiance of sunlight. The little birds sang merrily in the hedgerows and the leafy thickets as though they would burst their tiny throats with singing, and the rooster crowed, loud and lusty, from the farmyard. Everything was so bright and lovely that the young King, with the visor of his helmet raised to let in the cool of the gentle breeze, would sometimes sing out very joyfully as he went along. So King Arthur traveled in that gay and tender summer season, when the earth was young, in the days of long ago.

Now you must remember that when King Arthur came from Caerleon to the castle of Tintagalon, he brought four young knights with him to keep him company. These were the knights: there was Sir Gawain, the son of King Lot and Queen Margaise; and there was Sir Uwain, the son of King Uriens and Queen Morgan le Fay (and these two were the King's nephews, by his half sisters); and there was Sir Pelleas; and there was Sir Geraint, the son of Erbin. These were the four noble young knights who had come with King Arthur from Camelot to Tintagalon.

*King Arthur comes upon a knight entertaining the ladies in green*.

Now it happened, as King Arthur rode along so merrily in the summertime, that he came to a certain part of the road where he saw before him a tall and handsome tower standing on a green hillock right beside the road. And there on the balcony of that tower stood three lovely damsels, dressed all in green taffeta. And on the high road in front of the castle there was a knight in very fine armor. The knight sat on a noble war-horse, and in his hands he held a lute, and he played on the lute and sang in a voice of extraordinary sweetness. While he sang, those three ladies in green taffeta listened to him with very cheerful faces. And whenever the knight stopped singing, the three ladies would clap their hands with great applause and beg him to sing to them again; and he would do so very readily.

King Arthur saw all this, and it seemed to him a very pleasant sight, so that he was greatly delighted by it.

And as he drew near, he saw that the knight who sat on his horse there, playing the lute and singing to it, was none other than Sir Geraint, the son of Erbin. For that knight wore the figure of a gryphon on his crest, and the emblem on his shield was two gryphons rearing up and facing each other on a blue field, and King Arthur knew that this was the crest and the emblem of Sir Geraint. And when the King saw who the knight was who sat there singing, he laughed to himself, and at once closed his visor and made himself ready for whatever encounter might happen. So he drew near to where the knight sang and the ladies listened.

Now when Sir Geraint saw King Arthur coming, he stopped singing and hung his lute behind him across his shoulder. Then, looking up at the three lovely ladies above him, he said: "Ladies, you have been pleased to listen to the songs I have attempted entirely in your honor. Now, also in your honor, I will perform a deed of knightly prowess, which I very much hope will bring you great glory. For if you will be pleased to lend me the encouragement that your great beauty can so easily give, you will see me, I have no doubt, completely overthrow that knight over there, and all to your great credit and renown."

"Sir Knight," said the lady who spoke for the others, "you are truly a lord of noble bearing and very pleasing manners, and so we wish you great success in this undertaking. And we believe that you will succeed in what you are attempting."

At this Sir Geraint thanked the three damsels warmly for their words, and then he closed the visor of his helmet. And so, setting his spear and his shield, and saluting the three ladies very humbly, he rode out to meet King Arthur, who now sat a little way off, waiting very quietly and soberly for him to be ready.

Now Sir Geraint did not know King Arthur, because he wore no crest on his helmet and no emblem on his shield. So, as he saluted him, he spoke to him like this: "Ha! Sir, I do not know who you are, since you bear neither crest nor emblem. Even so, I have a mind to do you what honor I can by riding a tilt against you on behalf of those three damsels you see up there on that balcony. For I declare, and I am ready to prove it with my own knightly body, that those ladies are lovelier than your lady, whoever she may be."

"Sir Knight," said King Arthur, "I will gladly ride a course against you in honor of my lady. For I may tell you that she is a princess, and many people hold her to be the most beautiful lady in all the world. But I will fight you on one condition only, and the condition is this: that whoever is overthrown shall give himself as servant to the other for seven days, and in that time he shall do whatever is asked of him."

"I accept your challenge, Sir Unknown Knight," said Sir Geraint, "and when I have overthrown you, I will hand you over to those lovely ladies up there to be their servant for seven days. And I tell you that a great many knights would certainly think that both a pleasant and an honorable task."

"And if I should happen to overthrow you," said King Arthur, "I will send you to serve my lady for the same length of time, and that will be an even pleasanter and more honorable task than the one you have in mind for me."

*King Arthur overthrows Sir Geraint*.

So each knight saluted the other, and then each took up a position that would bring the encounter right below where the three lovely damsels looked down from the balcony. Then each knight set his spear and his shield, and when he was ready for the charge, each sat for a little while fully prepared. Then each shouted to his war-horse and drove his spurs into its flank and launched forward at wonderful speed to the attack. So they met in the very middle of the course with such fury that the noise of it was dreadful to hear. Each knight struck the other in the very center of his guard. And the spear of Sir Geraint burst into small pieces, right down to the handle; but King Arthur's spear held, and Sir Geraint was flung backward so violently that both he and his horse were thrown down into the dust with a din like a monstrous roar of thunder.

When Sir Geraint got back on his feet, he was so dazed for a while that he did not know where he stood, for he had never been thrown like that in all his life before. Then, quickly coming to himself again, he drew his sword at once and called on King Arthur, very hotly, to get down out of his saddle and fight him on foot.

"No, not so, Sir Knight," said King Arthur. "I will not fight you that way. Besides, you must not forget that you promised to give yourself to me as my servant for seven days. For I have certainly beaten you fairly in this encounter, and now you are pledged to be my servant."

Then Sir Geraint did not know what to say, for he was utterly ashamed and vexed at being overthrown. Even so, he saw that he must keep his knightly word and do what he had pledged himself to do. So he put up his sword again, though very unwillingly. "Sir Knight," he said, "I admit that I have been beaten in this encounter, and so I now yield myself to your commands, as I gave my word."

*King Arthur sends Sir Geraint to the Lady Guinevere*.

"Then here are my commands," said King Arthur. "My command is that you go at once to the Lady Guinevere at Cameliard, and that you tell her you have been overthrown by the knight to whom she gave her necklace as a token. And I also want you to obey her in everything she may command you to do, for the next seven days."

"Sir Knight," said Sir Geraint, "what you tell me to do, I will do just as you command."

Then he mounted his horse and went his way. And King Arthur went his way. And the three ladies who stood on the balcony of the castle were very glad that they had seen so noble a contest of arms as the one they had looked down on.

Now after King Arthur had traveled on for two or three leagues or more, he came to a stretch of moorland where there were many ditches of water, and where the heron and the marsh hen took shelter in the reeds. Here and there stood several windmills, their sails all turning slowly in the sunlight before a wind that blew across the flat plains of mud. And at this place there was a long, straight causeway, with two long rows of pollard willows, one on either side. Now when he had come near the middle of this causeway, King Arthur saw two knights sitting on their horses in the shade of a great windmill that stood on one side of the road. A huge shadow of the sails swept again and again across the road as the wheel of the mill turned slowly before the wind. And all around the mill, and everywhere about, there were great numbers of swallows darting this way and that, like bees around a hive in midsummer. And King Arthur saw that those two knights, as they sat in the shadow of the mill, were eating a great loaf of rye bread, fresh baked, with a crisp crust; and they were eating good white cheese, which the miller, all white with flour dust, was serving them. But when the two knights saw King Arthur, they stopped eating their bread and cheese at once and quickly closed their helmets. As for the miller, when he saw them getting ready like this, he hurried back into the mill and shut the door, and then went and looked out of a window right above where the knights were standing.

*King Arthur comes upon two knights at the windmill*.

But King Arthur was very merry to himself when he saw that those two knights were Sir Gawain and Sir Uwain. He knew that the one was Sir Gawain because the crest of his helmet was a leopard rearing up, and because he bore on his shield the emblem of a leopard rearing up on a red field. And he knew that the other was Sir Uwain, because he bore a unicorn on his crest, and because the emblem on his shield was a lady holding a naked sword in her hand, on a field of gold. So, while he was still some distance off, King Arthur closed his helmet so that those two young knights would not know who he was.

So when he had come close to the two knights, Sir Gawain rode forward a little way to meet him. "Sir Knight," he said, "you should know that this is truly dangerous ground you have ventured onto. For there is no side path from here across the marsh, and you cannot go forward without riding a tilt against me."

"Sir Knight," said King Arthur, "I am very willing to ride a tilt against you. Even so, I will only fight you on one condition, and it is this: that whoever is overthrown shall serve the other in everything for seven full days."

"I accept your challenge, Sir Knight," said Sir Gawain. For he said to himself, "Surely so very strong and skillful a knight as I am will easily bring about the overthrow of this unknown knight."

*King Arthur overthrows Sir Gawain*.

So each knight at once took his appointed place. And when each had set his spear and his shield, and prepared himself fully in every way, and rested for a little while, each suddenly shouted to his horse and drove his spurs into its flanks, and so they rushed to the encounter. Each knight struck the other in the middle of his guard, and the spear of Sir Gawain burst into fragments. But King Arthur's spear held, so that Sir Gawain was lifted clean out of his saddle and over the hindquarters of his horse. And indeed he fell into the dust with tremendous force. Nor could he get up at once after that fall, but lay all dazed for a little while. And when he did get up, he saw that the white knight who had overthrown him was sitting on his horse close beside him.

Then King Arthur spoke and said: "Sir Knight, I have overthrown you completely, and so now you must serve me according to your knightly word."

Then Sir Uwain, who sat on his horse nearby, spoke up. "Not so, Sir Knight," he said. "Not so, not until you have dealt with me. For I demand that you joust with me at once. And if I overthrow you, I will claim from you that you release my cousin from the service he has pledged himself to. But if you overthrow me, then I will serve you, just as he has pledged himself to serve you."

"Sir Knight," said King Arthur, "I accept your challenge with all my heart!"

*King Arthur overthrows Sir Uwain*.

So each knight took his appointed place and made himself ready for the encounter. Then they shouted and drove together, rushing at each other like two rams on a hillside. And the spear of Sir Uwain also shattered into pieces. But King Arthur's spear held, so that the girths of Sir Uwain's saddle burst apart, and both saddle and knight were swept off the horse's back with such violence that a falling tower could not have made a greater noise than Sir Uwain made when he struck the dust of that causeway.

*King Arthur sends the two knights to the Lady Guinevere*.

Then Sir Uwain got to his feet and stared at him, utterly amazed. King Arthur came up to him and spoke to him like this: "Ha, Sir Knight, it seems to me that you have been fairly beaten today. And so, by your promises, both you and that other knight there must carry out all my commands for seven full days to come. Now this is the command I lay on you both: that you go at once to the Lady Guinevere at Cameliard and bring her greetings from her knight. And you shall tell her that her knight, the one to whom she gave her necklace, has sent you, who are the sons of kings, to obey her. And whatever she commands you to do in the seven days to come, you shall carry out to the smallest grain."

"Sir Knight," said Sir Gawain, "we will do as you command, since we have pledged ourselves to it. But when these seven days have passed, I vow that I will seek you out and fight this combat out to the very end. For any knight may happen to be unhorsed, as I have been; yet I believe I may have better success against you if I fight you with every ounce of my strength."

"Sir Knight," said King Arthur, "it shall be just as you wish. Yet I truly believe that when these seven days have passed you will not have so great a desire to fight me as you have now."

When he had said this, King Arthur saluted the two knights and they saluted him. Then he turned his horse and went his way. And whenever he thought of how those two good knights had fallen before his charge, and of how astonished and ashamed they had been at their overthrow, he laughed aloud for pure fun, and told himself that he had never in all his life had so joyous an adventure as this.

So when Sir Uwain had mended the girths of his saddle, he and Sir Gawain mounted their horses and set off toward Cameliard in very low spirits.

Then the miller came out of the mill once more, delighted to have watched such a wonderfully knightly encounter from so safe a place.

And so King Arthur rode on, well content in his mind, until the afternoon began to slant toward evening; and by that time he had come near the arm of the forest that he had in mind as the right place to leave his horse and his armor.

Now as he drew near this edge of the forest, he saw before him by the roadside a gnarled and stunted oak tree. And he saw that a shield hung on the oak tree, and that beneath the shield these words were written in fine large letters:

*Whoever strikes this shield does so at the peril of his body*.

Then King Arthur was filled with a great spirit, and, lifting his spear, he struck that shield so that it rang like thunder.

*King Arthur strikes the shield of the White Knight*.

At once King Arthur heard a voice come out of the forest, crying, "Who has dared to strike my shield?" And straightaway a knight of large build came riding out of the forest on a white horse, like the one King Arthur himself was riding. And the trappings of the horse and the knight were all white, like those of King Arthur and his horse. The knight wore on his helmet, for his crest, a swan with outspread wings, and on his shield he bore three swans on a silver field. And by the crest and the arms on the shield, King Arthur knew that this knight was Sir Pelleas, who had come with him from Camelot to Tintagalon.

So when Sir Pelleas had come near to where King Arthur waited for him, he reined in his horse and spoke to him in a very stern voice. "Ho! Ho! Sir Knight," he said. "Why did you dare to strike my shield? Truly, that blow will bring you great danger and grief. Now get ready to defend yourself at once for what you have done."

"Wait! Wait, Sir Knight," said King Arthur. "It shall be as you wish, and I will fight you. Yet I will not try this adventure until you have agreed that the knight who is beaten in the encounter shall serve the other in whatever way that other may wish, for one week from today."

"Sir Knight," said Sir Pelleas, "I accept that risk. So now I bid you get ready at once for the encounter."

*King Arthur overthrows Sir Pelleas*.

Then each knight took his place and set his spear and shield. And when they were ready, they launched at each other at once with the force of two stones hurled from a catapult. So they met in the middle of the course, and once again King Arthur was entirely successful in his charge. For the spear of Sir Pelleas burst to pieces, and King Arthur's spear held; and Sir Pelleas was hurled out of his saddle with tremendous force, landing more than half a spear's length behind the hindquarters of his horse. Nor did he fully recover from that fall for a long time, so that King Arthur had to wait beside him for quite a while before he was able to lift himself up from the ground where he lay.

*King Arthur sends Sir Pelleas to the Lady Guinevere*.

"Ha! Sir Knight," said King Arthur, "it certainly has not gone well with you today. For you have been completely overthrown, and now you must at once make good your pledge to serve me for the next seven days. So this is my command to you: that you go at once to Cameliard and greet the Lady Guinevere from me, telling her that her knight, the one to whom she gave her necklace, has been victorious in battle against you. And I also command that you obey her for seven days in whatever she may command you to do."

"Sir Knight," said Sir Pelleas, "it shall be just as you order. Yet I wish I knew who you are, for I declare that I have never in all my life been overthrown as you have overthrown me. And indeed I think there are very few men in the world who could treat me the way you have treated me."

"Sir Knight," said King Arthur, "someday you shall know who I am. But for now I am bound to complete secrecy."

At that he saluted Sir Pelleas, and turned and rode into the forest and was gone.

And Sir Pelleas mounted his horse and made his way to Cameliard, very downcast and troubled in spirit, and yet wondering greatly who that knight could be who had treated him the way he had been treated.

*The Lady Guinevere is pleased with her champion*.

So that day there came to Cameliard, first Sir Geraint, and then Sir Gawain and Sir Uwain, and last of all Sir Pelleas. And when these four saw one another, they were all so ashamed that none of them dared look another in the face. And when they came before the Lady Guinevere and told her their situation, and told her how the knight who wore her necklace had overthrown them all and sent them there to serve her for a week, and when she considered how great and famous those four knights were in deeds of chivalry, she was filled with great pride that her knight had proven himself so mighty in the feats of arms he had undertaken. But she wondered greatly who that champion could be, and turned the question over and over in her mind. For it was a thing completely unheard of that one knight, in one day, with a single spear, should have overthrown five such well-proven and famous knights as Duke Mordaunt of North Umber, Sir Geraint, Sir Gawain, Sir Uwain, and Sir Pelleas. So she rejoiced greatly that she had given her necklace to so worthy a knight, and she was lifted up with extraordinary pleasure at the thought of the honor he had brought her.

*King Arthur takes up his disguise again*.

Now after King Arthur had entered the forest, he came after a while to the place where the woodcutters we spoke of earlier were at their work. He stayed with them that night, and when the next morning came, he left his horse and armor in their care, charging them to guard those things with every care and promising that they would be wonderfully rewarded for it. Then he set off from that place, meaning to return to Cameliard. And he was dressed in the frieze jerkin he had worn ever since he left Tintagalon.

And when he reached the edge of the forest, he put his cap of disguise on his head and so took on his humble appearance once more. So, with all his knightliness completely hidden, he went back to Cameliard to be the gardener's boy, just as he had been before.
