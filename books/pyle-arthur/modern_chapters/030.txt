Chapter 6: How the Lady of the Lake Took Back Her Necklace from Sir Pelleas

[Figure bt: The Lady of the Lake finds Sir Pelleas wounded — Pelleas lies pale in a bed in the hermit's cell, and the Lady of the Lake bends over him with her hand raised.]

*Sir Gawain challenges Sir Pelleas*.

Now when the next morning came, Sir Gawain called his squire to him and said, "Bring my armor here and buckle me into it." And the squire did so. Then Sir Gawain said, "Help me onto my horse," and the squire did so. The morning was still very early. The grass was all shining and sparkling with dew, and the little birds were singing so hard that it might have made anyone glad just to be alive. So, once Sir Gawain was in his armor and seated on his horse, he began to take more courage, and the dark clouds that had hung over him lifted a little. Then he spoke to his squire in a stronger voice and said, "Take this glove of mine and carry it to Sir Pelleas. Tell him that Sir Gawain is riding up and down in the meadow in front of the castle, and that there he challenges Sir Pelleas to meet him on horseback or on foot, whichever that knight may choose."

At these words the squire was greatly astonished. Sir Gawain and Sir Pelleas had always been such close friends that there was hardly another friendship like theirs in all that land, and their love for each other had become a saying on everyone's lips. But he kept his thoughts to himself and only said, "Won't you eat something before you go into battle?" And Sir Gawain said, "No, I will not eat until I have fought. So go and do as I have told you."

So Sir Gawain's squire went to Sir Pelleas in his pavilion, gave that knight Sir Gawain's glove, and delivered Sir Gawain's message. And Sir Pelleas said, "Tell your master that I will come out to meet him as soon as I have had my breakfast."

Now when the news of that challenge reached the ears of Sir Brandiles, Sir Mador de la Porte, Sir Uwain and Sir Marhaus, those knights were very much troubled by it, and Sir Uwain said to the others, "Sirs, let us go and find out what this business is about." So the four knights went to the white pavilion where Sir Pelleas was eating his breakfast.

When they came before Sir Pelleas, Sir Uwain said to him, "What is this quarrel between my kinsman and you?" And Sir Pelleas answered, "I will not tell you. So leave it alone and do not meddle in it."

Then Sir Uwain said, "Would you really fight in earnest with your own friend?" To which Sir Pelleas said, "He is my friend no longer."

Then Sir Brandiles cried out, "It is a great pity that a quarrel should come between such friends as you and Sir Gawain. Won't you let us make peace between you?" But Sir Pelleas replied, "You cannot make peace, for this quarrel cannot be stopped until it is ended."

Then those knights saw that their words were of no use, and they went away and left Sir Pelleas.

So when Sir Pelleas had finished his breakfast, he called for a squire named Montenoir and told him to buckle him into the red armor he had worn all this time, and Montenoir did so. Then, when Sir Pelleas was clad in that armor, he rode out into the meadow in front of the castle where Sir Gawain was waiting. When he got there, those four other knights came to him again and begged him to let peace be made between him and Sir Gawain. But Sir Pelleas would not listen to them, so they went away again and left him, and he rode out into the field in front of the castle of Grantmesnle.

By now a great crowd of people had come out onto the castle walls to watch that fight, for the news of it had spread all around the place. It had also become known that the knight who was to fight Sir Pelleas was that very famous royal knight, Sir Gawain, the son of King Lot of Orkney and a nephew of King Arthur, so all the people were very eager to see so famous a knight do battle.

The Lady Ettard came down to the walls as well, and took her place in a smaller tower that looked out over the field of battle. And when she had taken her place there, she saw that Sir Pelleas was wearing that necklace of emeralds and opals and gold over his armor. Her heart went out to him because of it, and so she hoped that he would be the winner in that encounter.

*Sir Pelleas and Sir Gawain do battle*.

*Sir Pelleas overthrows Sir Gawain*.

Then each knight took up whatever position seemed best to him, and each set his spear and his shield and made himself ready for the charge. When they were ready in every way, Sir Marhaus gave the signal. At once each knight left his place and dashed at the other as fast as lightning, and with such fury that the earth thundered and shook under their horses' hoofs. They met squarely in the middle of the course, each knight striking the other in the very center of his shield. In that meeting Sir Gawain's spear burst all the way to the hand guard, but the spear of Sir Pelleas held, so that Sir Gawain was hurled out of his saddle with terrible violence. He struck the ground so hard that he rolled over three times in the dust, and then he lay completely still, as though the life had gone out of him.

At this, all the people on the walls gave a great shout, for it had been a very noble feat of arms.

Then the four knights who had been watching that encounter hurried over to where Sir Gawain lay, and Sir Pelleas also rode back and sat on his horse close by. Sir Uwain and Sir Gawain's squire unlaced Sir Gawain's helmet as fast as they could, and they saw that his face was the color of ashes, and they could not tell that he was breathing at all.

Then Sir Marhaus said, "I believe you have killed this knight, Sir Pelleas." And Sir Pelleas said, "Do you think so?" "Yes," said Sir Marhaus, "and I think it a great pity." To which Sir Pelleas replied, "He has not suffered more than he deserved."

At these words Sir Uwain was filled with anger, and he cried out, "Sir Knight, I think you forget who this knight is. Not only is he a fellow companion of the Round Table, to whom you have vowed complete brotherhood, but he is also the son of a king and the nephew of King Arthur himself."

But Sir Pelleas kept a very steady face at this and answered, "I would not be sorry for this even if that knight were a king in his own right instead of only the son of a king."

Then Sir Uwain raised his voice in great anger and cried out at Sir Pelleas, "Go away! Or something very bad may happen to you." "Very well," said Sir Pelleas, "I will go."

With that he turned his horse and rode away from that place, and went into the woods, and so was gone from their sight.

*Sir Pelleas rides away into the forest*.

Then the others lifted up Sir Gawain and carried him to the pavilion that had belonged to Sir Pelleas, and there they laid him on Sir Pelleas's couch. But it was more than an hour before he came to himself again, and for a good part of that time the people around him believed that he was dead.

*Sir Pelleas is badly wounded*.

But not one of those knights knew the truth, which was this: Sir Pelleas had been so badly wounded in the side in that encounter that there was no hope he could live past that day. For though Sir Gawain's spear had burst, and though Sir Pelleas had overthrown him completely, the head of Sir Gawain's spear had pierced Sir Pelleas's armor and gone into his side, and had broken off there. So a piece of the iron spearhead as long as the width of a hand was left in the body of Sir Pelleas, a little above the waist. And all the while Sir Pelleas sat there talking so calmly to those four knights, he was in terrible pain, and the blood was running down inside his armor in streams. What with the loss of blood and the great agony he was suffering, his head swam as light as a feather the whole time he was talking with the others. But he did not say a word to them about the terrible wound he had received. Instead he rode away very proudly into the forest.

But once he was in the forest, he could not hold back any longer. He began to groan very sorely, and he cried out, "Alas! Alas! Surely I have got my death wound in this battle!"

Now it happened that morning that the damsel Parcenet had ridden out to fly a young gerfalcon, which is a great hunting falcon, and a dwarf who belonged to the Lady Ettard had ridden with her to keep her company. As the damsel and the dwarf rode along a certain stretch at the edge of the forest, not very far from Grantmesnle, where the thicker part of the woods began and the thinner part ended, the damsel heard a voice in the woods, lamenting in very great sorrow. So she stopped and listened, and after a while she heard that voice moaning loudly again. Then Parcenet said to the dwarf, "What is that I hear? Surely it is the voice of someone in great trouble. Let us go and see who it is that is moaning so miserably." And the dwarf said, "As you say."

*How Parcenet finds Sir Pelleas wounded in the forest*.

So the damsel and the dwarf went a little farther, and there they saw a knight sitting on a black horse under an oak tree. That knight was dressed all in red armor, so Parcenet knew it must be Sir Pelleas. She saw that he was leaning on the butt of his spear, which he had planted on the ground, and in that way he was holding himself up on his horse, from which he would otherwise have fallen because he was so weak. And all the while he was making that loud moaning that Parcenet had heard. Seeing him in such a sad state, Parcenet was overcome with great pity, and she hurried to him, crying out, "Oh! Sir Pelleas, what is the matter with you?"

Sir Pelleas looked at her as though she were very far away from him, and because his spirit was so faint, he saw her as if through a film of water. And he said very faintly, "Maiden, I am badly hurt." She said, "How are you hurt, Sir Pelleas?" And he answered, "I have a terrible wound in my side, for a spear point is stuck in it nearly a hand's width deep, so that it reaches almost to my heart. So I think that I will not live very long."

At this the maiden cried out, "Alas! Alas! What is this!" And she wept and lamented and wrung her hands with grief that so noble a knight should have come to such a dreadful state.

Then the dwarf who was with Parcenet, seeing how wild with sorrow she was, said, "Damsel, I know of a certain place in this forest (though it is quite a long way from here) where a very holy hermit lives, who is a wonderfully skillful healer. Now, if we can bring this knight to the chapel where that hermit lives, I believe he may be helped a great deal back to health and ease again."

At this Parcenet said, "Gansaret" (for Gansaret was the dwarf's name), "Gansaret, let us take this knight to that place as quickly as we can. For I am telling you the truth when I say that I love him very dearly." "Well," said the dwarf, "I will show you where that chapel is."

So the dwarf took Sir Pelleas's horse by the bridle and led the way through the forest, and Parcenet rode beside Sir Pelleas and held him up in his saddle. Every so often Sir Pelleas fainted from sickness and pain, so that he would have fallen if she had not held him. So they went on very sorrowfully, and so slowly that it was noon before they came to that very thick and lonely part of the forest where the hermit lived.

When they reached that place, the dwarf said, "There, damsel, is the chapel I told you about."

Then Parcenet raised her eyes and saw a little woodland chapel tucked in among the leafy trees of the forest. Around the chapel was a small open lawn sprinkled with flowers, and near the door of the hermit's dwelling was a spring of water as clear as crystal. It was a very hidden and lonely place, and very quiet and peaceful as well, for in front of the chapel they saw a wild doe and her fawn nibbling the tender grass and plants without any fear of harm. When the dwarf and the maiden and the wounded knight came near, the doe and the fawn looked up with great wide eyes and spread their big ears in wonder. Yet they did not run away, for they feared no harm, and after a while they went back to their grazing. All around the chapel, too, the branches of the trees were full of birds, singing and chirping very cheerfully. Those birds were waiting for their midday meal, which the hermit was in the habit of scattering for them.

(Now this was that same forest sanctuary where King Arthur had come the time he was so badly wounded by Sir Pellinore, as has been told earlier in this history.)

As the maiden and the dwarf and the wounded knight drew near the chapel, a little bell began ringing very sweetly, so that its sound echoed all through those quiet woods, for it was now the hour of noon. Sir Pelleas heard that bell as if from very far away. First he said, "Where have I come to?" and then he managed to cross himself. Parcenet crossed herself too, and the dwarf knelt down and crossed himself. Then, when the bell had stopped ringing, the dwarf called out in a loud voice, "Hello! Hello! Here is someone who needs help!"

*Parcenet and the dwarf bring Sir Pelleas to the hermit of the forest*.

Then the door of the sanctuary opened, and out came a very old and holy-looking man with a long white beard, as soft as finely combed wool. And as he came out, all the birds that were waiting there flew around him in great flocks, for they thought he had come out to feed them, so the hermit had to brush those little birds away with his hands as he walked over to where the three of them were standing.

When he reached them, he asked them who they were and why they had come there with that wounded knight. So Parcenet told him how things stood with them, and how they had found Sir Pelleas so badly wounded in the forest that morning and had brought him there.

When the hermit had heard her whole story, he said, "Very well, I will take him in." So he took Sir Pelleas into his cell, and when they had helped lay him on the couch, Parcenet and the dwarf went back home again.

After they had gone, the hermit examined Sir Pelleas's wound while Sir Pelleas lay in a deep faint. The faint was so deep that the hermit saw it was the faint that comes before death, and that the knight was near his end. So he said, "This knight is certain to die very soon, for there is nothing I can do to save him." So he left Sir Pelleas's side at once and hurried to prepare the last sacrament, the kind that might be given to a noble knight who was dying.

*The Lady of the Lake comes to Sir Pelleas*.

Now while the hermit was busy with this, the door suddenly opened, and into the room came a very strange lady. She was dressed all in green, and around her arms she wore bracelets of emeralds and opals set in gold. Her hair, which was very soft, was all black, and it was tied with a crimson ribbon. The hermit saw that her face was as white as ivory, and that her eyes were bright like jewels set in ivory, and so he knew that she was no ordinary mortal.

This lady went straight to Sir Pelleas and bent over him, so close that her breath touched his forehead. And she said, "Alas! Sir Pelleas, that you should lie like this." "Lady," said the hermit, "you may well say 'Alas,' for this knight has only a few minutes to live." To this the lady said, "Not so, holy man, for I tell you that this knight will live for a long time yet." And when she had said this, she bent down and took from around his neck that necklace of emeralds and opals and gold that he wore, and she hung it around her own neck.

When the hermit saw what she was doing, he said, "Lady, what are you doing, and why do you take that ornament from a dying man?"

But the lady answered very calmly, "I gave it to him, so I am only taking back what is mine. But now I beg you to leave me alone with this knight for a little while, for I have great hope that I may bring him back to life again."

Then the hermit was doubtful, and he said, "Do you mean to try to heal him by magic?" And the lady said, "If I do, it will not be by black magic."

So the hermit was satisfied, and he went away and left the lady alone with Sir Pelleas.

*The Lady of the Lake heals Sir Pelleas*.

Now as soon as the lady was alone with the wounded knight, she began doing a number of very strange things. First she brought out a lodestone of great strength and power, and she held it to the wound. And the iron of the spearhead came out of the wound, and as it came Sir Pelleas groaned with great pain. When the spear point came out, a great rush of blood burst forth like a crimson fountain. But the lady at once pressed a sweet-smelling cloth of fine linen against the wound and stopped the blood, and it bled no more, for she held it inside his veins by very powerful spells. Once the blood was stopped like this, the lady took from the front of her dress a small crystal bottle filled with a liquid of blue color and very rare fragrance. She poured some of this between the cold, gray lips of the knight, and when it touched his lips, life began to come back into his body. For after a little while he opened his eyes and looked around him with a very strange look. The first thing he saw was the lady dressed in green who stood beside him, and she was so beautiful that he thought he must have died and gone to Paradise. So he said, "Am I dead, then?"

"No, you are not dead," said the lady, "but you have been dangerously close to death." "Then where am I?" said Sir Pelleas. And she answered, "You are in a deep part of the forest, and this is the cell of a saintly hermit of the forest." At this Sir Pelleas said, "Who has brought me back to life?" At that the lady smiled and said, "I did."

For a little while Sir Pelleas lay very still. Then after a time he spoke and said, "Lady, I feel very strange." "Yes," said the lady, "that is because you now have a different life." Then Sir Pelleas said, "What has happened to me?" And the lady said, "This: to bring you back to life I gave you a drink of a certain potion, an *elixir vitae*, the water of life. So now you are only half what you were before. For while one half of you is mortal, the other half of you is fairy."

*Sir Pelleas loves the Lady of the Lake*.

Then Sir Pelleas looked up and saw that the lady wore around her neck the collar of emeralds and opals and gold that he had worn before. And at once his heart went out to her with a great and burning love, and he said, "Lady, you say that I am half fairy, and I can see that you are altogether fairy. Now I beg you to let me live from now on wherever you are." And the lady said, "It shall be as you ask, for it was for this very reason that I let you come so near to death, and then brought you back to life again."

Then Sir Pelleas said, "When may I go with you?" And she said, "In a little while, when you have had something to drink." "How can that be," said Sir Pelleas, "when I am still as weak as a little child?" To which the lady answered, "When you have drunk some water your strength will come back to you, and you will be completely well and whole again."

So the Lady of the Lake went out, and soon came back carrying a clay jug filled with water from the spring close by. And when Sir Pelleas had drunk that water, he suddenly felt his strength come back to him completely.

Yet he was not at all as he had been before. For now his body felt as light as air, and his soul swelled with a pure joy such as he had never felt before in all his life. So he rose at once from his bed of pain and said, "You have given me life again, and now I give that life to you forever."

Then the lady looked at him and smiled with great tenderness, and she said, "Sir Pelleas, I have cared for you dearly ever since the day I saw you, when you were a young knight, drinking a bowl of milk at a cottage in this forest. The day was warm and you had taken off your helmet, and a young milkmaid with a sunburned face and bare feet came and brought you a bowl of milk, and you drank it down with a hearty appetite. That was the first time I ever saw you, although you did not see me. Since that time I have felt great friendship for all your companions of King Arthur's Court, and for King Arthur himself, all for your sake."

Then Sir Pelleas said, "Lady, will you take me for your knight?" And she said, "Yes." Then Sir Pelleas said, "May I kiss you?" And she said, "Yes, if it pleases you." So Sir Pelleas kissed her on the lips, and so they were promised to each other.

Now let us go back to Parcenet and the dwarf.

*Parcenet brings news of Sir Pelleas to Sir Mador de la Porte*.

After those two had left the hermitage in the woods, they made their way back toward Grantmesnle. When they had nearly come out of the forest, at a place not far from the grove of trees where the knights-companion had set up their camp, they met one of those knights, dressed in half armor, and that knight was Sir Mador de la Porte. Parcenet called to him by name and said, "Alas! Sir Mador, I have only just left a hermit's cell in the forest, where I left Sir Pelleas wounded to death, and I fear he has only a little while to live."

Then Sir Mador de la Porte cried out, "Ha! Maiden, what is this you are telling me? That is very hard to believe, for when Sir Pelleas left us this morning he showed no sign of any wound or sickness of any kind."

But Parcenet answered, "Even so, I saw him myself lying in great pain and misery, and before he fell into his death faint, he told me himself that he had the iron of a spear in his side."

Then Sir Mador de la Porte said, "Alas! Alas! That is sad news! Now, damsel, with your kind permission, I will leave you and hurry to my companions to tell them this news." And Parcenet said, "Please do."

So Sir Mador de la Porte hurried to the pavilion where his companions were, and he told them the news he had heard.

By this time Sir Gawain had completely recovered from the violent fall he had taken that morning. So when he heard the news that Sir Mador de la Porte brought, he struck his hands together and cried out loud, "Woe is me! What have I done! First I betrayed my friend, and now I have killed him. I will go out at once to find him and to beg his forgiveness before he dies."

But Sir Uwain said, "What is this you mean to do? You are not fit yet to make any journey."

*Sir Gawain sets out to find Sir Pelleas*.

Sir Gawain said, "I don't care, for I am determined to go and find my friend." And he would not let any of his companions go with him. Instead, when he had told his squire to bring his horse, he mounted it and rode off into the forest alone, heading west, and grieving bitterly as he went.

Now when the afternoon had grown very late, so that the sun was sinking toward its setting and the light fell as red as fire through the forest leaves, Sir Gawain came to that hermit's cell where it stood in the silent and lonely part of the woods. He saw that the hermit was outside his cell, digging in a little garden of lentils. When the hermit saw the armed knight ride onto the lawn, all in the red light of the setting sun, he stopped digging and leaned on his trowel. Then Sir Gawain came near, and, sitting there on his horse, he told the holy man what errand had brought him.

To this the hermit said, "A lady came here several hours ago. She was dressed all in green and was so strange to look at that it was easy to see she was a fairy. By means of certain magic charms that lady cured your friend, and after she had healed him, the two of them rode away into the forest together."

Then Sir Gawain was utterly amazed, and he said, "This is a very strange thing you are telling me: that a dying knight should be brought back to life in so short a time, and should ride out so suddenly from his bed of pain. Now I beg you, tell me which way they went." The hermit said, "They went west." And when Sir Gawain heard this, he said, "I will follow them."

*Sir Gawain follows a strange light*.

So he rode away and left the hermit gazing after him. As he rode on his way, the twilight began to fall fast, so that after a while the woods grew very dark and strange all around him. But as the darkness came down, a very strange miracle happened. For there appeared in front of Sir Gawain a light of a pale blue color, and it went ahead of him and showed him the way. And he followed it, wondering greatly.

After he had followed the light for a very long time, he came at last, all of a sudden, to the place where the woods ended, and there lay a wide open plain that stretched very far. This plain was all lit up by a strange glow like the light of a clear full moon, although no moon was shining at that time. In that pale, silver light Sir Gawain could see everything wonderfully clearly. So he saw that he was on a plain covered all over with flowers of many kinds, and their perfume so filled the night that it seemed to press upon his chest with a great sweetness. And he saw that in front of him lay a great lake, very wide and still. All these things looked so strange in that light that Sir Gawain knew he had come into Fairyland. So he rode among the tall flowers toward that lake with a kind of fear, for he did not know what was going to happen to him.

As he drew near the lake, he saw a knight and a lady coming toward him. When they came close, he saw that the knight was Sir Pelleas, and that his face was very strange. And he saw that the lady was the one he had seen before, all dressed in green, when he had traveled in the Forest of Adventure with Sir Uwain and Sir Marhaus.

*Sir Gawain finds Sir Pelleas*.

When Sir Gawain first saw Sir Pelleas he was filled with great fear, for he thought it was a ghost he was looking at. But when he saw that Sir Pelleas was alive, a joy as great as that fear had been rose up in his heart, and he hurried toward Sir Pelleas. When he came near him, he leaped down from his horse, crying out, "Forgive me! Forgive me!" with the greatest passion. Then he would have taken Sir Pelleas in his arms, but Sir Pelleas drew back from Sir Gawain's touch, though not in any anger. And Sir Pelleas spoke in a voice that was very thin and had a silvery clearness, as though it came from far away, and he said, "Do not touch me, for I am not what I was before. I am not all human now, but part fairy. But as for my forgiveness: I forgive you for whatever wrong I may have suffered at your hands. And more than that, I give you my love, and I hope with all my heart for your joy and happiness. But now I am going away to leave you, dear friend, and perhaps I shall never see you again. So I leave this with you as my last wish: that you go back to King Arthur's Court and make your peace with the Queen. In that way you can bring them news of everything that has happened to me."

Then Sir Gawain cried out in great sorrow, "Where are you going?"

And Sir Pelleas said, "I am going to that wonderful city of gold and blue that lies over there in that valley of flowers."

Then Sir Gawain said, "I see no city, only a lake of water."

To this Sir Pelleas replied, "Even so, there is a city over there, and I am going to it. And so I bid you farewell now."

Then Sir Gawain looked into the face of Sir Pelleas and saw again how strange it looked in that light. For it was as white as ivory, and his eyes shone like jewels set in ivory, and a smile lay on his lips that grew neither larger nor smaller but always stayed exactly the same. (For those who were of that kind always had that strange look and smiled in that way: the Lady of the Lake, and Sir Pelleas, and Sir Lancelot of the Lake.)

*Sir Pelleas disappears into the lake*.

Then Sir Pelleas and the Lady of the Lake turned and left Sir Gawain where he stood, and they went toward the lake, and they rode into the lake. And as soon as the hoofs of Sir Pelleas's horse touched the water of the lake, Sir Pelleas was gone, and Sir Gawain saw him no more, though he stood there for a long time, weeping bitterly.

And so ends the story of Sir Pelleas.

But Sir Gawain went back to King Arthur's Court as he had promised Sir Pelleas he would, and he made his peace with Queen Guinevere. And after that, though the Queen did not love him, there was peace between them. And Sir Gawain told all these things to the Court of King Arthur, and everyone marveled at what he told.

And only twice after that was Sir Pelleas ever seen by any of his old companions.

And Sir Marhaus was made a Companion of the Round Table and became one of its foremost knights.

And the Lady Ettard took Sir Engamore back into her favor, and that summer they were married, and Sir Engamore became lord of Grantmesnle.

And so ends this story.
