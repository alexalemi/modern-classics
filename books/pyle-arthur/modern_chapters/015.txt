Chapter 5: How King Arthur Defeated King Leodegrance's Enemies, and How His Royalty Was Made Known

[Figure ax: The gardener's boy takes off his cap — King Leodegrance, robed and crowned, sits at his table in a dark room, while behind him, by the window, the gardener's boy stands lifting the cap from his head.]

*The Duke of North Umber and his six companions appear before the castle*.

Now, when the next day came, the Duke of North Umber and six knights who were his companions appeared on the field in front of the castle of Cameliard, just as he had declared that they would. And those seven champions appeared in very great state. In front of them rode seven heralds with trumpets and tabards, and behind them rode seven squires, each squire carrying the spear, the shield, the crest, and the banner of the knight who was his lord and master. The seven heralds blew their trumpets so tremendously loud that the sound reached the farthest corners of Cameliard, and people came running from everywhere. And while the heralds blew their trumpets, the seven squires shouted and waved the spears and the banners. So those seven knights rode in such proud state that those who watched them had hardly ever seen so splendid a show of chivalry.

So they paraded up and down that field three times, along its entire length. Meanwhile a great crowd of people, called there by the blowing of the heralds' trumpets, stood on the walls and gazed down at that noble spectacle. And all the Court of King Rience came out and stood on the plain in front of the King's pavilion, and they shouted and cheered for the Duke of North Umber and his six companions.

Meanwhile King Leodegrance of Cameliard was so cast down with trouble and shame that he did not wish to show his face, but hid himself away from his whole Court. Nor would he allow anyone to come into his presence at that time.

*The Lady Guinevere cheers her father*.

Nevertheless, the Lady Guinevere went to him with several of her damsels. And when the King would not let her in, she spoke to him through the door and gave him words of good cheer. "My lord King and father," she said, "I beg you to look up and take heart. For I promise you that there is one who has our cause in his hands, and that one is truly a very glorious champion. He will surely come by and by, before this day is over, and when he comes he will certainly overthrow our enemies."

But King Leodegrance did not open the door. He said: "My daughter, you say these things to comfort me. For in this time of trouble there is no help for me except God alone, and His good strong help and grace." And she said: "No, what I say is the truth. And the help that God will send you, He will certainly send through a worthy champion who at this very moment has our cause in his hand."

So spoke the Lady Guinevere. And although King Leodegrance did not come out, he was greatly comforted by what she had said to him.

*Five knights-defender appear at the field*.

So the whole morning went by, and part of the afternoon, and still no one appeared to take up the challenge the seven knights had declared. But while the sun was still three or four hours high, a cloud of dust suddenly appeared a great way off. And out of that cloud of dust there soon appeared five knights, riding toward the castle at great speed. When they came near the walls, the people saw that the one who rode in front of all the others was that same White Champion who had overthrown the Duke of North Umber before. They saw, too, that the four knights riding with the White Champion were very famous knights, of great prowess and glory in arms. For one was Sir Gawain, and another was Sir Uwain, and another was Sir Geraint, and another was Sir Pelleas. The people of the castle and the town knew those four knights, because they had stayed at Cameliard for two days; and they were so very renowned that folk crowded in from far and near to look at them whenever they went out walking.

So when the people on the walls saw who those knights were, and when they recognized the White Champion who had already brought them such great honor, they shouted aloud a second time, with a voice even mightier than the one they had shouted with the first time.

Now King Leodegrance heard the people shouting, and all at once hope woke up inside him. So he came out at once, as fast as he could, to see what was happening, and there he saw those five noble champions about to ride onto the field below the castle walls.

The Lady Guinevere heard the shouting too, and she came out also. And there was the White Champion, and those four other knights with him! When she saw that White Knight and his four companions-in-arms, her heart was ready to burst inside her for pure joy and gladness, so that she wept with the strength of her feeling, and laughed while she wept. She waved her handkerchief to those five noble lords and kissed her hand to them, and the five knights saluted her as they rode past her and onto the field.

Now when the Duke of North Umber learned that these five knights had come against him and his companions to take up his challenge, he came out of his pavilion at once and mounted his horse. And his companions came out and mounted their horses, and he and they rode out to meet those who had come against them.

*The Duke of North Umber refuses the combat*.

When the Duke of North Umber had come near enough, he saw that the leader of those five knights was the White Champion who had overthrown him before. So he said to the White Champion: "Sir Knight, I have already lowered myself once to deal with you, though you are a complete stranger to me and to everyone else here. Without asking anything about your rank, I ran a course with you, and by the luck of arms you overthrew me. Now this quarrel is more serious than that one. So my companions-in-arms and I will not run a course with you and your companions, nor will we fight with you at all, until I first know the rank of the man I am to fight. So I order you to declare yourself right now: who you are, and what your rank is."

Then Sir Gawain opened the visor of his helmet and said: "Sir Knight, look at my face, and know that I am Gawain, the son of King Lot. So you can see that my rank and station are even higher than your own. Now I declare to you that the White Knight over there is of such high rank that it is he who lowers himself when he fights with you, and not you who lower yourself to him."

"Ho, Sir Gawain!" said the Duke of North Umber. "What you say is a very strange thing, for there are truly few people in this world so exalted that they lower themselves in dealing with me. Even so, since you vouch for him, I cannot dispute what you say. But there is still another reason why we cannot fight with you. For look: we are seven tried and famous knights, and you are only five. So consider how unequal our forces are, and what great danger you are in by taking on so perilous an encounter."

Then Sir Gawain smiled very grimly at that Duke of North Umber. "Many thanks for your pity, Sir Duke," he said, "and for the tender concern you show for our safety. But even so, you may leave that matter to us, with your mind completely at ease. For I consider that the danger you seven are in is fully equal to ours. What's more, if you were anything but a belted knight, a plain man might suppose that you were more worried about your own safety in this affair than about ours."

At these words the face of the Duke of North Umber turned red all over, for he knew that in truth he had no great wish for this battle, and so he was ashamed at what Sir Gawain had said to him. Then each knight closed his helmet, and they all turned their horses. One party rode to one end of the field, and the other party rode to the other end, and there each took its stand in the place assigned to it.

They arranged themselves like this: King Arthur was in the middle, with two knights on either side of him; and the Duke of North Umber was in the middle, with three knights on either side of him. When they had lined up in this way, they set their spears and their shields and made themselves completely ready for the charge. Then King Arthur and Duke Mordaunt each gave a great shout, and the one party hurled itself against the other with such violence that the ground shook and thundered under the horses' hoofs, and the clouds of dust rose up toward the sky.

And so they met in the middle of the field with a crash so dreadful and violent that you could have heard it more than a mile away.

When the one party had passed the other, and the dust of the collision had risen, three of the seven had been overthrown, and not one of the five had lost his seat.

*King Arthur overthrows the Duke of North Umber*.

One of those who had been overthrown was Duke Mordaunt of North Umber. And he never got up again from the ground where he lay. For King Arthur had aimed his spear at the very center of his defenses, and the spear had held. Its point had pierced the Duke of North Umber's shield, and pierced his body armor; and the stroke was so violent that the Duke of North Umber had been lifted clean out of his saddle and hurled a full spear's length behind the tail of his horse. So that wicked man died. For as King Arthur rode past him, his evil soul left his body with a thin little noise like the squeaking of a bat, and the world was well rid of him.

Now when King Arthur turned around at the end of the course and saw that only four knights were left on their horses, out of all the seven he and his companions had charged against, he raised his spear, reined in his horse, and spoke to his knights like this: "Sirs, I am weary of all this fuss and quarreling, and I do not care to fight any more today. So go at once and do battle with those knights. As for me, I will wait here and watch your adventure."

"Lord," they said, "we will do our best, as you command."

So those four good knights did as he commanded, and they rode out at once against the other four, greatly encouraged that their King was watching what they did. And King Arthur sat with the butt of his spear resting on his foot and looked out over the field with great contentment of spirit and a steady face.

As for the four companions of the Duke of North Umber's party who were left, they did not come out to this second encounter with nearly as much eagerness as they had the first time. For now they knew very well how great the prowess of those other knights was, and they saw that their enemies were coming at them for this second encounter very fiercely, with great valor and eagerness. So their hearts melted away inside them with doubt and worry about how this second encounter would end.

Nevertheless, they made themselves ready with what courage they could, and came out as they were bound to do.

Then Sir Gawain rode straight at the foremost knight, a very well-known champion named Sir Dinador of Montcalm. And when he had come close enough to him, he raised himself up in his stirrups and struck Sir Dinador so fierce a blow that he split that knight's shield in two, and split his helmet, and a piece of the blade of his sword broke off and stuck fast in it.

*The knights-challenger flee before the knights-defender*.

When Sir Dinador felt that blow, his brains swam like water, and he had to catch hold of the horn of his saddle to keep himself from falling off. Then a great terror fell on him all at once, so that he wrenched his horse's rein to one side. And so he fled away from that place, with the terror of death hanging over him like a black cloud of smoke. And when his companions saw the stroke Sir Gawain had dealt, and saw Sir Dinador flee away from him, they too wrenched their reins to one side and fled away as fast as they could, filled with utter terror of their enemies. And Sir Gawain and Sir Uwain and Sir Geraint and Sir Pelleas chased them as they fled. They chased them straight through the Court of King Rience, so that the knights and nobles of that Court scattered this way and that like chaff before them. They chased those fleeing knights in among the pavilions of King Rience's Court, and no one stopped them. And when they had chased those knights clean away, they came back to the place where King Arthur still kept his post, steadily waiting for them.

Now when the people of Cameliard saw their enemies overthrown, and saw how those enemies fled away before the faces of their champions, they shouted with all their might and cheered and cheered. Nor did they stop their loud shouting when those four knights came back from chasing their enemies and rejoined the White Champion. And they cheered still more when those five knights rode across the drawbridge, and through the gateway of the town, and into the town itself.

So ended that great contest of arms, which was one of the most famous in all the history of chivalry of King Arthur's Court.

*King Arthur returns his armor to the merchant*.

Now when King Arthur had done what he had set out to do, and had come back into the town, he went to the merchant from whom he had gotten the armor he wore, and he gave that armor back to him. And he said, "Tomorrow, Sir Merchant, I will send you two bags of gold as rent for the armor you let me have."

To this the merchant said: "Lord, there is no need for you to repay me for that armor, for you have done Cameliard great honor by your prowess."

But King Arthur said: "Enough, Sir Merchant. You must not forbid what I say. So take what I will send you."

Then he went on his way, and, after setting his cap of disguise on his head, he came back into the Lady Guinevere's gardens once more.

Now when the next morning came, the people of Cameliard looked out, and saw that King Rience had gone clean away from in front of the castle. For that night he had taken down his pavilions, withdrawn his Court, and left the place where he and his people had camped for the past five days. He had taken the body of the Duke of North Umber with him, carrying it away on a litter surrounded by many lighted candles and borne along with strange and solemn ceremony. And when the people of Cameliard saw that he was gone, they were overjoyed, and made merry, and shouted and sang and laughed. For they did not know how deeply enraged King Rience was against them. His hatred of King Leodegrance before had been only a small flame compared with the anger that now possessed him.

That morning the Lady Guinevere walked into her garden, and Sir Gawain and Sir Uwain walked with her. And there she saw the gardener's boy again.

At that she laughed aloud, and said to those two knights, "Sirs, look! There is the gardener's boy, who wears his cap all the time because he has an ugly place on his head."

Then those two knights, who knew who that gardener's boy was, were terribly embarrassed at her words, and did not know what to say or where to look. And Sir Gawain spoke aside to Sir Uwain and said: "Heaven help us, that lady has no idea what kind of man that gardener's boy is. If she did, she would be more careful with her words."

The Lady Guinevere heard Sir Gawain speak, but she did not catch his words. So she turned to Sir Gawain and said: "Sir Gawain, perhaps it offends you that the gardener's boy should wear his cap in front of us, and maybe you will go and take it off his head, as you offered to do two or three days ago."

And Sir Gawain said: "Hush, Lady! You do not know what you are saying. That gardener's boy could more easily take my head off my shoulders than I could take his cap off his head."

At this the Lady Guinevere laughed openly. But in her heart she secretly turned that saying over and over, and wondered greatly what Sir Gawain meant by it.

*King Rience threatens King Leodegrance*.

Now about noon that day a herald came from King Rience of North Wales, and he appeared boldly before King Leodegrance where the King sat in his hall with a number of his people around him. And the herald said: "My lord King, my master, King Rience of North Wales, is greatly displeased with you. For you set certain knights upon Duke Mordaunt of North Umber, and those knights have killed that excellent nobleman, who was a close kinsman of King Rience. What is more, you have made no reply to the demands my master, King Rience, has made concerning the handing over to him of certain lands and castles on the border of North Wales. And so my master is offended with you beyond measure. My master, King Rience, therefore commands me to put two conditions before you, and these are the conditions: first, that you immediately hand over to him the White Knight who killed the Duke of North Umber; and second, that you promise at once that the lands in question will be handed over to King Rience without delay."

Then King Leodegrance rose from where he sat and spoke to the herald with great dignity. "Sir Herald," he said, "the demands King Rience makes on me pass all bounds of insolence. The death the Duke of North Umber suffered, he suffered because of his own pride and folly. Nor would I hand the White Knight over to your master, even if I were able to. As for the lands your master demands of me, you may tell King Rience that I will not hand over to him so much as a single blade of grass, or a single grain of corn that grows there."

And the herald said: "If that is your answer, King Leodegrance, then I am ordered to tell you that my master, King Rience of North Wales, will soon come here with a great army and take from you by force the things you will not give him in peace." And having said this, he left that place and went on his way.

*King Leodegrance talks with the Lady Guinevere*.

Now after the herald had gone, King Leodegrance went into his private room, and when he got there he sent secretly for the Lady Guinevere. So the Lady Guinevere came to him there. And King Leodegrance said to her: "My daughter, it has happened that a knight dressed all in white, and bearing no crest or sign of any kind, has twice come to our rescue and overthrown our enemies. Now everyone says that this knight is your own particular champion, and I hear that he wore your necklace as a favor when he first rode out against the Duke of North Umber. So I ask you, daughter, tell me who that White Champion is, and where he can be found."

At this the Lady Guinevere was overwhelmed with confusion, and she looked away from her father's face and said: "Truly, my Lord, I do not know who that knight may be."

Then King Leodegrance spoke very seriously to the Lady Guinevere. He took her by the hand and said: "My daughter, you have now come to the age when you must think of being married to a man who will cherish you as he should and protect you from your enemies. For I am growing old fast, and I cannot hope to defend you forever from the dangers that surround people of our rank. Besides, since King Arthur (who is a very great King indeed) has brought peace to this realm, all that noble court of chivalry that once gathered around me has scattered to other places, where greater adventures can be found than in my peaceful kingdom. So (as all the world has seen this past week) I now have not one single knight I can depend on to defend us in times of danger like these that now overshadow us. Now, my daughter, it seems to me that you could not hope to find anyone who could keep you as safe as this White Knight, for he truly seems to be a champion of extraordinary prowess and strength. So it would be well if you found your heart leaning toward him, as his seems to lean toward you."

Then the Lady Guinevere turned rosy red, as if with fire, all the way down to her throat. And she laughed, though the tears overflowed her eyes and ran down her cheeks. So she wept, and yet laughed as she wept. And she said to King Leodegrance: "My Lord and father, if I give my heart to anyone in the way you speak of, I will give it only to the poor gardener's boy who digs in my garden."

At these words King Leodegrance's face twisted up with violent anger, and he cried out: "Ha, Lady! Would you make a mockery and a joke of what I say?"

Then the Lady Guinevere said: "Indeed, my Lord, I am not joking and I am not mocking. And I tell you truly that this same gardener's boy knows more about the White Champion than anybody else in the whole world." Then King Leodegrance said: "What is this you are telling me?" And the Lady Guinevere said: "Send for that gardener's boy, and you will find out." And King Leodegrance said: "Truly, there is more in this than I can understand just now."

So he called the chief of his pages, whose name was Dorisand, and said to him: "Go, Dorisand, and bring the gardener's boy here from the Lady Guinevere's garden."

So Dorisand the page went as King Leodegrance commanded, and in a little while he came back, bringing the gardener's boy with him. And Sir Gawain and Sir Uwain and Sir Pelleas and Sir Geraint came with them. Those four lords stood across the room by the door where they had come in, but the gardener's boy came and stood beside the table where King Leodegrance sat. The King raised his eyes and looked at the gardener's boy, and he said: "Ha! Would you wear your cap in our presence?"

Then the gardener's boy said: "I cannot take off my cap."

But the Lady Guinevere, who stood beside King Leodegrance's chair, spoke up and said: "I beg you, Sir, to take off your cap to my father."

At that the gardener's boy said: "At your bidding I will take it off."

*King Arthur reveals himself to King Leodegrance*.

So he took the cap off his head, and King Leodegrance looked at his face and knew him. And when he saw who it was standing before him, he cried out aloud in pure amazement. "My Lord and my King!" he said. "What is this?" Then he rose from where he sat, and went and knelt on the ground before King Arthur. He put the palms of his hands together and placed his hands between the hands of King Arthur, and King Arthur took the hands of King Leodegrance in his own. And King Leodegrance said: "My Lord! My Lord! Is it you, then, who has done all these wonderful things?"

Then King Arthur said: "Yes. Such as those things were, I have done them." And he bent down and kissed King Leodegrance on the cheek, and lifted him to his feet, and gave him words of good cheer.

Now the Lady Guinevere, when she saw these things happening, was astonished beyond measure. And all at once she understood everything with amazing clearness. A great fear fell on her, so that she trembled all over and said to herself: "What things have I said to this great King! How I have mocked him and made a joke of him in front of everyone around me!" And at the thought of it she pressed her hand to her side to still the wild beating of her heart. So, while King Arthur and King Leodegrance exchanged royal greetings and compliments, she slipped away and went and stood across the room by the window, near the corner of the wall.

Then, after a while, King Arthur raised his eyes and saw her standing there far off. So he went straight to her, took her by the hand, and said: "Lady, how are you?"

And she said: "Lord, I am afraid of your greatness." And he said: "No, Lady. Rather it is I who am afraid of you. For your kind regard is dearer to me than anything else in all the world. Otherwise I would not have served for these twelve days as a gardener's boy in your garden, all for the sake of your good will." And she said: "You have my good will, Lord." And he said: "Do I have your good will in great measure?" And she said: "Yes, you have it in great measure."

*King Arthur is betrothed to the Lady Guinevere*.

Then he bent his head and kissed her in front of everyone who was there, and so their promise to marry was pledged.

Then King Leodegrance was filled with such great joy that he did not know how to contain himself.

Now, after these things, there followed a war with King Rience of North Wales. For Sir Kay and Sir Ulfius had gathered a great army, as King Arthur had told them to do, so that when King Rience came against Cameliard he was completely routed, his army was scattered, and he himself was chased, an outcast, into his mountains.

Then there was great rejoicing in Cameliard. For after his victory King Arthur stayed there for a while with a wonderfully splendid Court of noble lords and beautiful ladies. And there was feasting and jousting and many famous contests of arms, the like of which those parts had never seen before. And King Arthur and the Lady Guinevere were completely happy together.

Now one day, while King Arthur sat at a feast with King Leodegrance, and the two of them were brimming over with good cheer, King Leodegrance said to King Arthur: "My Lord, what shall I offer you as a dowry with my daughter, when you take her away from me to be your Queen?"

Then King Arthur turned to Merlin, who stood near him, and said: "Ha, Merlin! What shall I ask of my friend for that dowry?"

Merlin said to him: "My lord King, your friend King Leodegrance has one thing which, if he gives it to you, will greatly increase the glory and renown of your reign, so that its fame will never be forgotten."

And King Arthur said: "I bid you, Merlin, tell me what that thing is."

So Merlin said: "My lord King, I will tell you a story:

*Merlin tells of the Round Table*.

"In the days of your father, Uther Pendragon, I had a certain table made for him in the shape of a ring, and so men called it the Round Table. At this table there were seats for fifty men, and these seats were meant for the fifty knights who were the most worthy knights in all the world. The seats were of such a kind that whenever a worthy knight appeared, his name appeared in letters of gold on the seat that belonged to him; and when that knight died, his name would suddenly vanish from the seat he had once sat in.

"Now forty-nine of these seats were all alike (except only one that was set aside for the King himself, which was raised above the other seats and was skillfully carved and inlaid with ivory and gold), and the one seat was different from all the others, and it was called the Seat Perilous. For this seat was unlike the others both in how it was made and in what it meant. It was all skillfully set with gold and silver in strange designs, and it was covered with a canopy of satin embroidered with gold and silver, and it was altogether wonderfully magnificent to look at. And no name ever appeared on this seat, for only one knight in all the world could hope to sit in it and be safe. If any other knight dared to sit in it, either he would die a sudden and violent death within three days, or else some great misfortune would fall on him. That is why the seat was called the Seat Perilous.

"Now in the days of King Uther Pendragon, thirty-seven knights sat at the Round Table. And when King Uther Pendragon died, he gave the Round Table to his friend, King Leodegrance of Cameliard.

"And at the beginning of King Leodegrance's reign, twenty-four knights sat at the Round Table.

"But times have changed since then, and the glory of King Leodegrance's reign has grown pale beside the glory of your reign, so that his noble Court of knights has left him altogether. And so now not one name remains on all the fifty seats around the Round Table, except only the name of King Leodegrance. So now the Round Table lies under its pavilion, entirely unused.

"Yet if King Leodegrance will give you that Round Table, my lord King, as a dowry with the Lady Guinevere, it will lend your reign its greatest glory. For in your day every seat at that Table will be filled, even the Seat Perilous, and the fame of the knights who sit at it will never be forgotten."

"Ha!" said King Arthur. "That would indeed be a dowry worthy of any king to have with his queen."

*King Leodegrance gives the Round Table to King Arthur*.

"Then," said King Leodegrance, "you shall have that dowry with my daughter. And if it brings you great glory, then your glory will be my glory, and your renown will be my renown. For if my glory fades and your glory grows, why, is my child not your wife?"

And King Arthur said: "You speak well and wisely."

So King Arthur became the master of that famous Round Table. And the Round Table was set up at Camelot (which some people now call Winchester). And by and by there gathered around it such a company of knights as the world had never seen before that time, and will never see again.

That was the story of the beginning of the Round Table in King Arthur's reign.
