Chapter 4: What Happened to Sir Accolon, and How King Arthur Fought a Duel with Swords and Nearly Lost His Life

[Figure bl: Queen Morgan loses Excalibur's sheath — From the shore Morgan flings the jeweled sheath out over the lake, and a hand rises from the water to catch it.]

Here follows the account of what happened to Sir Accolon on the morning after he went aboard that magic ship with King Arthur, as I have told you.

*Sir Accolon finds himself beside a fountain*.

Now when Sir Accolon woke from that same sleep, it was with him just as it had been with King Arthur: at first he did not know whether he was still asleep and dreaming or whether he was awake. For he found himself lying beside a marble basin of clear water that gushed up very high from a silver pipe. And he saw that not far from this fountain stood a large pavilion of many-colored silk, at the edge of a fair green meadow.

Sir Accolon was utterly astonished to find himself in this place, when he had fallen asleep on board that ship, and he was afraid that all this was the work of some very evil spell. So he crossed himself and said, "God save King Arthur from all harm, for it seems to me that those damsels on that ship have worked some magic on us to separate us from each other." And as he said this, he got up from where he lay, meaning to look further into the matter.

Now, as he made some noise in stirring himself, a very ugly dwarf came out of the pavilion and greeted him with every courtesy and with great respect. Then Sir Accolon said to the dwarf, "You there, who are you?" And the dwarf answered, "Sir, I belong to the lady of that pavilion over there, and she has sent me to bid you welcome to this place and to invite you in to share a meal with her." "Ha!" said Sir Accolon, "and how did I come here?" "Sir," said the dwarf, "I do not know. But when we looked out this morning we saw you lying here beside the fountain."

Then Sir Accolon marveled greatly at what had happened to him, and after a while he said, "Who is your lady?" And the dwarf replied, "She is named the Lady Gomyne of the Fair Hair, and she will be more than glad of your company in her pavilion."

*Sir Accolon enters the pavilion of the Lady Gomyne*.

At this Sir Accolon rose, and when he had washed himself at the fountain and so refreshed himself, he went with the dwarf to the lady's pavilion. And when he came there he saw that in the middle of the pavilion stood a table of silver, spread with a fair white cloth and covered with very excellent food for a man to break his fast with.

The moment Sir Accolon came into the pavilion, the curtains on its far side were parted, and a very beautiful lady came in from an inner room and welcomed Sir Accolon to that place. And Sir Accolon said to her, "Lady, I think you are very kind to invite me into your pavilion like this." "No, sir," said the lady, "it takes no great effort to be kind to a knight as worthy as you." Then she said to Sir Accolon, "Sir, will you sit here at the table with me and have your breakfast?"

Sir Accolon was very glad of this, for he was hungry. And the lady's beauty pleased him a great deal, so that it gave him great joy to be in her company.

So the two of them sat at the table in a very cheerful and pleasant mood, and the dwarf waited on them.

Now after Sir Accolon and the Lady of the Pavilion had eaten their breakfast, she spoke to him like this: "Sir Knight, you seem to be a very strong and worthy lord, and one well used to feats of arms and to proving his courage in battle."

To this Sir Accolon replied, "Lady, it is not fitting for me to speak of my own worth. But this much I may freely say: I have taken part in a number of fights, as a belted and spurred knight may do, and I believe both my friends and my enemies have had reason to say that I have always done my duty to the best of my power."

Then the damsel said, "I believe you are a very brave and worthy knight. And being such a knight, you might be of service to a good and worthy knight who sorely needs the kind of service one knight can give another."

Sir Accolon said, "What is that service?" And the damsel replied, "I will tell you. Not far from this place there lives a certain knight named Sir Ontzlake, who has an elder brother named Sir Damas. This Sir Damas has treated Sir Ontzlake very badly in many ways and has robbed him of nearly all his inheritance, so that only a little is left to Sir Ontzlake of all the great possessions that once were his father's. But Sir Damas begrudges Sir Ontzlake even that small holding, so that Sir Ontzlake has to keep what he has by whatever force of arms he can manage himself. Now Sir Damas has found himself a champion, a man of great strength and courage, and through this champion Sir Damas challenges Sir Ontzlake's right to keep even that small part of the lands that once were his father's. So if Sir Ontzlake wants to keep what is his, he must soon fight a battle for it.

"Now this is a very sad case for Sir Ontzlake. For a short time ago he was wounded by a spear at a tournament and pierced through both his thighs, and so he cannot now sit on his horse and defend his rights against attack. So it seems to me that a knight could have no better chance to show his prowess than in defending a cause as sad as this one."

So the lady spoke, and Sir Accolon listened with great attention to everything she said. And when she had finished he said, "Lady, I would indeed be very willing to defend Sir Ontzlake's right. But I have no armor, and I have no weapons to fight with."

Then the damsel smiled very kindly at Sir Accolon and said to him, "Sir, Sir Ontzlake can easily fit you out with armor that will be entirely to your liking. And as for weapons, I have in this pavilion a sword that has only one other like it in all the world."

At this she rose and went back into the curtained room she had come from, and soon she came back carrying something wrapped in a scarlet cloth. And she opened the cloth before Sir Accolon's eyes, and what she had there was King Arthur's sword Excalibur, in his sheath. Then the damsel said, "This sword shall be yours if you will take up this quarrel on behalf of Sir Ontzlake."

*The Lady Gomyne shows Excalibur to Sir Accolon*.

*Sir Accolon agrees to fight for Sir Ontzlake*.

Now when Sir Accolon saw that sword he did not know what to think, and he said to himself, "Surely either this is Excalibur or else it is his twin brother." With that he drew the blade out of its sheath, and it shone with extraordinary splendor. Then Sir Accolon said, "I do not know what to think, for pure wonder. This sword is the very image of another sword that I know of." When he said this the damsel smiled at him again, and she said, "I have heard it said that there is another sword in the world like this one."

Then Sir Accolon said, "Lady, to win this sword for myself I would be willing to fight in any battle whatever." And the damsel replied, "Then if you will fight this battle for Sir Ontzlake, you may keep that sword for your own." And at that Sir Accolon was glad beyond all measure.

So it came about that, through the tricks of Queen Morgan le Fay, King Arthur was brought, without knowing it, to fight a battle against a knight he loved very much, and that knight had Excalibur to use against his own master. For all these things had happened through the cunning of Morgan le Fay.

Now a fair field was made ready for the battle, in a place that suited both Sir Damas and Sir Ontzlake. They came there on the day appointed, each with his champion and his attendants, Sir Ontzlake being carried there in a litter because of the bad wound in his thighs. A great many other people came as well to watch the combat, for the news of it had spread far and wide all around that place. So when everything was ready, the two knights who were to fight on that field were brought inside the barriers, each fully armed and each mounted on a very good horse.

Now King Arthur was dressed all in Sir Damas's armor, and Sir Accolon was dressed in armor that belonged to Sir Ontzlake. And each one's head was covered by his helmet, so that neither of the two knew the other.

Then the herald came forward and announced that the battle was about to begin, and each knight at once made himself ready for the charge. Then the word to charge was given, and the two rushed out, each from his own end of the field, with such speed and fury that it was wonderful to see. They met in the middle of the course with a roar like thunder, and each knight's spear burst into small pieces, right down to the stump he held in his hand. At that each knight leaped from his horse with great skill and quickness and let it run loose in the field. And each threw aside the stump of his spear and drew his sword, and they came at each other with all the fury of battle.

*Vivien comes to the field of battle*.

It was at this time that Vivien came to that place, at Merlin's bidding. And she brought with her a train of followers so splendid and so beautiful that a great many people looked at her with great pleasure. So Vivien and her followers took their stand at the barriers, where they could see everything that happened. And Vivien watched the two knights, and she could not tell which one was King Arthur and which was his enemy. So she said, "Well, I will do as Merlin wished me to do. But I must watch this battle for a while before I can tell which one is King Arthur, for it would be a pity to cast my spells on the wrong knight."

*King Arthur is badly wounded by Excalibur*.

So the two knights came together in battle on foot. First they thrust at each other, and then they both struck at the same moment. And King Arthur's sword did not bite into Sir Accolon's armor, but Sir Accolon's sword bit very deep into King Arthur's armor and wounded him so badly that the blood ran down inside his armor in great streams. After that they struck very often and very powerfully, and as it was at first, so it was afterward: Sir Accolon's sword always bit into King Arthur's armor, and King Arthur's sword did not bite into his enemy's armor at all. So in a little while King Arthur's armor was stained red all over with the blood that flowed from a great many wounds, while Sir Accolon did not bleed at all, because of the sheath of Excalibur that he wore at his side. And King Arthur's blood ran down onto the ground, so that all the grass around was soaked with it. So when King Arthur saw how the ground was wet with his own blood, while his enemy did not bleed at all, he began to fear that he would die in that battle, and he said to himself, "How is this? Has the power gone out of Excalibur and his sheath? If it were not so, I would think that the sword that cuts me so badly is Excalibur, and that this sword is not Excalibur."

Then a great despair of death came over him, and he ran at Sir Accolon and struck him so hard a blow on the helmet that Sir Accolon nearly fell to the ground.

But with that blow King Arthur's sword snapped off short at the crosspiece of the hilt and fell into the grass among the blood, and the pommel and the crosspiece were all that King Arthur held in his hand.

Now that blow made Sir Accolon wild with rage, and he ran at King Arthur meaning to strike him some terrible blow. But when he saw that King Arthur had no weapon, he stopped his attack and said, "Sir Knight, I see that you have no weapon and that you have lost a great deal of blood. So I demand that you yield to me as a beaten knight."

Then King Arthur was again greatly afraid that his death was near. Yet because he was a king, it was not possible for him to yield to any knight. So he said, "No, Sir Knight, I may not yield to you, for I would rather die with honor than yield without honor. For though I lack a weapon, there are particular reasons why I may not lack honor. So you may kill me as I stand here without a weapon, and that will be your shame and not mine."

"Well," said Sir Accolon, "as for the shame, I will not spare you unless you yield to me." And King Arthur said, "I will not yield." Then Sir Accolon said, "Then stand away from me, so that I may strike you." And when King Arthur had done as Sir Accolon told him, Sir Accolon struck him such a dreadful blow that the King fell to his knees. Then Sir Accolon raised Excalibur to strike King Arthur again, and at that all the people who were there cried out to him to spare so honorable a knight. But Sir Accolon would not spare him.

*Vivien puts a spell on Sir Accolon*.

Then Vivien said to herself, "Surely that must be King Arthur who is so near to death. And I vow that it would be a great pity for him to die after he has fought so fiercely." So when Sir Accolon raised his sword the second time to strike his enemy, Vivien struck her hands together with great force and at the same moment cast a spell of such power that it seemed to Sir Accolon, that very instant, as though he had been dealt a very heavy blow on the arm. For with that spell his arm went numb all the way from his fingertips to the hollow of his armpit, and at that Excalibur fell out of Sir Accolon's hands into the grass.

Then King Arthur looked at the sword, and he saw that it was Excalibur, and in that moment he knew that he had been betrayed. So he cried out three times in a very loud voice, "Treason! Treason! Treason!" And with that he set his knee on the blade, and before Sir Accolon could stop him he had seized it in his hands.

*King Arthur overcomes Sir Accolon*.

Then it seemed to King Arthur that a great power had come into him because of that sword. So he rose from his knees and ran at Sir Accolon and struck him so hard that the blade cut through his armor to the depth of half a hand's breadth. And he struck him again and again, and Sir Accolon cried out in a loud voice and fell down on his hands and knees. Then King Arthur ran to him and caught hold of the sheath of Excalibur and tore it away from Sir Accolon and flung it aside, and at once Sir Accolon's wounds burst out bleeding heavily. Then King Arthur seized Sir Accolon's helmet and wrenched it off his head, meaning to kill him.

Now because King Arthur was blinded by his own blood, he did not recognize Sir Accolon, and he said, "Sir Knight, who are you, who have betrayed me?" And Sir Accolon said, "I have not betrayed you. I am Sir Accolon of Gaul, and I am a knight in good standing at King Arthur's Court."

But when King Arthur heard this he cried out aloud and said, "How is this? Do you know who I am?" And Sir Accolon said, "No, I do not know you." Then King Arthur said, "I am King Arthur, your master." And at that he took off his helmet, and Sir Accolon knew him.

And when Sir Accolon saw King Arthur, he fainted away and lay like a dead man on the ground. And King Arthur said, "Take him away."

Then, when the people who were there understood who King Arthur was, they burst over the barriers and ran toward him with a great cry of pity. And King Arthur would have left that place, but at that moment he too fainted away from the great loss of blood that had come from him. And all those who stood around were filled with sorrow, thinking that he was dying, and they lamented without end.

Then Vivien came out into the field and said, "Let me have him, for I believe I shall be able to cure his wounds." So she commanded that two litters should be brought, and she placed King Arthur in one litter and Sir Accolon in the other, and she carried them both away to a priory of nuns, a convent that was not far from that place.

*Vivien heals King Arthur*.

When Vivien had come there, she examined King Arthur's wounds and bathed them with a very precious balm, so that they began to heal at once. As for Sir Accolon, she would have nothing to do with his wounds, but let one of her attendants bathe him and dress his hurts.

Now when the next morning came, King Arthur had recovered so much that he was able to get up, though he was very weak and sick nearly to death. So he rose from his couch, and he would not let anyone stop him, and he wrapped a cloak around himself and went to the place where Sir Accolon lay. When he came there he questioned Sir Accolon very closely, and Sir Accolon told him everything that had happened to him after he left the ship, and how the strange damsel had given him a sword to fight with. So when King Arthur had heard all that Sir Accolon had to tell him, he said, "Sir, I think you are not to blame in this matter. But I very much fear that there is treachery here, meant to bring about my ruin."

Then he went out from that place, and he found Vivien and said to her, "Damsel, I beg you to dress that knight's wounds with the same balm that you used to dress mine." "Lord," said Vivien, "I cannot do that, for I have no more of that balm." But what she said was false, for she did have more of that balm. She simply did not choose to use it on Sir Accolon.

*Sir Accolon dies of his wounds*.

So that afternoon Sir Accolon died of the wounds he had received in his battle with King Arthur.

And that same day King Arthur summoned Sir Damas and Sir Ontzlake to come before him. They came and stood before him, but they were so filled with terror of his majesty that they did not have the strength to stand, and they fell down on their knees to him.

*King Arthur deals with Sir Damas and Sir Ontzlake*.

Then King Arthur said, "I will pardon you, for you did not know what you were doing. But you, Sir Damas, are, I believe, a very false and treacherous knight. So I shall take from you all your possessions except the one single castle that your brother had, and that I shall give to you. But all your other possessions I shall give to Sir Ontzlake. And I further command that you shall never again have the right to ride any horse but a palfrey, for you are not worthy to ride a war horse as a true knight has the right to do. And I command you to set free at once all those knights who were my companions in captivity, and to pay them for all the harm you have done them, as a court of chivalry shall decide."

With that he dismissed the two knights, and they were very glad that he had dealt with them so mercifully.

Conclusion

*Queen Morgan comes to King Arthur*.

Now shortly after that combat between King Arthur and Sir Accolon, the news of it was brought to Queen Morgan le Fay. And the next day after that she heard that Sir Accolon was dead, and she could not understand how her plans could have gone so wrong. Then she was uncertain how much King Arthur might know of her treachery, so she said to herself, "I will go and see my brother the King, and if he knows of my treason, I will beg him to pardon my wrongdoing." So, after inquiring carefully where King Arthur lay, she gathered together her court of knights and squires and set out for that place.

She came there on the fifth day after the battle, and when she arrived she asked those who were attending the King how he was faring. They answered her, "He is asleep, and he must not be disturbed." To this Queen Morgan le Fay replied, "No matter. I am not to be kept out, for I must see him and speak with him at once." And they did not dare to stop her, because she was the King's sister.

*Queen Morgan steals the sheath of Excalibur*.

So Queen Morgan went into the room where the King lay, and he did not wake at her coming. Then Queen Morgan was filled with hatred and a great desire for revenge, and she said to herself, "I will take Excalibur and his sheath and carry them away with me to Avalon, and my brother shall never see them again." So she went very softly to where King Arthur lay, and she looked at him as he slept, and she saw that he had Excalibur beside him and was holding the hilt of the sword in his hand as he slept. Then Queen Morgan said, "Alas for this! For if I try to take Excalibur away from him, perhaps he will wake and kill me for my treason." Then she looked and saw where the sheath of Excalibur lay at the foot of the couch. So she took the sheath of Excalibur very softly and wrapped it up in her cloak and went out of the room, and King Arthur did not wake at her going.

So Queen Morgan came out of the King's chamber and said to those who were attending him, "Do not wake the King, for he is sleeping very soundly." With that she mounted her horse and rode away from that place.

Now after a good while King Arthur woke, and he looked for the sheath of Excalibur, but he saw that it was gone. So he said at once, "Who has been here?" Those attending him answered, "Queen Morgan le Fay has been here. She came in and saw you, and went her way without waking you." Then King Arthur's heart misgave him, and he said, "I fear that she has dealt treacherously with me from the beginning to the end of these adventures."

At that he got up and summoned all his knights and squires and mounted his horse to pursue Queen Morgan, although he was still very sick and faint from his deep wounds and his loss of blood.

Now, just as the King was about to set out, Vivien came to him where he was, and she said, "Lord, take me with you. For if you do not, you will never recover the sheath of Excalibur, and you will never catch up with Queen Morgan le Fay." And King Arthur said, "Come with me, damsel, in God's name." So Vivien went with him in pursuit of Queen Morgan.

Now after a while, as she fled, Queen Morgan le Fay looked behind her, and she saw that Vivien was riding with King Arthur's party. At that her heart failed her, and she said, "I fear that I am now completely ruined. For I helped that damsel to gain so much knowledge of magic that I shall have no spells to save myself from her counter-spells. But at any rate, King Arthur shall never have the sheath of Excalibur again to help him in his hour of need."

*Queen Morgan throws the sheath of Excalibur into the lake*.

Now at that time they were passing along the shore of a lake of considerable size. So Queen Morgan le Fay took the sheath of Excalibur in both her hands and swung it by its belt above her head, and she threw it far out into the water.

And then a very strange miracle happened. For suddenly a woman's arm rose out of the water, and it was clothed in white. And it was adorned with many bracelets. And the hand of that arm caught the sheath of Excalibur and drew it down under the water, and no one ever saw that sheath again.

So the sheath of Excalibur was lost, and that was a grievous thing for King Arthur in later days, as you may one day read.

*Queen Morgan works her magic*.

Now after Queen Morgan le Fay had thrown the sheath of Excalibur into the lake, she went on a little farther, to a very lonely place where a great many rocks and stones lay scattered about on the ground. And when she had come to that place she worked very powerful spells of magic that Merlin had taught her. And by means of those spells she turned herself and all her court and all their horses into large round stones of many different sizes.

Then, a little while later, King Arthur came to that place with his knights and squires. And his heart was very heavy, for he had seen from a great distance how Queen Morgan le Fay had thrown the sheath of Excalibur into the lake.

Now when the King and his Court had come to that spot, the damsel Vivien called out to him to stop, and she said to him, "Lord, do you see all those great round stones?" "Yes," said the King, "I see them." Then Vivien said, "Those stones are Queen Morgan le Fay and the court that was with her. For this magic she has worked, to change herself and them into stones, is a certain thing that Merlin taught her. Now I myself know that magic, and I also know how to undo it whenever I wish. So, if you will promise to punish that wicked woman at once for all her treason by taking her life, then I will bring her back to her true shape, so that you may have her in your power."

*King Arthur rebukes Vivien*.

Then King Arthur looked at Vivien with great displeasure, and he said, "Damsel, you have a cruel heart! You yourself have suffered no harm at the hands of Queen Morgan. Why, then, would you have me kill her? If it were not for all you have done for me, I would be very much offended with you. As for her, I forgive her all of this, and I shall forgive her again and again and yet again if she sins against me. For her mother was my mother, and the blood that flows in her veins and in mine comes from the same fountainhead, and so I will do no evil thing against her. Let us go back the way we came."

Then Vivien looked at King Arthur very bitterly, and she laughed with great scorn and said, "You are both a fool and a feeble-minded dotard." And with that she vanished from the sight of everyone.

And after that, because King Arthur had rebuked her for her wickedness in front of others, she hated him even more than Morgan le Fay had hated him.

Some time after that, King Arthur heard how Merlin had been tricked by Vivien, and he grieved very bitterly that Merlin was lost to the world in that way.

So ends the story of the passing of Merlin.
