Chapter 6: How King Arthur Was Married in Royal State, and How the Round Table Was Founded

[Figure ay: King Arthur meets the Lady Guinevere — On a cobbled street hung with banners, the King in a long patterned robe takes the hand of Guinevere, who stands under a canopy among her ladies and knights.]

And now the early fall of the year had come: that pleasant season when the meadows and the open uplands were still green with the summer that had only just passed; when the sky, too, was like a summer sky, wonderfully blue and full of great floating clouds; when a bird might sing here and another there, a short song in memory of springtime; when all the air was mild with warmth, and yet the leaves everywhere were turning brown and red and gold, so that when the sun shone through them it was as though a cloth of gold, embroidered with brown and crimson and green, hung over your head. At this season of the year it is a wonderful pleasure to be out in the fields among the nut trees with hawk and hound, or to travel far and wide through the yellow world, whether on horseback or on foot.

Now this was the time of year that had been set for the marriage of King Arthur and the Lady Guinevere at Camelot, and in that place there was wonderful pomp and glory and splendor. All the world was astir and bubbling over with joy, for everyone was very glad that King Arthur was going to have a Queen.

*How the town of Camelot was decorated*.

To prepare for that great occasion, the town of Camelot was decked out most magnificently. The stony street along which the Lady Guinevere must come to the King's royal castle was strewn thick with freshly cut rushes, smoothly laid. In many places it was spread besides with carpets of fine pattern, fit to lay on the floor of some handsome hall. All the houses along the way were hung with fine woven hangings worked with threads of azure and crimson, and everywhere flags and banners floated in the warm and gentle breeze against the blue sky. So all the world seemed alive with bright colors, and when you looked down that street, it was as though you saw a winding path of the greatest beauty and gaiety stretched out before you.

And so the King's wedding day came, bright and clear and wonderfully radiant.

King Arthur sat in his hall, surrounded by his Court, waiting for news that the Lady Guinevere was on her way. And about the middle of the morning a messenger came riding in haste on a milk-white horse. The clothes of that messenger and the trappings of his horse were all of cloth of gold embroidered with scarlet and white, and the messenger's tabard was set with many jewels of different kinds, so that he glittered from far off as he rode, a sight of rare splendor.

This herald came straight into the castle where the King sat waiting, and he said: "Arise, my lord King, for the Lady Guinevere and her Court are drawing near to this place."

At that the King rose at once with great joy, and immediately he rode out with his Court of Knights in great state. And as he went down that marvelously decorated street, all the people cheered aloud as he passed, so that he smiled and bowed his head from side to side; for that day he was happy beyond words and loved his people with a wonderful warmth.

So he rode on to the town gate, and out through it, and on into the country beyond, where the broad, well-trodden highway ran winding down beside the shining river between the willows and the osiers.

*Of the Court of the Lady Guinevere*.

And then King Arthur and those with him caught sight of the Princess's Court, appearing in the distance, and they rejoiced greatly and hurried forward with all speed. And as they came near, the sun fell on robes of silk and cloth of gold, and on golden chains and the jewels that hung from them, so that all that noble company around the Lady Guinevere's litter flashed and sparkled with dazzling brightness.

For seventeen of the noblest knights of the King's Court, in full armor, whom he had sent as an escort to the lady, rode in great splendor around the litter in which the Princess lay. The frame of that litter was of richly gilded wood, and its curtains and cushions were of crimson silk embroidered with threads of gold. And behind the litter, in gay and joyful array and shining with many colors, rode the Court of the Princess: her ladies-in-waiting, gentlemen, ladies, pages, and attendants.

So the King's party and the Lady Guinevere's drew closer and closer, until they met and mingled with each other.

*King Arthur greets the Lady Guinevere*.

Then King Arthur at once got down from his noble horse, and, clothed in all his royalty, he went on foot to the Lady Guinevere's litter, while Sir Gawain and Sir Uwain held his horse's bridle. One of her pages drew back the silken curtains of the litter, and King Leodegrance gave her his hand, and she stepped down at once, wrapped, as it were, in the most wonderful beauty. King Leodegrance led her to King Arthur, and King Arthur came to her and placed one hand beneath her chin and the other on her head, and bent his face and kissed her on her smooth cheek, which was warm and fragrant and as soft as velvet, and without any blemish at all. And when he had kissed her cheek, everyone there raised their voices in a great shout, crying out for joy that those two noble souls had met at last.

That was how King Arthur welcomed the Lady Guinevere and King Leodegrance, her father, on the highway below the walls of the town of Camelot, about a mile and a half from that place. And no one who was there ever forgot that meeting, for it was full of wonderful grace and noble courtliness.

Then King Arthur and his Court of knights and nobles brought King Leodegrance and the Lady Guinevere with great ceremony to Camelot and to the royal castle, where rooms were given to everyone, so that the whole place was alive with joy and beauty.

*King Arthur and the Lady Guinevere are married*.

And when high noon came, the whole Court went in great state and ceremony to the cathedral, and there, surrounded by wonderful magnificence, those two noble souls were married by the Archbishop.

And all the bells rang out joyfully, and all the people standing outside the cathedral cheered loudly, and then the King and the Queen came out, all shining, like the sun for splendor and like the moon for beauty.

*Of the feast at the King's castle*.

In the castle a great noonday feast was spread, and four hundred and eighty-six lordly and noble people sat down to it: kings, knights, and nobles, with queens and ladies in magnificent dress. And near the King and the Queen sat King Leodegrance and Merlin, and Sir Ulfius, and Sir Ector the trustworthy, and Sir Gawain, and Sir Uwain, and Sir Kay, and King Ban, and King Pellinore, and many other famous and high-born people, so that no one had ever seen such magnificent courtliness as was seen at that famous wedding feast of King Arthur and Queen Guinevere.

And that day was famous in the history of chivalry for another reason, too, for in the afternoon the famous Round Table was founded, and that Round Table was the very flower and the greatest glory of King Arthur's reign.

For about the middle of the afternoon the King and Queen, with Merlin going before them and all that splendid Court of kings, lords, nobles, and knights in full array following after, went in procession to the place where Merlin, partly by magic and partly by skill, had had a most wonderful pavilion built over the Round Table where it stood.

*Of the pavilion of the Round Table*.

And when the King and the Queen and the Court had gone inside, they were amazed at the beauty of that pavilion, for it seemed to them a great open space, like some marvelous Fairyland. The walls were all richly gilded and painted with wonderful figures of saints and angels, clothed in ultramarine and crimson, and all those saints and angels were shown playing on different musical instruments that seemed to be made of gold. Overhead, the roof of the pavilion was made to look like the sky, all of cerulean blue sprinkled with stars. And in the middle of that painted sky was an image, as it were, of the sun in his glory. And underfoot was a pavement all of marble, set in squares of black and white, and blue and red, and many other colors.

In the middle of the pavilion was a Round Table, with seats around it for exactly fifty people. At each of the fifty places was a golden chalice filled with fragrant wine, and at each place was a golden plate holding a small loaf of fine white bread. And when the King and his Court entered the pavilion, music suddenly began to play with a wonderful sweetness.

Then Merlin came and took King Arthur by the hand and led him away from Queen Guinevere. And he said to the King, "Look! This is the Round Table."

And King Arthur said, "Merlin, what I see is wonderful beyond telling."

*King Arthur is seated at the Round Table*.

After that Merlin showed the King the various marvels of the Round Table. First he pointed to a high seat, wonderfully made of precious woods and gilded, so that it was very beautiful, and he said, "Look, lord King: that seat is called the Seat Royal, and it is yours to sit in." And as Merlin spoke, letters of gold suddenly appeared on the back of that seat, and the letters of gold spelled out the name,

*Arthur, King*.

And Merlin said, "Lord, that seat may well be called the center seat of the Round Table, for truly you are the very center of all that is most worthy in true knighthood. And so that seat shall be called the center of all the other seats."

Then Merlin pointed to the seat that stood opposite the Seat Royal, and that seat too was wonderful to look at, as has been told before in this history. And Merlin said to the King: "My lord King, that seat is called the Seat Perilous, for only one man in all this world shall sit in it, and that man is not yet born on the earth. And if any other man dares to sit in it, he will suffer either death or some sudden and terrible misfortune for his boldness. That is why that seat is called the Seat Perilous."

"Merlin," said the King, "everything you tell me is so marvelous that it is beyond understanding. Now I beg you to hurry and find at once enough knights to fill this Round Table, so that my glory may be wholly complete."

Then Merlin smiled at the King, though not cheerfully, and said, "Lord, why are you in such a hurry? Know this: when every seat of this Round Table is filled, then your glory will be fully achieved, and from that moment your day will begin to decline. For when any man has reached the crown of his glory, then his work is done, and God breaks him, as a man might break a chalice from which a drink so perfect has been drunk that no lesser wine may ever be allowed to stain it. So when your work is done and ended, God will shatter the chalice of your life."

Then the King looked very steadily into Merlin's face and said, "Old man, what you say is always full of wonder, for you speak words of wisdom. Even so, since I am in God's hands, I do wish for my glory and for His good will to be accomplished, even though He should break me entirely when I have served His purposes."

"Lord," said Merlin, "you speak like a worthy king, and with a very great and noble heart. Even so, I cannot fill the Round Table for you at this time. For although you have gathered about you the very noblest Court of Chivalry in all of Christendom, there are only thirty-two knights here present who may be thought worthy to sit at the Round Table."

"Then, Merlin," said King Arthur, "I ask you to choose those thirty-two for me at once."

"I will do so, lord King," said Merlin.

*Merlin chooses the knights of the Round Table*.

Then Merlin looked around, and he saw King Pellinore standing a little way off. Merlin went to him and took him by the hand. "Look, my lord King," he said. "Here is the knight who, next to you yourself, is at this time the most worthy in all the world to sit at this Round Table. For he is very gentle in his bearing toward the poor and needy, and at the same time he is so terribly strong and skillful that I do not know whether you or he is more to be feared when knight meets knight in combat."

Then Merlin led King Pellinore forward, and on the high seat that stood at the left hand of the Seat Royal there suddenly appeared the name,

*Pellinore*.

The name was blazoned in letters of gold that shone with an extraordinary luster. And when King Pellinore took his seat, everyone standing around gave him a great and loud cheer that went on for a long time.

Then, after Merlin had chosen King Arthur and King Pellinore in this way, he chose from King Arthur's Court the following knights, thirty-two in all. These were the knights of great renown in chivalry who first founded the Round Table, and for that reason they were named "The Ancient and Honorable Companions of the Round Table."

To begin with, there were Sir Gawain and Sir Uwain, who were the King's nephews, and they sat near him on his right hand. There was Sir Ulfius, who held his seat for only four years and eight months, until the time of his death; after that Sir Gaheris, who was squire to his brother Sir Gawain, held that seat. And there was Sir Kay the Seneschal, who was the King's foster brother; and there was Sir Baudwin of Britain, who held his seat for only three years and two months, until his death, and after that Sir Agravain held that seat. And there were Sir Pelleas and Sir Geraint and Sir Constantine, son of Sir Caderes the Seneschal of Cornwall (the same Constantine who was king after King Arthur). And there were Sir Caradoc, and Sir Sagramore, called the Desirous, and Sir Dinadan, and Sir Dodinas, called the Savage, and Sir Bruin, called the Black, and Sir Meliot of Logres, and Sir Aglovale and Sir Durnore and Sir Lamorak (and these three young knights were sons of King Pellinore). And there were Sir Griflet and Sir Ladinas and Sir Brandiles and Sir Persavant of Ironside, and Sir Dinas of Cornwall, and Sir Brian of Listinoise, and Sir Palomides and Sir Degraine, and Sir Epinogres, the son of the King of North Umberland and brother of the enchantress Vivien, and Sir Lamiel of Cardiff, and Sir Lucan the Butler, and Sir Bedevere his brother (the same who carried King Arthur to the fairy ship when he lay so grievously wounded, near to death, after the last battle he ever fought). These thirty-two knights were the Ancient Companions of the Round Table. Others were added to them, until there were forty-nine in all, and then Sir Galahad was added, and with him the Round Table was made wholly complete.

Now as each of these knights was chosen, at the very moment Merlin took him by the hand, that knight's name suddenly appeared in letters of gold, very bright and shining, on the seat that belonged to him.

But when all had been chosen, King Arthur saw that the seat at the right hand of the Seat Royal had not been filled, and that it bore no name. And he said to Merlin: "Merlin, how is this? The seat at my right hand has not been filled, and it bears no name."

And Merlin said: "Lord, there will be a name on it in a very little while, and he who sits in it will be the greatest knight in all the world, until the knight comes who will fill the Seat Perilous. For he who is coming will surpass all other men in beauty and in strength and in knightly grace."

And King Arthur said: "I wish he were with us now." And Merlin said: "He is coming soon."

So the Round Table was founded with great pomp and great ceremony of state. First the Archbishop of Canterbury blessed each and every seat, moving from place to place surrounded by his holy attendants. The choir among them sang most musically in harmony, while others swung censers from which rose a very fragrant smoke of frankincense, filling that whole pavilion with a scent of heavenly blessedness.

And when the Archbishop had blessed every one of those seats, each chosen knight took his own place at the Round Table, and his squire came and stood behind him, holding above the knight's head the little banner with his coat of arms upon the spear point. And everyone standing around that place, knights and ladies alike, raised their voices in a loud cheer.

*Of the ceremony of installation of the Round Table*.

Then all the knights stood up, and each knight held up before him the cross of his sword hilt, and each knight repeated, word for word, what King Arthur spoke. And this was the covenant of their Knighthood of the Round Table: That they would be gentle to the weak; that they would be brave against the strong; that they would be terrible to the wicked and the evildoer; that they would defend the helpless who called on them for help; that all women should be sacred to them; that they would stand ready to defend one another whenever such defense was needed; that they would be merciful to all men; that they would be gentle in deed, true in friendship, and faithful in love. This was their covenant, and every knight swore to it on the cross of his sword, and as a sign of it he kissed the hilt. And at that, everyone standing around once more gave a loud cheer.

Then all the knights of the Round Table sat down, and each knight broke bread from the golden plate and drank wine from the golden chalice that stood before him, giving thanks to God for what he ate and drank.

And that was how King Arthur was married to Queen Guinevere, and how the Round Table was founded.

Conclusion

So ends this Book of King Arthur, which I have told with such joy of spirit that, now that I close this first part of my work, it is a very great pleasure to look forward to writing a second part, which follows right after.

In that part I shall tell the history of several very noble worthies who belonged to the Court of the King, and it seems to me a good thing to spend time with the history of such noble and honorable knights and gentlemen. For truly, anyone might well enjoy reading such a history, and hearing those worthies speak, and seeing how they behaved in times of trial and trouble. For their example will surely help all of us to behave in the same way when we find ourselves in the same case.
