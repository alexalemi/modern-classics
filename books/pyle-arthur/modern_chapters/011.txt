Chapter 1: How King Arthur Went to Tintagalon with Four of His Court, and How He Disguised Himself for a Secret Purpose

[Figure ar: The Lady Guinevere — Guinevere sits in a garden with long dark hair, a jeweled band on her head and a necklace at her throat, her hand raised as she speaks.]

[Figure as: Headpiece to Part Three: a crowned lady in a winged headdress holds up a sword before her in a woodland.]

*How King Arthur held a feast at Caerleon on Usk*.

Now one day King Arthur proclaimed a high feast, which was held at Caerleon on Usk. Many noble guests were invited, and an extremely splendid Court gathered at the King's castle. For at that feast there sat seven kings and five queens in royal state, and there were high lords and beautiful ladies of rank, sixty-seven of them in all; and there was a great crowd of those famous knights of the King's Court who were counted the most renowned in arms in all of Christendom. And of all this great gathering of kings, lords, and knights, not one man looked sideways at his neighbor with suspicion, but all were united in good fellowship. So when the young King looked around him and saw such peace and friendship among all these noble lords, where before there had been quarreling and ill will, he said to himself, "Truly, it is wonderful how this reign of mine has knit men together in kindness and good fellowship!" And because of thoughts like these, his spirit took wing like a bird and sang within him.

Now while the King sat feasting like this, suddenly there came a herald, a messenger from the west country. And the herald came and stood before the King and said, "Greetings to you, King Arthur!"

Then the King said, "Speak, and tell me what your message is."

*A herald-messenger comes from the west country*.

To this the herald replied: "I come from King Leodegrance of Cameliard, who is in great trouble. For this is how things stand. His enemy and yours, King Rience of North Wales (the one who once, in scorn of you, commanded you to send him your beard to trim his cloak), is making several demands of my master, King Leodegrance, and King Leodegrance is entirely unwilling to grant them. And King Rience of North Wales threatens to bring war into Cameliard because King Leodegrance does not grant those demands at once. Now King Leodegrance no longer has such a host of knights and armed men around him as he once had to defend his kingdom against attack. For since you in your majesty have brought peace to this realm, and have brought low the power of all the kings beneath you, those knights who once made the Court of King Leodegrance so famous have gone elsewhere to look for better chances to show their great courage and skill at arms than his peaceful Court can offer. And so my master, King Leodegrance, begs help from you, who are his King and High King."

King Arthur, and all the Court that feasted with him, listened to what the herald said in complete silence. And the King's face, which a moment before had been open and cheerful, grew clouded and dark with anger. "Ha!" he cried. "This is no good news, truly, that you have brought here to our feast. Now I will give your master, King Leodegrance, whatever help I can in this hour of need, and I will give it quickly. But tell me, sir herald, what things is it that King Rience demands of your master?"

"That I will tell you, Lord," said the herald. "First, King Rience demands from my master a great part of those lands of Cameliard that lie along the borders of North Wales. Second, he demands that the Lady Guinevere, the King's daughter, be given in marriage to Duke Mordaunt of North Umber, who is a kinsman of King Rience. And that Duke, though he is a mighty warrior, is so evil to look at, and so violent of temper, that I believe there is no one like him for ugliness or for madness of mood in all the world."

*King Arthur is very angry at the message the herald brings*.

Now when King Arthur heard what the messenger said, he was seized at once with an extraordinary fit of anger. His eyes seemed, as it were, to shoot out sparks of pure light; his face flamed like fire; and he ground his teeth together like the stones of a hand mill grinding grain. Then he rose at once from the chair where he sat and went out of that place, and everyone who saw his anger shuddered at it and turned their eyes away from his face.

Then King Arthur went by himself into an inner room of the castle, and there he walked up and down for a long while, and in all that time no one of his household dared come near him. And the reason for the King's fury was this. Ever since he had lain wounded and sick almost to death in the forest, he had remembered how the Lady Guinevere had suddenly appeared before him like some tall, straight, shining angel come down to him out of Paradise, all full of pity, and extremely beautiful. So at the thought of that wicked, mad Duke Mordaunt of North Umber demanding to marry her, he was seized with a rage so violent that it shook his spirit like a mighty wind.

So for a long while he walked up and down in his fury, as I have said, and no one dared come near him, but everyone stood far off, watching him from a distance.

Then, after a while, he gave orders that Merlin and Sir Ulfius and Sir Kay should come to him where he was. And when they had come, he talked with them for a good while. He told Merlin to make ready to go on a journey with him, and he told Sir Ulfius and Sir Kay to gather a large army of chosen knights and armed men, and to bring that army straight into the country around the royal castle of Tintagalon, which stands close to the borders of North Wales and of Cameliard.

So Sir Ulfius and Sir Kay set about doing as King Arthur commanded, and Merlin also set about doing as he commanded. And the next day King Arthur and Merlin set out for Tintagalon across the forest country of Usk, together with certain famous knights of the King's Court who had proved themselves the best at arms of all those around him: namely, Sir Gawain and Sir Uwain (who were the King's nephews), and Sir Pelleas, and Sir Geraint, the son of Erbin.

*How King Arthur came to Tintagalon*.

They traveled all that day and part of the next, and they met with no adventure or misadventure of any kind. And so at last they came to that large and noble castle called Tintagalon, which guards the country bordering on Cameliard and North Wales. Here King Arthur was welcomed with great rejoicing, for wherever the King went the people loved him very dearly. So the folk of Tintagalon were very glad when he came to them.

Now on the morning after King Arthur came to Tintagalon (the summer night having been very warm), he and Merlin were glad to get up early and go out to enjoy the dewy freshness of the early day. So in the cool of the morning they walked together in the garden, which was a very pleasant place, beneath the shadow of a tall, straight tower. And all around were many trees giving good shade, where the little birds sang sweetly in the cheerfulness of the summer weather.

And here King Arthur opened his heart very freely to Merlin, and he said: "Merlin, I truly believe that the Lady Guinevere is the most beautiful lady in all the world. And so my heart always seems to be completely filled with love for her, so much so that I think of her all day long (whether I am eating, or drinking, or walking, or sitting still, or going about my business), and I dream of her many times at night as well. And it has been this way with me, Merlin, ever since a month ago, when I lay sick in that hermit's cell in the forest, and she came and stood beside me like a shining angel out of Paradise. So I am not willing that any man other than I should have her for his wife.

*King Arthur opens his heart to Merlin*.

"Now I know very well that you are wonderfully skilled in those arts of magic that can change a man's appearance so that even those who know him best cannot recognize him. So I very much want you to disguise me in such a way that I can go into Cameliard without anyone knowing me, and live there in such a way that I can see the Lady Guinevere every day. For I tell you truly, I greatly wish to look upon her in such a way that she will not know in the least that I am watching her. I would also like to see for myself how great the dangers are that surround King Leodegrance, for the King is my very good friend."

"My Lord King," said Merlin, "it shall be as you wish, and this very morning I will disguise you so well that no one in all the world will be able to tell who you are."

So that morning, a little before the early hour of prayer, Merlin came to the King where he was and gave him a little cap. And the cap was of such a kind that when the King put it on his head, he took on in an instant the appearance of a rough country fellow from the farmlands. Then the King ordered a jerkin of coarse wool to be brought to him, and with this he covered his royal and knightly clothes, and with it he hid the golden collar and the jewel hanging from it, which he always wore around his neck. Then, putting the cap on his head, he at once took on the look of that peasant farmhand.

*King Arthur leaves Tintagalon in disguise*.

And so, completely disguised, he left Tintagalon without anyone knowing him, and made his way on foot to the town of Cameliard.

Now toward the slanting of the day he drew near that place, and there in front of him he saw a large and important town of many handsome houses with red walls and shining windows. The houses of the town all sat on a high, steep hill, one above another, and the town itself was surrounded by a great wall, high and strong. A great castle guarded the town, and the castle had very many towers and roofs. And all around the tower there were many lovely gardens and lawns and meadows, and several orchards and groves of trees with thick and pleasant shade. Now at that time of day the sky behind the tower was, as it were, one single flame of fire, so that the towers and the battlements of the castle, and its roofs and chimneys, stood out completely black against the brightness of the light. And great flocks of pigeons circled the towers of the castle, flying on and on against that fiery sky. And because King Arthur was weary from walking all day, it seemed to him that he had hardly ever in all his life seen so lovely and pleasant a place as that fine castle with its gardens and lawns and groves of trees.

*King Arthur comes to Cameliard*.

So King Arthur came to the castle of Cameliard in the guise of a poor peasant from the countryside, and no one in all the world knew who he was.

When he had reached the castle, he asked for the head gardener. And when he got to speak with the gardener, he begged to be taken on as a servant in that part of the garden that belonged to the Lady Guinevere's dwelling. The gardener looked him over and saw that he was tall and strong and well built, so he liked him very much and took him into his service, just as he wished.

And that is how King Arthur of Britain became a gardener's boy at Cameliard.

*King Arthur lives as gardener's boy at the castle*.

Now the King was very glad to be in that garden, for in this pleasant summer season the Lady Guinevere came every day to walk with her damsels among the flowers, and King Arthur, disguised as a peasant gardener's boy, saw her a great many times when she came there.

So King Arthur stayed in that place for more than a week, and he did not care at all that in all that time he enjoyed none of the splendor of a king, but was only a gardener's boy in the castle garden of Cameliard.

Now it happened one day, when the weather was very warm, that one of the damsels who waited on the Lady Guinevere got up very early in the morning, while the air was still cool and fresh. Leaving the Lady Guinevere still asleep, this damsel, whose name was Mellicene of the White Hand, went into the anteroom, and opening its window, looked out into the rose garden that lay next to the Lady Guinevere's private rooms.

Now in that place there stood a carved marble figure of a youth holding a marble pitcher in his arms, and a fountain of water, as clear as crystal, flowed out of the pitcher into a marble basin. The figure, and the fountain, and the marble basin into which the fountain flowed all lay beneath the shade of a linden tree. And all around grew a thick tangle of roses, so that the place was completely hidden except from those windows of the castle that were above it.

*The damsel sees a knight at the fountain*.

And so, as the damsel looked down toward it out of the window, she saw a very wonderful sight. For there was a strange knight, kneeling beside the fountain and bathing his face and his chest in its crystal water. The damsel saw that the sunlight fell down through the leaves of the linden tree and lay on that strange knight. And she saw that his hair and his beard were the color of red gold, shining wonderfully in the brightness of the morning. And she saw that his brow and his throat and his chest were as white as alabaster. And she saw that around his neck and shoulders there hung a golden collar of marvelous beauty, so that when the sunlight shone on it, it flashed like pure lightning.

So, seeing this strange sight, which was like a vision, the damsel Mellicene stood for a long while, quite spellbound with wonder and delight. She did not know whether what she saw was a dream or not, nor whether the one who sat there was a spirit or a man of flesh and blood.

Then, by and by, coming partly to herself after her astonishment, she drew back softly from the window, turned around, and ran swiftly down the turret stairs, and so came out into that lovely, blooming garden at the foot of the tower. She ran through the garden as fast and as quietly as she could, and so came down a pathway to the marble fountain and the linden trees and the rosebushes around it, where she had just now seen that strange knight bathing in the crystal water.

*The damsel finds only the gardener's boy*.

But King Arthur had heard the damsel coming, and had quickly put the cap back on his head. So when the damsel Mellicene got there, she found no one by the fountain but the gardener's boy. She demanded of him, "Who are you, fellow? And why are you sitting here by the fountain?"

And he answered her, "I am the gardener's lad who came here a little while ago to take service in this place."

"Then tell me, fellow," she said, "and tell me truly. Who was that young knight who was here beside the fountain just now, and where has he gone?" "Lady," he said, "there has been no one at this fountain today but me."

"No, fellow," she cried, "you are deceiving me, for I assure you that I saw with my own eyes, just now, a strange young knight sitting here bathing himself in the water of this fountain." And the gardener's boy said, "Lady, what I have told you is the very truth, for indeed no one has been here this morning but me. So if you think you saw anyone else, you are certainly mistaken."

At this the damsel fixed her gaze on him in great bewilderment. She wondered at it very much as well, for she could not altogether disbelieve him. And yet she could not entirely believe him either, because her eyes had seen what they had seen, and she knew she had not been mistaken. So she did not know what to think, and because she was so puzzled, she felt very annoyed with that gardener's boy. "Truly," she said, "if you are deceiving me, I will certainly make you suffer a great deal of pain, for I will have you whipped with cords." With that she turned and went away from that place, wondering greatly at that strange thing and what it could all mean.

That morning she told the Lady Guinevere everything she had seen, but the Lady Guinevere only laughed at her and teased her, telling her that she had been asleep and dreaming when she saw that vision. And indeed, the damsel herself had begun to think this must be so. All the same, after that she looked out of her window every morning, though for a long while she saw nothing, for King Arthur did not come back to that place for some time.

And so, by and by, there came another morning when she looked out of the window, and there sat that strange knight by the fountain once more, just as he had sat before. And he bathed his face and his chest in the water as he had done before. And he looked as handsome and as noble as he had looked before, and his hair and his young beard shone like gold in the sun as they had shone before. And this time she saw that his golden collar lay on the edge of the fountain beside him, and it sparkled with great splendor in the sunlight while he bathed his chest. Then, after the damsel had watched him for a good while, she ran as fast as she could to the room where the Lady Guinevere still lay, and she cried out loudly, "Lady! Lady! Wake up and come with me! For that same young knight I saw before is bathing himself at the fountain under the linden tree right now!"

Then the Lady Guinevere, wondering greatly, got up very quickly, and dressing herself in all haste, went with the damsel to the window that looked out on that part of the garden.

*The Lady Guinevere sees the knight at the fountain*.

And there she herself saw the young knight as he washed himself at the fountain. She saw that his hair and his beard shone like gold in the sunlight. She saw that his undershirt was of purple linen threaded with gold. And she saw that beside him lay that skillfully made collar of gold, set with many jewels of different colors, and the collar shone with great splendor where it lay on the marble rim of the fountain.

For a while she gazed, utterly astonished. Then she told the damsel Mellicene to come with her, and with that she turned and went down the turret stairs and out into the garden, just as her damsel had done before. Then, as that damsel had done, she hurried as fast as she could down the pathway toward the fountain.

But when she got there, she found no young knight, but only the gardener's boy, exactly as had happened to the damsel Mellicene before. For King Arthur had heard her coming, and had at once put that enchanted cap on his head. Then the Lady Guinevere wondered greatly to find only the gardener's boy there, and she did not know what to think of so strange a thing. So she demanded of him, just as Mellicene had done, where the young knight had gone whom she had seen there at the fountain just a moment ago. And the gardener's lad answered her as before: "Lady! There has been no one at this place at any time this morning but me."

Now when King Arthur had put on his cap as the Lady came, he had, in his great haste, forgotten his golden collar, and Guinevere saw it lying there, shining very brightly, beside the edge of the fountain. "What is this?" she said. "Would you dare to make a fool of me? Now tell me, you fellow, do gardeners' boys in the land you come from wear golden collars around their necks, like that collar lying over there beside the fountain? Now, if I had you soundly whipped, it would be no more than you deserve. But take that trinket over there and give it to the one it rightly belongs to, and tell him from me that it ill becomes a true belted knight to hide himself away in a lady's private garden." Then she turned with the damsel Mellicene and left that place and went back to her rooms.

And yet, all that day, as she sat over her embroidery, she never stopped marveling and wondering how it could be that the strange young knight had vanished so suddenly and left only the poor gardener's boy in his place. And for a long time she could not solve that strange riddle.

Then, all of a sudden, at the time when the heat of the day was sloping toward the cooler part of the afternoon, she sat up because of a thought that had come to her in an instant. So she called the damsel Mellicene to her, and told her to go and tell the gardener's lad to bring her a basket of fresh roses at once to decorate her tower room.

*The gardener's boy wears his cap before the Lady Guinevere*.

So Mellicene went and did as she was told, and after a good while the gardener's lad came carrying a great basket of roses. And he was wearing his cap on his head. And when all the damsels waiting on the Lady Guinevere saw that he wore his cap in her presence, they cried out against him, and Mellicene of the White Hand demanded of him, "What! What is this, Sir Lout? Do you know so little of what is owed to a king's daughter that you wear your cap even in the presence of the Lady Guinevere? Now I order you to take that cap off your head this instant."

And King Arthur answered her, "Lady, I cannot take off my cap."

The Lady Guinevere said, "And why can't you take off your cap, you surly fellow?"

"Lady," he said, "I cannot take off my cap because I have an ugly spot on my head."

"Then wear your cap," said the Lady Guinevere. "Only bring the roses to me."

*The Lady Guinevere discovers the knight of the fountain*.

So at her command he brought the roses to her. But when he had come near the lady, she suddenly snatched at the cap and plucked it off his head. And in that instant he was transformed, for instead of the gardener's boy there stood before the Lady Guinevere and her damsel the figure of a noble young knight with hair and beard like threads of gold. Then he let his basket of roses fall, so that the flowers were scattered all over the floor, and he stood and looked at everyone there. And some of the damsels waiting on the Lady Guinevere shrieked, and others stood still in pure amazement and did not know how to believe what their eyes were seeing. But not one of those ladies knew that the man she was looking at was King Arthur. Still, the Lady Guinevere remembered that this was the knight she had found so badly wounded, lying in the hermit's cell in the forest.

Then she laughed and tossed him back his cap. "Take your cap," she said, "and go on your way, you gardener's boy with the ugly spot on his head." She said this because she wanted to tease him.

But King Arthur did not answer her. Instead, very soberly, he put his cap back on his head at once. And so, taking on his humble disguise once more, he turned and left that place, leaving the roses scattered all over the floor just as they had fallen.

And after that, whenever the Lady Guinevere came upon the gardener's lad in the garden, she would say to her damsel, in a voice loud enough for him to hear, "Look! There is the gardener's lad who has an ugly spot on his head, so that he always has to wear his cap to hide it."

So she spoke openly, teasing him. But privately she told her damsels to say nothing about these things, and to keep everything that had happened to themselves.
