Chapter 1: How a Wounded Knight Came to King Arthur's Court, How a Young Knight Tried to Avenge Him and Failed, and How the King Took Up the Challenge Himself

[Figure am: In the Valley of Delight — King Arthur on his horse looks down at a fair damsel with long hair, who walks beside him in a flowering valley below a castle.]

[Figure an: Headpiece to Part Two: a knight with a lance rides at a gallop across a meadow, with a castle and trees behind him.]

*How King Arthur stayed in the Forest of Usk*.

Now it happened one pleasant time in the springtime season that King Arthur and his Court were making a royal journey through the part of Britain that lies close to the Forests of the Usk. The weather at that time was extremely warm, and so the King and his Court stopped in the forest, under the trees, in the cool and pleasant shade the place offered. And there the King rested for a while on a couch of rushes spread with scarlet cloth.

The knights who were with the Court at that time were Sir Gawain, and Sir Uwain, and Sir Kay, and Sir Pelleas, and Sir Bedevere, and Sir Caradoc, and Sir Geraint, and Sir Baudwin of Britain, and Sir Constantine of Cornwall, and Sir Brandiles, and Sir Mador de la Porte; and nowhere in the world could you have found a company of knights as noble and as high as these.

Now as the King lay dozing, and as these worthy knights sat talking cheerfully together, there came suddenly a great bustle and stir at the edge of the Court, and soon a very sad and sorrowful sight appeared. For a knight came toward them, badly wounded, held up on his horse by a golden-haired page dressed in white and azure. The knight's clothing and his horse's trappings were also of white and azure, and on his shield he bore the device of a single silver lily on a field of pure azure.

*How the wounded knight comes into the forest*.

But the knight was in a very pitiful state. His face was as pale as wax and hung down on his chest. His eyes were glazed and saw nothing of what went on around him, and his fine clothing of white and blue was all red with the lifeblood that ran from a great wound in his side. And as they came along, the young page wept and lamented so that it wrung your heart to hear him.

Now as they came near, King Arthur roused himself and cried out, "Alas! What sorrowful sight is this I see? Now hurry, my lords, and bring help to that knight; and you, Sir Kay, go quickly and bring that fair young page here, so that we may hear at once from his own lips what misfortune has happened to his lord."

So several of the knights hurried at the King's command and gave the wounded knight every help they could, and they carried him to King Arthur's own pavilion, which had been pitched a little way off. And when he had been brought there, the King's surgeon attended him at once, although his wounds were of such a kind that he could not hope to live very long.

Meanwhile Sir Kay brought that fair young page before the King where he sat, and the King thought he had hardly ever seen a more beautiful face. And the King said, "Please tell me, Sir Page, who your master is, and how he came to be in such a sad and pitiful state as the one we have just seen."

*The page tells the story of the wounded knight*.

"That I will, Lord," said the youth. "You must know that my master is called Sir Myles of the White Fountain, and that he comes from the country to the north of here, a good distance away. In that country he is the lord of seven castles and several noble estates, so, as you can see, he is a man of considerable importance. A fortnight ago (moved to it, no doubt, by the high spirits of the springtime), he set out with only me as his squire, for he had a mind to seek adventure as a good knight-errant should. And we had several adventures, and in every one of them my lord was completely successful; for he overcame six knights in different places and sent them all to his castle to bear witness to his valor before his lady.

"At last, this morning, at a certain place a good distance from here, we came upon a fair castle in the forest. It stood in a valley surrounded by open stretches of level lawn, scattered with many flowers of every kind. There we saw three fair damsels tossing a golden ball from one to another. The damsels were dressed all in flame-colored satin, and their hair was the color of gold. And as we came near them they stopped their game, and the one who was the chief of those damsels called out to my lord, asking him where he was going and what his errand was.

"My lord answered her that he was a knight-errant in search of adventure. At this the three damsels laughed, and the one who had spoken first said, 'If you are looking for adventure, Sir Knight, perhaps I can help you to one that will satisfy you to your heart's content.'

"To this my master replied, 'Please, fair damsel, tell me what that adventure may be, so that I can try it at once.'

"Then this lady told my master to take a certain path and to follow it for a league or a little more, about three miles. He would then come, she said, to a stone bridge that crossed a violent stream, and she promised him that there he would find adventure enough to satisfy any man.

"So my master and I went that way, as the damsel had directed us, and after a while we came to the bridge she had spoken of. And there, beyond the bridge, stood a lonely castle with a tall, straight tower, and in front of the castle was a wide and level lawn of well-trimmed grass. Just beyond the bridge stood an apple tree hung all over with a great many shields. And halfway across the bridge hung a single shield, entirely black, and beside it hung a hammer of brass; and beneath the shield these words were written in red letters:

*Whoever strikes this shield does so at his peril*.

"Now my master, Sir Myles, when he read those words, went straight to that shield, seized the hammer that hung beside it, and struck it such a blow that it rang like thunder.

"At that, as if in answer, the portcullis of the castle was raised, and a knight came riding out at once, armored all in black from head to foot. His clothing and the trappings of his horse, and all its gear besides, were black as well.

*The page tells of the Sable Knight*.

"Now when that Sable Knight saw my master, he came riding swiftly across the meadow to the far end of the bridge. When he got there he drew rein, saluted my master, and called out, 'Sir Knight, I demand to know why you struck that shield. Now let me tell you this: because of your boldness, I will take your own shield from you and hang it on that apple tree, where you can see all those other shields hanging.' To this my master replied, 'That you will not do unless you can overcome me, knight against knight.' And with that he at once made his shield ready and put himself in position for a charge.

"So my master and this Sable Knight, having made themselves ready for the encounter, soon rode together with all their might and main. They met in the middle of the course, and there my master's spear burst into splinters. But the Sable Knight's spear held, and it pierced through Sir Myles's shield and into his side, so that both he and his horse were thrown violently into the dust. He was so badly wounded that he could not get up again from the ground where he lay.

"Then the Sable Knight took my master's shield and hung it in the branches of the apple tree where the other shields were hanging. And then, without paying any more attention to my master or asking how badly he was hurt, he rode back into his castle, and the portcullis was closed behind him at once.

"So, after he had gone, I got my master onto his horse with great labor and at once took him away from there, not knowing where I could find shelter for him, until I came to this place. And that, my lord King, is the true story of how my master came by the deadly wound he has suffered."

"Ha! By the glory of Paradise!" cried King Arthur. "I count it a great shame that in my kingdom, and so near my Court, strangers should be treated as discourteously as Sir Myles has been treated. For it is certainly discourteous to leave a fallen knight on the ground without stopping to ask how badly he is hurt. And it is still more discourteous to take away the shield of a fallen knight who has fought a good fight."

And all the knights of the King's Court cried out in the same way against the discourtesy of that Sable Knight.

*Griflet asks a favor*.

Then a certain squire who served the King in person, named Griflet, who was much loved by his royal master, came forward and knelt before the King, and cried out in a loud voice, "I ask a favor of you, my lord King! And I beg you to grant it to me!"

Then King Arthur raised his eyes to the youth as he knelt in front of him, and said, "Ask, Griflet, and your favor will be granted."

At that Griflet said, "This is what I ask: I ask that you make me a knight at once, and that you let me go out and try to punish this unkind knight by overthrowing him, and so win back those shields he has hung on that apple tree."

Then King Arthur was very troubled in his spirit, for Griflet was still only a squire and altogether untried in arms. So he said, "Listen, you are still too young to fight so powerful a knight as this black champion must be, who has overthrown so many knights without coming to any harm himself. Please, dear Griflet, think again and ask some other favor."

But young Griflet only cried out all the more, "A favor! A favor! And you have granted it to me!"

At this King Arthur said, "You shall have your favor, though my heart warns me that you will suffer great harm and misfortune from this adventure."

*King Arthur makes Griflet a knight*.

So that night Griflet kept watch over his armor in a chapel in the forest, and in the morning, after receiving the Sacrament, he was made a knight by King Arthur's own hand, and no knight could have had a greater honor than that. Then King Arthur fastened the golden spurs to Sir Griflet's heels with his own hand.

So Griflet was made a knight. And mounting his charger, he rode straight off on his adventure, full of happiness and singing for pure pleasure.

And it was at this time that Sir Myles died of his wound. For it is often so in this world: death and misfortune come to some, while others laugh and sing with hope and joy, as though such grievous things as sorrow and death could never happen in the world they live in.

Now that afternoon King Arthur sat waiting very anxiously for news of that young knight. But no news came until toward evening, when some of his attendants came hurrying to him, announcing that Sir Griflet was coming back, but without his shield, and in such a state that it looked as though some great misfortune had happened to him. And right after them came Sir Griflet himself, held up on his horse on one side by Sir Constantine and on the other by Sir Brandiles. Sir Griflet's head hung down on his chest, and his fine new armor was all broken and stained with blood and dust. He looked so pitiful that King Arthur's heart tightened with sorrow to see that young knight in so sad a state.

So, at King Arthur's command, they brought Sir Griflet to the Royal Pavilion and laid him down on a soft couch. Then the King's surgeon examined his wounds and found that the head of a spear, and part of its shaft, were still stuck through Sir Griflet's side, so that he was in terrible and grievous pain.

And when King Arthur saw how dangerously hurt Sir Griflet lay, he cried out, "Alas, my dear young knight! What has happened to you, to bring you to such a sorry state as this I see?"

*Sir Griflet tells how he was hurt*.

Then Sir Griflet, speaking in a very weak voice, told King Arthur how he had fared. He said that he had ridden on through the forest until he found the three beautiful damsels the page of Sir Myles had spoken of. And he said that these damsels had told him how he should go about his adventure. He said that he had found the bridge where the shield and the brass hammer hung, and that he had seen the apple tree hung full of shields there. And he said that he had struck the Sable Knight's shield with the brass hammer, and that the Sable Knight had then come riding out against him. But this knight, he said, did not seem to want to fight him. Instead, he called out to him, very nobly, that he was too young and too untried in arms to fight a seasoned knight, and so he advised Sir Griflet to give up that adventure before it was too late. But in spite of this advice Sir Griflet would not give it up, and declared that he would certainly fight that knight in black. Now at the very first charge Sir Griflet's spear had burst into pieces, but the Sable Knight's spear had held, and it had pierced through Sir Griflet's shield and into his side, giving him the grievous wound he now suffered. And Sir Griflet said that the Sable Knight had then very courteously lifted him back onto his horse (though he had kept Sir Griflet's shield and hung it on the tree with the others hanging there), and had then sent him on his way; and so he had managed to ride back, though in great pain and misery.

*King Arthur is very angry*.

Then King Arthur was wild with anger and greatly troubled in his mind, for he truly loved Sir Griflet very dearly. So he declared that now he himself would go out to punish that Sable Knight and humble him with his own hand. And though the knights of his Court tried hard to talk him out of that adventure, he still declared that he would bring that proud knight low with his own hand, and that he would undertake the adventure, with God's grace, the very next day.

He was so troubled that he could hardly eat his food that evening for anger, and he would not go to his couch to sleep. Instead, after asking Sir Griflet very carefully where he could find that valley of flowers and those three damsels, he spent the night walking up and down his pavilion, waiting for the day to dawn.

Now as soon as the birds first began to chirp and the east began to brighten with the coming daylight, King Arthur called his two squires. With their help he put on his armor and mounted a milk-white war horse, and soon he set out on the adventure he had made up his mind to undertake.

*King Arthur sets out on his adventure*.

And truly it is a very pleasant thing to ride out at the dawning of a springtime day. For then the little birds sing their sweetest songs, all joining in one joyful medley, so that you can hardly tell one note from another, so many are the voices in that pretty round of song. Then the growing things of the earth smell sweetest in the freshness of the early day: the fair flowers, the shrubs, and the blossoms on the trees. Then the dew sparkles all over the grass like an unbelievable multitude of jewels of every color. Then the whole world is sweet and clean and new, as though it had just been freshly made for the one who comes out to roam so early in the morning.

So King Arthur's heart swelled with great joy, and he sang an old-fashioned song as he rode through the forest in search of that knightly adventure.

*King Arthur comes to the Valley of Delight*.

So, at about noon, he came to that part of the forest he had heard about several times before. For suddenly he saw before him a wide and gently sloping valley, with a stream as bright as silver running down it. And the whole valley was strewn with countless fair and fragrant flowers of every kind. In the middle of the valley stood a handsome castle with tall red roofs and many bright windows, so that it seemed to King Arthur a very fine castle indeed. And on a smooth green lawn he saw the three damsels dressed in flame-colored satin that the page of Sir Myles and Sir Griflet had spoken of. They were playing ball with a golden ball, and the hair of each of them was the color of gold. And it seemed to King Arthur, as he came nearer, that they were the most beautiful damsels he had ever seen in all his life.

Now as King Arthur came up to them, the three stopped tossing the ball, and the fairest of all the damsels asked him where he was going and what errand he was bound on.

Then King Arthur answered, "Ha, fair lady! Where should a belted knight ride on such a day as this, and on what business, but in search of adventure, as is right for a knight-errant of proper strength of heart and body?"

*The damsels greet King Arthur*.

Then the three damsels smiled at the King, for he was very handsome and they liked him very well. "Alas, Sir Knight!" said the one who had spoken before. "Please don't be in such a hurry to take on a dangerous adventure. Stay with us instead for a day, or two, or three, to feast and make merry with us. For good cheer surely does a great deal to gladden the heart, and we would love to enjoy the company of so gallant a knight as you seem to be. That castle is ours, and this whole bright valley is ours, and those who have visited it are pleased, because of its joyfulness, to call it the Valley of Delight. So stay with us a little while, and don't be in such a hurry to go on."

"No," said King Arthur, "I cannot stay with you, fair ladies, for I am bent on an adventure you will know very well when I tell you that I am looking for that Sable Knight, who has overcome so many other knights and taken away their shields. So I ask you, of your kindness, to tell me where I can find him."

"Heaven's grace!" cried the one who spoke for the others. "This is surely a sorry adventure you are seeking, Sir Knight! For already, in these last two days, two knights have tried their strength against that knight, and both have come to great pain and disgrace. Even so, if you will undertake this danger, you shall not go until you have eaten and refreshed yourself." And as she said this, she lifted a little ivory whistle that hung from her neck on a golden chain and blew it very shrilly.

In answer to this summons three fair young pages came out of the castle, dressed all in flame-colored clothes, carrying between them a silver table covered with a white cloth. And after them came five more pages dressed the same way, bringing flagons of white wine and red, dried fruits and sweetmeats, and small loaves of fine white bread.

*King Arthur eats and drinks in the Valley of Delight*.

Then King Arthur got down from his war horse very gladly, for he was both hungry and thirsty. He sat down at the table with the damsels beside him and ate with great enjoyment, talking pleasantly all the while with those fair ladies, who listened to him with great cheerfulness of spirit. Yet he did not tell them who he was, though they wondered greatly who this noble warrior might be who had come into that place.

So, when he had satisfied his hunger and thirst, King Arthur mounted his horse again, and the three damsels went with him a little way across the valley, he riding on his horse and they walking beside him. After a while he saw a dark pathway leading into the forest on the far side; and when he had come to it, the lady who had spoken to him before said, "That is the way you must take if you mean to go on with this adventure. So farewell, and may good fortune go with you; for truly, you are the most pleasant-spoken knight who has come this way in a long time."

Then King Arthur, after saluting those ladies very courteously, rode away, full of joy over the pleasant adventure he had just passed through.

Now when King Arthur had gone some distance, he came after a while to a certain place where charcoal burners worked at their trade. For here there were many mounds of earth, all smoking from the smoldering logs inside them, while the whole air was filled with the smell of the damped-down fires.

As the King came near this spot, he soon saw that something was going on that was badly wrong. For in the open clearing he saw three sooty fellows with long knives in their hands chasing one old man whose beard was as white as snow. And he saw that this venerable old man, who was richly dressed in black, and whose horse stood a little way off, was running this way and that as though to escape those wicked men, and that he seemed very hard pressed and in great danger of his life.

"By heaven!" said the young King to himself. "Here, surely, is someone in bad need of help." And with that he cried out in a great voice, "Stop, villains! What are you doing?" And at once he set spurs to his horse, lowered his spear into position, and drove down on them with a noise as loud as thunder.

But when the three wicked fellows saw the armed knight thundering down on them like this, they dropped their knives at once and, with loud cries of fear, ran off this way and that until they had escaped into the thickets of the forest, where a man on horseback could not hope to follow them.

*King Arthur rescues Merlin from the three villains*.

Then, having driven those wicked fellows away, King Arthur rode up to the man he had rescued, meaning to offer him comfort. And as he came near, he saw to his surprise that the old man was the Enchanter Merlin. Yet how Merlin had come there so suddenly, when only a little while before he had been at the King's Court at Caerleon, and what he was doing in that place, the King could not understand at all. So he spoke to the Enchanter like this: "Ha, Merlin! It seems to me that I have saved your life. For surely you would not have escaped from the hands of those wicked men if I had not happened to come this way just now."

"Do you think so, Lord?" said Merlin. "Now let me tell you that although I may have seemed to be in danger, I could have saved myself very easily if I had wanted to. But just as you saw me in this pretended danger, so you may know that a real danger, far greater than this one, lies before you, and there will be no knight-errant to rescue you from it. So I beg you, Lord, to take me with you on this adventure you have set out on, for I tell you that you will certainly suffer great sorrow and pain in it."

"Merlin," said King Arthur, "even if I were facing my death, I would not turn back from this adventure. But as for the advice you give me, it seems to me it would be very wise to take you with me, if such danger lies ahead of me as you say."

And Merlin said, "Yes, it would be very wise for you to do so."

So Merlin mounted his palfrey, and he and King Arthur rode off together from that place in pursuit of the adventure the King had undertaken.
