Chapter 4: How the Four Knights Served the Lady Guinevere

[Figure aw: Four knights serve the gardener's boy — Arthur, as the gardener's boy in a rough cap, sits at a garden table while richly dressed knights stand behind him, one pouring him a drink.]

*The gardener scolds his boy*.

Now when King Arthur came back to Cameliard once more (which was in the afternoon of the second day), he found the gardener waiting for him, and the gardener was filled with fury. He had brought a long birch rod with him, meaning to punish his boy with it as soon as he came back to the garden. So when he saw King Arthur he said: "You good-for-nothing! Why did you leave your work to go gadding about?" And King Arthur laughed and said: "Do not touch me." At this the gardener grew so angry that he caught the King by the collar of his jerkin, meaning to beat him, and said: "Do you laugh at me, you rascal, and make fun of me? Now I'll give you a good beating for the wrong you've done."

Then, when King Arthur felt that man's hand laid on him, and when he heard the words the gardener spoke in his anger, his royal spirit swelled up within him, and he cried out: "Ha, wretch! Would you dare to lay your hands upon my sacred person?" And as he said this, he seized the gardener by the wrists, took the rod straight out of his hand, and struck him with it across the shoulders. And when that poor fellow felt himself caught in the powerful grip of the angry King, and felt the rod across his shoulders, he at once set up a great outcry, although the blow did not hurt him in the least. "Now get out of here!" said King Arthur, "and trouble me no more, or I will treat you in a way you will not like at all." With that he let the poor man go; and the gardener was so dazed with terror that the earth and the sky both swam before his eyes. For King Arthur's eyes had flashed at him like lightning, and those two hands had held his wrists with wonderful strength. So, when the King let him go, he took himself off as fast as he could, trembling all over and sweating with fear.

*The gardener complains to the Lady Guinevere*.

So he went straight to the Lady Guinevere and complained to her of the way he had been treated. "Lady," he said, weeping at the memory of his fright, "my boy goes off for a day or more, I don't know where; and when I try to whip him for leaving his work, he takes the rod right out of my hand and beats me with it. So now, I beg you, deal with him as he deserves, and have some strong men drive him out of this place with sticks."

Then the Lady Guinevere laughed. "Let him be!" she said. "Don't meddle with him any more; for indeed he seems to be a very saucy fellow. As for you, pay no attention to his coming or his going, and perhaps I will deal with him myself in whatever way is fitting."

So the gardener went his way, greatly wondering that the Lady Guinevere should be so mild with that unruly boy. And the Lady Guinevere went her way, very merry. For she had begun to think that there must truly be some excellent reason why, whenever the White Champion who did such wonderful deeds came to Cameliard, the gardener's boy should be gone; and why, whenever that same Champion went away, the gardener's boy should come back again. So she suspected a great many things, and was wonderfully merry and cheerful in her spirits.

*The Lady Guinevere mocks the gardener's boy*.

Now that same afternoon the Lady Guinevere happened to walk in the garden with her damsels, and with her walked those four noble knights whom her White Champion had sent to her: that is, Sir Gawain, Sir Uwain, Sir Geraint, and Sir Pelleas. And the gardener's lad was digging in the garden; and as they passed by the place where he was, the Lady Guinevere laughed out loud and cried: "Look! Look, Sirs and Ladies! There is a very saucy fellow for a gardener's lad, for he always wears his cap, even when he stands in the presence of lords and ladies."

Then Sir Gawain spoke up and said: "Is that so? Then I will go to that rascal this minute and take his hat off for him, and in a way he will like so little that I don't believe he will ever offend by wearing it in our presence again."

At this the Lady Guinevere laughed a great deal. "Let him be!" she said. "Let him be, Sir Gawain! It would not become a knight as noble as you to have anything to do with that saucy fellow. Besides, he assures us all that he has an ugly spot on his head, so let him wear his cap, in God's mercy."

*The Duke of North Umber issues a second challenge*.

So the Lady Guinevere, though she suspected a very great deal, was still pleased to make fun of the one she suspected.

Now by that day Duke Mordaunt of North Umber had entirely recovered from the painful hurts he had suffered when the White Champion overthrew him. So, when the next morning came, he appeared before the castle again as he had appeared before, clad from head to foot in armor. But this time two heralds rode before him, and when the Duke and the two heralds had come to that part of the meadows that lay just in front of the castle of Cameliard, the heralds blew their trumpets very loudly. At the sound of the trumpets many people came and gathered on the walls; and King Leodegrance came too, and took his stand on a lesser tower that looked down on the plain where the Duke of North Umber and the two heralds were. Then the Duke of North Umber lifted his eyes and saw King Leodegrance standing above him on the top of that tower, and he cried out in a loud voice: "Ho there, King Leodegrance! Do not think that because I was thrown from my horse by a piece of bad luck in a contest of arms, you are therefore rid of me. All the same, I now make you this fair offer. Tomorrow I shall appear before this castle with six knights as my companions. Now, if you have any seven knights who are able to stand against me and my companions in a contest of arms, whether with spears or with swords, on horseback or on foot, then I will bind myself to give up every claim whatever to the hand of the Lady Guinevere. But if you cannot provide such champions to fight successfully against me and my companions, then I will not only lay claim to the Lady Guinevere, but I will also seize, and hold as my own, three certain castles of yours that stand on the borders of North Umber. And I will likewise seize, and hold as my own, all the lands and fields that belong to those same castles. What is more, this challenge of mine will hold only until sunset tomorrow; after that it will be null and void. So, King Leodegrance, you had best see to it at once that you find such champions as can defend you against these demands."

*King Leodegrance is downcast*.

At this the two heralds blew their trumpets once more, and Duke Mordaunt of North Umber turned his horse around and rode away from that place. Then King Leodegrance also went his way, very sorrowful and downcast in his spirits. For he said to himself: "Is it at all likely that another champion will come to me, like that wonderful White Champion who came two days ago, I don't know from where, to defend me against my enemies? And as for that same White Champion: just as I do not know where he came from, so I do not know where he has gone. How then shall I know where to look for him, to beg his help again in this time of my great need?" So he went his way, very sorrowful, not knowing what to do to defend himself. And being so deeply troubled in his spirit, he went straight to his own room and shut himself in; and he would see no one and speak to no one, but gave himself up entirely to sorrow and despair.

*The Lady Guinevere begs the four knights for help*.

Now in this time of need the Lady Guinevere thought of those four knights who had been pledged to serve her for seven days. So she went to them where they were and spoke to them like this: "Sirs, you were sent here pledged to serve me for seven days. Now I command you to take up this challenge of Duke Mordaunt's at my bidding, and I very much wish you to go out tomorrow to meet this Duke of North Umber and his companions in battle. For you are tremendously mighty knights, and I believe you can easily defend us against our enemies."

But Sir Gawain said, "No, Lady; no! For though we are pledged to your service, we are not pledged to the service of King Leodegrance, your father. Nor do we have any quarrel of any kind with this Duke of North Umber, or with his six companions. For we are knights of King Arthur's Court, and we may not, except at his command, take up any foreign quarrel in the service of another king."

Then the Lady Guinevere was very angry, and she said with great heat: "Either you are a wonderfully faithful lord to your King, Sir Gawain, or else you are afraid to meet this Duke of North Umber and his companions."

And at this speech of the Lady Guinevere's, Sir Gawain was very angry too, and he answered: "If you were a knight and not a lady, Dame Guinevere, you would think three or four times before you found the courage to say those words to me." With that he got up and left the place, his face all burning with anger. And the Lady Guinevere also left that place and went to her bower, her own private rooms, where she wept a great deal, both from sorrow and from anger.

*King Arthur sends the gardener on an errand*.

Now all this while King Arthur had known very well everything that happened; so by and by he got up and went out and found the gardener. And he took the gardener firmly by the collar of his coat and held him where he stood. And he said to him: "You there! I have an order to give you, and you will carry out that order to the letter. If you do not, a great deal of pain may come to you." And as he spoke, he thrust his hand into the front of his jerkin and drew out the necklace of pearls that the Lady Guinevere had taken from her own neck and given him. And he went on to the gardener: "You will take this necklace to the Lady Guinevere, and you will say this to her: that she is to send me at once bread and meat and wine and sweets from her own table. And you will tell her that I wish her to call those four knights, that is, Sir Gawain, Sir Uwain, Sir Geraint, and Sir Pelleas, and that she is to order those four to come and serve me with those things for my refreshment. And you are to tell her that she must lay her commands on those knights that they are to go on serving me in whatever I may command, and that from now on they are to be my servants and not hers. These are the orders I lay on you: that you are to say these things to the Lady Guinevere."

Now when the gardener heard these words he was so astonished that he did not know what to think, for he believed the gardener's lad had gone completely mad. So he raised his voice and cried out loud, "What's this? What are you saying? Truly, if I did such a thing as you tell me to do, either it would cost me my life or else it would cost you yours. For who would dare to say such words to the Lady Guinevere?"

But King Arthur said: "All the same, you will certainly do as I command you, fellow. For if you disobey in a single point, I promise you it will go very badly with you. For I have it in my power to make you suffer as you have never suffered before."

At this the gardener said, "I will go." For he said to himself, "If I do as this fellow tells me, then the Lady Guinevere will have him punished severely, and so I will be revenged on him for what he did to me yesterday. Besides, it annoys me terribly to have a lad working in the garden who behaves the way this fellow does. So," he said, "I will go." So he took the necklace of pearls that King Arthur gave him, and he went off, and after a while he found the Lady Guinevere where she was. And when he had found her, he spoke to her like this:

"Lady, my garden boy has certainly gone completely mad. For, with threats of some great harm he would do me if I did not carry out his errand, he has sent me to offer you a very grievous insult. He has sent me with this string of big beads to give to you; and he tells me to tell you that you are to send him bread and meat and sweets and wine, such as you have at your own table; and he tells me to tell you that these things are to be served to him by the four noble knights who came here the day before yesterday. And he says you are to command those same knights to obey him in whatever he may command, because from now on they are to be his servants and not yours. And truly, Lady, he would not listen to a word I said to him against it, but he threatened me with terrible harm if I did not come here and give you this message."

Now when the Lady Guinevere heard what the gardener said, and when she saw the necklace she had given to the White Champion, and when she knew that the White Champion and the gardener's boy were indeed one and the same, she was lifted up with such joy that she did not know whether to laugh or to cry for pure happiness. So she rose and took the necklace of pearls, and she told the gardener to come with her. Then she went on until she found those four knights, and when she had found them she spoke to them like this:

*The Lady Guinevere commands the four knights to serve the gardener's boy*.

"My lords, a little while ago, when I commanded you to take up my quarrel with Duke Mordaunt of North Umber for my sake, you would not do it. And you, my lord Gawain, spoke such angry words as it is not fitting for one who serves to speak to his mistress, much less for a knight to speak to the daughter of a king. So I have decided that you shall do a certain thing as a penance; and if you refuse to do it, I will know very well that you do not mean to keep the word you pledged to my knight when he overthrew all four of you in fair combat. Now this is my command: that you take certain food prepared for my table, meats and white bread and sweets and wine, and that you carry that food to my gardener's boy, whose cap, Sir Gawain, you threatened so bravely to take away from him this very morning. And you four are to serve the food to him as though he were a royal knight. And when you have served him, you are to obey him in whatever he may order. I lay this on you as a penalty because you did not take up my quarrel as true knights should; for from now on you are to be servants to that gardener's boy and not to me. So now you are to go to the buttery of the castle, the storeroom where the food and drink are kept, and you are to tell the sewer, the officer in charge of serving the table, to give you meats such as are served at my own table. And you are to serve the food on silver plates, and the wine in silver cups and goblets. And you are to wait on that gardener's boy as though he were a great lord of the highest fame and renown."

So spoke the Lady Guinevere; and when she had finished speaking, she turned and left those four knights, and took the gardener with her. And he was so astonished at what he had heard that he did not know whether he had gone mad or the Lady Guinevere had gone mad. And the Lady Guinevere told the gardener to go to the gardener's boy and tell him that everything would be done just as he had commanded. And so the gardener did as he was told.

*The four knights are angry*.

Now let us turn to those four knights whom the Lady Guinevere had left behind. For they were bewildered and dumbfounded at the strange commands she had laid on them. And when they had recovered from their amazement, they were burning with such fierce indignation that for a time they could not tell whether what they saw with their eyes was the light of day or utter darkness. Not one of them could look another in the face, so overcome were they with shame at the insult that had been put on them. Then Sir Gawain spoke up, and his voice shook so with his great anger that he could hardly hold it steady enough to get his words out. "Sirs," he said, "don't you see how this lady has deliberately put a great insult on us because we would not do what she told us to do this morning, and because we would not take up her quarrel against the Duke of North Umber? Well, we will indeed serve this gardener's boy just as she has ordered. We will serve him with meat and drink as she has commanded, and we will do him service as she has told us to. But mark this: we are no longer her servants, but his servants, and so we may serve him in whatever way we choose. So, when we have carried out her commands and have served him with meat and drink, and when we have obeyed all the orders he gives us, then I swear that I, with my own hand, will kill that gardener's boy. And when I have killed him, I will put his head in a bag, and I will send that bag to the Lady Guinevere by the lowest messenger I can find for the purpose. And so this proud lady will receive an insult as great as the insult she has put on us." And they all said that what Sir Gawain had planned should be done exactly as he had said.

*The four knights serve the gardener's boy*.

So those four lords went to the sewer of the castle, and they asked for the best of the food that was to be served to the Lady Guinevere: meats and bread and sweets and wine. Then they took silver plates and platters and put the food on them; and they took silver cups and silver goblets and poured the wine into them; and they went out with these things. And when they had come around to the back of the castle, near the stables, they found the gardener's boy, and they told him to sit down and eat and drink. And they waited on him as though he had been some great lord. And not one of those four knights knew who he was, or that he was the great King whose servants, in truth, they were. For he wore his cap of disguise on his head, and so they took him for nothing but a poor peasant fellow.

Now when Sir Uwain saw that he still kept his cap on in front of them, he spoke to him with great indignation, saying: "Ha, you villain! Would you wear your cap even in the presence of great princes and lords like us?"

To this Sir Gawain said, "Let it be; it doesn't matter." And then he said very bitterly to the gardener's boy: "Eat well, fellow! For you will hardly eat another meal on this earth."

To this the gardener's boy answered: "Sir Knight, perhaps that will be for another will than yours to decide. For maybe I shall eat many other meals besides this one. And maybe you will serve at them as you are serving me now." And those four lords were astonished beyond measure that he should speak to them so calmly and without any sign of fear.

Then, after he had eaten, the gardener's boy said to those knights, "There, Sirs, I have had enough and I am finished; and now I have other commands for you to carry out. My next command is that you get ready at once to ride out with me, and for that purpose you are to put on full armor. And you, Sir Gawain, are to go to the head stable-keeper of this castle and order him to make ready the Lady Guinevere's palfrey, so that I can ride out on it at once. And when you are all armed, and when everything has been arranged as I command, you are to bring that palfrey to the postern gate of the castle, and there I will meet you and ride out with you."

And Sir Gawain said: "It shall be done in every way just as you command. But when we ride out of this castle, it will be a sorry journey for you."

And the gardener's boy said: "I do not think so, Sir Gawain."

Then those four went away and did as the gardener's boy had commanded. And when they had made themselves ready in full armor, and had got the Lady Guinevere's palfrey, they went to the postern gate, and there the gardener's boy met them. And when he saw that they stayed sitting on their horses and did not stir when he came, he said: "Ha, Sirs! Is this how you treat the one you have been ordered to serve? Now I order you, Sir Gawain and Sir Uwain, to get down and hold my stirrup for me; and I order you, Sir Geraint and Sir Pelleas, to get down and hold my palfrey for me while I mount."

Then those four noble knights did as they were commanded. And Sir Gawain said: "You may command as you please, and I advise you to make the most of it while you can; for you will have only a little while longer to enjoy the great honor that has fallen on you. For that honor which has fallen on you, mark my words, will soon crush you to death."

And the gardener's boy said: "Not so; I believe I shall not die just yet." And again those four lords were greatly astonished at how calm he was.

And so they rode out from that place; and the gardener's boy would not allow them to ride either in front of him or beside him, but commanded them to ride behind him, since they were still his servants.

So they rode as he had ordered them for a good long way. Then, after they had gone forward a great distance, they drew near to a gloomy and dismal woodland that lay entirely outside the country around Cameliard. And when they had come close to this woodland, Sir Gawain rode a little forward, and he said: "Sir Gardener's Boy, do you see that woodland over there? Now, when we come into it you will die at once, and by a sword that has never yet been touched by any but noble or knightly blood."

And King Arthur turned around in his saddle and said: "Ha! Sir Gawain! Would you ride forward like this, when I have ordered you to ride behind me?"

*King Arthur reveals himself to the four knights*.

And as he spoke he took the cap off his head, and suddenly they all saw that it was King Arthur who rode with them.

Then a great silence of pure astonishment fell on them all, and each man sat as though he had been turned into a statue of stone. And it was King Arthur who spoke first. He said: "Ha! What now, Sir Knights? Have you no words of greeting for me? Truly, you have served me with a very bad grace this day. What is more, you have threatened to kill me; and now, when I speak to you, you say nothing in reply."

Then those four knights at once cried out aloud; and they leaped down from their horses and knelt in the dust of the road. And when King Arthur saw them kneeling there, he laughed with great joy of spirit, and he told them to mount their horses again, for time was passing and there was much to do.

So they mounted their horses and rode away, and as they went along the King told them everything that had happened to him, so that they were greatly amazed, and gave high praise to the knightly way he had carried himself through all those excellent adventures. And they rejoiced greatly that they had a king to rule over them who had so high and knightly a spirit.

So they rode to that edge of the forest where King Arthur had left his horse and his armor.
