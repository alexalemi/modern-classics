Chapter 3: How King Arthur Found a Noble Sword in a Most Wonderful Way, and How He Fought Again with It and Won

[Figure ap: Excalibur, the sword — An arm in an embroidered sleeve rises from the lake holding up the shining sword, and King Arthur, crowned, stands in the brass swan-boat behind it.]

*King Arthur longs to renew his battle*.

Now, as soon as King Arthur had been healed by that extraordinary balsam of the grievous wounds he had received in his battle with King Pellinore, he found himself seized by a fierce desire to meet his enemy again, to try the outcome of battle with him once more, and so win back the honor he had lost in that fight. On the morning of the fourth day, being entirely cured, and having eaten his breakfast, he went for a refreshing walk along the edge of the forest, listening all the while to the cheerful sound of the wood-birds singing their morning songs with all their might and main. Merlin walked beside him, and King Arthur told Merlin what was on his mind: that he meant to meet King Pellinore once more in knightly combat. And he said, "Merlin, it troubles me very sorely to have come off so badly in my last encounter with King Pellinore. Truly, he is the very best knight I have ever met in all the world. Even so, things might have gone differently for me if I had not broken my sword and so left myself altogether defenseless. However that may be, I have made up my mind to try this adventure once more, and I will do so as soon as I possibly can."

To this Merlin replied, "You are certainly a very brave man to have so much appetite for battle, considering how close you came to your death not even four days ago. Yet how can you hope to undertake this adventure without proper preparation? For look: you have no sword, and you have no spear, and you do not even have your misericord to fight with. How then can you hope to try this adventure?"

And King Arthur said, "That I do not know. Nevertheless, I will look for some weapon as soon as I can. For even if I have no better weapon than an oak club, I would still try this battle again with so poor a tool as that."

*Merlin tells King Arthur of Excalibur*.

"Ha! Lord," said Merlin, "I can see that you are entirely set on renewing this quarrel. So I will not try to hold you back from it, but will do everything in my power to help you get what you want. Now, to that end, I must tell you that in one part of this forest (which is, indeed, a very strange place) there is a certain woodland, sometimes called Arroy and at other times called the Forest of Adventure. It has that name because no knight ever enters it without some adventure happening to him. And close to Arroy is a land of enchantment, which has been seen several times. That is a very wonderful land, for in it there is a wide and great lake, which is also enchanted. And in the middle of that lake, for some time now, there has been seen what looks like a woman's arm, exceedingly beautiful and clothed in white samite, which is a rich silk. And the hand of this arm holds a sword of such surpassing excellence and beauty that no eye has ever seen its like. The name of this sword is Excalibur, and it was given that name by those who have seen it because of its marvelous brightness and beauty. For it has happened that several knights have already seen that sword and have tried to win it for their own, but until now no one has been able to touch it, and many have lost their lives in that adventure. For whenever any man comes near it, either he sinks into the lake, or else the arm vanishes entirely, or else it is drawn down beneath the lake. And so no man has ever been able to take possession of that sword. Now, I am able to lead you to that Lake of Enchantment, and there you may see Excalibur with your own eyes. Then, when you have seen him, perhaps you will want to win him; and if you are able to do that, you will have a sword very well fitted for battle."

"Merlin," said the King, "this is a very strange thing you are telling me. Now I want beyond all measure to try to win this sword for my own, and so I beg you to lead me with all speed to this enchanted lake you tell me of." And Merlin said, "I will do so."

So that morning King Arthur and Merlin took their leave of that holy hermit (the King kneeling in the grass to receive his blessing), and, departing from that place, they went into the deeper forest once more, making their way toward that part of it which was known as Arroy.

*Merlin and King Arthur follow a white doe*.

After a while they came to Arroy, and it was about noon. And when they had entered those woodlands they came to a certain little clearing, and in that clearing they saw a white doe with a golden collar around her neck. King Arthur said, "Look, Merlin, there is a wonderful sight." And Merlin said, "Let us follow that doe." At that the doe turned, and they followed it. By and by, as they followed it, they came to an opening in the trees where there was a little lawn of sweet, soft grass. Here they saw a bower, a little shelter of leaves and branches, and in front of the bower was a table spread with a fine snow-white cloth and set with refreshments: white bread, wine, and meats of several kinds. At the door of this bower there stood a page, dressed all in green, whose hair was as black as ebony, and whose eyes were as black as jet and exceedingly bright. When this page saw King Arthur and Merlin, he greeted them and welcomed the King very pleasantly, saying, "Ha! King Arthur, you are welcome to this place. Now please get down from your horse and refresh yourself before you go any farther."

At this King Arthur wondered whether there might be some enchantment here meant to do him harm, for he was astonished that a page in the deep forest should know him so well. But Merlin told him to be of good cheer, and said, "Truly, Lord, you may freely share in this meal, which, I may tell you, was prepared especially for you. What is more, you may take it as a sign that this adventure will turn out very happily."

*King Arthur is refreshed in a mysterious way*.

So King Arthur sat down at the table with a glad and easy heart (for he was hungry), and that page and another just like him waited on him, serving him all the food on silver plates and all the wine in golden goblets, just as he was used to being served in his own court, except that these things were far more skillfully made and fashioned, and far more beautiful, than the tableware of the King's court.

Then, after he had eaten his fill, and had washed his hands in a silver basin that the first page offered him, and had dried his hands on a fine linen napkin that the other page brought him, and after Merlin had also refreshed himself, they went on their way, greatly rejoicing at this pleasant adventure, which, it seemed to the King, could only mean that his undertaking would turn out very well.

Now, about the middle of the afternoon, King Arthur and Merlin came suddenly out of the forest onto a fair and level plain, covered all over with so many flowers that no one could imagine how many there were or how beautiful.

*King Arthur comes to a strange land*.

This was a very wonderful land, for all the air seemed to be made of gold, it was so bright and so strangely radiant. Here and there upon that plain stood various trees all in blossom, and the fragrance of the blossoms was so sweet that the King had never smelled anything like it. In the branches of those trees were a multitude of birds of many colors, and the melody of their singing filled the heart of anyone who heard it with delight. And in the middle of the plain was a lake of water as bright as silver, and all around the edges of the lake grew incredible numbers of lilies and daffodils. Yet, although this place was so exceedingly beautiful, there was nowhere in it a single sign of human life of any kind; it seemed as lonely as the empty sky on a summer day. So, because of all the marvelous beauty of the place, and its strangeness, and its utter loneliness, King Arthur understood that he must have come into a land of powerful enchantment, where perhaps there lived a fairy of very high rank. And so a kind of fear wrapped itself around his spirit as he pushed his great milk-white war-horse through that long, beautiful grass, all sprinkled with flowers, and he did not know what strange things were about to happen to him.

*King Arthur sees Excalibur*.

*King Arthur meets the Lady of the Lake*.

When he had come to the edge of the lake, he saw there the miracle that Merlin had told him of before. For there, in the middle of the stretch of water, was what looked like a fair and beautiful arm, like a woman's, clothed all in white samite. The arm was circled with several bracelets of worked gold, and the hand held a sword of marvelous workmanship high in the air above the surface of the water. Neither the arm nor the sword moved so much as a hair's breadth, but they stood as still as a carved statue upon the surface of the lake. And the sun of that strange land shone down upon the hilt of the sword, which was of pure gold set with jewels of many kinds, so that the hilt of the sword and the bracelets around the arm glittered in the middle of the lake like some single star of surpassing splendor. King Arthur sat on his war-horse and gazed from a distance at the arm and the sword, and he marveled greatly at them. Yet he did not know how he could reach that sword, for the lake was wonderfully wide and deep, and so he did not know how he might get to it to make it his own. And as he sat turning this over in his mind, he suddenly became aware of a strange lady who was coming toward him through the tall flowers that bloomed along the edge of the lake. When he saw her coming toward him, he quickly got down from his war-horse and went forward to meet her, with the bridle-rein over his arm. And when he had come near her, he saw that she was extraordinarily beautiful: her face was as clear as wax, and her eyes were perfectly black, and they were as bright and glistening as two jewels set in ivory. He saw that her hair was like silk, and as black as anything could be, and so long that it reached the ground as she walked. The lady was dressed all in green, except that a fine cord of crimson and gold was woven into the braids of her hair. Around her neck hung a very beautiful necklace of several strands of opals and emeralds, set in skillfully worked gold, and around her wrists were bracelets of the same kind, of opals and emeralds set in gold. So when King Arthur saw how wonderful she looked, like an ivory statue of surpassing beauty dressed all in green, he immediately knelt before her in the midst of all those flowers, and said, "Lady, I can see for certain that you are no mortal maiden, but one of the fairy folk. And I can see that this place, because of its extraordinary beauty, can be nothing other than some part of Fairyland into which I have come."

And the Lady answered, "King Arthur, you speak the truth, for I am indeed of Fairyland. What is more, I may tell you that my name is Nimue, and that I am the chief of those Ladies of the Lake you may have heard people speak of. You should also know that what you see over there as a wide lake is, in truth, a plain like this one, all covered with flowers. And you should know too that in the middle of that plain there stands a castle of white marble and of ultramarine, the deep blue of lapis, brightened with gold. But so that mortal eyes may not see our dwelling place, my sisters and I have caused this appearance of a lake to spread over that castle, so that it is entirely hidden from sight. And no mortal man may cross that lake except in one way; any other way, he will certainly die in it."

"Lady," said King Arthur, "what you tell me fills me with very great wonder. And indeed, I am afraid that in coming here I have done wrong by intruding upon the solitude of your dwelling place."

"No, not so, King Arthur," said the Lady of the Lake, "for in truth you are very welcome here. What is more, I may tell you that I feel a greater friendship for you and for the noble knights of your court than you could easily know. But I ask you, of your courtesy, to tell me what it is that brings you to our land."

"Lady," said the King, "I will tell you the whole truth. Not long ago I fought a battle with a certain sable knight, in which I was sorely and grievously wounded, and in which I burst my spear and snapped my sword and lost even my misericord, so that I had not a single thing left to me by way of a weapon. In this extremity Merlin, here, told me of Excalibur, and of how he is held up forever by an arm in the middle of this magical lake. So I came here, and I find it just as he said. Now, Lady, if it is possible, I would gladly win that excellent sword, so that with it I might fight my battle through to the very end."

"Ha! my lord King," said the Lady of the Lake, "that sword is not an easy thing to win, and what is more, I may tell you that several knights have lost their lives trying to do what you have in mind. For, in truth, no man may win that sword over there unless he is without fear and without reproach."

"Alas, Lady!" said King Arthur, "that is a sad saying indeed for me. For though I may not be lacking in knightly courage, yet in truth there are many things for which I reproach myself. Nevertheless, I would gladly attempt this thing, even if it puts me in great danger. So I beg you, tell me how I may best undertake this adventure."

*The Lady of the Lake summons a boat*.

"King Arthur," said the Lady of the Lake, "I will do as I say, and help you in what you wish in this matter." At that she lifted a single emerald that hung by a small gold chain at her belt, and the emerald was skillfully carved into the shape of a whistle. She set the whistle to her lips and blew on it very shrilly. And at once there appeared upon the water, a great way off, something that shone very brightly. It came toward them at great speed, and as it drew near, they saw that it was a boat made all of carved brass. The prow of the boat was carved into the head of a beautiful woman, and on either side were wings like the wings of a swan. And the boat moved upon the water like a swan, very swiftly, so that long lines like silver threads stretched far away behind it across the face of the water, which otherwise was as smooth as glass. When the brass boat had reached the bank, it rested there and moved no more.

Then the Lady of the Lake told King Arthur to get into the boat, and so he got in. And the moment he had done so, the boat moved away from the bank as swiftly as it had come. Merlin and the Lady of the Lake stood at the edge of the water and gazed after King Arthur and the brass boat.

And King Arthur saw that the boat floated swiftly across the lake to the place where the arm held up the sword, and that the arm and the sword did not move, but stayed where they were.

*King Arthur wins Excalibur*.

Then King Arthur reached out and took the sword in his hand, and at once the arm sank out of sight beneath the water. And King Arthur held the sword, and its scabbard, and its belt in his hand, and they were his own.

Then truly his heart swelled with joy as if it would burst in his chest, for Excalibur was a hundred times more beautiful than he had thought possible. And so his heart was close to breaking with pure joy at having won that magic sword.

Then the brass boat carried him very quickly back to the land again, and he stepped ashore where the Lady of the Lake and Merlin stood. And when he stood on the shore, he thanked the Lady beyond measure for all she had done to help him in his great undertaking, and she answered him with cheerful and pleasant words.

Then King Arthur bowed to the lady, as was fitting, and when he had mounted his war-horse, and Merlin had mounted his palfrey, his gentle riding horse, they rode away from there about their business. And all the while the King's heart was still swelling with pure delight at having for his own that beautiful sword, the most beautiful and the most famous sword in all the world.

That night King Arthur and Merlin stayed with the holy hermit at the forest sanctuary. And when the next morning came (the King having bathed in the ice-cold forest spring, and being wonderfully refreshed by it), they set out, thanking that saintly man for the shelter he had given them.

Then, about noon, they reached the valley of the Sable Knight, and there everything was arranged exactly as it had been when King Arthur was there before: that gloomy castle, the lawn of smooth grass, the apple tree hung all over with shields, and the bridge on which hung that single black shield.

"Now, Merlin," said King Arthur, "this time I strictly forbid you to interfere in this quarrel. And you are not, on pain of my displeasure, to use any of your magic arts on my behalf. So listen to what I say, and heed it with all possible care."

*King Arthur challenges King Pellinore to battle again*.

At once the King rode out onto the bridge, and, seizing the brass hammer, he struck the sable shield with all his might and main. Immediately the portcullis of the castle was let down, as I told you before, and in the same way as that other time the Sable Knight rode out, already armed and equipped for the encounter. He came to the head of the bridge, and there King Arthur spoke to him: "Sir Pellinore, we now know each other entirely well, and each of us judges that he has a quarrel with the other. You judge that I, for reasons of my own that seemed right to me, have taken away your royal rank and have driven you into this lonely forest. I judge that you have set yourself up here to do injury and insult to the knights and lords and other people of this kingdom of mine. So, since I am here as a knight-errant, I challenge you to fight me, man to man, until one of us has conquered the other."

King Pellinore bowed his head in agreement to this speech, and then he wheeled his horse, rode off a little distance, and took his place where he had stood before. And King Arthur also rode off a little distance and took up his position where he had stood before. At the same time there came out of the castle one of those tall pages dressed all in black, patched with crimson, and he gave King Arthur a good, stout spear of ash wood, well seasoned and never yet used in battle. And when the two knights were ready, they shouted and drove their horses together, and each struck the other so squarely in the middle of his defenses that the spears shattered in their hands, bursting into small splinters just as they had done before.

Then each of the two knights at once leapt from his horse with great skill and grace, and each drew his sword. And they fell to fighting so furiously and so violently that two wild bulls on the mountains could not have fought a more desperate battle.

*King Arthur overcomes King Pellinore*.

But now, with Excalibur to help him in the battle, King Arthur soon overcame his enemy. For he gave him several wounds and yet received none himself; he did not shed a single drop of blood in that whole fight, though in a little while his enemy's armor was all stained with crimson. And at last King Arthur struck so mighty a blow that King Pellinore was entirely stunned by it, so that his sword and his shield dropped from their guard, his legs trembled beneath him, and he sank to his knees on the ground. Then he called on King Arthur to have mercy, saying, "Spare my life, and I will yield myself to you."

*King Arthur demands two of the sons of King Pellinore as hostages*.

And King Arthur said, "I will spare you, and I will do more than that. For now that you have yielded yourself to me, I will give you back your power and your rank. I bear you no ill will, Pellinore. Nevertheless, I cannot allow any rebels against my power in this realm. For, as God is my judge, I declare that I keep only the good of the people of my kingdom in my sight. And so whoever is against me is also against them, and whoever is against them is also against me. But as a pledge of your good faith toward me in the future, I shall require you to send me your two eldest sons, Sir Aglovale and Sir Lamorak, as hostages for your good will. Your young son, Durnore, you may keep with you for your comfort."

So those two young knights came to the court of King Arthur, and they became very famous knights, and in time they were made Companions of the Round Table, in great honor.

And King Arthur and King Pellinore went together into King Pellinore's castle, and there King Pellinore's wounds were dressed and he was made comfortable. That night King Arthur stayed in King Pellinore's castle, and when the next morning came, he and Merlin returned to the King's court, which was waiting for him in the forest at the place where he had set it up.

*How King Arthur rode through the forest with great joy and delight*.

Now King Arthur took very great pleasure as he and Merlin rode back together through that forest, for it was the leafiest time of all the year, when the woodlands dress themselves in their best clothes of clear, bright green. Every shady hollow and little wooded valley was full of the perfume of the thickets, and in every tangled depth the little bird sang with all his might and main, as though he would burst his little throat with the melody of his song. The ground beneath the horses' feet was so soft with fragrant moss that the ear could not hear any sound of hoofbeats on the earth. And the bright yellow sunlight came down through the leaves, so that the whole ground was scattered with a great multitude of trembling circles, like pure yellow gold. And every now and then that sunlight would fall upon the armed knight as he rode, so that every little while his armor seemed to catch fire with a great glory, shining like a sudden bright star among the dark shadows of the woodland.

So it was that King Arthur took great joy in that forest land, for he was without ache or pain of any kind, and his heart was lifted very high by the wonderful success of the adventure he had undertaken. For in that adventure he had not only turned a very bitter enemy into a friend who would be of great use and comfort to him, but he had also won for himself a sword whose like the world had never seen before. And whenever he thought of that wonderfully splendid sword that now hung at his side, and whenever he remembered that Fairyland into which he had wandered, and what had happened to him there, his heart would grow so full of pure joy that he hardly knew how to contain himself, because of the great delight that filled his whole being.

And indeed, I know of no greater good I could wish for you in all your life than that you might enjoy the kind of happiness that comes to someone when he has done his very best and has succeeded completely in what he set out to do. For then the whole world seems to be filled with a bright, shining light, and the body seems to grow so light that the feet are lifted from all heaviness and touch the earth very lightly, because of the lightness of the spirit within. And that is why, if I had it in my power to give you the very best the world has to give, I would wish that you might win your battle as King Arthur won his battle that time, and that you might ride homeward in such triumph and joy as filled him that day, and that the sunlight might shine around you as it shone around him, and that the breezes might blow and all the little birds might sing with all their might and main as they sang for him, and that your heart, too, might sing its song of rejoicing in the pleasantness of the world in which you live.

*Merlin tells King Arthur of the virtues of Excalibur's sheath*.

Now, as they rode together through the forest like this, Merlin said to the King, "Lord, which would you rather have, Excalibur, or the sheath that holds him?" To which King Arthur replied, "I would rather have Excalibur than his sheath ten thousand times over." "In that you are wrong, my Lord," said Merlin, "for let me tell you that though Excalibur is of so fine a temper that he can cut in two either a feather or a bar of iron, yet his sheath is of such a kind that whoever wears it can suffer no wound in battle, nor can he lose a single drop of blood. As proof of this, you may remember that in your recent battle with King Pellinore you suffered no wound, nor did you lose any blood."

Then King Arthur turned a face of great displeasure upon his companion and said, "Now, Merlin, I declare that you have taken from me all the glory of the battle I have just fought. For what credit can there be to any knight who fights his enemy by means of enchantment such as you tell me of? Indeed, I have a mind to take this glorious sword back to that magic lake and throw it in, where it belongs. For I believe that a knight should fight by his own strength, and not by magic."

"My Lord," said Merlin, "you are certainly entirely right in what you hold. But you must bear in mind that you are not like an ordinary knight-errant. You are a King, and your life does not belong to you, but to your people. So you have no right to put it in danger, but should do everything in your power to preserve it. And that is why you should keep that sword, so that it may safeguard your life."

Then King Arthur thought over that saying for a long while in silence, and when he spoke, these were his words: "Merlin, you are right in what you say. For the sake of my people, I will keep both Excalibur, to fight for them, and his sheath as well, to preserve my life for their sake. Nevertheless, I will never use him again except in serious battle." And King Arthur held to that promise, so that from then on he never fought in sport except with the lance and on horseback.

King Arthur kept Excalibur as the greatest treasure of all his possessions. For he said to himself, "Such a sword as this is fit for a king above other kings and a lord above other lords. Now, since God has seen fit to place this sword in my keeping in so marvelous a way as happened, He must mean that I am to be His servant to do unusual things. And so I will treasure this noble weapon not so much for its excellent worth as because it shall be to me a sign of those great things that God, in His mercy, has plainly ordained for me to do in His service."

So King Arthur had a strong chest made for Excalibur, a coffer bound around with many bands of wrought iron, studded all over with great iron nails, and locked with three great padlocks. In this strongbox he kept Excalibur lying on a cushion of crimson silk and wrapped in swathings of fine linen, and very few people ever saw the sword in its glory, except when it shone like a sudden flame in the uproar of battle.

For when the time came for King Arthur to defend his realm or his people from their enemies, he would take out the sword and fasten it at his side. And when he did so, he was like a hero of God armed with a blade of shining lightning. Yes, at such times Excalibur shone with so terrible a brightness that the very sight of it would shake the spirit of every wrongdoer with such great fear that he would, in a way, suffer the pangs of death before the edge of the blade had ever touched his flesh.

So King Arthur treasured Excalibur, and the sword stayed with him all his life, so that the name of Arthur and the name of Excalibur are one. And so I believe that this sword is the most famous of any sword that was ever seen or heard of in all the courts of chivalry.

As for the sheath of the blade, King Arthur lost that through the treachery of one who should by rights have been his dearest friend (as you will hear before long), and in the end the loss of that miraculous sheath brought him a very great deal of pain and sorrow.

All that, too, you will read about, God willing, in due time.

So ends the story of the winning of Excalibur. And may God give to you in your life His Truth to help you, like a shining sword, to overcome your enemies; and may He give you Faith (for Faith holds Truth as a scabbard holds its sword), and may that Faith heal all your wounds of sorrow as the sheath of Excalibur healed all the wounds of him who wore that excellent weapon. For with Truth and Faith belted upon you, you will be as well able to fight all your battles as was that noble hero of old whom men called King Arthur.
