Chapter 1: How a White Hart Appeared Before King Arthur, How Sir Gawain and His Brother Gaheris Chased It, and What Happened to Them on That Quest

[Figure bv: Sir Gawain, the son of Lot, King of Orkney — Gawain stands with his helmet under his arm and a shield bearing a rearing griffin, an eagle on his surcoat.]

[Figure bw: Headpiece to the Story of Sir Gawain: a crowned King on a white horse rides with an armored knight through a dark wood.]

Once upon a time King Arthur, together with Queen Guinevere and all his Court, was making a royal journey through a part of his kingdom that lay a good way from Camelot. At this time the King traveled in very great state, and Queen Guinevere had her own Court around her, so there were many squires and pages. And so, counting the knights, lords, and ladies in attendance, more than a hundred and twenty people were traveling with the King and Queen.

Now it happened that the season was very warm, so that when the middle of the day came the King ordered a number of pavilions to be set up, where they might rest until the heat of the day had passed. So the attendants set up three pavilions in a pleasant glade at the edge of the forest.

When this had been done, the King gave orders that the tables at which they were to eat their midday meal should be set out beneath the shade of that grove of trees. For a gentle wind was blowing and many birds were singing, so that it was very pleasant to sit in the open air.

So the attendants of the Court did as the King commanded, and the tables were set on the grass in the shade, and the King and Queen and all the lords and ladies of their Courts sat down to that cheerful meal.

*A white hart and a white hound appear before King Arthur at his feast*.

Now while they sat there feasting, well content, with much laughter and pleasant talk among themselves, there suddenly came a great outcry from the woods close by. And with that, out of the cover of that leafy wilderness burst a very beautiful white hart (which is a full-grown stag), chased by a white brachet (which is a kind of hunting hound) just as beautiful. There was not a hair on either of these animals that was not as white as milk, and each wore around its neck a collar of gold that was very beautiful to see.

The hound chased the white hart with a tremendous outcry and baying, and the hart fled in utter terror. In this way they ran three times around the table where King Arthur and his Court sat at their meal. Twice during that chase the hound caught the hart and nipped it on the haunch, and each time the hart leaped away; and everyone sitting there saw that there was blood in two places on its haunch where the hound had nipped it. But each time the hart escaped from the hound, and the hound chased after it, yelling and baying so loudly that King Arthur and Queen Guinevere and all their Court were annoyed at the noise and uproar those two creatures made. Then the hart fled back into the forest by another path, and the hound chased it, and both were gone, and the baying of the hound sounded farther and farther away as it ran off into the woods.

Now, before the King and Queen and their Court had recovered from their astonishment at these things, there suddenly appeared, at the very spot in the forest where the hart and the hound had come out, a knight and a lady. The knight was very lordly in his bearing, and the lady was extremely beautiful. The knight wore half-armor, and the lady was dressed in green as though for the hunt. The knight rode a dapple-gray war horse, and the lady a spotted palfrey. With them were two squires, also dressed for the hunt.

When these people saw the large company gathered there, they stopped as though surprised. And while they stood there, another knight suddenly appeared on a black horse, clad in full armor, and he seemed to be very angry. For he charged at the half-armed knight and struck him such a terrible blow with his sword that the first knight fell from his horse and lay on the ground as though he were dead. At that the lady who was with him shrieked in great grief.

*King Arthur and his Court see a knight carry off a lady as his prisoner*.

Then the knight in full armor on the black horse rode up to the lady and seized her. He lifted her from her palfrey and laid her across the front of his saddle, and with that he rode back into the forest. The lady screamed so violently that it was a great pity to hear her, but the knight paid no attention to her shrieks and carried her off by main force into the forest.

Then, after he and the lady were gone, the two squires came and lifted the wounded knight onto his horse, and then they too went away into the forest and were gone.

King Arthur and his Court watched all this from a distance, and they were so far away that they could not stop that knight on the black horse from carrying the lady off into the forest. Nor could they bring help to the other knight in half-armor, whom they had seen struck down like that. So they were very much grieved by what they had seen and did not know what to make of it. Then King Arthur said to his Court, "Sirs, is there not one of you who will follow this adventure, and find out what it means that we have just seen, and make that knight tell why he behaved as he did?"

*Sir Gawain and Gaheris take on this adventure*.

At this Sir Gawain said, "Lord, I will be very glad indeed to take on this adventure, if I have your permission." And King Arthur said, "You have my permission." Then Sir Gawain said, "Lord, I wish you would also let me take my younger brother, Gaheris, with me as my squire on this undertaking. For he is growing up fast, and yet he has never seen any real adventure at arms." So King Arthur said, "You have my permission to take your brother with you."

At this Gaheris was very glad, for he had an adventurous spirit, and so the thought of going with his brother on this quest gave him great pleasure.

So the two of them went to Sir Gawain's pavilion, and there Gaheris, acting as his squire, helped Sir Gawain put on his armor. Then they rode out on the quest that Sir Gawain had undertaken.

They traveled a very long way, following the direction they had seen the hart take when it fled from the hound. And whenever they met any of the forest folk, they would ask them which way the white brachet and the white hart had gone, and which way the knight and the lady had gone. And so they followed that adventure at a good pace.

*They come upon two knights fighting*.

After a while, when they had gone a long way and the afternoon was far along, they suddenly became aware of a great noise of combat, like the sound of a fierce battle. So they followed this sound, and after a while they came to an open meadow with very fair and level turf. There they saw two knights fighting with furious passion and with deadly purpose. Then Sir Gawain said, "What's this? Let's go and see." So he and Gaheris rode forward to where those two knights were fighting, and as they came near, the two knights stopped their combat and rested on their weapons. Then Sir Gawain said, "Ha! Sirs, what's the matter here, and why are you fighting each other so furiously?" One of the knights said to Sir Gawain, "Sir, this does not concern you." And the other said, "Don't meddle with us, for this battle is of our own choosing."

"Sirs," said Sir Gawain, "I would be very sorry to interfere in your quarrel. But I am in pursuit of a white hart and a white brachet that came this way, and also of a knight who carried off a lady along the same path. Now I would be very much obliged to you if you would tell me whether you have seen anything of either of them."

*One of the knights tells their story*.

Then the knight who had spoken first said, "Sir, this is a very strange thing, for it was on account of that very white hart and that brachet, and of that knight and lady, that we two were fighting the battle you just saw. For this is how it was. We two are brothers, and we were riding together in perfect friendship when that hart and that hound came this way. Then my brother said he very much hoped the white hart would escape from the hound, and I said I hoped the hound would catch the hart and bring it down. Then that knight came by with the lady, his captive, and I said I would follow that knight and rescue the lady, and my brother said that he would take on that adventure himself.

"Over these points we fell into a dispute. For it seemed to me that I felt great affection for that hound, while my brother felt just as remarkable a fondness for the white hart; and it seemed to me that since I had spoken first, I should have the right to follow that adventure. But my brother was fond of the hart, and he thought that since he was the elder of us two, he had the better right to the adventure. So we quarreled, and before long we fell to that fight in which you saw us."

At this Sir Gawain was very much astonished, and he said, "Sirs, I can't understand how so great a quarrel could come out of so small a dispute. And truly it is a great pity for two brothers to quarrel as you have done, and to give each other such bad cuts and wounds as I can see you have both received."

"Sir," said the second knight, "I think you are right, and I now find that I am very much ashamed of that quarrel." And the other said, "I too am sorry for what I have done."

Then Sir Gawain said, "Sirs, I would be very glad indeed if you would tell me your names." And one knight said, "I am called Sir Sorloise of the Forest." And the other said, "I am called Sir Brian of the Forest."

Then Sir Sorloise said, "Sir Knight, I would think it a very great courtesy if you would tell me who you are."

"I would be very glad to do that," said Sir Gawain, and with that he told them his name and rank. Now when they heard who Sir Gawain was, those two knights were very much astonished and pleased, for no one in all the courts of chivalry was more famous than Sir Gawain, the son of King Lot of Orkney. So those two brothers said, "It is certainly a great joy to us to meet so famous a knight as you, Sir Gawain."

Then Sir Gawain said, "Sir Knights, that hart and that hound came only a short while ago to where King Arthur and Queen Guinevere and their Courts of lords and ladies were feasting. And there, too, we all saw that knight seize the lady and make her his captive. And so my brother and I have come out at King Arthur's command to discover what it was that we saw. Now I will think it a very great courtesy on your part if you will give up this adventure and go in friendship to the King's Court, and tell him what you saw, and how you quarreled, and how we met. For otherwise I will have to fight you both myself, and that would be a great pity, since you are tired from battle and I am fresh."

Then these two knights said, "Sir, we will do as you wish, for we have no desire to fight so powerful a knight as you."

So those two knights went off to King Arthur's Court as Sir Gawain had directed, and Sir Gawain and his brother rode on with their adventure.

*Sir Gawain and Gaheris meet a knight beside the river*.

After a while they came near a great river, and there they saw ahead of them a single knight in full armor, who carried a spear in his hand and a shield hanging from his saddle. Sir Gawain hurried forward and called out loudly to the knight, and the knight stopped and waited until Sir Gawain had caught up with him. And when Sir Gawain came up to that knight he said, "Sir Knight, have you seen a white hart and a white hound go by this way? And have you seen a knight carrying off a captive lady?"

To this the knight said, "Yes, I saw them both, and I am following them right now, meaning to find out where they are going." Then Sir Gawain said, "Sir Knight, I tell you not to follow this adventure any farther, for I am set on it myself. So I want you to give it up, so that I may take it on in your place." "Sir," said the other knight, speaking very hotly, "I don't know who you are, and I don't much care. But as for following this adventure, I tell you that I mean to follow it to the end, and so I will, no matter who tries to stop me."

At that Sir Gawain said, "Sir, you will not go forward on this adventure unless you first fight me." And the knight said, "Sir, I am very willing to do that."

*Sir Gawain overthrows the knight*.

So each knight took the position on that field that seemed best to him, and each put himself on guard and set his shield and his spear. Then, when they were ready in every way, they at once charged against each other, rushing together with great speed and with such a thunder that the ground trembled and shook beneath them. So they met in the middle of the course, and the spear of the strange knight burst into small pieces, but Sir Gawain's spear held. And with it he hurled that knight out of his saddle with such violence that he struck the ground with a crash like an earthquake.

Then Sir Gawain rode back to where his enemy lay (for that knight was not able to get up), and he took the helmet off the fallen knight's head and saw that he was very young and handsome.

Now when the fresh air struck the knight's face, he soon woke from his faint and came back to his senses. At that Sir Gawain said, "Do you yield to me?" And the knight said, "I do." Then Sir Gawain said, "Who are you?" And the knight said, "I am called Sir Alardin of the Isles." "Very well," said Sir Gawain. "Then this is my command to you: you will go to King Arthur's Court and give yourself up to him as a captive taken by my prowess. And you are to tell him all that you know of the hart and the hound and the knight and the lady. And you will tell him everything that has happened to you in this encounter."

So the knight said that he would do that, and with that they parted, the one party going one way and the other party going the other.

After that Sir Gawain and his brother Gaheris rode a long way, until at last they came through a forest out onto an open plain, and by now it was about sunset. And there in the middle of the plain they saw a very stately and noble castle with five towers, and of very great strength.

*Sir Gawain and Gaheris find the dead brachet*.

And right there they saw a sight that filled them with great sorrow. For they saw the dead body of that white brachet lying beside the road like any carrion. And they saw that the hound had been shot through with three arrows, so they knew it had been killed very violently.

Now when Sir Gawain saw that beautiful hound lying dead like that, he was filled with great sorrow. "What a pity it is," he cried, "that this noble hound should be killed like this! For I think it was the most beautiful hound I ever saw in all my life. There has surely been great treachery against it here, for it has been foully dealt with because of that white hart it was chasing. Now I make a vow that if I can find that hart, I will kill it with my own hands, because it was in that chase that this hound met its death."

After that they rode on toward the castle, and as they came near, there, grazing on the meadows in front of the castle, they saw that white hart with the golden collar.

Now as soon as the white hart saw those two strangers, it fled at great speed toward the castle and ran into the castle courtyard. And when Sir Gawain saw the stag, he gave chase at great speed, and Gaheris followed after his brother.

*Sir Gawain kills the white hart*.

So Sir Gawain chased the white hart into the courtyard of the castle, and from there it could not escape. Then Sir Gawain leaped down from his horse, drew his sword, and killed the hart with a single stroke of his weapon.

He did this in great haste. But when he had done it, and it was too late to undo it, he bitterly regretted what he had done.

Now at all this commotion the lord and the lady of that castle came out. The lord was very haughty and noble in his looks, and the lady was extraordinarily graceful and very beautiful. Sir Gawain looked at the lady and thought he had hardly ever seen so beautiful a lady, and so he was sorrier than ever that he had killed that white hart in his haste.

But when the lady of the castle saw the white hart lying dead on the stone pavement of the courtyard, she struck her hands together and shrieked so shrilly and so loudly that it pierced the ears to hear her. And she cried out, "Oh, my white hart, are you dead, then?" And with that she began to weep passionately. Then Sir Gawain said, "Lady, I am very sorry for what I have done, and I wish I could undo it." Then the lord of that castle said to Sir Gawain, "Sir, did you kill that stag?" "Yes," said Sir Gawain. "Sir," said the lord of the castle, "you have done a very wicked thing, and if you will wait a little while I will take full vengeance on you." To which Sir Gawain said, "I will wait for you as long as you please."

*The knight of the castle attacks Sir Gawain*.

Then the lord of the castle went into his chamber and put on his armor, and in a little while he came out in a fury. "Sir," said Sir Gawain, "what is your quarrel with me?" And the lord of the castle said, "That you have killed the white hart that was so dear to my lady." To which Sir Gawain said, "I would never have killed the white hart if it weren't that, because of it, the white brachet was so treacherously killed." At this the lord of the castle was angrier than ever, and he rushed at Sir Gawain and struck him before he was ready. The blow cut through the shoulder plate of his armor and through the flesh to the bone of his shoulder, so that Sir Gawain was in great agony from the stroke. Then Sir Gawain was filled with rage at the pain of the wound, and he struck the knight such a terrible blow that it cut through his helmet and into the bone beneath. At that the knight fell to his knees from the fierceness of the blow and could not get up again. Then Sir Gawain seized his helmet and tore it off his head.

At this the knight said in a weak voice, "Sir Knight, I beg you for mercy, and I yield myself to you."

*Sir Gawain moves to kill the knight of the castle*.

But Sir Gawain was in a furious rage because of the unexpected blow he had received and the great agony of his wound, and so he would show no mercy, but raised his sword, meaning to kill that knight.

*Sir Gawain strikes the lady of the castle without meaning to*.

Then the lady of the castle saw what Sir Gawain meant to do, and she broke away from her damsels and ran and threw herself on the knight, to shield him with her own body. At that very moment Sir Gawain was striking and could not stop his blow. Even so, he managed to turn his sword in his hand so that its edge did not strike the lady. But the flat of the sword struck her on the neck a very grievous blow, and the blade cut her a little, so that the blood ran down her smooth white neck and over her neckerchief. And with the force of the blow the lady fell down and lay on the ground as though she were dead.

Now when Sir Gawain saw that, he thought he had killed the lady in his haste, and he was filled with dread at what he had done, and he cried, "Woe is me! What have I done?"

"Alas!" said Gaheris, "that was a very shameful blow you struck. And the shame of it is mine too, because you are my brother. Now I wish I had never come with you to this place."

Then Sir Gawain said to the lord of the castle, "Sir, I will spare your life, for I am very sorry for what I have done in my haste."

But the knight of the castle was filled with great bitterness, because he thought his lady was dead, and so he cried out in despair, "I will not take your mercy now, for you are a knight without mercy and without pity. And since you have killed my lady, who is dearer to me than my life, you may kill me too. For that is the only service you can do me now."

But by now the lady's damsels had come to her where she lay, and the chief of them cried out to the lord of the castle, "Ah, sir, your lady is not dead! She has only fainted, and she will soon come to herself again."

Then, when the lord of the castle heard that, he began to weep freely for pure joy; for now that he knew his lady was alive, he could not contain himself for gladness. With that Sir Gawain came to him and lifted him up from the ground where he was, and kissed him on the cheek. Then some others came and carried the lady away to her chamber, and there in a little while she recovered from her faint and was little the worse for the blow she had received.

That night Sir Gawain and his brother Gaheris stayed with the knight and the lady, and when the knight learned who Sir Gawain was, he felt it a great honor to have so famous a knight in his castle. So they feasted together that evening in great friendship.

Now when they had refreshed themselves, Sir Gawain said, "I beg you, sir, to tell me what was the meaning of the white hart and the white brachet that led me into this adventure."

To this the lord of the castle (whose name was Sir Ablamor of the Marise) said, "I will do so." And with that he spoke as follows:

"You must know, sir, that I have a brother who has always been very dear to me, and when I took this lady of mine for my wife, he took her sister for his wife.

*The lord of the castle tells the story of the white hart and the white hound*.

"Now my brother lived in a castle near this one, and we visited back and forth in great friendship. But one day it happened that my lady and my brother's lady were riding through this forest together, talking very pleasantly. At that moment a lady appeared to them, extremely beautiful and very strange in her looks, for I do not think either my lady or her sister had ever seen anyone like her before.

"This strange lady brought those two ladies a white hart and a white brachet, and she held the hart and the hound each by a silver chain fastened to a golden collar around its neck. The white hart she gave to my lady, and the white brachet she gave to my lady's sister. And then she went away, leaving them very glad.

"But their gladness did not last long. For ever since that time there has been nothing but discord between my brother and me, and between my lady and her sister, because the white hound has always hunted the white hart to destroy it. And so my lady and I have taken great offense at my brother and his lady because they did not keep the white brachet at home. And so it has happened that several times we have tried to destroy the hound, so that my brother and his lady have taken just as much offense at us.

"Now today I happened to be near the edge of the forest to the east of us, when I heard a great outcry in the woods. Soon the white hart that belonged to my lady came fleeing through the woods, and the white brachet that belonged to my brother's lady was chasing it; and my brother and his lady and two squires were riding hard after the hart and the brachet.

"Then I was very angry, for it seemed to me that they were chasing that white hart to spite my lady and me, and so I followed after them as fast as I could.

"So I came upon them at the edge of the woods, near where a number of pavilions were set up in the shade of a grove of trees in the middle of the meadow. And there, in my anger, I struck my brother a great blow, so that I knocked him down from his horse. And I seized his lady and threw her across the front of my saddle and carried her away here to this castle. And here I have kept her, out of revenge, because they chased the white hart that belonged to my lady. For my lady loved that hart as she loved nothing else in the world, except me."

"Sir," said Sir Gawain, "this is a very strange business. Now I beg you to tell me what that lady looked like who gave the white hart and the white hound to those two ladies." "Sir," said the knight, "she was dressed all in crimson, and around her throat and arms she wore a great many ornaments of gold set with stones of many colors. Her hair was red like gold and was caught up in a net of gold, and her eyes were very black and shone with extraordinary brightness, and her lips were like coral, so that she had a very strange appearance."

*Sir Gawain hears of Vivien*.

"Ha!" said Sir Gawain. "From that description I think that lady could have been none other than the sorceress Vivien. For these days she spends all her time doing mischief like this by her enchantments, out of pure spite. And indeed, I think it would be a very good thing if she were put out of this world, so that she could do no more such mischief. But tell me, sir, where is that lady now, your wife's sister?" "Sir," said the knight, "she is in this castle, and she is a prisoner of honor." "Well," said Sir Gawain, "since both the hart and the hound are now dead, you surely can have no more quarrel with her and your brother. And so I beg you to set her free, and to be friends again with one another, in good will, as you were before." And the lord of the castle said, "Sir, it shall be so."

And so he set the lady free then and there, and after that there was friendship between them, as Sir Gawain had directed.

And the next day Sir Gawain and his brother Gaheris returned to the King's Court, and Sir Gawain told King Arthur and his Court everything that had happened, hiding nothing from them.

*Queen Guinevere is displeased with Sir Gawain*.

Now Queen Guinevere was very much displeased when she heard how Sir Gawain had refused to show mercy to that knight, and how he had struck the lady with his sword. So she said quietly to one of those who stood near her, "It seems to me a very strange thing for a belted knight to do, to refuse mercy to a fallen enemy and to strike a lady with his sword. For I should think that any sword that had drawn a lady's blood in that way would be dishonored forever. And I cannot think that anyone who would strike a lady like that could hold himself blameless before his vow of knighthood."

*Sir Gawain breaks his sword*.

Sir Gawain overheard this, and he was extremely angry at it. But he hid his anger at the time. Only after he had gone away did he say to his brother Gaheris, "I believe that lady hates me with all her heart. But someday I will show her that I have more courtesy in me, and am gentler, than she believes. As for my sword, since she thinks it is dishonored by that blow, I will not use it anymore." So he took the sword out of its sheath and broke it across his knee and threw it away.

Now all this has been told to prepare you for what follows. For there you will learn what great and noble things Sir Gawain could do when the need came. For perhaps you who have read this story may feel, as Queen Guinevere did, that Sir Gawain was not truly as courteous as a belted knight should have been in the adventure I have just told.
