Chapter 3: How Queen Morgan le Fay Came Back to Camelot Meaning to Harm King Arthur, and How the King and Others Went Hunting, and What Came of It

[Figure bk: Queen Morgan le Fay — The Queen sits in a jeweled cap and a broad necklace, holding an open book on her lap and looking out coldly.]

Now, after Merlin had left the Court with Vivien in the way I have already told you, Queen Morgan le Fay came back to Camelot. There she came to King Arthur and knelt before him, bowing her face, with a look of great humility. And she said, "Brother, I have thought a great deal about the things that have happened, and I see that I did very wrong to speak against you as I did, and to rebel against your royalty as I did. So I beg you to forgive me my evil words and my evil thoughts against you."

*Queen Morgan le Fay and King Arthur are reconciled*.

Then King Arthur was very much moved. He came to Queen Morgan and took her by the hand and lifted her to her feet, and he kissed her brow and her eyes, saying, "My sister, I bear you no ill will. There is nothing but love for you in my heart." And so Queen Morgan le Fay stayed at the Court just as she had before, for King Arthur believed that the two of them were reconciled.

*King Arthur shows Excalibur to Queen Morgan*.

Now one day Queen Morgan and the King fell into a friendly talk about Excalibur, and Queen Morgan le Fay said she very much wished to see that noble weapon more closely than she had yet been able to, and King Arthur said that he would show it to her sometime. So the next day he said, "Sister, come with me and I will show you Excalibur." With that he took Queen Morgan by the hand and led her into another room, where there stood a strong wooden chest bound with bands of iron. Then the King opened the chest, and there Queen Morgan le Fay saw Excalibur, lying in his sheath. And King Arthur said to her, "Lady, take this sword and look at it as much as you please." So Queen Morgan took Excalibur into her hands and lifted him out of the chest. And she drew the sword from the sheath, and the blade flashed like lightning. Then she said, "Sir, this is a very beautiful sword, and I wish I might take it away with me and keep it for a little while, so that I could enjoy it to the full."

Now King Arthur wished to show the Queen great courtesy at this time when they had made their peace, so he said to her, "Take it, and be its keeper for as long as you like." So Queen Morgan took Excalibur and his sheath and carried them away with her to her lodging, and she hid the sword in the bed where she slept.

Then Queen Morgan sent for goldsmiths, eight of them, and for armorers, eight of them, and for skilful jewelers, eight of them, and she said to them, "Make me a sword exactly like this sword that I have here, in every single detail." And then she showed them Excalibur in his sheath. So these goldsmiths and armorers and jewel-cutters worked with great diligence, and in two weeks they had made a sword so exactly like Excalibur that no eye could have told the one from the other. And Queen Morgan le Fay kept both swords by her until her plans should be carried out.

Then one day it happened that King Arthur proclaimed a hunt, and he and all his Court were to take part in it.

*Queen Morgan le Fay gives a horse to King Arthur*.

Now the day before the hunt, Queen Morgan le Fay came to King Arthur and said, "Brother, I have here for you a very beautiful and noble horse, which I mean to give you as a gift of love." With that she called aloud, and two grooms came leading a horse as black as jet, all decked with trappings and harness of silver. And the horse was so extraordinarily beautiful that neither King Arthur nor anyone with him had ever seen its equal. So a wonderful delight filled the King at the sight of that horse, and he said, "Sister, this is the noblest gift anyone has given me for a long time." "Ha, brother," said Queen Morgan, "so the horse pleases you?" "Yes," said King Arthur, "it pleases me more than any horse I have ever seen." "Then," said Queen Morgan, "think of it as a gift of reconciliation between you and me. And as a sign of that reconciliation, I beg you to ride that horse on the hunt tomorrow." And King Arthur said, "I will do so."

So the next day he rode out to the hunt on that horse, as he had said he would.

*King Arthur rides hunting*.

Now some time after noon it happened that the hounds started a hart of extraordinary size, and the King and all his Court followed the chase with great eagerness. But King Arthur's horse soon outran all the other horses except one, which belonged to a very honorable and worthy knight of the Court named Sir Accolon of Gaul. So Sir Accolon and the King rode at a great pace through the forest, and they were so eager in the chase that they did not notice where they were riding. At last they overtook the hart and found it trapped in a very thick and tangled part of the forest, and there King Arthur killed the stag, and so the chase was ended.

*King Arthur and Sir Accolon of Gaul are lost in the forest*.

After this the King and Sir Accolon would have gone back the way they had come, but soon they found that they were lost in the maze of the woods and did not know where they were. For they had followed the chase so far that they were in a completely strange country. So they wandered this way and that for a long time, until evening, and by then they were worn down with hunger and weariness. Then King Arthur said to Sir Accolon, "Sir, it seems to me that we shall have nowhere to rest tonight except under a tree in this forest."

To this Sir Accolon answered, "Lord, if you will take my advice, you will let our horses find their own way through this wilderness. Perhaps, by the instinct such creatures have, they will bring us to some place where people live."

This advice seemed very good to King Arthur, so he did as Sir Accolon advised: he let his bridle rein go loose and allowed his horse to go wherever it liked. So King Arthur's horse went along a certain path, and Sir Accolon followed after the King. And they went a good long way like this, while the night came down upon them in the forest.

But before it was entirely dark they came out of the forest into an open place, where they saw before them a very wide estuary, like an inlet of the sea. In front of them lay a beach of sand, very smooth and white, and the two of them went down to that beach and stood on the shore. And they did not know what to do, for there was no house or dwelling in sight in any direction.

*King Arthur and Sir Accolon see a wonderful ship*.

Now while they stood there in doubt, they suddenly saw a ship a very great distance away. And as the ship came nearer they saw that it was very strange and wonderful to look at. It was painted in many different colors, very gay and brilliant, and its sails were all of silk cloth, woven in many colors and embroidered with figures like the figures of a tapestry. And King Arthur was very greatly amazed at the sight of that ship.

As they stood there watching it, they saw that it was drawing nearer and nearer to the place where they were, and after a little while it ran itself aground on the sandy shore not very far away from them.

Then King Arthur said to Sir Accolon, "Sir, let us go down to the water's edge, where we can look into this ship. For I have never seen anything like it in all my life, and I have a notion that it may be a fairy ship."

So the two of them went to where the ship lay, and they stood on the shore and looked down into it. At first they thought there was no one aboard, for it seemed to be entirely deserted. But as they stood there marveling at how wonderful the ship was and at the way it had come there, they suddenly saw the curtains that hung before a cabin at the far end of the ship part, and out of that place came twelve very beautiful damsels. Each of them was dressed in a rich gown of scarlet satin, very bright and shining, and each wore a circlet of gold around her head, and each had many bracelets of gold upon her arms. These damsels came forward to where the two knights were, and they said, "Welcome, King Arthur!" And they said, "Welcome, Sir Accolon!"

King Arthur was very much astonished that they should know him, and he said, "Fair ladies, how is this? You seem to know me very well, but I do not know you. Who are you, that you know me and my companion and call us by our names?"

The chief of those damsels answered, "Sir, we are part fairy, and we know all about you. We know that you have been following a very long chase, and we know that you are weary and hungry and thirsty. So we beg you to come aboard this ship and rest and refresh yourselves with food and drink."

Now this seemed to King Arthur a very fine adventure, so he said to Sir Accolon, "Sir, I have a great mind to go aboard this ship and follow this adventure to the end." And Sir Accolon said, "Lord, if you go, I will go too."

*King Arthur and Sir Accolon enter the ship of damsels*.

So the ladies let down a gangplank from the ship, and King Arthur and Sir Accolon drove their horses up the gangplank and aboard. And the ship sailed away the way it had come, very swiftly. By now it was early night, and the moon hung very round and full in the sky, like a disk of pure shining silver.

Then the twelve damsels helped King Arthur and Sir Accolon to dismount. Some of them took the horses away, and others led the two knights into a fair room at the end of the ship. And in this room King Arthur saw that a table had been set as though for their entertainment, spread with a linen cloth and laid with many kinds of savory meats, with small loaves of white bread, and with several different sorts of excellent wine. At the sight King Arthur and Sir Accolon were greatly cheered, for they were very hungry indeed.

So they sat down at the table at once and ate and drank with great heartiness. And while they did so, some of the damsels served them their food, and others kept them in pleasant conversation, and others played music on lutes and citterns to entertain them. So they feasted and were very merry.

But after a while a very great drowsiness began to come over King Arthur, though he thought it had come upon him from the weariness of the chase. So soon he said, "Fair damsels, you have refreshed us a great deal, and this has been a very pleasant adventure. But now I wish you had a place for us to sleep."

The chief of the damsels replied, "Lord, this ship has been made ready for your refreshment, so everything you could need has been prepared for you in full."

With that, some of the twelve damsels led King Arthur to a bedchamber that had been prepared for him, and others led Sir Accolon to another chamber prepared for him. And King Arthur marveled at the beauty of his room, for he thought he had never seen a more splendidly furnished bedchamber than the one he had just entered. So King Arthur lay down in great comfort of body, and he fell at once into a deep and gentle sleep, without any dream or disturbance of any kind.

*King Arthur finds himself in a dreadful prison*.

Now when King Arthur woke from that sleep, he was astonished beyond all measure, so that he did not know whether he was still asleep and dreaming or whether he was awake. For he was lying on a pallet of straw in a very dark and dismal room, all of stone. And he saw that this room was a dungeon, and all around him he heard the sound of many voices complaining in misery. Then King Arthur said to himself, "Where is the ship I was on last night, and what has become of those ladies I spoke with?"

At that he looked around him, and he saw that he really was in a dungeon, and that there were many knights in a very sad state all around him. So he understood that they too were captives, and that it was they who had made that sound of miserable lamenting which he had heard as he woke.

Then King Arthur roused himself from where he lay, and he saw that all the knights who were prisoners there were strangers to him: he did not know them and they did not know him. And there were twenty-two of these knights held prisoner in that place.

Then King Arthur said, "Sirs, who are you, and where am I now?" And the chief of the knights who were prisoners answered, "Sir, like you, we are prisoners in a dungeon of this castle. And the castle belongs to a certain knight named Sir Damas, who is called le Noir, which means the Black."

Then King Arthur marveled greatly at what had happened to him, and he said, "Sirs, a very strange thing has happened to me. For last night I fell asleep in a very wonderful ship that I believe was a fairy ship, and a knight was with me as my companion. And now this morning I wake up alone in this dungeon, and I do not know how I came here."

"Sir," said the knight who was speaking for the others, "last night you were brought here by two men dressed in black, and they laid you down on that pallet without waking you. So it is very plain to me that you are in the same plight as we are, and that you are a prisoner of this Sir Damas le Noir."

Then King Arthur said, "Tell me, who is this Sir Damas? For I declare I have never heard of him before." "I will tell you," said the captive knight, and he did so, like this:

"I believe," he said, "that this Sir Damas is the falsest knight alive, for he is full of treachery and lies, and in his heart he is a complete coward. Yet he is a man of very great wealth and very powerful in these parts.

*The prisoner knight tells King Arthur about Sir Damas*.

"Now there are two brothers. Sir Damas is one, and the other is named Sir Ontzlake, and Sir Damas is the elder and Sir Ontzlake the younger. When the father of these two knights died, he left them equal shares of his inheritance. But now it has come about that Sir Damas holds nearly all those estates, and Sir Ontzlake has only one castle, which he now holds by force of arms and by his own courage. For though Sir Damas is a coward in his heart, he has more craft and cunning than any man I ever heard of. And so it has turned out that of their father's inheritance Sir Damas has everything, and Sir Ontzlake has nothing but that one castle and the lands that go with it.

"Now you would think Sir Damas would be satisfied with all this. Yet he is not satisfied. He covets that one castle and that small estate of his brother's so much that he can hardly take any pleasure in life for wanting it. Yet he does not know how to get that estate away from his brother. For Sir Ontzlake is a very excellent knight, and the only way Sir Damas could lay hands on his land would be to fight his brother man to man in a contest of arms, and that he is afraid to try.

"So for a long time Sir Damas has been searching for a knight who will take up his cause for him and fight Sir Ontzlake on his behalf. And so every knight he can capture he brings to this castle, and he gives each one a choice: either to take up his cause against his brother, or else to stay in this place as his prisoner, with no ransom ever accepted. He has captured all of us like that, and he has demanded of each of us that we fight for him. But not one of us will take up the cause of a knight as wicked as Sir Damas, and so we all remain his prisoners."

"Well," said King Arthur, "this is a very strange business. But I think that if Sir Damas asks me, I will take up his cause, for I would rather do that than stay a prisoner here all my life. But if I take on this battle and win it, then afterward I will settle with Sir Damas himself, in a way that I do not believe will be very much to his liking."

*A damsel comes to King Arthur*.

*King Arthur agrees to fight for Sir Damas*.

Now a little while after this, the door of the prison was opened by the porter, and in came a very fair young damsel. She came to King Arthur and said to him, "How are you faring?" "I cannot tell," said King Arthur, "but it seems to me I am in a very sorry plight in this place." "Sir," said the damsel, "it grieves me to see so noble-looking a knight in so sad a case. But if you will undertake to fight with your own body for the cause of the lord of this castle against his enemy, then you shall have leave to go wherever you please." King Arthur answered her, "Lady, this is a very hard choice, that either I must fight a battle I do not care about or else stay a prisoner here, with no ransom, for all my days. But I would rather fight than live here all my life, and so I will undertake that adventure as you wish me to. But if I fight for the lord of this castle, and if by the grace of Heaven I win that battle, then all these, my companions in prison, must go free with me."

To this the damsel said, "Very well, so be it, for that will satisfy the master of this castle."

Then King Arthur looked more closely at the maiden, and he said, "Damsel, it seems to me that I know your face, for I think I have seen you somewhere before this." "No, sir," she said, "that can hardly be, for I am the daughter of the lord of this castle."

But in this she lied. For she was one of Morgan le Fay's damsels, and she was one of those who had lured King Arthur into the ship the night before, and it was she who had brought him to that castle and handed him over to Sir Damas. And she had done all these things at the command of Queen Morgan le Fay.

Then King Arthur said, "But if I fight this battle, you must carry a message for me to the Court of King Arthur, and that message must be delivered to Queen Morgan le Fay, into her own hands. Then, when that is done, I will fight this battle for the cause of Sir Damas." And the damsel said, "It shall be done."

*Queen Morgan sends a false sword to King Arthur*.

So King Arthur wrote a sealed letter to Queen Morgan le Fay, asking her to send him his sword Excalibur, and he sent that message to her. And when Queen Morgan received the letter she laughed and said, "Very well. He shall have a sword that will please his eye just as well as Excalibur." And with that she sent him the other sword, the one she had had made exactly like Excalibur.

Then Sir Damas sent word to his brother, Sir Ontzlake, that he now had a champion to fight on his behalf and win back all that part of their inheritance which Sir Ontzlake still kept from him.

Now when Sir Ontzlake received this message he was thrown into great distress of mind. For a little while before, he had been very badly wounded in a tournament, where a spear had been driven through both his thighs, so that he was now lying in bed with that wound and had no power to get up. So he did not know what to do, for he could not fight on his own behalf, and he had no one to fight for him.
