Chapter 2: How King Arthur Got Lost in the Forest and Met with a Very Strange Adventure in a Castle He Came To

[Figure bx: King Arthur finds the old woman in the hut — The crowned King stoops in the doorway of a hut toward a bent old woman seated by the fire.]

Now it happened, some while after this, that King Arthur was at Tintagalon on certain affairs of state. And Queen Guinevere with her Court and the King's Court made a royal journey from Camelot to Caerleon, and there they stayed, waiting until the King should finish his business at Tintagalon and join them at Caerleon.

It was the springtime of the year, and everything was very bright and merry, and so King Arthur was seized with a great longing for adventure. So he called to him a certain squire of whom he was especially fond, named Boisenard, and he said to him, "Boisenard, this day is so pleasant that I hardly know how to contain myself for the joy I take in it. It seems to me that my heart is nearly ready to burst with a great pleasure of longing. So I have a mind to go roaming, with only you for company."

To this Boisenard said, "Lord, I know of nothing that would give me greater pleasure than that."

So King Arthur said, "Very well. Then let us leave this place in such a way that no one will know we have gone. We will ride to Caerleon and surprise the Queen by arriving there when she does not expect us."

So Boisenard brought armor without any emblem on it, and he dressed the King in that armor. Then the two of them rode out together, and no one knew that they had left the castle.

*King Arthur sets out with his squire*.

And when they came out into the fields, King Arthur whistled and sang and joked and laughed and made himself merry. For he was like a war-horse turned out to grass, who glories in the sunshine and the warm air and becomes like a young colt again.

So after a while they came into the forest and rode along with great contentment of spirit. They took this path and they took that path for no reason except that the day was so bright and merry. And so, by and by, they lost their way in the mazes of the woodland and did not know where they were.

*They are lost in the forest*.

Now when they found themselves lost like this, they traveled more carefully, going first this way and then that way, but in no way could they find their way out of their tangle. And so night fell while they still did not know where they were. Everything grew very dark and gloomy, and the woodland all around them was full of strange and unfamiliar sounds.

Then King Arthur said, "Boisenard, this is a very puzzling fix, and I do not know how we shall find lodging for tonight."

To this Boisenard said, "Lord, if I have your permission, I will climb one of these trees and see if I can find any sign of a dwelling in this wilderness." And King Arthur said, "Do so, I ask you."

*Boisenard sees a light*.

So Boisenard climbed a very tall tree, and from the top of it he saw a light a great distance away. And he said, "Lord, I see a light in that direction." And with that he came down from the tree again.

So King Arthur and Boisenard went in the direction where Boisenard had seen the light. And by and by they came out of the forest into an open place, where they saw a very great castle with several tall towers, very grim and forbidding to look at. And it was from this castle that the light Boisenard had seen was shining. So the two of them rode up to the castle, and Boisenard called out loudly and pounded on the castle gate. At once a porter came and demanded to know what they wanted. Boisenard said to him, "You there, we want to come in and lodge here tonight, for we are very weary." The porter said, "Who are you?", speaking very roughly and rudely to them, for in the darkness he could not see what rank they were. Then Boisenard said, "This is a knight of very high rank, and I am his squire. We have lost our way in the forest, and now we come here looking for shelter."

"Sir," said the porter, "if you know what is good for you, you will sleep in the forest rather than come into this place. For this is no very good refuge for knights-errant to shelter in."

At this King Arthur spoke to the porter, for what the porter had said stirred up a great curiosity in him. So he said, "No, we will not go away from here, and we demand to lodge here tonight."

Then the porter said, "Very well; you may come in." And with that he opened the gate, and they rode into the courtyard of the castle.

*King Arthur and his squire enter the castle*.

Now at the noise of their coming a great many lights appeared within the castle, and a number of attendants came running out. Some of these helped King Arthur and Boisenard to dismount, and others took the horses, and still others brought basins of water for them to wash in. And after they had washed their faces and hands, other attendants led them into the castle.

As they came into the castle, they heard a great noise of very many people talking and laughing together, with the sound of singing and of harping. And so they came into the hall of the castle, and they saw that it was lit with a great many candles and tapers and torches. Here they found a crowd of people gathered at a table spread for a feast. At the head of the table sat a knight who was well on in years, with hair and beard as white as milk. Yet he was extremely strong and sturdy of build, with wonderfully broad shoulders and a great barrel of a chest. This knight had a very stern and forbidding look. He was dressed all in black, and around his neck he wore a chain of gold with a locket of gold hanging from it.

Now when this knight saw King Arthur and Boisenard come into the hall, he called out to them in a very loud voice, inviting them to come and sit with him at the head of the table. And they did so. Those at the head of the table made room for them, and so they sat there beside the knight.

King Arthur and Boisenard were extremely hungry, and so they ate with a great appetite and enjoyed the hospitality they were given. Meanwhile the knight kept them in very pleasant conversation, talking to them about whatever would entertain them most. So after a while the feast was over and they stopped eating.

*The knight of the castle challenges King Arthur to an adventure*.

Then, all of a sudden, the knight said to King Arthur, "Sir, you are young and bold of spirit, and I do not doubt that you have a great heart inside you. What do you say now to a little sport between the two of us?" At this King Arthur looked at that knight very steadily, and he believed that the knight's face was not as old as it seemed. For his eyes were extremely bright and shone like sparks of fire, and so Arthur was doubtful, and he said, "Sir, what sport do you have in mind?" At this the knight burst out laughing very heartily, and he said, "The sport I have in mind is a very strange one, for it is this: that you and I shall prove to each other how much courage each of us has." And King Arthur said, "How shall we prove that?" The knight answered, "This is what we shall do. You and I will stand out in the middle of this hall, and you shall have leave to try to strike off my head. And if I can take that blow without dying of it, then I shall have leave to strike off your head in the same way."

At this speech King Arthur was filled with great dread, and he said, "That is a very strange sport for two men to take up."

Now when King Arthur said this, everyone in the hall burst out laughing beyond all measure, as though they would never stop their merriment. Then, when they had grown somewhat quiet again, the knight of the castle said, "Sir, are you afraid of that sport?" At this King Arthur grew very angry, and he said, "No, I am not afraid. No man has ever yet had reason to say that I showed myself afraid of anyone." "Very well," said the knight of the castle, "then let us try that sport I spoke of." And King Arthur said, "I am willing."

Then Boisenard came to King Arthur where he stood, and he said, "Lord, do not enter into this thing. Let me take on this venture in your place instead, for I am certain that some great treachery is being plotted against you." But King Arthur said, "No. No man shall take my danger upon himself. I will bear my own danger without calling on any man to bear it for me." So he said to the knight of the castle, "Sir, I am ready for that sport you spoke of. But who is to strike the first blow, and how shall we draw lots for it?" "Sir," said the knight of the castle, "there will be no lots drawn. For since you are the guest in this place, you shall have the first try at that sport."

With that the knight stood up and laid aside his black robe. Underneath he was dressed in a shirt of fine linen, very skilfully worked, and he wore crimson hose. Then he opened that linen shirt at the throat and turned down its collar so as to lay his neck bare to the blow. And he said, "Now, Sir Knight, you will have to strike well if you want to win at this sport."

*King Arthur cuts off the head of the knight of the castle*.

But King Arthur showed no dread of that task. He rose and drew Excalibur, so that the blade of the sword flashed with an extraordinary brightness. Then he measured his distance, and lifted the sword, and struck the knight of the castle on the neck with all his might. And the blade cut through the knight's neck with wonderful ease, so that the head flew off the body and landed a long way away.

But the trunk of that knight's body did not fall. Instead it stood there, and it walked over to where the head lay, and the hands of the trunk picked up the head and set it back on the body. And there was that knight, as sound and whole as he had ever been in all his life.

At this all the people of the castle shouted and laughed with great merriment, and they called out to King Arthur that now it was his turn to try that sport. So the King made himself ready, laying aside his surcoat and opening his shirt at the throat, as the knight of the castle had done. And at that Boisenard broke into loud weeping. Then the knight of the castle said, "Sir, are you afraid?" And King Arthur said, "No, I am not afraid. Every man must come to his death some time, and it seems that my time has now come, and that I am to lay down my life in this foolish way through no fault of my own."

Then the knight of the castle said, "Well, stand a little way off, so that I do not strike you too close and so lose the force of my blow."

*The knight torments King Arthur*.

So King Arthur stood out in the middle of the hall, and the knight of the castle swung his sword several times but did not strike. And several times, too, he laid the blade of the sword on King Arthur's neck, and it was very cold. Then King Arthur cried out in great passion, "Sir, it is your right to strike, but I beg you not to torment me like this." "No," said the knight of the castle, "it is my right to strike when it pleases me, and I will not strike a moment before. If it pleases me, I will torment you for a long while before I kill you." So he laid his sword on King Arthur's neck several more times. And King Arthur said nothing more, but bore that torment with a very steady spirit.

Then the knight of the castle said, "You seem to be a very brave and honorable knight, and I have a mind to make a bargain with you." And King Arthur said, "What is that bargain?" "It is this," said the knight of the castle. "I will spare your life for a year and a day, if you will pledge me your knightly word to come back here at the end of that time."

Then King Arthur said, "Very well; so be it." And with that he pledged his knightly word to return at the end of that time, swearing to the pledge on the cross of Excalibur's hilt.

*The knight of the castle sets King Arthur a riddle*.

Then the knight of the castle said, "I will make another bargain with you." "What is it?" said King Arthur. "My second bargain is this," said the knight of the castle. "I will give you a riddle. If you answer that riddle when you come back here, and make no mistake in your answer, then I will spare your life and set you free." And King Arthur said, "What is the riddle?" And the knight answered, "The riddle is this: What is it that a woman wants most of all in the world?"

"Sir," said King Arthur, "I will try to find the answer to that riddle. And I thank you for sparing my life for as long as you have, and for giving me the chance to escape my death." At this the knight of the castle smiled very sourly, and he said, "I do not offer you this out of mercy, but because I take pleasure in tormenting you. For what delight can you have in living your life when you know that you must surely die at the end of one short year? And what pleasure can you have in living even that year, when you will be tormented with worry to find the answer to my riddle?"

Then King Arthur said, "I think you are very cruel." And the knight said, "I do not deny it."

So that night King Arthur and Boisenard slept at the castle, and the next day they went on their way from there. And King Arthur was very heavy and troubled in spirit. Even so, he ordered Boisenard to say nothing about what had happened, but to keep it secret. And Boisenard did as the King commanded and said nothing about that adventure.

Now in the year that followed, King Arthur set his affairs in order. He also searched everywhere to find the answer to that riddle. Many people gave him answers in plenty. One said that a woman most wanted wealth, and another said she most wanted beauty; one said she wanted the power to please, and another said that she most wanted fine clothes. And one said this, and another said that, but no answer seemed to King Arthur good and fitting for his purpose.

So the year went by until only two weeks were left. Then King Arthur could not bear to stay where he was any longer, for it seemed to him that his time was very close at hand. He was filled with a very bitter anxiety of soul, and so he was very restless to be gone.

So he called Boisenard to him, and he said, "Boisenard, help me arm, for I am going away."

Then Boisenard burst into a flood of tears, and he said, "Lord, do not go."

At this King Arthur looked very sternly at his squire and said, "Boisenard, what is this? Would you tempt me to break my honor? It is not very hard to die, but it would be very bitter to live my life in dishonor. So tempt me no more, but do as I bid you and hold your peace. And if I do not come back a month from now, then you may tell everything that has happened. And you may tell Sir Constantine of Cornwall that he is to search the papers in my cabinet, and there he will find everything that is to be done if death should overtake me."

*King Arthur sets out to return to the castle of the evil knight*.

So Boisenard put a plain suit of armor on King Arthur, though he could hardly see what he was doing for the tears that streamed from his eyes. And over the King's armor he laced a surcoat without any emblem, and he gave the King a shield without any emblem. Then King Arthur rode away without caring where his road took him. And of everyone he met he asked what it was that a woman most wanted, and no one could give him an answer that seemed to him to be the right one. And so he was in great doubt and torment of spirit.

*King Arthur comes to the hut of an old woman*.

Now on the day before King Arthur was to keep his bargain at that castle, he was wandering through the nearby forest in great anguish of soul, for he did not know what to do to save his life. As he wandered, he suddenly came upon a small hut built under an overhanging oak tree, so that it was very hard to tell where the oak ended and the hut began. And all around lay a great many large rocks covered with moss, so that the King might very easily have passed the hut by, except that he saw smoke rising from it, as from a fire burning inside. So he went to the hut and opened the door and went in. At first he thought there was no one there, but when he looked again he saw an old woman sitting bent over a small fire that burned on the hearth. And King Arthur had never seen such an ugly old hag as the one who sat there bending over that fire. Her ears were very huge and flapping, and her hair hung down over her head like snakes. Her face was covered all over with wrinkles, so that there was no place at all on it without a wrinkle. Her eyes were bleary and covered over with a film, and the eyelids were red as if from constant weeping. She had only one tooth in her mouth, and her hands, which she spread out to the fire, were like claws of bone.

Then King Arthur greeted her, and she greeted the King, and she said to him, "My lord King, where do you come from? And why have you come to this place?"

King Arthur was greatly astonished that this old woman should know who he was, and he said, "Who are you, who seem to know me?" "Never mind that," she said. "I am one who wishes you well. So tell me what trouble it is that brings you here at this time." So the King told that old woman all his trouble, and he asked her whether she knew the answer to the riddle, "What is it that a woman most wants?" "Yes," said the old woman, "I know the answer to that riddle very well. But I will not tell it to you unless you promise me something in return."

At this King Arthur was filled with very great joy that the old woman knew the answer to the riddle; but he was also filled with doubt about what she would ask of him. And so he said, "What is it you must have in return for that answer?"

Then the old woman said, "If I help you to answer your riddle rightly, you must promise that I shall become the wife of one of the knights of your Court, whichever one I may choose, when you go home again."

"Ha!" said King Arthur. "How can I promise that on anyone else's behalf?" At this the old woman said, "Are not the knights of your Court so noble that they will do that to save you from death?" "I believe they are," said King Arthur. And with that he thought for a long while, saying to himself, "What will my kingdom do if I die now? I have no right to die." So he said to the old woman, "Very well, I will make that promise."

*The old woman tells King Arthur how to answer the riddle*.

Then she said to the King, "This is the answer to the riddle: What a woman wants most is to have her own way." And the answer seemed to King Arthur to be entirely right.

Then the old woman said, "My lord King, you have been tricked by that knight who led you into this trouble. For he is a great conjurer and a magician of a very wicked kind. He does not carry his life inside his body, but in a crystal globe, which he wears in a locket hanging around his neck. That is why, when you cut his head off his body, his life stayed in that locket and he did not die. But if you had destroyed that locket, he would have died at once."

"I will remember that," said King Arthur.

So King Arthur stayed with that old woman that night, and she refreshed him with food and drink and looked after him very well. And the next morning he set out for the castle where he had made his bargain, and his heart was more cheerful than it had been for a whole year.
