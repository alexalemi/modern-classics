Foreword

[Figure bb: The ornamented title of The Book of Three Worthies, in an arched frame hung with ribbons, garlands and carved figures.]

[Figure bc: The Lady of the Lake — The Lady of the Lake sits on the grass by the water with long dark hair and a jeweled collar, a swan flying above her head.]

[Figure bd: Headpiece to the Foreword of The Book of Three Worthies: a young woman with flowers in her hair sits by the water writing on a long scroll.]

Here begins the Second Book of the History of King Arthur. It is called The Book of Three Worthies, because it tells of three very excellent and honorable lords of the Court of King Arthur.

Of these three, the first is Merlin the Wise; the second is Sir Pelleas, who was called the Gentle Knight; and the third is Sir Gawain, the son of King Lot of Orkney and the Isles.

So now, very soon, follows the story of the passing of Merlin the Wise. In it you will see how the very wisdom that Merlin had in such great measure was the cause of his own undoing. And so I hope that you yourselves will take that story to heart, and that you will see to it that the gifts of mind or of person which God gives you are never so misused, by you or by others, that they become the means of bringing about your own downfall.

For it will not excuse you in any way that, as you journey on through your life, you will find many men who, like Merlin, have been given by the grace of God very great gifts of talent, which they might very easily use for the great good of mankind, but which they so misuse that they bring all the greater ruin upon themselves and all the greater harm upon other men. For if you should prove so weak or so wicked as to misuse your talents in that way, to the harm of others and of yourself, it will not make your fault any the less that others have done greater evil than you.

So let this story of Merlin be a warning to you, I beg you all. For though I do not believe that Merlin ever meant his powers of magic to do harm to others, yet, because of his folly, they did as much harm as if he himself had planned to do evil with them. Yes; it is hard to tell whether the wickedness or the follies of men do the greater harm in the world. So take good care to guard yourself, not only against sin, but against folly and weakness as well.
