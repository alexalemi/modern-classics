Chapter 4: How Queen Guinevere Quarreled with Sir Gawain, and How Sir Gawain Left King Arthur's Court for a While

[Figure br: The Lady of the Lake sits by the fountain in Arroy — The Lady of the Lake sits among the rocks and ferns of a dark wood, drawing a long strand of her hair through her fingers.]

Now, just as much as Queen Guinevere thought highly of Sir Pelleas, she disliked Sir Gawain. For although many people said that Sir Gawain had a silver tongue, and although at times he could talk in such a way as to win others over to whatever he wanted, he was still of a proud temper, and very stern and haughty. So he would not always put up with it when the Lady Guinevere ordered him about as she ordered the other knights of that Court. What is more, she could never forget how Sir Gawain had refused her that time at Cameliard, when she begged him and his companions for help in her time of trouble, nor how discourteous his words to her had been then. So there was no great liking between these two proud souls, for Queen Guinevere held to her own way and Sir Gawain held to his own way, whatever happened.

*Sir Gawain and others sit beneath the Queen's window*.

Now it happened once that Sir Gawain and Sir Griflet and Sir Constantine of Cornwall were sitting and talking with five ladies of the Queen's Court in a garden of trimmed and interwoven hedges that lay below the Lady Guinevere's tower, and they were having a very pleasant time together. Sometimes they would talk and make merry with jokes and tales, and at other times one or another of them would take up a lute they had brought with them, and play on it, and sing.

Now while these lords and ladies sat enjoying their pleasant talk and singing like this, Queen Guinevere was sitting at a window that overlooked the garden. It was not very high above the ground, so she could overhear everything they said. But the lords and ladies had no idea that the Queen could hear them, so they talked and laughed very freely, and the Queen greatly enjoyed their conversation and the music they made.

That day was wonderfully mild and warm, and since it was well on toward the close of the afternoon, those lords and ladies were dressed in very bright and festive clothes. Of all who were there, Sir Gawain was the most splendidly dressed, for he wore sky-blue silk embroidered with silver thread. And Sir Gawain was playing the lute and singing a ballad in a very pleasing voice, so that Queen Guinevere, sitting by the open window, was well content to listen to him.

*Sir Gawain strikes the Queen's hound*.

Now there was a certain greyhound that Queen Guinevere was wonderfully fond of, so fond that she had put a collar of gold set with carbuncles, those deep red jewels, around its neck. Just then the hound came running into the garden, and his feet were wet and muddy with earth. Hearing Sir Gawain singing and playing on the lute, the hound ran to him and jumped up on him. This made Sir Gawain very angry. He clenched his fist and struck the hound on the head with his knuckles, so that the hound howled loudly.

But when Queen Guinevere saw that blow she was deeply offended, and she called out from her window, "Why do you strike my dog, Sir?" And the lords and ladies below in the garden were very much surprised, and very much embarrassed, to find that the Queen was so close to them that she could overhear everything they had said and see everything they did.

But Sir Gawain spoke up very boldly and said, "Your dog insulted me, Lady, and whoever insults me, I strike."

Then Queen Guinevere grew very angry with Sir Gawain, and she said, "Your words are too bold, Sir." And Sir Gawain said, "Not too bold, Lady; only bold enough to defend my rights."

*Of the quarrel of the Queen and Sir Gawain*.

At these words the Lady Guinevere's face blazed like fire, and her eyes shone very bright, and she said, "I am sure you forget whom you are speaking to, Sir Knight." At that Sir Gawain smiled very bitterly and said, "And you, Lady, forget that I am the son of a king so powerful that he needs no help from any other king to defend his rights."

At these words everyone there fell as silent as if they had been turned to stone, for that speech was extremely bold and haughty. They all looked at the ground, for they did not dare to look at either Queen Guinevere or Sir Gawain. And the Lady Guinevere was silent too, for a long time, trying to recover herself after that speech; and when she spoke, it was as if she were half choked with anger. She said, "Sir Knight, you are proud and arrogant beyond all measure, for I never heard of anyone who dared to answer his Queen the way you have spoken to me. But this is my Court, and I may give orders in it as I choose. So I now command you to be gone, and to show your face no more, either here or in the Hall or in any of the places where I hold my Court. For you are an offense to me, and in none of these places shall you have leave to show your face until you ask my pardon for the insult you have given me." Then Sir Gawain stood up and bowed very low to Queen Guinevere, and he said, "Lady, I go. And I will not come back here until you are willing to tell me that you are sorry for the discourteous way you have treated me, both now and at other times, in front of my peers."

With these words Sir Gawain left that place, and he did not turn his head to look behind him. And Queen Guinevere went into her room and wept in secret, out of anger and out of shame. For in truth she was deeply grieved at what had happened; and yet she was so proud that she would never have taken back the words she had spoken, even if she could have done so.

Now when the news of that quarrel had gone around the castle, it reached the ears of Sir Uwain. So Sir Uwain went straight to Sir Gawain and asked him what was the matter, and Sir Gawain, who was like a man half out of his mind and in great despair, told him everything. Then Sir Uwain said, "You were certainly wrong to speak to the Queen as you did. All the same, if you are banished from this Court, I will go with you, for you are my first cousin and my companion, and my heart holds fast to you." So Sir Uwain went to King Arthur and said, "Lord, my cousin Sir Gawain has been banished from this Court by the Queen. And though I cannot say that he has not deserved that punishment, I would gladly ask your leave to go with him."

At this King Arthur was very much grieved, but he kept a steady face and said, "Sir, I will not keep you from going wherever you please. As for your kinsman, I dare say he gave the Queen such great offense that she could do nothing other than what she did."

*Sir Gawain and Sir Uwain leave the Court*.

So Sir Uwain and Sir Gawain both went to their lodgings and ordered their squires to arm them. Then they and their squires rode out of Camelot and took their way toward the forest country.

There those two knights and their squires traveled all that day until the gray of the evening, at the hour when the birds were singing their last songs before closing their eyes for the night. Seeing that the evening was coming on fast, the knights were afraid they would not be able to find a kindly lodging before night came down on them, and they talked together a great deal about it. But as they came to the top of a certain hill, they saw below them a valley, very lovely and well farmed, with many cottages and small farms. And in the middle of that valley was a fine abbey, very beautiful to look at. So Sir Gawain said to Sir Uwain, "If that abbey over there is an abbey of monks, I believe we shall find excellent lodging there tonight."

*They come to an abbey of monks*.

So they rode down into the valley and to the abbey, and at the small gate they found a porter, from whom they learned that it was indeed an abbey of monks. At this they were very glad, and rejoiced greatly.

When the abbot of that abbey learned who they were, and of what rank and high station, he was extremely pleased to welcome them, and he brought them into the part of the abbey where he himself lived. There he made them welcome and had a good supper set before them, which made them very happy. Now the abbot was a merry soul and took great pleasure in talking with strangers, so he asked those two knights very carefully why they were wandering abroad. But they told him nothing about the quarrel at Court, only that they were in search of adventure. At this the abbot said, "Ha, Sirs! If you are in search of adventures, you may find one not very far from here."

*The abbot tells the knights of a good adventure*.

So Sir Gawain said, "What adventure is that?" And the abbot answered, "I will tell you. If you travel eastward from this place, you will come after a while to a spot where you will find a very fair castle of gray stone. In front of that castle you will find a good level meadow, and in the middle of the meadow a sycamore tree, and in the sycamore tree a shield, which certain ladies insult in a very curious way. If you forbid those ladies to insult that shield, you will discover a very good adventure."

Then Sir Gawain said, "That is a very strange business. Tomorrow morning we will go to that place and try to find out what sort of adventure it may be." And the abbot said, "Do so," and laughed heartily.

So when the next morning came, Sir Gawain and Sir Uwain said farewell to the abbot and took their leave of that place, riding away eastward as the abbot had advised. After they had ridden in that direction for two or three hours or more, they saw before them the edge of a forest, all green and shady with leaves and very cheerful in the warmth of the early summer day. And there, right at the edge of the woods, stood a fair, strong castle of gray stone, with windows of glass shining very bright against the sky.

*Sir Gawain and Sir Uwain see the damsels attacking the shield*.

Then Sir Gawain and Sir Uwain saw that everything was just as the abbot had said, for in front of the castle was a smooth, level meadow with a sycamore tree in the middle of it. As they drew near, they could see that a black shield hung in the branches of the tree, and in a little while they could make out that it bore the emblem of three white goshawks with their wings spread. But what was very extraordinary was this: in front of that shield stood seven young damsels, with very lovely faces, and these seven damsels kept heaping insults on the shield. Some of the damsels struck at it every now and then with peeled willow switches, and others threw lumps of clay at it, so that the shield was badly smeared and spoiled. Now near the shield was a very noble-looking knight dressed all in black armor and sitting on a black war-horse, and it was easy to see that the shield belonged to that knight, for he had no other shield. Yet even though it was very likely his shield, the knight made no protest, either by word or by deed, to stop those damsels from insulting it.

Then Sir Uwain said to Sir Gawain, "That is a very strange thing I see over there. Probably one of us is meant to fight that knight." And Sir Gawain said, "Maybe so." Then Sir Uwain said, "If that is so, then I will take on the adventure." "No," said Sir Gawain, "I will take it on myself, since I am the elder of us two, and the more seasoned in knighthood." So Sir Uwain said, "Very well. Let it be so, for you are a much more powerful knight than I am, and it would be a pity for one of us to fail in this undertaking." At that Sir Gawain said, "Leave it to me, then, and I will undertake it."

So he set spurs to his horse and rode swiftly to where the damsels were insulting the black shield. And he set his spear in rest and shouted in a loud voice, "Get away! Get away!" When the damsels saw the armed knight riding at them like that, they ran away from him, shrieking.

Then the Black Knight, who was sitting his horse not far off, rode forward to Sir Gawain in a very stately way and said, "Sir Knight, why do you interfere with those ladies?" And Sir Gawain answered, "Because they were insulting what seemed to me to be a noble and knightly shield." At this the Black Knight spoke very haughtily and said, "Sir Knight, that shield belongs to me, and I assure you that I am very well able to take care of it without the interference of any other defender." To which Sir Gawain said, "It would not seem so, Sir Knight."

Then the Black Knight said, "Sir, if you think you are better able to take care of that shield than I am, I think you would do very well to make your words good with your body." To this Sir Gawain said, "I will do my best to show you that I am better able to guard that shield than you are, who own it."

*Sir Gawain and the Black Knight join in battle*.

At this the Black Knight, without any more talk, rode to the sycamore tree and took down the shield that hung there. He fastened the shield on his arm, took his spear in hand, and made himself ready. And Sir Gawain likewise made himself ready, and then each knight took up whatever position on the field seemed best to him.

Now when the people of that castle saw that a combat of arms was about to take place, they crowded in great numbers onto the walls, so that as many as forty ladies and squires and people of every rank were looking down from the walls on that field of battle.

So when the knights were all ready, Sir Uwain gave the signal to charge, and each knight shouted aloud and drove his spurs into his horse and rushed forward to the attack with a noise as loud as thunder.

*The Black Knight overthrows Sir Gawain*.

Now Sir Gawain thought that he would easily beat his opponent in this charge and would be able to throw him out of his saddle without much trouble, for there was hardly a knight in that kingdom equal to Sir Gawain in prowess. Indeed, he had never yet been unhorsed in combat by anyone except King Arthur. So when the two of them rode against each other, Sir Gawain thought it certain that his opponent would fall before him. But it was not so. For in that charge Sir Gawain's spear was broken into many pieces, but the Black Knight's spear held, so that Sir Gawain was hurled violently out of the saddle and struck the dust with a terrible crash. And he was so astonished at that fall that it seemed to him not as though he had fallen from his saddle, but as though the earth had risen up and struck him. So he lay for a while, quite stunned by the blow and by his astonishment.

But when he heard the shouts of the people on the castle wall, he roused himself at once from where he lay in the dust, and he was so filled with rage and shame that he was like a man completely drunk. So he drew his sword and rushed furiously at his enemy, meaning to cut him down by main strength. Then the other knight, seeing him come at him like this, leaped down from his own saddle at once, drew his sword, and set himself ready either to attack or to defend. So they lashed at each other, circling this way and that and striking with such fury that the blows they gave were terrible to see. But when Sir Uwain saw how fierce that fight was, he set spurs to his horse and pushed it in between the two fighters, crying out loud, "Sir Knights! Sir Knights! What is this? There is no reason here for such a desperate battle." But Sir Gawain cried out very furiously, "Leave off! Leave off, and stand aside! This quarrel is none of your business." And the Black Knight said, "On horseback or on foot, I am ready to meet that knight at any time."

But Sir Uwain said, "No. You will fight no more in this quarrel. Shame on you, Gawain! Shame on you, to pick such a desperate quarrel with a knight who only met you in a friendly way in a fair contest!"

Then Sir Gawain saw that Sir Uwain was both just and right, and he put up his sword in silence, although he was ready to weep with vexation at the shame of being overthrown. And the Black Knight put up his sword too, and so peace was made between the two of them.

Then the Black Knight said, "I am glad that this quarrel is over, for I can see, Sirs, that you are surely knights of great nobility and good breeding. So I wish that from now on we might be friends and companions instead of enemies. And so I beg you to come with me a little way from here, to where I have made my lodging, so that we may rest and refresh ourselves in my pavilion."

To this Sir Uwain said, "I thank you for your courtesy, Sir Knight, and we will go with you with all the pleasure in the world." And Sir Gawain said, "I am content." So the three knights left the field of battle at once.

*Sir Gawain and Sir Uwain come to the pavilion of the Black Knight*.

When they had come to the edge of the forest, Sir Gawain and Sir Uwain saw a very fine pavilion of green silk set up beneath the trees. And around that pavilion were many attendants of various kinds, all dressed in green and white. So Sir Gawain could see that the knight who had overthrown him was certainly someone of very high rank, and this comforted him greatly. Then the squires of the three knights came and took off their helmets, each squire his own knight's, so that the knights might be comfortable. And when this was done, Sir Gawain and Sir Uwain saw that the Black Knight was very handsome, with a ruddy face and hair as red as copper. Then Sir Uwain said to the knight, "Sir Unknown Knight, this knight, my companion, is Sir Gawain, son of King Uriens of Gore, and I am Uwain, the son of King Lot of Orkney. Now I ask you to make yourself known to us in the same way."

"Ha," said the other, "I am glad that you are such very famous and royal knights, for I am of royal blood too. I am Sir Marhaus, the son of the King of Ireland."

Then Sir Gawain was very glad to discover how exalted was the rank of the knight who had overthrown him, and he said to Sir Marhaus, "Sir, I swear that you are one of the most formidable knights in the world. For you have done to me today what only one knight in all the world has ever done before, and that is King Arthur, who is my uncle and my lord. Now you must certainly come to King Arthur's Court, for he will be wonderfully glad to see you, and maybe he will make you a Knight of his Round Table, and there is no honor in all the world as great as that." He said this without thinking; and then he remembered. At that he struck his fist against his forehead and cried out, "Aha! Aha! Who am I to invite you to King Arthur's Court, when only yesterday I was disgraced and banished from it?"

Then Sir Marhaus was very sorry for Sir Gawain, and asked about the trouble that weighed on him, and Sir Uwain told Sir Marhaus all about the quarrel. At that Sir Marhaus was even sorrier for Sir Gawain, and he said, "Sirs, I like you both wonderfully well, and I would gladly become your companion in whatever adventures you are going to undertake. For now I need not stay here any longer. You must know that I was bound to defend those ladies who attacked my shield until I had overthrown seven knights on their behalf. And I must tell you that Sir Gawain was the seventh knight I have overthrown. So, since I have now overthrown him, I am released from my obligation and may go with you."

*Sir Marhaus tells his story*.

Then Sir Gawain and Sir Uwain were very much astonished that any knight should be under so strange an obligation as that, to defend the very people who were attacking his shield, and they begged Sir Marhaus to tell them why he had been bound to keep such a pledge. So Sir Marhaus said, "I will tell you. It was like this. Some time ago I was traveling in these parts with a hawk on my wrist. I was dressed very lightly at the time, in holiday clothes: I wore a tunic of green silk, with one stocking green and the other white. And I had nothing on me for defense but a light buckler, which is a small round shield, and a short sword. Now I came to a certain stream of water, very deep and swift, and I saw ahead of me a stone bridge crossing it, but so narrow that only one horseman could cross at a time. So I rode onto the bridge, and I was partway across when I saw a knight in armor coming the other way. And behind the knight, riding on a pillion, a cushion fixed behind his saddle, sat a very fair lady with golden hair and a very proud bearing. Now when that knight saw me on the bridge, he shouted, 'Get back! Get back, and let me pass!' But this I would not do. Instead I said, 'No, Sir Knight. Since I have come this far onto the bridge, I surely have the right of way to finish crossing, and it is for you to wait and let me over.' But the knight would not do so. He at once made ready to attack, and came straight at me on the bridge, meaning either to kill me or to drive me back to the other end of it. But this he was not able to do, for I defended myself very well with my light weapons. And I pushed my horse so hard against his horse that I drove him backward off the bridge and into the water, and the horse and the knight and the lady all fell into it with a terrible uproar.

"At this the lady shrieked loudly, and both she and the knight were on the point of drowning in the water, for the knight was clad entirely in armor and could not lift himself above the flood. So, seeing the danger they were in, I leaped off my horse into the water, and with great effort, and at great risk to myself, I was able to bring them both to land.

"But that lady was very much offended with me, because her fine clothes were soaked and spoiled by the water, and she scolded me fiercely. So I knelt down before her and begged her pardon with all humility, but still she went on scolding me. Then I offered to do any penance she might lay on me. At this the lady seemed to be a good deal softened, for she said, 'Very well, I will set you a penance.' And when her knight had recovered, she said, 'Come with us.' So I mounted my horse and followed them. And after we had gone a good distance we came to this place, and here she gave me these commands. 'Sir Knight,' she said, 'this castle belongs to me and to this knight who is my lord. Now this shall be your penance for the insult you have given me: you shall take your shield and hang it up in that sycamore tree, and every day I will send certain damsels of my own out of the castle. They shall abuse that shield, and you shall not only put up with whatever insult they offer it, but you shall defend them against all comers until you have overcome seven knights.'

"And so I have done, until this morning, when you, Sir Gawain, came here. You are the seventh knight I have fought, and since I have overcome you, my penance is over and I am free."

Then Sir Gawain and Sir Uwain wished Sir Marhaus great joy that his penance was finished, and each side was very well pleased with the other. So Sir Gawain and Sir Uwain stayed that night in Sir Marhaus's pavilion, and the next morning they got up, washed themselves in a forest stream, and set out from that place.

So they went into the forest country once more and made their way along certain paths, they did not know where; and they traveled all that morning and on until the afternoon had come.

*The three knights enter the Forest of Adventure*.

Now as they traveled like this, Sir Marhaus said suddenly, "Sirs, do you know where we have come to?" "No," they said, "we do not." Then Sir Marhaus said, "This part of the forest is called Arroy, and it is also called the Forest of Adventure. For it is very well known that when a knight, or a party of knights, enters this forest, they will surely meet with an adventure of some kind, from which some come out with honor while others fail." And Sir Uwain said, "I am glad we have come here. Now let us go on into this forest."

So the three knights and their squires went on into that woodland, where the silence was so deep that even the tread of their horses on the earth could hardly be heard. There was no note of any bird and no sound of any voice, and hardly any light made its way into the gloom of those woods. So the knights said to one another, "This is truly a very strange place, and maybe a place of enchantment."

*They see a white fawn in the forest*.

*They see a beautiful lady in the forest*.

Now when they had come into the very heart of these dark woods, they suddenly saw on the path ahead of them a fawn as white as milk. And around the fawn's neck was a collar of pure gold. The fawn stood still and looked at them, but when they came near it, it turned and ran off along a very narrow path. Then Sir Gawain said, "Let us follow that fawn and see where it goes." And the others said, "We are willing." So they followed that narrow path until suddenly they came out on a little open lawn, very bright with sunlight. In the middle of the lawn was a fountain of water, and there was no fawn to be seen. But there beside the fountain sat a wonderfully beautiful lady, dressed all in green. The lady was combing her hair with a golden comb, and her hair was as black as a raven's wing. On her arms she wore marvelous bracelets of emeralds and opals set in skilfully worked gold. What is more, the lady's face was as white as ivory, and her eyes were bright like jewels set in ivory. Now when this lady saw the knights, she stood up and laid aside her golden comb and tied up her locks with ribbons of scarlet silk; and then she came to the knights and greeted them.

At once the three knights got down from their horses, and Sir Gawain said, "Lady, I believe you are not a mortal woman, but one of the fairy folk." To this the lady said, "Sir Gawain, you are right," and Sir Gawain wondered that she should know his name so well. Then he said to her, "Lady, who are you?" And she answered, "My name is Nimue, and I am the chief of those Ladies of the Lake you may have heard of. For it was I who gave King Arthur his sword Excalibur, since I am a great friend to King Arthur and to all the noble Knights of his Court. That is how I know you all. And I know that you, Sir Marhaus, will become one of the most famous Knights of the Round Table." And all three of them marveled at the lady's words. Then she said, "Please tell me, what are you looking for in these parts?" And they said, "We are looking for adventure." "Well," she said, "I will bring you to an adventure, but it is Sir Gawain who must undertake it." And Sir Gawain said, "That is very welcome news." Then the lady said, "Take me up behind you on your saddle, Sir Gawain, and I will show you that adventure." So Sir Gawain took the lady up behind him on his saddle, and as he did, she brought with her a fragrance such as he had never known before. That fragrance was so delicate that it seemed to Sir Gawain as if the whole forest were breathing out the perfume that the Lady of the Lake carried with her.

So the Lady of the Lake led them by many winding ways out of that part of the forest, and along various roads and paths, until they came out into open country, very fruitful and pleasant to see. And she led them up a very high hill, and from the top of the hill they looked down on a fruitful, level plain spread out below them like a table. And they saw that in the middle of the plain stood a noble castle built all of red stone and red brick, and they saw that there was a small town built of red brick too.

*The three knights see a very strange fight*.

Now as they sat their horses there on the hilltop, they suddenly saw a knight dressed all in red armor come riding out of a grove of trees. They saw the knight ride up and down the meadow in front of the castle, and they saw that he was challenging those inside the castle. Then they saw the drawbridge of the castle suddenly let down, and ten knights in full armor came riding out. And they saw those ten knights attack the one knight in red armor, and they saw the one knight attack the ten. They saw that for a while the ten held out against the one, but he attacked them so terribly that he struck down four of them very quickly. Then they saw the rest break and flee before that one knight, and the Red Knight chased the others around the meadow with great fury. And they saw him strike one out of his saddle, and then another, and another, until only two of those knights were left.

Then Sir Gawain said, "That is certainly a wonderful thing to see." But the Lady of the Lake only smiled and said, "Wait a little."

So they waited, and they saw that when the Red Knight had struck down all his enemies except those two, and had put those two in great danger of their lives, he suddenly sheathed his sword and surrendered himself to them. And they saw the two knights bring the Red Knight to the castle, and when they had brought him there, a lady on the castle wall spoke to the Red Knight, it seemed, with very violent language. And they saw the two knights take the Red Knight and tie his hands behind his back, and tie his feet together under his horse's belly, and drive him away from that place.

All this they saw from the top of the hill, and the Lady of the Lake said to Sir Gawain, "There you will find your adventure, Sir Gawain." And Sir Gawain said, "I will go." And the Lady of the Lake said, "Do so."

And with that she vanished from their sight, and they were greatly amazed.
