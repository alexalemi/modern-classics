TEXT ANALYSIS — Howard Pyle, The Story of King Arthur and His Knights (1903)

Source: Project Gutenberg #60184 (illustrated HTML of the 1903 Scribner
first edition). 113,160 words in 35 files. See prep.py for structure.


1. WHAT THE BOOK IS

The first of Pyle's four Arthur books (Champions of the Round Table 1905,
Sir Launcelot and His Companions 1907, The Grail and the Passing of Arthur
1910). Written and drawn by Pyle for young readers, after his Robin Hood
(1883). Two Books in one volume:

  The Book of King Arthur: Prologue (Uther, Merlin's sword in the anvil);
    Part One, The Winning of Kinghood (Kay's broken sword; Arthur draws
    the sword; the kings' trial); Part Two, The Winning of a Sword (Sir
    Myles, the Sable Knight = King Pellinore, the Lady of the Lake,
    Excalibur); Part Three, The Winning of a Queen (Arthur disguised as a
    gardener's boy at Cameliard; the White Champion; Guinevere; the
    wedding and the founding of the Round Table).
  The Book of Three Worthies: Foreword, Prologue (the war of the five
    kings, Morgan's island of Avalon, Arthur's healing); Part One, The
    Story of Merlin (Vivien, the Valley of Joyousness, Merlin's
    entombment, Morgan's plot, Accolon, the lost scabbard); Part Two, The
    Story of Sir Pelleas (the May-day quest, Ettard, Parcenet, Gawain's
    betrayal, the Lady of the Lake's necklace); Part Three, The Story of
    Sir Gawain (the white hart, Arthur in the enchanted castle, the old
    woman in the hut, Gawain's marriage — Pyle's version of the loathly
    lady).

Pyle's sources are Malory (often closely, often freely) plus Tennyson
(Vivien) and his own invention (Tintagalon, Arthur as a gardener's lad,
Parcenet, much of the Pelleas story's second half). He rearranges family
trees: in Pyle, Igraine's daughter Margaise marries Uriens of Gore and
Morgan le Fay marries Lot of Orkney (Malory has it the other way round:
Morgause marries Lot). These are HIS choices and stand.


2. STYLE, TONE, AND WHOSE ARCHAISM IT IS

Pyle wrote in 1902 in an English no one ever spoke: mock-Elizabethan
costume ("thou", "quoth he", "I do perceive", "Lo!", "hight", "wist",
"certes", "gramercy", "damoiselle", "in this wise", "for to"). screen.py
scores it arch 26.3. THE COSTUME IS PYLE'S INVENTION, NOT THE PERIOD'S
(memory: whose-archaism-is-it). It is not Malory's fifteenth-century
English either. Taking it off removes nothing Pyle's own first readers
had: an American child in 1903 read this as "olden-days" flavour, which a
modern reader gets from the armour, the castles and the ceremony, not
from "thee".

What is genuinely Pyle's, and must survive:
  - WONDER. The marvels are told plainly and lovingly — the blue-steel
    blade, the lake of enchantment, the white hart. Keep every colour,
    jewel, flower, cloth and light he names.
  - CEREMONY. Courtesy is the subject. Knights greet, challenge, yield,
    swear and give thanks with formality. Keep the formality in the
    CONTENT (titles, bows, "my lord", "fair lady", "Sir Knight", oaths)
    while making the grammar modern.
  - THE NARRATOR. Pyle talks to the reader: "So hearken unto that which I
    have written", "as you shall presently hear", the moral addresses at
    the ends of Parts ("may that Faith heal all your wounds of sorrow").
    Every one stays, as warm direct address ("Now listen to what
    follows", "I hope that you too...").
  - THE MORAL. Pyle believes knighthood is a model of courage, humility
    and courtesy for children. His sermons are sincere; render them
    sincerely, without irony and without trimming.
  - HUMOR. Gawain's rough tongue, Kay's bluster, the Duke of North
    Umber's pomposity, the gardener's lad's cap. Keep the comedy sharp.

Sentences: Pyle chains clauses with "and ... and ... wherefore ... so
that". For readers of 10-14, break the chains into two or three
sentences; keep the "And so...", "Then..." rhythm of a told tale.


3. VOICE TARGET

A storyteller reading aloud to a bright twelve-year-old: vivid, clear,
stirring, a little grand where the moment is grand. Plain modern words.
Contractions in dialogue are allowed where they make speech natural
(villains, Gawain, Kay, servants), sparingly in the narration and in
solemn or royal speech. No slang, no anachronism ("okay", "kids",
"guys"), no winks at the reader.

5. VOCABULARY MAPPINGS (archaic -> modern)

  thou/thee/thy/ye -> you/your; hath/doth/-eth/-est -> modern verbs
  quoth he -> he said; spake -> spoke; brake -> broke; smote -> struck
  hight -> named, called; wist -> knew; wot -> know
  certes -> truly / surely; gramercy -> thank you / many thanks
  lo! / behold! -> Look! / and there... / and suddenly (vary; often
     dissolve into the sentence: "Lo! beyond the bridge was a castle" ->
     "And there, beyond the bridge, stood a castle")
  I do perceive -> I see / I can tell
  wherefore -> so / and that is why; whereupon -> at that
  whiles -> while; betwixt -> between; nigh -> near / nearly
  for to (do) -> to (do); an (if) -> if; belike -> probably
  assay (n./v.) -> test, trial, attempt / try; "made assay" -> tried
  approve himself -> prove himself; avouch -> vouch, swear
  puissant -> mighty; dolorous -> sorrowful, grievous
  damoiselle / damsel -> damsel (kept: a young lady, as in Malory);
     "maiden" for variety where natural
  esquire -> squire; esquire-at-arms -> squire
  Seneschal -> KEPT, glossed once (003): "Seneschal — the officer who
     runs the king's household"
  pavilion -> pavilion (a large tent; glossed once), tent for variety
  umbril / umbrel -> visor; habergeon / hauberk -> coat of mail
  Faerie / Faëry -> Fairyland; "of Faëry" -> fairy
  Courts of Chivalry -> tournaments / the courts of chivalry
  mandate -> summons, command
  rightwise King -> rightful King
  King-born -> born to be king
  ne'theless -> nevertheless / even so
  anon / presently (= soon) -> soon, right away
  "in this wise" -> "like this" / "these words"; often drop
  "made an end of speaking" -> "finished speaking"
  "of a sudden" -> suddenly
  Numbers: "four hundred, eighty and six" -> four hundred and eighty-six.


6. CHALLENGES

  - FALSE FRIENDS: "gentle" = well-born, courteous; "presently" = soon
    (sometimes "at once"); "cunning" = skilful (cunning work); "lusty" =
    strong, vigorous; "silly" (rare) = simple; "quick" = alive; "glad"
    stays; "an affair-at-arms" = a fight/duel; "condition" = rank;
    "quality" = rank; "despite" = contempt, insult; "fain" = gladly.
  - Pyle's feast days: Christmastide, Candlemas (early February — say
    so once), Easter, Pentecost (Whitsunday). Keep them.
  - Pyle's book citations (Robert de Boron's Roman de Merlin; "the old
    book"): keep as he gives them.
  - Inscriptions on the sword and shields: set as their own paragraph in
    italics (*...*), one line, modern words, keeping the formula: "Whoever
    pulls this sword out of the anvil is the rightful King of England,
    born to the crown."


7. CONTENT NEEDING CARE (the Verne rule: render the author's world as his)

  - Violence: jousts, sword fights, a few deaths (the Duke of North Umber,
    Accolon), wounds and blood described plainly. Keep at Pyle's level —
    neither sharpen nor soften.
  - Vivien and Merlin: Pyle makes Vivien a beautiful, cold, ambitious
    damsel who charms an old man's love and turns his magic against him.
    No sexual content in Pyle; add none.
  - Ettard, Pelleas, Gawain: Pyle softens Malory's version (Malory's
    night in the pavilion is gone; Gawain courts Ettard while under the
    spell of the Lady of the Lake's necklace). Keep Pyle's version.
  - Sir Gawain's story uses the beheading game (Arthur strikes off the
    knight-enchanter's head and the knight picks it up) and the riddle of
    what women most desire ("their own will"). Keep both as Pyle tells
    them.
  - The old woman in the hut: Pyle describes her ugliness at length
    before she becomes the beautiful lady. Keep the description as his;
    don't make it crueller or kinder.
  - Pyle's Christianity (blessings, prayers, the Archbishop, "by the Grace
    of God"): keep as sincere.
  - Ideals of womanhood and service ("the Lady Guinevere, the most
    beautiful lady in the world"; damsels rewarded with knights) are his
    world; keep them, no editorial distance.
