Howard Pyle wrote and drew this book in Wilmington, Delaware, in 1902 and 1903, and Charles Scribner's Sons published it in November 1903. Pyle was born in Wilmington in 1853 and was by then one of the best-known illustrators in America. He drew for Harper's magazines, he had written and illustrated The Merry Adventures of Robin Hood in 1883, and he ran a school for illustrators whose students included N. C. Wyeth, Frank Schoonover and Jessie Willcox Smith. He wrote this book for young readers, and he drew every picture in it: the full-page plates, the decorated panel at the head of each chapter and the small drawings at the ends. It was the first of four Arthur books. The Story of the Champions of the Round Table followed in 1905, then The Story of Sir Launcelot and His Companions in 1907 and The Story of the Grail and the Passing of Arthur in 1910. Pyle died in Florence the next year.

His main source was Sir Thomas Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur of 1485, which this collection also has in full. Pyle follows Malory for the sword in the anvil, Excalibur and the Lady of the Lake, Pellinore, Accolon's fight with Arthur, and the stories of Pelleas and Ettard and of Gawain and the white hart. He changes a good deal too. Arthur winning Guinevere while disguised as a gardener's boy is not in Malory. The damsel who traps Merlin is Malory's Nimue; Pyle calls her Vivien, the name Tennyson gave her in Idylls of the King. Pyle also rearranges the royal family: in his Prologue Morgan le Fay marries King Lot and her sister Margaise marries King Uriens, which is the reverse of Malory. He is not always consistent about this himself, and later chapters give Gawain and Uwain different parents. Those slips are left as he wrote them.

Pyle wrote in an old-fashioned English that nobody had ever actually spoken, full of "thou," "quoth he" and "Lo!" It was his own invention, meant to make the book feel ancient to an American child in 1903. It is not Malory's English either. This edition takes that costume off and keeps everything else: every episode and speech, the jousts and feasts, the colors of every banner, and Pyle's habit of stopping to talk to his reader about courage and courtesy. He meant those talks seriously. Pyle also printed short summaries in the margins beside the paragraphs, and these appear as the italic lines in the text.

Where a character is the same person as in our Malory, the name is spelled the same way: Gawain, Lancelot, Uwain, Pelleas, Morgan le Fay. Pyle's own characters keep his names. His pictures keep their original lettering, so a plate may say "Sir Gawaine" or "Queen Morgana" above a page that says Gawain or Morgan. The plates are reproduced from a scan of a 1909 printing of the Scribner edition, made from the same plates as the first.
