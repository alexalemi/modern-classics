Lewis Carroll published this in 1896, two years before he died, and hoped it would be taken up in schools and in private families as a healthy mental recreation, which he said would repay ten times the labour he had spent on it. The advertisement at the front promises Parts II and III — "should life, and health, and opportunity be granted me" — and lists their contents. He never wrote them.

Part I teaches the whole of elementary formal logic — propositions, syllogisms, soriteses, fallacies — and teaches it as a board game. You draw a square and divide it into cells, each standing for a combination of attributes. You lay a red counter in a cell to say that something of that kind exists, and a grey one to say that nothing does, and then you read the conclusion off the board. Carroll sold the blank diagrams and nine counters through Macmillan for threepence. He is writing for children of twelve and fourteen, and he does not talk down to them for a sentence.

The difficulty is not that the book is hard. It is that the logic that came after him threw his vocabulary away, so a reader with modern training is worse off than one with none. A Premiss is what we spell premise. The Eliminands are the terms a syllogism gets rid of; the Retinends are the ones it keeps. Univ. is the Universe of Discourse, the class of things under discussion. The diagram has a compass geography — the North Half, the Inner portion, the West — and those are coordinates on the board, not descriptions. All of it is kept here exactly as he wrote it, capital letters included, because in this book the words are the machine and it does not run without them. The opposite decision was taken for his Pillow Problems, also in this collection, where the old notation carries no part of the argument and has been modernised; A Tangled Tale and Euclid and His Modern Rivals are here as well.

He is funny throughout. Cats understand French. Three ruffians plead guilty, so no verdict is returned and a whole syllogism collapses. A friend founds a club on strictly logical principles and announces that some of its members are millionaires, though the club has no members at all. The address "To Learners" is a short account of how to read a hard book: begin at the beginning, do not dip, do not go on until you understand, and find a genial friend to talk the difficulties over with.

Two things have been added and one has been changed. The original prints three hundred and eight diagrams and never says what is drawn on them, so a caption has been written for each, naming which cell holds which counter; several hundred page references now point to chapters instead. And five exercises whose terms were racial or antisemitic have had those terms replaced. The logic is indifferent to what the classes are called, and this is a book that asks a child of twelve to handle the propositions as their own.
