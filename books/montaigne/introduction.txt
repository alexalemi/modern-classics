Michel de Montaigne was born in 1533 on his family's estate near Bordeaux and spent his working life as a magistrate in the Bordeaux parlement. In 1571, at thirty-eight, he retired to a tower on the estate, had sentences from his favourite authors painted on the beams of the ceiling, and started writing. The first two books came out in 1580; a third, along with additions to the others, followed in 1588. France spent those years in a civil war over religion, which he lived in the middle of and mostly declines to discuss directly.

He called the pieces essais, meaning attempts or trials, and the word has been the name of the form ever since. What he was trying was his own judgement, on paper, to find out what he thought. So the essays wander. He starts on one subject, remembers a story from Plutarch, goes after it, comes back, notices he has contradicted what he said ten pages earlier, and says so with no embarrassment at all. That is the method, not a failure of it. The quotations are constant, mostly Latin poets and historians; this edition gives them in English rather than printing the Latin and a translation both.

His subject is himself, stated plainly and without any pretence that he is a representative case. He writes about his bad memory, his height, his fear of death and how he argued himself out of it, his kidney stones and what passing one is actually like, what he eats, how he sleeps, and what he thinks about while riding. He also writes about torture, colonisation and the burning of witches, and he is harder on his own century than almost anyone in it: the essay on cannibals, about Brazilians he questioned through an interpreter, works round to the conclusion that the people roasting each other in France are the barbarians, and the essay on cripples says that it is putting a very high value on your guesses to roast a man alive for them.

This is a selection, not the whole book. Montaigne wrote a hundred and seven essays; here there are twenty-three, along with the short note to the reader that opens the original, arranged as he arranged them, in three books. Most of the best known are here: on friendship, on cannibals, on the education of children, on solitude, on books, on repentance, on cripples, on coaches, and the long final essay on experience. Five famous ones are not. The Apology for Raymond Sebond, which is longer than everything in this volume put together and contains both his motto, what do I know, and the passage about his cat, is absent, as are Of Vanity, Upon Some Verses of Virgil, Of Presumption and Of Physiognomy.

The English follows Charles Cotton's translation of 1685 and 1686.
