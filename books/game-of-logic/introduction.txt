Lewis Carroll was the pen name of Charles Dodgson, a mathematics lecturer at Christ Church, Oxford, and the author of Alice's Adventures in Wonderland. The Game of Logic was his first attempt to teach logic to children. It was first printed in 1886 and published by Macmillan in 1887, and every copy came with an envelope holding a card board and nine counters, four red and five grey. The book is the rulebook for that board.

The idea is simple and was new. A proposition such as "Some new Cakes are nice" is shown by putting a counter on a square divided into compartments: the upper half holds the new Cakes, the left half the nice ones, and so on. A red counter means there is at least one thing in that compartment, a grey counter means there is none, and a counter placed on a dividing line means there is something on one side or the other. Two propositions laid out on the larger board, with its inner square for a third attribute, can then be read off to give a conclusion. That is a syllogism, found by moving counters instead of by memorising rules. Carroll worked the method out more fully in Symbolic Logic, which this collection also has in both a retelling and his original text.

He wrote it for children and never talks down to them. The Cakes are new or old, nice or not-nice; the examples include dragons, crocodiles, lobsters and a man who asks how many strawberries grow in the sea. The second chapter asks questions and the third answers them, so a reader can test himself and then look. The last chapter, Hit or Miss, is a long list of pairs of premisses for practice, some of which lead to no conclusion at all.

This is a restored edition, and the words are Carroll's. The text is Project Gutenberg's, checked against two scans of the 1887 printing, which corrected about a dozen words the transcription had changed or dropped. Carroll's italics, which the transcription had turned into capitals, are restored with the case the print gives them. The diagrams were set in type in 1887, and they are redrawn here from the transcription and checked one by one against the printed page. Each has a description for readers who cannot see it. Where Carroll sends the reader to a page for an answer, the reference now names the section. The practice sentences are reproduced as he printed them, including several about Jews and Gentiles.
