Nobody knows whether Aesop wrote any of these fables, or quite whether there was an Aesop. The earliest mention of him is in Herodotus, in the fifth century BC, who mentions him only in passing: a maker of fables, once a slave of a man named Iadmon of Samos, who was put to death at Delphi. Later stories added that he was ugly and quick-tongued. The fables themselves were passed on and added to for centuries, set in verse by Phaedrus in Latin and Babrius in Greek in the early centuries AD, and collected again and again after them. Aesop's Fables is the name of a tradition, not of a book any one man sat down and wrote.

This translation was made by V. S. Vernon Jones (1875–1955), a Cambridge academic, and published in 1912 by William Heinemann in London and Doubleday, Page in New York. It has 284 fables. It was a large illustrated gift book of the kind Arthur Rackham had made his name with, after Rip Van Winkle in 1905 and Peter Pan in Kensington Gardens the year after. Rackham drew thirteen colour plates and more than fifty drawings in black and white, plus small headpieces and silhouettes that the book does not list.

Vernon Jones writes short, plain sentences, and the conventions are easy to learn. The animals are characters and take capital letters, as people's names do. Most fables end on a one-line moral set apart on its own line, but not all of them; the first in the book, The Fox and the Grapes, ends on the fox's own remark and leaves the reader to draw the moral.

The introduction is by G. K. Chesterton, a London journalist who had published Orthodoxy in 1908 and the first Father Brown stories in 1911. His argument is that a fable and a fairy tale are opposites. A fairy tale needs a human hero, because somebody has to break the enchantment. A fable can have no real people in it at all: its animals are fixed, like pieces in chess, the fox always crooked and the lion always stronger than the wolf. His comparison of Aesop with Uncle Remus refers to the elderly former slave who tells the animal stories in Joel Chandler Harris's books, the first of them issued at the end of 1880 and dated 1881.

This is a restored edition, not a retelling: Vernon Jones' words and Chesterton's are unchanged. The Project Gutenberg transcription it is built from gives the plates either a bare title or no description at all. This edition keeps the captions the 1912 book printed and adds alt text for every plate and decoration. It also adds modern typesetting, a proper ebook, and this introduction.
