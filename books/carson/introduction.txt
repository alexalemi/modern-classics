Rachel Carson is remembered for Silent Spring, her 1962 account of what synthetic pesticides were doing to birds, rivers and people. Before she was a famous author she was a government writer. Trained as a marine biologist, she joined the U.S. Bureau of Fisheries in 1935 to write radio scripts and stayed until 1952. The bureau became part of the new Fish and Wildlife Service in 1940, and by 1949 she was editor-in-chief of everything it published. She left after The Sea Around Us, published in 1951, made her name.

Conservation in Action was a series of illustrated booklets the Service published between 1947 and 1950, each describing one or more of the national wildlife refuges: what the place is, which birds and animals depend on it, and what the Service was doing there. Carson planned the series. She wrote four of the booklets herself and wrote the fifth gathered here, on Bear River, with Vanez T. Wilson, a refuge biologist. She visited the refuges, often with the Service's artists Shirley A. Briggs and Katherine L. Howe, whose drawings and photographs fill most of these pages; Bob Hines illustrated Bear River.

The booklets are in series order. Chincoteague describes a barrier island on the Virginia coast where greater snow geese spend the winter. Parker River covers the marshes and dunes of Plum Island in Massachusetts. Mattamuskeet is a shallow lake in eastern North Carolina, a winter home for Canada geese and whistling swans. Guarding Our Wildlife Resources explains the whole refuge system and why migratory birds need a chain of protected places along their flyways. Bear River is a marsh at the mouth of the river where it enters the Great Salt Lake in Utah, where the refuge was created to fight the outbreaks of botulism that had killed waterfowl by the hundreds of thousands.

These were working documents, written to explain public land to the public. They deal with hunting, food plantings and water levels as plainly as with nesting and migration. They also show Carson's prose at work between her first book and her second: exact about the natural history, alert to the sound of a place, and patient with the reader.

This is a restored edition of the five booklets as printed, with their own drawings, photographs and maps, 137 in all. Pictures keep the captions printed with them, and each has a description written for this edition. Each booklet's text was read from page images and checked page by page against the scans' own text, and a second copy of every booklet was compared. The title pages give each booklet's credits; the department lines, prices and library stamps are omitted. As works of the United States government, the booklets are in the public domain.
