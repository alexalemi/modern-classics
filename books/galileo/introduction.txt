Galileo Galilei was sixty-eight when this book was printed in Florence in 1632, and the most famous scientist in Europe. Twenty-two years earlier he had pointed a telescope at the sky and reported mountains on the moon, four moons going round Jupiter and phases on Venus. In 1616 the church had declared the claim that the Earth moves around the sun to be contrary to Scripture, and Galileo had been told not to hold or defend it. The Dialogue was licensed on the understanding that it laid out the arguments on both sides and reached no verdict.

It is a conversation over four days in a palace in Venice between three men. Salviati puts the Copernican case. Sagredo is the host, quick and amused, an intelligent amateur who asks the questions a reader would ask. Simplicio defends Aristotle and Ptolemy, and he loses. Galileo named the first two after real friends of his who were dead by 1632; the third name is his own invention and it is not flattering.

He wrote it in Italian rather than Latin, deliberately. Latin was the language of the universities and of the men who had ruled against him. Italian was the language of everybody else, and the book is written for them: arguments built out of ships and cannon and birds and dropped stones, geometry explained in words, and long stretches that are funny on purpose. It is also rude. Simplicio quotes authorities, gets tied in knots, and is teased for it, and that tone is part of what made the book dangerous.

Day One takes apart the old division between a corruptible Earth and a perfect unchanging sky. Day Two argues that the Earth turns daily, and contains the passage everyone remembers: shut yourself in the cabin below the deck of a ship with some flies and a bowl of water, and nothing you can do in there will tell you whether the ship is moving. Day Three argues the yearly orbit. Day Four explains the tides as a result of the Earth's two motions sloshing the seas about, which was the argument Galileo was proudest of and is wrong. It is translated here straight, with full conviction and no correction, because that is how he wrote it.

The book sold out. Within months it was stopped, and in 1633 Galileo was summoned to Rome, tried by the Inquisition, made to kneel and abjure, and sentenced to house arrest. He spent his last nine years confined to his villa outside Florence, going blind, and died there in 1642. The Dialogue stayed on the church's list of forbidden books for two hundred years.

This translation is from Galileo's own Italian.
