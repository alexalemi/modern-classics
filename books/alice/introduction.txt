Lewis Carroll was the pen name of Charles Lutwidge Dodgson (1832–1898), a mathematics lecturer at Christ Church, Oxford. On 4 July 1862 he and a friend rowed the three daughters of the Dean of Christ Church up the Thames, and he told them a story about the middle one, Alice Liddell. At her request he wrote it down, and he later expanded it into this book. The poem at the front of the book, "All in the golden afternoon", is about that trip. Prima, Secunda and Tertia are the three sisters.

Alice follows a White Rabbit down a hole and spends the rest of the book changing size and arguing with the creatures she meets. Almost every one of them contradicts her, corrects her grammar or tells her she is wrong. Much of the comedy is logic taken literally, and the rest is parody. Nearly all of the verse, "How doth the little crocodile", "You are old, Father William", "Twinkle, twinkle, little bat", parodies poems Victorian children had to learn by heart, most of them now forgotten. The Mouse's tale in Chapter III is printed in the shape of a tail, a pun on "tale" that Alice misunderstands.

The pictures are John Tenniel's. He was the chief cartoonist of Punch, and Carroll, who paid for the book's production himself, chose him and directed him closely. Tenniel drew on the wood and the Dalziel brothers engraved his drawings. Macmillan published the book in 1865, but Tenniel was unhappy with how his pictures had printed, and the first printing was withdrawn. The book was printed again and issued that winter with the date 1866. That is the edition reprinted here, and these are the forty-two pictures readers have known ever since.

This is a restored edition: Carroll's words are unchanged. The text is the Project Gutenberg transcription, checked word by word against a scan of Macmillan's 1867 printing (the "Sixth Thousand", printed from the 1866 type) and a second scan of 1872. Where the transcription followed the text Carroll revised for later printings, this edition restores the words of 1866: the Owl shares its pie with an oyster, not a panther, and the Lobster's song stops where the book first stopped it. About eighty readings were restored this way, each one read on the page. The opening poem, which the transcription leaves out, is restored from the same printing. Every picture is reproduced from that scan and placed where the page places it. The poems keep their stanzas and indents, and the Mouse's tale keeps the shape it has on the page. The edition also adds a description of every picture, modern typesetting, a proper ebook, and this introduction.
