Niccolò Machiavelli spent fourteen years as a working civil servant of the Florentine republic, running its correspondence and going out on embassies to the King of France, to the Emperor, and to Cesare Borgia, whom he watched at close range while Borgia took and then lost a state in central Italy. In 1512 the republic fell and the Medici came back. Machiavelli was dismissed from his post. The next year his name appeared on a list of conspirators, and he was arrested and tortured on the rope before an amnesty let him out. He withdrew to a small farm south of the city, and there, in a few months of 1513, he wrote this.

It is short — twenty-six chapters — and it is addressed to Lorenzo de' Medici, of the family that had just ruined him. The dedication says plainly what it is for. He has nothing to give but what he knows about how power is taken and kept, so he is giving that, and he would like to be employed again. He never was. The book was not printed until 1532, five years after his death.

The argument is that a ruler who behaves the way rulers are supposed to behave will be destroyed by the many who do not, and that politics therefore has to be described as it is rather than as it ought to be. From that come the parts everyone has heard of: that it is safer to be feared than loved if you cannot be both, that a prince must be a fox as well as a lion, that armed prophets win and unarmed ones fail, and that fortune governs about half of what happens and skill the other half. The examples are recent and specific — Borgia, Alexander VI, Julius II, the French and Spanish armies then marching up and down Italy — and the last chapter is a direct appeal to throw those armies out.

The same man, working at the same farm in the same years, also wrote the Discourses on Livy: three books, far longer than this one, arguing that a republic run by its own citizens is better than any prince and that a people is wiser and steadier than a monarch. What to do with that fact is an argument four hundred years old and still open. Rousseau read The Prince as satire and called it a book for republicans. Others read it as straight advice, or as an unemployed official telling a ruler what a ruler would like to hear. Nothing here settles it.

This retelling is made from W. K. Marriott's 1908 English rather than from Machiavelli's Italian. It is the complete text: the dedication, all twenty-six chapters, and the closing exhortation, with none of the cold advice softened.
