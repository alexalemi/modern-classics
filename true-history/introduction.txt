Lucian was born at Samosata, a town on the upper Euphrates in what is now southern Turkey, around AD 125. He grew up speaking Syriac, learned Greek, and made his living as a travelling public speaker before turning to satire. He wrote this one around AD 175, in two short books.

It begins with a promise. Other writers, he says, report marvels they never saw and expect to be believed; he is going to report marvels he never saw and tell you so up front. The one true statement in the book is that he is lying. He names the men he is mocking in the first page — Ctesias, who wrote up an India he had never visited, and Iambulus, and above all Homer's Odysseus, who sat at a royal dinner table and told his hosts about the bag of winds and the one-eyed giant and was believed.

Then he does it better. The ship sails west past the Pillars of Hercules, finds a river running with wine and women growing out of the ground as vines, and is picked up by a whirlwind and dropped on the moon, which turns out to be at war with the sun over who gets to colonise the Morning Star. The armies include cavalry mounted on three-headed vultures and fleas the size of elephants. Later the ship is swallowed whole by a whale with nations living inside it, and later still the narrator reaches the island where the famous dead live and gets Homer to settle, in person, which lines of his the critics have wrongly cut.

The joke is the deadpan. Everything is reported in the flat, careful voice of a historian filing a report — exact distances, exact troop counts, the full text of the peace treaty — and the numbers never wobble no matter what is being counted. That precision is kept here, because it is where the comedy lives. This is also the earliest surviving story of a voyage to the moon and of a war between worlds, which it became by accident: Lucian was making fun of travel writers, not inventing a genre. The still on the cover is from Georges Méliès' film of 1902.

One thing about this edition. It is not translated from Lucian's Greek. It is a modernisation of Francis Hickes' English version of 1634, whose sentences are magnificent and nearly impenetrable, and the work here was getting Lucian's speed back out of them. That is worth saying plainly rather than implying otherwise.

It is about sixteen thousand words. It ends by promising a sequel, which never came.
