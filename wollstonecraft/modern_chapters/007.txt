Chapter Four: On the Degraded State to Which Various Causes Have Reduced Women

(Part 2 of 2)

Another argument that has weighed a great deal with me must, I think, have some force with every considerate and benevolent heart. Girls who have been educated in this feeble way are often cruelly left by their parents with no provision at all, and so depend not only on their brothers' reason but on their brothers' generosity. Those brothers are, to put the case at its fairest, decent sorts of men, and give as a favour what children of the same parents had an equal right to. A docile woman may stay in this ambiguous, humiliating position for some time with a tolerable degree of comfort. But when the brother marries, as he probably will, she stops being regarded as the mistress of the house and is looked at sideways as an intruder, an unnecessary burden on the benevolence of its master and his new partner.

Who can count the misery that so many unfortunate beings, weak in mind and body alike, suffer in such a position — unable to work and ashamed to beg? The wife, a cold-hearted, narrow-minded woman — and this is not an unfair supposition, since the present mode of education does no more to enlarge the heart than the understanding — is jealous of the small kindnesses her husband shows his relations; and, her sensibility never rising as far as humanity, she resents seeing *her* children's property lavished on a helpless sister.

These are matters of fact that have come under my eye again and again. The consequence is obvious. The wife resorts to cunning to undermine an habitual affection she is afraid to oppose openly, and neither tears nor caresses are spared until the spy has been worked out of her home and thrown on a world she is unprepared for; or else sent off, as a great effort of generosity or out of some regard for propriety, with a small allowance and an uncultivated mind, into joyless solitude.

These two women may be much on a par as to reason and humanity, and if their situations were exchanged might have acted just the same selfish part. But if they had been educated differently the case would have been very different too. The wife would not have had that kind of sensibility whose centre is herself, and reason might have taught her not to expect her husband's affection — nor even to be flattered by it — if it led him to violate earlier duties. She would want to love him not merely because he loved her but on account of his virtues; and the sister might have been able to fight her own way instead of eating the bitter bread of dependence.

I am persuaded that cultivation opens the heart as well as the understanding, and that strengthening the bodily organs does too, though that may seem less obvious. I am not talking now about momentary flashes of feeling, but about the affections. And perhaps the hardest task in educating either sex is to adjust the instruction so that it does not narrow the understanding while the heart is being warmed by the generous juices of spring, just raised by the electric fermentation of the season; nor dry up the feelings by employing the mind on investigations remote from life.

When women do receive a careful education, they are made either into fine ladies, brimful of sensibility and teeming with capricious fancies, or into merely capable managing women. The latter are often friendly, honest creatures with a shrewd kind of good sense joined to worldly prudence, which often makes them more useful members of society than the fine sentimental lady, though they have neither greatness of mind nor taste. The intellectual world is shut against them. Take them out of their family or their neighbourhood and they come to a standstill; the mind finds nothing to do, since literature offers a fund of amusement that they have never tried to enjoy, and often try to despise. The sentiments and taste of more cultivated minds look ridiculous to them even in people whom chance and family connection have led them to love; and in mere acquaintances they think it all affectation.

A man of sense can only love such a woman on account of her sex, and respect her because she is a trustworthy servant. To keep his own peace he lets her scold the servants and go to church in clothes made of the very best material. A man of her own size of understanding would probably not get on with her so well, since he might want to trespass on her prerogative and manage some of the household himself. Yet women whose minds have not been enlarged by cultivation, or whose natural selfishness of feeling has not been expanded by reflection, are very unfit to manage a family; for by an undue stretch of power they are always tyrannizing to maintain a superiority that rests on nothing but the arbitrary distinction of money. Sometimes the evil is more serious, and the servants are deprived of innocent indulgences and made to work beyond their strength so that the capable woman can keep a better table and outshine her neighbours in finery and display. If she attends to her children, it is generally to dress them expensively; and whether that attention comes from vanity or from fondness, it is equally pernicious.

Besides, how many women of this description pass their days, or at least their evenings, in discontent. Their husbands acknowledge that they are good managers and chaste wives, and then leave home in search of more agreeable — may I be allowed a significant French word? — more *piquant* society. And the patient drudge who does her task like a blind horse in a mill is cheated of her proper reward, since the wages owed her are her husband's caresses; and women who have so few resources in themselves do not bear the loss of this natural right very patiently.

A fine lady, on the other hand, has been taught to look down with contempt on the vulgar employments of life, though she has only been encouraged to acquire accomplishments that rise a single degree above sense; for not even bodily accomplishments can be acquired with any precision unless the understanding has been strengthened by exercise. Without a foundation of principles, taste is superficial, and grace must come from something deeper than imitation. The imagination, however, is heated and the feelings made fastidious, if not sophisticated, when no counterweight of judgment has been acquired — while the heart still remains artless, even as it grows too tender.

These women are often lovable, and their hearts are really more open to general benevolence, more alive to the sentiments that civilize life, than the square-elbowed family drudge's. But, wanting a due proportion of reflection and self-government, they can only inspire love; they are their husbands' mistresses while they keep any hold on their affections, and the platonic friends of their husbands' male acquaintance. These are the fair defects of nature, the women who seem to have been created not to enjoy the fellowship of men but to save them from sinking into absolute brutality by rubbing off the rough corners of their characters, and by playful dalliance to give some dignity to the appetite that draws them there. Gracious Creator of the whole human race! Have you created such a being as woman, who can trace your wisdom in your works and feel that you alone are by your nature exalted above her — for no better purpose than this? Can she believe she was made only to submit to man, her equal, a being sent into the world as she was to acquire virtue? Can she consent to be occupied merely in pleasing him, merely in adorning the earth, when her soul is capable of rising to you? And can she rest supinely dependent on man for reason, when she ought to be climbing the steep paths of knowledge alongside him?

If love is the supreme good, then let women be educated for nothing but to inspire it, and let every charm be polished to intoxicate the senses. But if they are moral beings, let them have a chance of becoming intelligent; and let their love for men be only a part of that glowing flame of universal love which, after encircling humanity, rises as grateful incense to God.

Fulfilling domestic duties takes a great deal of resolution, and a serious kind of perseverance that needs a firmer support than emotions, however lively and true to nature they may be. To set an example of order, which is the soul of virtue, some austerity of behaviour must be adopted, and that is hardly to be expected from a being who has been made from infancy the weathercock of her own sensations. Anyone who rationally intends to be useful must have a plan of conduct; and in discharging the simplest duty we are often obliged to act against the immediate impulse of tenderness or compassion. Severity is often the surest as well as the most sublime proof of affection. It is the lack of this power over the feelings, and of that lofty, dignified affection which makes a person prefer the future good of the person they love to a present gratification, that leads so many fond mothers to spoil their children; and it has made it a real question whether neglect or indulgence does more harm. I am inclined to think indulgence has done most.

Everyone seems to agree that children should be left in the care of women during childhood. Now from all the observation I have been able to make, women of sensibility are the least fit for this task, because, carried away by their feelings, they will infallibly spoil a child's temper. The management of the temper, the first and most important branch of education, needs the sober steady eye of reason and a plan of conduct equally far from tyranny and from indulgence — and those are exactly the extremes people of sensibility fall into by turns, always shooting past the mark. I have followed this line of reasoning much further, until I concluded that a person of genius is the most unsuitable person imaginable to employ in education, public or private. Minds of that rare species see things too much in the mass, and seldom if ever have a good temper. That habitual cheerfulness we call good humour is perhaps as rarely combined with great mental powers as it is with strong feelings. And people who follow the flights of genius with interest and admiration, or who with cooler approval absorb the instruction a profound thinker has laboriously prepared for them, ought not to be disgusted if they find the first choleric and the second morose; for liveliness of fancy, and a tenacious grasp of mind, are scarcely compatible with the pliant urbanity that leads a man to bend at least a little to the opinions and prejudices of others instead of confronting them roughly.

But in discussing education or manners, minds of the superior class are not what we have to consider; they may be left to chance. It is the multitude of moderate abilities who need instruction and who take the colour of the atmosphere they breathe. This respectable crowd, men and women alike, should not have their sensations heightened in the hothouse of luxurious idleness at the expense of their understanding; for unless there is some ballast of understanding they will never become either virtuous or free, and an aristocracy founded on property or on sterling talents will always sweep the alternately timid and ferocious slaves of feeling before it.

To take another view of the subject: the arguments men have used to degrade women, morally and physically, are numberless, and they are all brought forward with a show of reason because they are supposed to be drawn from nature. I must notice a few.

The female understanding has often been spoken of with contempt for reaching maturity sooner than the male. I shall not answer this argument by pointing to the early proofs of reason, and of genius, in Cowley, Milton and Pope; I shall simply appeal to experience to decide whether young men who are introduced into company early — and examples abound now — do not acquire the same precocity. The fact is so notorious that merely mentioning it must bring before anyone who goes into the world at all the image of a number of swaggering apes of men, whose understandings have been narrowed by being brought into the society of men when they ought to have been spinning a top or trundling a hoop.

Some naturalists have also asserted that men do not reach their full growth and strength until thirty, but that women are mature by twenty. I think they are reasoning on false ground, led astray by the male prejudice that makes beauty the perfection of a woman — mere beauty of feature and complexion, in the vulgar sense of the word, while male beauty is allowed some connection with the mind. Strength of body, and that character in the face which the French call a *physionomie*, women no more acquire before thirty than men do. The little artless tricks of children are, it is true, particularly pleasing and attractive; yet once the pretty freshness of youth has worn off, those artless graces become studied airs and disgust everyone of taste. In a girl's face we look only for vivacity and a bashful modesty; but once the springtide of life is over we look for soberer sense in a face, and for the traces of passion rather than the dimples of animal spirits, expecting to see that individuality of character which is the only thing that fastens the affections. Then we want to converse, not to fondle; to give scope to our imaginations as well as to the sensations of our hearts.

At twenty the beauty of the two sexes is equal, but men's libertinism leads them to make the distinction, and superannuated coquettes commonly agree with them, for when they can no longer inspire love they pay for the vigour and vivacity of youth. The French, who let more mind into their notion of beauty, prefer women of thirty. What I mean is that they allow a woman to be at her most perfect when vivacity gives place to reason and to that majestic seriousness of character which marks maturity, the resting point. In youth, up to twenty, the body shoots out; until thirty the solid parts are gaining density, and the flexible muscles, growing more rigid every day, give the face its character — that is, they trace the workings of the mind with the iron pen of fate, and tell us not only what powers are within but how they have been used.

It is worth observing that the animals who arrive slowly at maturity live longest and are of the noblest species. Men cannot, however, claim any natural superiority from the grandeur of longevity, for in that respect nature has not distinguished the male.

Polygamy is another physical degradation, and a plausible argument for a custom that blights every domestic virtue is drawn from the well-attested fact that in the countries where it is established more girls are born than boys. This looks like an indication of nature, and apparently reasonable speculations must give way to nature. A further conclusion obviously offers itself: if polygamy is necessary, then woman must be inferior to man and made for him.

We are very ignorant about how the foetus is formed in the womb, but it seems to me likely that some accidental physical cause may account for this phenomenon and show that it is not a law of nature. I have met with some pertinent observations on the subject in Forster's Account of the Isles of the South Sea, which will explain what I mean. After observing that among animals the sex with the more vigorous and hotter constitution always prevails and reproduces its own kind, he adds: "If this is applied to the inhabitants of Africa, it is evident that the men there, being accustomed to polygamy, are enervated by the use of so many women and are therefore less vigorous; while the women, on the contrary, are of a hotter constitution, not only because of their more irritable nerves, more sensitive organization and livelier fancy, but also because in their marriages they are deprived of the share of physical love which in a monogamous condition would all be theirs. And so, for these reasons, most children are born female."

"In the greater part of Europe it has been proved by the most accurate lists of mortality that the proportion of men to women is nearly equal, or that, if there is any difference, more males are born, in the proportion of 105 to 100."

The necessity of polygamy, then, does not appear. Yet when a man seduces a woman, it ought, I think, to be called a *left-handed* marriage, and the man should be *legally* obliged to maintain the woman and her children, unless adultery, which is a natural divorce, cancelled the obligation. And this law should stay in force as long as women's weakness allows the word seduction to be used as an excuse for their frailty and want of principle — as long, that is, as they depend on men for a living instead of earning it by the exercise of their own hands or heads. But such women should not be called wives in the full meaning of the relationship, or the whole purpose of marriage would be subverted, and all those endearing charities that flow from personal fidelity and give the tie its sanctity, when neither love nor friendship unites the hearts, would melt away into selfishness. The woman who is faithful to the father of her children deserves respect and should not be treated like a prostitute — though I readily grant that if a man and a woman need to live together in order to bring up their children, nature never intended a man to have more than one wife.

Still, highly as I respect marriage as the foundation of almost every social virtue, I cannot help feeling the liveliest compassion for those unfortunate women who are cut off from society and torn by a single mistake from all the affections and relationships that improve the heart and mind. Often it does not even deserve the name of a mistake, for many innocent girls become the dupes of a sincere and affectionate heart, and still more are — as one may emphatically call it — *ruined* before they know the difference between virtue and vice; prepared for infamy by their education, they become infamous. Refuges and Magdalen hospitals are not the proper remedies for these abuses. It is justice, not charity, that the world is short of.

A woman who has lost her honour imagines she cannot fall lower; and as for recovering her former station, that is impossible, since no effort can wash the stain away. Losing every spur in this way, and having no other means of support, prostitution becomes her only refuge, and her character is quickly depraved by circumstances the poor wretch has little power over, unless she possesses an uncommon share of sense and loftiness of spirit. Necessity never makes prostitution the business of a man's life, though numberless women are made systematically vicious this way. And that comes in great part from the idleness in which women are educated, always taught to look up to a man for a living and to consider their persons the proper return for his exertions in supporting them. Meretricious airs and the whole science of wantonness then have a more powerful stimulus behind them than either appetite or vanity — which lends force to the prevailing opinion that with chastity a woman loses everything respectable about her. Her character depends on observing one virtue, though the only passion fostered in her heart is love. Indeed a woman's honour is not even made to depend on her own will.

When Richardson makes Clarissa tell Lovelace that he had robbed her of her honour, he must have had strange notions of honour and of virtue. For miserable beyond all names of misery is the condition of a being who could be degraded without its own consent. I have heard this excess of strictness defended as a salutary error. I shall answer in the words of Leibniz: "Errors are often useful, but usually to remedy other errors."

Most of the evils of life come from a desire for present enjoyment that outruns itself. The obedience required of women in marriage comes under this description. The mind, naturally weakened by depending on authority, never exerts its own powers, and the obedient wife is turned into a weak, indolent mother. Or, supposing that is not always the consequence, a future state of existence is hardly taken into the reckoning when nothing but negative virtues are cultivated. For in writing about morals, and particularly when women are the subject, writers have too often considered virtue in a very limited sense, and made its foundation *solely* worldly utility. Indeed a still more fragile base has been given to this stupendous structure, and the wayward, fluctuating feelings of men have been made the standard of virtue. Yes — virtue, like religion, has been made subject to the decisions of taste.

It would almost raise a smile of contempt, if the vain absurdities of men did not strike us on every side, to see how eager men are to degrade the very sex from whom they pretend to receive the chief pleasure of life; and I have often turned Pope's sarcasm back on them with complete conviction — or rather, it has seemed to me to apply to the whole human race. A love of pleasure or of power seems to divide humankind between them, and the husband who lords it in his little harem thinks only of his pleasure or his convenience. Indeed an intemperate love of pleasure carries some prudent men, or worn-out libertines who marry to have a safe companion, to such lengths that they seduce their own wives. Marriage banishes modesty, and chaste love takes flight.

Love, considered as an animal appetite, cannot feed on itself for long without expiring; and this extinction in its own flame may be called the violent death of love. But a wife who has been made licentious this way will probably try to fill the gap left when her husband's attentions stop, for she cannot contentedly become merely an upper servant after being treated like a goddess. She is still handsome; and instead of transferring her fondness to her children she dreams only of enjoying the sunshine of life. Besides, there are many husbands so devoid of sense and of parental affection that during the first effervescence of voluptuous fondness they refuse to let their wives nurse their own children. The wives are only to dress and live to please them — and love, even innocent love, soon sinks into lasciviousness when a duty is sacrificed to indulging it.

Personal attachment is a very happy foundation for friendship; yet when even two virtuous young people marry, it might be a good thing if some circumstance checked their passion — if the memory of a previous attachment or a disappointed affection made it, on one side at least, a match founded rather on esteem. In that case they would look beyond the present moment and try to make the whole of life respectable by forming a plan to regulate a friendship that only death ought to dissolve.

Friendship is a serious affection, the most sublime of all, because it is founded on principle and cemented by time. The exact opposite may be said of love. To a great extent love and friendship cannot exist in the same breast; even when they are inspired by different objects they weaken or destroy each other, and for the same object they can only be felt one after the other. The vain fears and fond jealousies that fan the flame of love, when they are judiciously or artfully managed, are both incompatible with the tender confidence and sincere respect of friendship.

Love as the glowing pen of genius has drawn it does not exist on earth, or exists only in those exalted, fervid imaginations that have sketched such dangerous pictures. Dangerous, because they not only offer a plausible excuse to the voluptuary who disguises sheer sensuality under a sentimental veil, but because they spread affectation and take away from the dignity of virtue. Virtue, as the very word implies, should look serious if not austere; and to try to dress her up in the garb of pleasure, because the word has been used as another name for beauty, is to set her on a quicksand — a most insidious attempt to hasten her fall under the appearance of respect. Virtue and pleasure are not in fact so closely allied in this life as some eloquent writers have laboured to prove. Pleasure prepares the fading wreath and mixes the intoxicating cup; but the fruit virtue gives is the reward of toil, and, seen gradually as it ripens, offers nothing but a calm satisfaction — indeed, appearing to be simply the result of the natural tendency of things, it is hardly noticed at all. Bread, the common food of life, is seldom thought of as a blessing, and yet it supports the constitution and preserves health; while feasts delight the heart of man, though disease and even death lurk in the cup or the dainty that raises the spirits or tickles the palate. In the same way the lively, heated imagination draws its picture of love as it draws every other picture, in the glowing colours that a daring hand will steal from the rainbow, when it is directed by a mind condemned in a world like this to prove its noble origin by panting after an unattainable perfection, forever pursuing what it admits to be a fleeting dream. An imagination of this vigorous cast can give existence to insubstantial forms and stability to the shadowy reveries the mind naturally falls into when realities are found flat. It can then paint love with heavenly charms and dote on the grand ideal object; it can imagine a degree of mutual affection that shall refine the soul and not expire once it has served as a "ladder to heavenly things"; and, like devotion, make the soul absorb every meaner affection and desire. In each other's arms, as in a temple whose summit is lost in the clouds, the world is to be shut out, and every thought and wish that does not nourish pure affection and permanent virtue. Permanent virtue! Alas, Rousseau, respectable visionary, your paradise would soon be violated by some unexpected guest walking in. Like Milton's, it could only hold angels, or men sunk below the dignity of rational creatures. Happiness is not material; it cannot be seen or handled. Yet the eager pursuit of a good that everyone shapes to his own fancy proclaims man the lord of this lower world, and an intelligent creature who is not to receive happiness but to acquire it. Those who complain of the delusions of passion, therefore, do not recollect that they are exclaiming against a strong proof of the immortality of the soul.

But leaving superior minds to correct themselves and pay dearly for their experience, I must point out that it is not against strong, persevering passions that I want to guard the female heart by exercising the understanding, but against romantic, wavering feelings; for these daydreams of paradise are more often the effect of idleness than of a lively fancy.

Women seldom have enough serious employment to silence their feelings. A round of little cares or vain pursuits frittering away all strength of mind and body, they naturally become objects of sense and nothing more. In short, the whole tenor of female education — the education society gives — tends to make the best-disposed romantic and inconstant, and the rest vain and mean. In the present state of society I am afraid this evil can hardly be remedied in the slightest degree; but should a more praiseworthy ambition ever gain ground, women may be brought nearer to nature and reason, and become more virtuous and useful as they grow more respectable.

But I will venture to assert that their reason will never gain strength enough to regulate their conduct while making an appearance in the world is the first wish of most of humankind. To this weak wish the natural affections and the most useful virtues are sacrificed. Girls marry merely to *better themselves*, to borrow a significant common phrase, and have such perfect power over their hearts as never to let themselves *fall in love* until a man with a superior fortune makes an offer. I mean to enlarge on this subject in a later chapter; a hint is enough at present, though women are so often degraded by letting the selfish prudence of age chill the ardour of youth.

From the same source flows the opinion that young girls ought to devote a great part of their time to needlework — an employment that contracts their faculties more than any other that could have been chosen for them, by confining their thoughts to their own persons. Men order their clothes to be made and have done with the subject; women make their own clothes, necessary or ornamental, and talk about them continually, and their thoughts follow their hands. It is not the making of necessities that weakens the mind, but the frippery of dress. When a woman in the lower ranks of life makes her husband's and children's clothes, she does her duty; that is part of her business. But when women work only to dress better than they could otherwise afford, it is worse than a sheer waste of time. To make the poor virtuous they must be employed; and women in the middle rank of life, if they did not ape the fashions of the nobility without catching their ease, might employ them, while they managed their own families, taught their own children, and exercised their own minds. Gardening, experimental science and literature would give them things to think about and matter for conversation that would exercise their understandings to some degree. The conversation of French women, who are not so rigidly nailed to their chairs twisting lappets and knotting ribbons, is often superficial; but I maintain it is not half so insipid as that of those English women whose time is spent making caps and bonnets and all the mischief of trimmings, not to mention shopping and bargain-hunting and the rest. And it is the decent, prudent women who are most degraded by these practices, since their motive is simply vanity. The wanton who exercises her taste to make herself alluring has something more in view.

All these observations branch out of a general one I have made before, and which cannot be insisted on too often: that whether we are speaking of men, of women or of professions, it will be found that the employment of the thoughts shapes the character, generally and individually. Women's thoughts hover forever around their own persons — and is it surprising that their persons are reckoned their most valuable possession? Yet some degree of liberty of mind is necessary even to form the person, and this may be one reason why some gentle wives have so few attractions besides sex. Add to this that sedentary employments make most women sickly, and that false notions of female excellence make them proud of this delicacy, though it is another fetter, and one that cramps the activity of the mind by calling attention continually to the body.

Women of quality seldom do any of the manual part of their dress, so only their taste is exercised; and, thinking less about the finery once the business of the dressing table is over, they acquire an ease that rarely appears in the bearing of women who dress merely for the sake of dressing. In fact the observation about the middle rank, the one where talents thrive best, does not extend to women; for those of the higher class, by catching at least a smattering of literature and talking with men on general topics, acquire more knowledge than the women who ape their fashions and their faults without sharing their advantages. As for virtue, in the widest sense of the word, I have seen most of it in low life. Many poor women maintain their children by the sweat of their brow and hold together families that the vices of the fathers would have scattered; but gentlewomen are too indolent to be actively virtuous, and are softened by civilization rather than refined by it. Indeed the good sense I have met with among poor women who have had few advantages of education, and who have yet acted heroically, has strongly confirmed me in the opinion that trifling employments have made women trifling. Men take the body, and leave the mind to rust; so that while physical love enervates man, being his favourite recreation, he will go on trying to enslave woman. And who can say how many generations may be needed to give vigour to the virtue and talents of the freed descendants of abject slaves?

In tracing the causes that have, in my opinion, degraded women, I have confined myself to those that act universally on the morals and manners of the whole sex; and it seems clear to me that they all spring from a want of understanding. Whether that comes from a physical or an accidental weakness of the faculties, time alone can determine. For I shall lay no great weight on the example of a few women who, having received a masculine education, have acquired courage and resolution. I only maintain that the men who have been placed in similar situations have acquired a similar character — I am speaking of whole bodies of men — and that men of genius and talent have emerged from a class in which women have never yet been placed.
