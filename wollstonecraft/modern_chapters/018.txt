Chapter Twelve: On National Education

(Part 2 of 2)

But nothing of this kind could happen in an elementary day school where boys and girls, rich and poor, met together. And to prevent any of the distinctions of vanity they should be dressed alike, and all obliged to submit to the same discipline or leave the school. The schoolroom ought to be surrounded by a large piece of ground where the children could take useful exercise, for at this age they should not be confined to any sedentary employment for more than an hour at a time. But these relaxations might all be made part of an elementary education, since many things amuse and improve the senses when they are introduced as a kind of show, though children would turn a deaf ear to their principles dryly laid down — botany, mechanics and astronomy, for instance. Reading, writing, arithmetic, natural history and some simple experiments in natural philosophy might fill up the day; but these pursuits should never encroach on gymnastic games in the open air. The elements of religion, history, the history of humankind, and politics might also be taught by conversation, in the Socratic form.

After the age of nine, girls and boys intended for domestic employment or for a trade ought to be moved to other schools and given instruction suited in some measure to what each is destined for, the two sexes still being together in the morning; but in the afternoon the girls should attend a school where plain sewing, dressmaking, millinery and so on would be their occupation.

Young people of superior ability or fortune might now be taught in another school the dead and living languages, the elements of science, and might continue the study of history and politics on a wider scale, which would not exclude literature. Girls and boys still together, I hear some readers ask? Yes. And I should fear no other consequence than that some early attachment might form — which, while it had the best possible effect on the young people's moral character, might not perfectly suit their parents' plans; for it will be a long time, I am afraid, before the world is so enlightened that parents anxious only to make their children virtuous will let them choose their own companions for life.

Besides, this would be a sure way of promoting early marriages, and from early marriages the most wholesome physical and moral effects naturally flow. What a different character a married citizen takes on from the selfish coxcomb who lives only for himself, and who is often afraid to marry in case he should not be able to live in a certain style. Great emergencies excepted, which would rarely occur in a society founded on equality, a man can only be prepared to discharge the duties of public life by habitually practising the lesser duties that form the man.

Under this plan of education boys' constitutions would not be ruined by the early debaucheries that now make men so selfish, nor would girls be made weak and vain by idleness and frivolous pursuits. But I am presupposing such a degree of equality between the sexes as would shut out gallantry and coquetry, while still allowing friendship and love to temper the heart for the discharge of higher duties.

These would be schools of morality. And with human happiness allowed to flow from the pure springs of duty and affection, what advances might the human mind not make? Society can only be happy and free in proportion as it is virtuous; but the distinctions now established in society corrode all private virtue and blast all public virtue.

I have already inveighed against the custom of tying girls to their needlework and shutting them out of all political and civil employment; for by narrowing their minds in this way we make them unfit to fulfil the very duties nature has assigned them.

Occupied only with the little incidents of the day, they necessarily grow up cunning. My very soul has often sickened at the sly tricks women practise to get some foolish thing their silly hearts are set on. Not allowed to dispose of money or call anything their own, they learn to turn the market penny; or, if a husband gives offence by staying out, or stirs up some feeling of jealousy, a new gown or some pretty trinket smooths Juno's angry brow.

But these *littlenesses* would not degrade their character if women were led to respect themselves, if political and moral subjects were opened to them. And I will venture to say that this is the only way to make them properly attentive to their domestic duties. An active mind embraces the whole circle of its duties and finds time enough for all of them. It is not, I insist, a bold attempt to emulate masculine virtues that leads women away from duty; it is not the enchantment of literary pursuits or the steady investigation of scientific subjects. No — it is indolence and vanity, the love of pleasure and the love of power, that reign supreme in an empty mind. I say empty emphatically, because the education women now receive hardly deserves the name. The little knowledge they are led to acquire during the important years of youth relates only to accomplishments — and accomplishments with no bottom to them; for unless the understanding is cultivated, every grace is superficial and monotonous. Like the charms of a made-up face, they only strike the senses in a crowd; at home, lacking mind, they lack variety. The consequence is obvious: in the gay scenes of dissipation we meet the artificial mind and the artificial face, for those who fly from solitude dread the domestic circle next after solitude. Having no power to amuse or interest, they feel their own insignificance, or find nothing to amuse or interest themselves with.

Besides, what can be more indelicate than a girl's coming out in the fashionable world — which, in other words, means bringing a marriageable miss to market, her person carried richly caparisoned from one public place to another? And yet, mixing in the giddy circle under restraint, these butterflies long to flutter free; for the first affection of their souls is for their own persons, which their attention has been called to with the most assiduous care while they were being prepared for the season that decides their fate for life. Instead of pursuing this idle routine and sighing after tasteless show and heartless state, with what dignity might young people of both sexes form attachments in the schools I have sketched, where, as they grew older, dancing, music and drawing might be allowed as relaxations — for at these schools young people of fortune ought to stay, more or less, until they came of age. Those intended for particular professions might attend the schools set aside for their special instruction three or four mornings a week.

I only drop these observations as hints at present — an outline of my plan rather than a digested version of it. But I must add that I strongly approve of one regulation mentioned in the pamphlet I referred to earlier: making children and young people independent of their masters with respect to punishment. They should be tried by their peers, which would be an admirable way of fixing sound principles of justice in the mind, and might have the happiest effect on the temper, which is soured or irritated very early by tyranny, until it becomes peevishly cunning or ferociously overbearing.

My imagination darts forward with benevolent fervour to greet these amiable and respectable groups, in spite of the sneers of cold hearts, who are at liberty to utter with frigid self-importance the damning epithet: romantic. I shall try to blunt its force by repeating the words of an eloquent moralist: "I do not know whether the illusions of a truly humane heart, whose zeal makes everything easy, are not preferable to that rough and repelling reason which always finds its first obstacle to whatever would promote the public good in its own indifference to it."

I know that libertines will also exclaim that a woman would be unsexed by acquiring strength of body and mind, and that beauty, soft bewitching beauty, would no longer adorn the daughters of men. I am of a very different opinion, for I think we should then see dignified beauty and true grace, produced by the concurrence of many powerful physical and moral causes. Not a relaxed beauty, it is true, nor the graces of helplessness, but the kind we see in the relics of antiquity, which makes us respect the human body as a majestic building fit to receive a noble inhabitant.

I do not forget the popular opinion that the Greek statues were not modelled from nature — that is, not from the proportions of a particular man, but that beautiful limbs and features were selected from various bodies to make a harmonious whole. This may be true in some degree. The fine ideal picture in an exalted imagination might be superior to the materials the sculptor found in nature, and so it might properly be called a model of humanity rather than of a man. But it was not the mechanical selection of limbs and features; it was the ebullition of a heated fancy bursting out, and the fine senses and enlarged understanding of the artist selected the solid matter he drew into that glowing focus.

I said it was not mechanical, because a whole was produced: a model of that grand simplicity, those concurring energies, which arrest our attention and command our reverence. For a servile copy even of beautiful nature produces nothing but an insipid lifeless beauty. Yet, quite apart from these observations, I believe the human form must have been far more beautiful once than it is now, because extreme indolence, barbarous bandaging and the many other causes that act forcibly on it in our luxurious state of society did not then retard its expansion or deform it. Exercise and cleanliness appear to be not only the surest means of preserving health but of promoting beauty, considering the physical causes alone. Yet that is not sufficient; moral causes must work with them, or beauty will be merely the rustic kind that blooms on the innocent, wholesome faces of some country people whose minds have never been exercised. To make the person perfect, physical and moral beauty ought to be attained at the same time, each lending and receiving force from the combination. Judgment must sit on the brow, affection and fancy beam in the eye, and humanity curve the cheek, or the sparkle of the finest eye and the elegant finish of the fairest features are vain; while in every movement that displays the active limbs and well-knit joints, grace and modesty should appear. But this fair assemblage is not brought together by chance. It is the reward of exertions that support one another; for judgment can only be acquired by reflection, affection by the discharge of duties, and humanity by the exercise of compassion towards every living creature.

Humanity to animals should be particularly instilled as part of a national education, for it is not at present one of our national virtues. Among the lower classes, tenderness towards their humble dumb servants is more often found in a savage state than a civilized one. For civilization prevents the kind of contact that creates affection in the rude hut or the mud cabin, and leads uncultivated minds, depraved only by the refinements prevailing in the society where they are trodden underfoot by the rich, to domineer over animals in revenge for the insults they must bear from their own superiors.

This habitual cruelty is first caught at school, where it is one of the boys' rare sports to torment the miserable creatures that come their way. The transition, as they grow up, from barbarity towards animals to domestic tyranny over wives, children and servants is very easy. Justice, and even benevolence, will not be a powerful spring of action unless it extends to the whole of creation. Indeed I believe it may be laid down as an axiom that those who can watch pain unmoved will soon learn to inflict it.

The vulgar are swayed by present feelings and by habits they have picked up by accident; but not much dependence can be placed on partial feelings, however just they are, for when they are not invigorated by reflection, custom weakens them until they are hardly felt at all. The sympathies of our nature are strengthened by pondering and deadened by thoughtless use. Macbeth's heart smote him more for one murder, the first, than for the hundred that followed to back it up. But when I used the word vulgar, I did not mean to confine the remark to the poor; a partial humanity founded on present sensations or on whim is quite as conspicuous among the rich, if not more so.

The lady who sheds tears over a bird starved in a snare, and curses the devils in human shape who goad a poor ox to madness or whip a patient donkey tottering under a load beyond its strength, will nevertheless keep her coachman and horses waiting whole hours for her while the sharp frost bites, or the rain beats against the well-closed windows that do not let in a breath of air to tell her how roughly the wind is blowing outside. And the woman who takes her dogs to bed and nurses them with a parade of sensibility when they are sick will let her babies grow up crooked in a nursery. This illustration is drawn from fact. The woman I am referring to was handsome, reckoned very handsome by people who do not miss the mind when the face is plump and fair; but her understanding had not been drawn away from female duties by literature, nor her innocence debauched by knowledge. No: she was quite feminine, in the masculine sense of the word. And so far from loving the spoiled brutes that filled the place her children ought to have occupied, she only lisped out a pretty mixture of French and English nonsense to please the men who flocked round her. The wife, the mother and the human creature were all swallowed up in the artificial character that an improper education and the selfish vanity of beauty had produced.

I do not like to make a distinction without a difference, and I confess I have been as much disgusted by the fine lady who took her lapdog to her bosom instead of her child as by the ferocity of a man who, beating his horse, declared that the animal knew when it was doing wrong as well as any Christian.

This brood of folly shows how mistaken those people are who, if they allow women to leave their harems, do not cultivate their understandings in order to plant virtues in their hearts. For if they had sense they might acquire the domestic taste that would lead them to love their whole family in reasonable order, from the husband down to the house dog; nor would they ever insult humanity in the person of the meanest servant by paying more attention to an animal's comfort than to a fellow creature's.

My observations on national education are obviously only hints; but what I chiefly want to press is the necessity of educating the sexes together in order to perfect both, and of making children sleep at home so that they may learn to love home — yet, so that private affections support the public ones instead of smothering them, of sending them to school to mix with a number of equals, since it is only by the jostlings of equality that we can form a just opinion of ourselves.

To make humankind more virtuous, and consequently happier, both sexes must act on the same principle. But how can that be expected when only one of them is allowed to see the reasonableness of it? To make the social compact truly equitable, and to spread the enlightening principles that alone can better the fate of humanity, women must be allowed to found their virtue on knowledge, which is scarcely possible unless they are educated in the same pursuits as men. For they are now made so inferior by ignorance and low desires that they do not deserve to be ranked with men; or else, by the serpentine wrigglings of cunning, they climb the tree of knowledge and gather just enough to lead men astray.

It is plain from the history of every nation that women cannot be confined to merely domestic pursuits, for they will not fulfil their family duties unless their minds take a wider range; and while they are kept in ignorance, they become slaves to pleasure in exactly the proportion in which they are slaves to men. Nor can they be shut out of great enterprises, though the narrowness of their minds often makes them spoil what they cannot understand.

The libertinism, and even the virtues, of superior men will always give women of a certain description great power over them; and these weak women, under the influence of childish passions and selfish vanity, will throw a false light over the very objects that the men who ought to be enlightening their judgment look at through their eyes. Men of fancy, and those sanguine characters who mostly hold the helm of human affairs, generally relax in the society of women; and surely I need not cite for even the most superficial reader of history the many examples of vice and oppression that the private intrigues of female favourites have produced, not to dwell on the mischief that naturally arises from the blundering interference of well-meaning folly. For in the transaction of business it is much better to deal with a knave than a fool, because a knave sticks to some plan, and any plan of reason can be seen through much sooner than a sudden flight of folly. The power that vile and foolish women have had over wise men who possessed sensibility is notorious. I shall mention only one instance.

Who ever drew a more exalted female character than Rousseau, though on the whole he was constantly labouring to degrade the sex? And why was he so anxious to do it? Truly, to justify to himself the affection that weakness and virtue had made him cherish for that fool Theresa. He could not raise her to the common level of her sex, and so he laboured to bring women down to hers. He found her a convenient humble companion, and pride made him determined to find some superior virtue in the being he had chosen to live with. But did her conduct during his life, and after his death, not show plainly how grossly mistaken he was when he called her a celestial innocent? Indeed, in the bitterness of his heart he laments himself that once his bodily infirmities kept him from treating her as a woman, she ceased to have any affection for him. And that was very natural, for, having so few sentiments in common, what was there to hold her once the tie of sex was broken? To hold the affection of a woman whose sensibility is confined to one sex, indeed to one man, requires sense enough to turn sensibility into the broad channel of humanity. Many women have not mind enough to feel affection for a woman, or friendship for a man. But the weakness of sex that makes a woman depend on a man for her subsistence produces a kind of cattish affection, which leads a wife to purr about her husband as she would about any man who fed and caressed her.

Men are, however, often gratified by this kind of fondness, which is confined in a beastly way to themselves; but should they ever become more virtuous, they will want to converse at their fireside with a friend, once they have stopped playing with a mistress.

Besides, understanding is necessary to give variety and interest even to sensual enjoyments; for low indeed in the intellectual scale is the mind that can go on loving when neither virtue nor sense gives an animal appetite any human appearance. But sense will always preponderate; and if women in general are not brought more onto a level with men, some superior women, like the Greek courtesans, will gather men of ability round them and draw many citizens away from families they would have stayed at home in, if their wives had had more sense, or the graces that come from exercising the understanding and the fancy, which are the lawful parents of taste. A woman of talent, unless she is absolutely ugly, will always obtain great power, raised by the weakness of her sex; and as men acquire virtue and delicacy by exercising their reason, they will look for both in women — but women can only acquire them in the same way men do.

Have the women of France or Italy confined themselves to domestic life? Though they have had no political existence so far, have they not had great illicit sway, corrupting themselves and the men whose passions they played on? In short, in whatever light I look at the subject, reason and experience convince me that the only way of leading women to fulfil their peculiar duties is to free them from all restraint by allowing them to share in the inherent rights of humankind.

Make them free, and they will quickly become wise and virtuous, as men become more so; for the improvement must be mutual, or else the injustice that one half of the human race is obliged to submit to will recoil on their oppressors, and the virtue of man will be worm-eaten by the insect he keeps under his feet.

Let men take their choice. Man and woman were made for each other, though not to become one being; and if men will not improve women, they will deprave them.

I am speaking of the improvement and emancipation of the whole sex; for I know that the behaviour of a few women who by accident, or by following a strong natural bent, have acquired a share of knowledge beyond the rest of their sex has often been overbearing. But there have been instances of women who attained knowledge without discarding modesty, and who did not always appear pedantically to despise the ignorance they were labouring to disperse in their own minds. The outcry that any advice about female learning commonly produces, especially from pretty women, often arises from envy. When they happen to see that even the lustre of their eyes and the flippant sportiveness of a refined coquetry will not always secure them attention through a whole evening, if a woman of more cultivated understanding tries to give the conversation a rational turn, the common consolation is that such women seldom get husbands. What arts have I not seen silly women use to interrupt by *flirtation* — a very significant word for such a manoeuvre — a rational conversation that had made the men forget they were pretty women.

But allowing what is very natural in us, that the possession of rare abilities really is calculated to excite an overweening pride, disgusting in men and women alike — in what a state of inferiority must the female faculties have been rusting when so small a portion of knowledge as those women attained who have been sneeringly called learned could be considered singular? Singular enough to puff up its possessor and excite envy in her contemporaries, and in some of the other sex. Indeed, has a little rationality not exposed many women to the severest censure? I am referring to well-known facts, for I have often heard women ridiculed, and every little weakness of theirs exposed, merely because they took the advice of some medical man and departed from the beaten track in the way they treated their infants. I have heard this barbarous hatred of innovation carried further still, and a sensible woman stigmatized as an unnatural mother who had been wisely solicitous to preserve her children's health, when in the midst of all her care she lost one of them to some accident of infancy that no prudence could have warded off. Her acquaintances observed that this was the consequence of newfangled notions — the newfangled notions of ease and cleanliness. And those who pretend to experience, though they have long clung to prejudices that in the opinion of the most sagacious physicians have thinned the human race, almost rejoiced at a disaster that gave a kind of sanction to established practice.

Indeed, if it were only on this account, the national education of women is of the utmost consequence; for what a number of human sacrifices are made to that Moloch, prejudice! And in how many ways are children destroyed by the lasciviousness of men? The want of natural affection in many women, drawn away from their duty by the admiration of men, and the ignorance of others, make human infancy a far more perilous state than that of animals; and yet men are unwilling to put women in a position to acquire understanding enough even to know how to nurse their own babies.

This truth strikes me so forcibly that I would rest the whole tendency of my reasoning on it; for whatever tends to incapacitate a woman as a mother takes her out of her sphere.

But it is vain to expect the present race of weak mothers either to take the reasonable care of a child's body that is needed to lay the foundation of a good constitution — supposing it does not suffer for the sins of its father — or to manage its temper so judiciously that as it grows up the child will not have to throw off everything its mother, its first instructor, taught it directly or indirectly. And unless the mind has uncommon vigour, womanish follies will stick to the character all through life. The weakness of the mother will be visited on the children. And while women are educated to rely on their husbands' judgment, this must always be the consequence; for there is no improving an understanding by halves, nor can any being act wisely by imitation, because in every circumstance of life there is a kind of individuality that requires an exertion of judgment to modify the general rule. The being who can think justly along one track will soon extend its intellectual empire; and the woman who has judgment enough to manage her children will not submit, right or wrong, to her husband, or patiently to social laws that make a nonentity of a wife.

In public schools, women should be taught the elements of anatomy and medicine as a guard against the errors of ignorance, not only so that they can take proper care of their own health but so that they can be rational nurses to their infants, their parents and their husbands; for the bills of mortality are swollen by the blunders of self-willed old women who administer remedies of their own without knowing anything of the human frame. It is proper too, even from a purely domestic point of view, to make women acquainted with the anatomy of the mind, by letting the sexes associate together in every pursuit, and by leading them to observe the progress of the human understanding in the improvement of the sciences and arts — never forgetting the science of morality, nor the study of the political history of humankind.

A man has been called a microcosm, and every family might likewise be called a state. States, it is true, have mostly been governed by arts that disgrace human character; and the want of a just constitution and of equal laws has so confused the notions of the worldly wise that they more than question the reasonableness of contending for the rights of humanity. So morality, polluted in the national reservoir, sends off streams of vice to corrupt the constituent parts of the body politic. But if nobler, or rather juster, principles regulated the laws — which ought to be the government of society, rather than the men who execute them — duty might become the rule of private conduct.

Besides, by exercising their bodies and their minds, women would acquire that mental activity so necessary in a mother, together with the fortitude that distinguishes steadiness of conduct from the obstinate perverseness of weakness. For it is dangerous to advise the indolent to be steady, because they instantly become rigorous, and to save themselves trouble punish with severity the faults that a patient fortitude of reason would have prevented. But fortitude presupposes strength of mind — and is strength of mind to be acquired by indolent acquiescence? By asking for advice instead of exerting the judgment? By obeying out of fear instead of practising the forbearance we all need to have shown us? The conclusion I wish to draw is obvious. Make women rational creatures and free citizens, and they will quickly become good wives and mothers — that is, if men do not neglect the duties of husbands and fathers.

In discussing the advantages that a combined public and private education, of the kind I have sketched, might reasonably be expected to produce, I have dwelt most on those that particularly concern the female world, because I think the female world is oppressed. Yet the gangrene produced by the vices that oppression breeds is not confined to the diseased part; it pervades society at large. So that when I wish to see my sex become more like moral agents, my heart leaps at the prospect of the general spread of that sublime contentment which only morality can spread.
