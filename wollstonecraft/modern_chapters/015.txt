Chapter Ten: Parental Affection

Parental affection is perhaps the blindest form of a perverse self-love; for we do not have, as the French do, two separate terms to distinguish the pursuit of a natural and reasonable desire from the ignorant calculations of weakness. Parents often love their children in the most brutal way and sacrifice every other duty to push them forward in the world — to promote, such is the perversity of unprincipled prejudice, the future welfare of the very beings whose present lives they are embittering by the most despotic stretch of power. Power, in fact, is always true to its own vital principle, for in every shape it wants to reign without check or enquiry. Its throne is built across a dark abyss that no eye must dare to explore, in case the baseless structure should totter under investigation. Obedience, unconditional obedience, is the catchword of tyrants of every description; and, to make assurance doubly sure, one kind of despotism supports another. Tyrants would have cause to tremble if reason ever became the rule of duty in any of the relations of life, for the light might spread until perfect day appeared. And when it did appear, how men would smile at the bogeys they started at during the night of ignorance, or in the twilight of timid enquiry.

Parental affection is in many minds nothing but a pretext for tyrannizing where it can be done with impunity; for only good and wise men are content with the kind of respect that will bear discussion. Convinced that they have a right to what they insist on, they do not fear reason, or dread having subjects that come back to natural justice sifted, because they firmly believe that the more enlightened the human mind becomes, the deeper root just and simple principles will take. They do not rest in expedients, or grant that what is metaphysically true can be practically false; disdaining the shifts of the moment, they wait calmly until time sanctions innovation and silences the hiss of selfishness or envy.

If the power of reflecting on the past, and of darting the keen eye of contemplation into the future, is humanity's grand privilege, it must be admitted that some people enjoy that prerogative in a very limited degree. Everything looks wrong to them; and, unable to distinguish the possible from the monstrous, they are afraid where no fear ought to find a place, running from the light of reason as though it were a firebrand. Yet the limits of the possible have never been fixed in a way that could stop a sturdy innovator's hand.

Woman, however, being a slave to prejudice in every situation, seldom exercises an enlightened maternal affection: she either neglects her children or spoils them with improper indulgence. Besides, the affection some women have for their children is, as I have called it before, often quite brutish, for it wipes out every spark of humanity. Justice, truth, everything is sacrificed by these Rebekahs, and for the sake of their own children they violate the most sacred duties, forgetting the common relationship that binds the whole human family together. Yet reason seems to say that anyone who lets one duty or one affection swallow up all the rest has not heart or mind enough to fulfil even that one conscientiously. It then loses the venerable look of a duty and takes on the fantastic shape of a whim.

Since the care of children in infancy is one of the grand duties nature attaches to women, that duty would supply many forcible arguments for strengthening the female understanding, if it were properly considered.

The formation of the mind must begin very early, and the temper in particular needs the most judicious attention — attention that women cannot give if they love their children only because they are their children, and look no further for the foundation of their duty than the feelings of the moment. It is this want of reason in their affections that makes women run so often into extremes, and be either the fondest or the most careless and unnatural of mothers.

To be a good mother, a woman must have sense, and that independence of mind which few women possess who have been taught to depend entirely on their husbands. Meek wives generally make foolish mothers: they want their children to love them best and to take their side in secret against the father, who is held up as a scarecrow. If the children are to be punished, though they have offended the mother, the father must inflict the punishment; he must be judge in every dispute. But I shall discuss this more fully when I come to private education. I only mean to insist here that unless a woman's understanding is enlarged, and her character made firmer by being allowed to govern her own conduct, she will never have sense enough, or command of temper enough, to manage her children properly. Her parental affection hardly deserves the name when it does not lead her to nurse her children herself; for discharging that duty is equally calculated to inspire maternal and filial affection, and it is the indispensable duty of men and women alike to fulfil the duties that give birth to the affections, which are the surest preservatives against vice. Natural affection, so called, I believe to be a very weak tie: affections must grow out of the habitual exercise of a mutual sympathy — and what sympathy does a mother exercise who sends her baby out to a nurse, and only takes it back from the nurse to send it away to school?

In the exercise of their natural feelings, providence has given women a natural substitute for love, for the time when the lover becomes only a friend and mutual confidence takes the place of overstrained admiration. A child then gently twists the slackening cord tight again, and a shared care produces a new shared sympathy. But a child, though it is a pledge of affection, will not enliven that affection if both father and mother are content to hand the charge over to hired help; for those who do their duty by proxy should not complain if they miss the reward of duty. Parental affection produces filial duty.
