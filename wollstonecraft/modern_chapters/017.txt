Chapter Twelve: On National Education

(Part 1 of 2)

The good effects of attending to private education will always be very limited, and the parent who really puts his own hand to the plough will always be disappointed to some degree, until education becomes a grand national concern. A man cannot retire into a desert with his child; and if he did, he could not bring himself back to childhood and become the proper friend and playfellow of an infant or a boy. And when children are confined to the society of men and women, they very soon acquire that premature manhood which stops the growth of every vigorous power of mind or body. To open their faculties they must be stirred up to think for themselves, and that can only be done by mixing a number of children together and making them pursue the same objects jointly.

A child very soon contracts a numbing indolence of mind, which he seldom has vigour enough to shake off, when he merely asks a question instead of looking for the information, and then relies implicitly on the answer he gets. With children of his own age this could never happen; and the subjects of enquiry, though they might be influenced, would not be entirely under the direction of men, who frequently damp abilities, if they do not destroy them, by bringing them forward too hastily — and too hastily they will infallibly be brought forward if the child is confined to the company of a man, however sagacious the man may be.

Besides, the seeds of every affection ought to be sown in youth, and the respectful regard felt for a parent is very different from the social affections that are to make the happiness of later life. Those rest on equality, and on an exchange of sentiments unclogged by the watchful seriousness which prevents argument even if it does not compel submission. However great a child's affection for his parent, he will always be longing to play and chatter with other children; and the very respect he feels — for filial esteem always has a dash of fear mixed in — will, if it does not teach him cunning, at least keep him from pouring out the little secrets that first open the heart to friendship and confidence and lead gradually to a wider benevolence. On top of that, he will never acquire the frank openness of manner that young people can only get by being often in company where they dare to say what they think, neither afraid of being reproved for presumption nor laughed at for folly.

Forcibly struck by the reflections that the sight of schools as they are presently conducted naturally suggests, I once gave my opinion rather warmly in favour of a private education; but further experience has led me to see the subject in a different light. I still think, however, that schools as they are now run are hotbeds of vice and folly, and that the knowledge of human nature supposed to be gained there is merely a cunning selfishness.

At school boys become gluttons and slovens and, instead of cultivating domestic affections, rush very early into the libertinism that destroys the constitution before it is formed, hardening the heart as it weakens the understanding.

I should in fact be against boarding schools if it were for no other reason than the unsettled state of mind that waiting for the holidays produces. The children's thoughts are fixed on them with eager anticipation for at least half the time, to put it moderately; and when the holidays come they are spent in complete dissipation and beastly indulgence.

But when children are brought up at home, though they may follow a plan of study more orderly than can be managed when nearly a quarter of the year is spent in idleness and as much again in regret and anticipation, they acquire too high an opinion of their own importance from being allowed to tyrannize over the servants, and from the anxiety most mothers show on the score of manners, who, eager to teach a gentleman's accomplishments, stifle a man's virtues in the bud. Brought into company when they ought to be seriously employed, and treated like men while they are still boys, they become vain and effeminate.

The only way to avoid two extremes equally damaging to morality would be to contrive some way of combining a public and a private education. Two natural steps might be taken to make men into citizens, and they seem to lead straight to the point: the domestic affections, which first open the heart to the various modifications of humanity, would be cultivated, while the children were nevertheless allowed to spend a great part of their time on equal terms with other children.

I still remember with pleasure the country day school, where a boy trudged in of a morning, wet or dry, carrying his books and his dinner if the distance was considerable. No servant led master by the hand then; once he had put on coat and breeches he was left to shift for himself and to come home alone in the evening to recount the day's exploits at his father's knee. His father's house was his home, and was fondly remembered ever after. Indeed, I appeal to certain superior men who were educated this way: has the memory of some shady lane where they conned their lesson, or of some stile where they sat making a kite or mending a bat, not endeared their country to them?

But what boy ever remembered with pleasure the years he spent in close confinement at an academy near London — unless he happened to recall the poor scarecrow of an usher he tormented, or the tart-seller he snatched a cake from to devour with the cattish appetite of selfishness? At boarding schools of every description, the recreation of the junior boys is mischief and of the senior boys vice. Besides, in the great schools what can be more damaging to the moral character than the system of tyranny and abject slavery established among the boys — to say nothing of the slavery to forms, which makes religion worse than a farce? For what good can be expected of a boy who takes the sacrament of the Lord's Supper to avoid forfeiting half a guinea, which he probably then spends in some sensual way? Half the boys' energy goes into evading the obligation to attend public worship — and well it may, for such constant repetition of the same thing must be a very irksome restraint on their natural liveliness. Since these ceremonies have the most fatal effect on their morals, and since a ritual performed by the lips while the heart and mind are far away is no longer stored up by our church as a bank to draw on for the fees of poor souls in purgatory, why should they not be abolished?

But the fear of innovation in this country extends to everything. It is only a covert fear, the apprehensive timidity of indolent slugs who guard by sliming it over the snug place they regard as a hereditary estate, and who eat, drink and enjoy themselves instead of fulfilling the duties, a few empty forms excepted, that the place was endowed for. These are the people who insist most strenuously that the founder's will must be observed, crying out against every reform as though it were a violation of justice. I am referring particularly to the relics of popery kept up in our colleges, where the Protestant members seem to be such sticklers for the established church — but their zeal never makes them lose sight of the spoils of ignorance that rapacious priests of superstitious memory scraped together. No: wise in their generation, they venerate the prescriptive right of possession as a stronghold, and still let the sluggish bell tinkle for prayers as it did in the days when the elevation of the host was supposed to atone for the sins of the people, in case one reformation should lead to another and the spirit kill the letter. These Roman customs have the most damaging effect on the morals of our clergy. The idle vermin who two or three times a day perform in the most slovenly fashion a service they think useless but call their duty soon lose any sense of duty at all. At college, forced either to attend public worship or to dodge it, they acquire a habitual contempt for the very service whose performance is to enable them to live in idleness. It is mumbled over as a piece of business, as a stupid boy repeats his lesson; and often the college slang escapes the preacher the moment he has left the pulpit, and even while he is eating the dinner he earned so dishonestly.

Nothing indeed could be more irreverent than the cathedral service as it is now performed in this country, nor does it contain a feebler set of men than the slaves of this childish routine. A disgusting skeleton of the former state is still exhibited, but all the solemnity that engaged the imagination, even if it did not purify the heart, has been stripped away. The performance of high mass on the continent must impress every mind where a spark of fancy glows with that awful melancholy, that sublime tenderness, so near akin to devotion. I do not say that these devotional feelings are of more moral use than any other emotion of taste; but I maintain that theatrical pomp which gratifies the senses is to be preferred to a cold parade that insults the understanding without ever reaching the heart.

Among remarks on national education such observations cannot be out of place, especially as the supporters of these establishments, degenerated into puerilities, claim to be the champions of religion. Religion, pure source of comfort in this vale of tears! How your clear stream has been muddied by the dabblers who have presumptuously tried to confine within one narrow channel the living waters that flow always towards God, the sublime ocean of existence! What would life be without the peace that only the love of God, when it is built on humanity, can give? Every earthly affection turns back at intervals to prey on the heart that feeds it; and the purest outpourings of benevolence, so often rudely damped by other people, must rise as a freewill offering to Him who gave them birth, and whose bright image they faintly reflect.

In public schools, however, religion, confounded with irksome ceremonies and unreasonable restraints, takes on its most ungracious aspect — not the sober austere one that commands respect while it inspires fear, but a ludicrous cast that serves to point a pun. For in fact most of the good stories and smart sayings that enliven spirits concentrated at cards are manufactured out of the very incidents that the men who countenance the abuse in order to live off the spoils are labouring to give a droll turn to.

There is not, perhaps, in the kingdom a more dogmatic or more luxurious set of men than the pedantic tyrants who live in colleges and preside over public schools. The holidays are equally damaging to the morals of masters and pupils; and the intercourse the masters keep up with the nobility introduces into their families the same vanity and extravagance that banish domestic duties and comforts from the lordly mansion whose state is so awkwardly aped on a smaller scale. The boys, who live at great expense with the masters and assistants, are never domesticated, though that is why they were placed there; for after a silent dinner they swallow a hasty glass of wine and retire to plan some mischievous trick, or to ridicule the person or manners of the very people they have just been cringing to, and whom they ought to consider as standing in for their parents.

Can it be surprising, then, that boys shut out from social conversation like this become selfish and vicious? Or that a bishop's mitre often ends up on the brow of one of these diligent pastors?

The desire to live in the same style as the rank just above them infects every individual and every class of people, and meanness is the companion of this ignoble ambition. But the most debasing professions are those whose ladder is patronage — and it is generally out of one of these professions that the tutors of youth are chosen. Can men whose conduct must be regulated by a cautious prudence forever on the watch for preferment be expected to inspire independent sentiments?

So far from thinking about the morals of boys, several schoolmasters have argued in my hearing that they only undertook to teach Latin and Greek, and that they had done their duty by sending some good scholars up to college.

A few good scholars may have been formed by emulation and discipline, I grant; but to bring these clever boys forward, the health and morals of a great many others have been sacrificed.

The sons of our gentry and wealthy commoners are mostly educated at these establishments — and will anyone claim that the majority, making every allowance, come under the description of tolerable scholars?

It is not for the benefit of society that a few brilliant men should be brought forward at the expense of the multitude. It is true that great men seem to spring up as great revolutions occur, at proper intervals, to restore order and blow aside the clouds that thicken over the face of truth; but let more reason and virtue prevail in society and these strong winds would not be needed. Public education of every description should be directed towards forming citizens; but if you wish to make good citizens, you must first exercise the affections of a son and a brother. That is the only way to expand the heart; for public affections, like public virtues, must always grow out of the private character, or they are only meteors shooting across a dark sky and vanishing as they are gazed at and admired.

Few people, I believe, have had much affection for humankind who did not first love their parents, their brothers and sisters, and even the household animals they first played with. The exercise of youthful sympathies forms the moral temperature; and it is the memory of these first affections and pursuits that gives life to the ones that come later under the direction of reason. It is in youth that the fondest friendships are formed, the genial juices rising at the same time and mixing kindly; or rather the heart, tempered to receive friendship, gets into the habit of looking for pleasure in something nobler than the churlish gratification of appetite.

To inspire a love of home and of domestic pleasures, then, children ought to be educated at home; for riotous holidays only make them fond of home for their own sakes. Yet the holidays, which do not foster domestic affections, continually disturb the course of study and make any plan of improvement that involves temperance abortive. Still, if they were abolished, children would be entirely separated from their parents, and I doubt whether they would become better citizens by sacrificing those preparatory affections, and destroying the force of the relationships that make married life as necessary as it is respectable. But if a private education produces self-importance, or isolates a man within his family, the evil is only shifted and not remedied.

This train of reasoning brings me back to a subject I mean to dwell on: the necessity of establishing proper day schools.

But these should be national establishments; for while schoolmasters depend on the caprice of parents, little exertion can be expected of them beyond what is needed to please ignorant people. Indeed, the necessity of a master's giving the parents some sample of a boy's abilities, which is shown to every visitor during the holidays, does more mischief than would at first be supposed. For these samples are seldom done entirely by the child itself, to put it moderately; so the master countenances falsehood, or else winds the poor machine up to some extraordinary exertion that damages the wheels and stops the progress of gradual improvement. The memory is loaded with unintelligible words to make a display with, while the understanding acquires no distinct ideas at all. But only that education deserves emphatically to be called a cultivation of the mind which teaches young people how to begin to think. The imagination should not be allowed to debauch the understanding before it has gained strength, or vanity will become the forerunner of vice; for every way of exhibiting a child's attainments is injurious to its moral character.

How much time is lost in teaching children to recite what they do not understand, while the mammas, seated on benches in their best array, listen with astonishment to the parrot-like prattle uttered in solemn cadences with all the pomp of ignorance and folly! Such exhibitions only serve to drive the spreading fibres of vanity through the whole mind, for they teach children neither to speak fluently nor to behave gracefully. So far from it, that these frivolous pursuits might comprehensively be called the study of affectation. We now rarely see a simple, bashful boy, though few people of taste were ever disgusted by the awkward sheepishness so natural to that age, which schools and an early introduction into society have turned into impudence and apish grimace.

Yet how can any of this be remedied while schoolmasters depend entirely on parents for a living, and while so many rival schools hang out their lures to catch the attention of vain fathers and mothers, whose parental affection only makes them wish their children to outshine the neighbours'?

Without great good luck, a sensible, conscientious man would starve before he could get a school going, if he disdained to fool weak parents by practising the secret tricks of the trade.

Even in the best-regulated schools, where swarms of children are not crammed together, many bad habits must be picked up; but at common schools the body, heart and understanding are equally stunted. For parents are often only in search of the cheapest school, and the master could not live if he did not take many more pupils than he can manage himself, nor will the scanty pittance allowed for each child let him hire enough ushers to help with the mechanical part of the business. Besides, whatever appearance the house and garden may make, the children get no comfort from either, since they are continually reminded by irksome restrictions that they are not at home, and the state rooms and the garden must be kept in order for the recreation of the parents — who visit the school on a Sunday and are impressed by the very parade that makes their children's situation uncomfortable.

With what disgust have I heard sensible women — for girls are more restrained and cowed than boys — speak of the wearisome confinement they endured at school. Not allowed, perhaps, to step off one broad walk in a superb garden, and obliged to pace stupidly up and down it with a steady deportment, heads up, toes turned out and shoulders braced back, instead of bounding about as nature directs in order to complete her own design, in all the various attitudes that are so good for the health. The pure animal spirits that make mind and body shoot up and unfold the tender blossoms of hope are turned sour, and vent themselves in vain wishes or pert complaints that contract the faculties and spoil the temper. Or else they mount to the brain and, sharpening the understanding before it has gained proportionate strength, produce that pitiful cunning which so disgracefully characterizes the female mind — and which will always characterize it, I fear, while women remain the slaves of power.

The little respect the male world pays to chastity is, I am persuaded, the grand source of many of the physical and moral evils that torment humankind, as well as of the vices and follies that degrade and destroy women. And yet at school boys infallibly lose the decent bashfulness that might have ripened into modesty at home.

I have already criticized the bad habits women pick up when they are shut up together; and I think the observation may fairly be extended to the other sex, until we draw the natural inference I have had in view all along — that to improve both sexes they ought to be educated together, not only in private families but in public schools. If marriage is the cement of society, everyone should be educated on the same model, or the intercourse of the sexes will never deserve the name of fellowship; nor will women ever fulfil the duties peculiar to their sex until they become enlightened citizens, until they become free by being enabled to earn their own subsistence independently of men — in the same sense, to prevent misunderstanding, in which one man is independent of another. Indeed, marriage will never be held sacred until women, by being brought up alongside men, are prepared to be their companions rather than their mistresses; for the mean doublings of cunning will always make them contemptible while oppression makes them timid. I am so convinced of this that I will venture to predict that virtue will never prevail in society until the virtues of both sexes are founded on reason, and until the affections common to both are allowed to gain their proper strength by the discharge of mutual duties.

If boys and girls were allowed to pursue the same studies together, those graceful decencies might be instilled early that produce modesty, without the distinctions of sex that taint the mind. Lessons in politeness, and the whole formula of decorum that treads on the heels of falsehood, would be made useless by a habitual propriety of behaviour — not put on for visitors like the courtly robe of politeness, but the sober effect of a cleanliness of mind. Would this simple elegance of sincerity not be a chaste homage paid to domestic affection, far beyond the meretricious compliments that shine with a false lustre in the heartless intercourse of fashionable life? But until more understanding prevails in society there will always be a want of heart and of taste, and the harlot's rouge will take the place of that heavenly glow which only virtuous affections can give a face. Gallantry, and what is called love, may exist without simplicity of character; but the main pillars of friendship are respect and confidence, and esteem is never founded on it-cannot-tell-what.

A taste for the fine arts needs a great deal of cultivation — but not more than a taste for the virtuous affections; and both presuppose that enlargement of mind which opens so many sources of mental pleasure. Why do people hurry off to noisy scenes and crowded parties? I should answer: because they lack activity of mind, because they have not cherished the virtues of the heart. They see and feel only in the gross, and are forever pining after variety, finding everything simple insipid.

This argument can be carried further than philosophers realise. For if nature destined women in particular for the discharge of domestic duties, she made them susceptible to the attached affections in a high degree. Now women are notoriously fond of pleasure, and by my definition they naturally must be, because they cannot enter into the fine detail of domestic taste, lacking the judgment that is the foundation of all taste. For the understanding, in spite of sensual cavillers, reserves to itself the privilege of conveying pure joy to the heart.

With what a languid yawn have I seen an admirable poem thrown down that a man of true taste returns to again and again with rapture; and, while a melody has almost suspended my breathing, a lady has asked me where I bought my gown. I have seen an eye glance coldly over a most exquisite picture and then rest, sparkling with pleasure, on a rudely sketched caricature; and while some terrific feature of nature has spread a sublime stillness through my soul, I have been asked to notice the pretty tricks of a lapdog that my perverse fate obliged me to travel with. Is it surprising that such a tasteless being should caress the dog rather than her children, or prefer the rant of flattery to the simple accents of sincerity?

To illustrate the remark I must be allowed to observe that men of the first genius and the most cultivated minds have appeared to have the highest relish for the simple beauties of nature; and they must have felt forcibly what they have described so well, the charm that natural affections and unsophisticated feelings spread round the human character. It is this power of looking into the heart, and of vibrating in response to every emotion, that lets the poet personify each passion and the painter sketch with a pencil of fire.

True taste is always the work of the understanding employed in observing natural effects; and until women have more understanding it is vain to expect them to have any domestic taste. Their lively senses will always be at work hardening their hearts, and the emotions struck out of them will go on being vivid and transitory, unless a proper education stores their minds with knowledge.

It is the want of domestic taste, and not the acquisition of knowledge, that takes women out of their families and tears the smiling baby from the breast that ought to feed it. Women have been left in ignorance and slavish dependence for many, many years, and still we hear of nothing but their fondness for pleasure and for power, their preference for rakes and soldiers, their childish attachment to toys, and the vanity that makes them value accomplishments above virtues.

History brings forward a fearful catalogue of the crimes their cunning has produced, when the weak slaves have had address enough to overreach their masters. In France, and in how many other countries, have men not been the luxurious despots and women the crafty ministers? Does that prove that ignorance and dependence make women domestic? Is their folly not a byword among the libertines who relax in their company? And do men of sense not constantly lament that an immoderate fondness for dress and dissipation takes the mother of a family away from home forever? Their hearts have not been debauched by knowledge, nor their minds led astray by scientific pursuits; and yet they do not fulfil the duties that as women nature calls on them to fulfil. On the contrary, the state of warfare that exists between the sexes makes them use the wiles that frustrate the more open designs of force.

When, therefore, I call women slaves, I mean it in a political and civil sense; for indirectly they get too much power, and are debased by their efforts to obtain an illicit sway.

Let an enlightened nation try what effect reason would have in bringing them back to nature and to their duty; and, allowing them to share the advantages of education and government with men, let it see whether they will become better as they grow wiser and freer. They cannot be injured by the experiment, for it is not in the power of man to make them more insignificant than they are at present.

To make this practicable, day schools for particular ages should be set up by government, in which boys and girls could be educated together. The school for the younger children, from five to nine years old, ought to be absolutely free and open to every class. A sufficient number of masters should be chosen by a select committee in each parish, to whom any complaint of negligence or the like could be made, if it were signed by six of the children's parents.

Ushers would then be unnecessary; for I believe experience will always show that this kind of subordinate authority is particularly harmful to the morals of the young. What can tend to deprave the character more than outward submission and inward contempt? And how can boys be expected to treat an usher with respect when the master seems to regard him as a servant, and almost to countenance the ridicule that becomes the boys' chief amusement during playtime?
