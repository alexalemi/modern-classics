Charles Dodgson taught mathematics at Christ Church, Oxford, for twenty-six years, and published the Alice books under the name Lewis Carroll. This one comes out of the day job. In 1879 he printed a defence of Euclid's Elements as a school textbook, and cast it as a farce in four acts.

Minos, a college examiner, is marking papers at midnight, his hair radiating in all directions from much running of fingers through it. He falls asleep over the heap. The ghost of Euclid appears. So does Herr Niemand — Mr Nobody — the phantasm of a German professor, who arrives as counsel for each of thirteen modern geometry manuals in turn, puts their case in complete good faith, and loses every time.

The quarrel was a live one. A crowd of new textbooks was then trying to push Euclid out of English schools, and Dodgson examines them one at a time on a single question: how do you know that two lines will ever meet? That is Euclid's twelfth axiom, the one about parallels, which had embarrassed geometers for two thousand years because it looks as though it ought to be provable and is not. Every rival has a way round it. Carroll shows that not one of the ways works, and that most of them smuggle the assumption back in under another name. His real subject is what a proof is for, and what a sequence of theorems owes a beginner who has to learn them in order.

The comedy is in the manners. Minos is exhausted, sardonic and unfailingly polite; he gives a logical fallacy the mark that cricketers call a duck's egg and thermometers call zero. Niemand is earnest and doomed, and as his position gets worse he takes to coughing, then to refilling his pipe, then to threatening to vanish. Nobody sneers, and the courtesy is the joke. In the middle of an argument about parallel lines a man walks into an Egyptian restaurant and orders a dish whose name runs to a hundred and seventy Greek letters.

Almost nobody has read it. The text here is from a scan of the revised edition of 1885, and much of the work was repair. The printing marks each speech with an abbreviated name, the scanner lost about fifty of them, and a speech handed to the wrong man reads perfectly and argues the opposite; every tag was checked back against the play's own cast, and the geometrical labels against the diagrams they name. Three more of Carroll's mathematical books — Symbolic Logic, Pillow Problems and A Tangled Tale — are also in this collection.
