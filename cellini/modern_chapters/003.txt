Book One, Chapters 15 to 22

Chapter 15

Going on working with this Master Pagolo Arsago, I earned a great deal, always sending the greater part to my good father. At the end of two years, at my good father's entreaty, I went back to Florence, and set to work again with Francesco Salimbene, with whom I earned very well and wore myself out learning. Taking up again with that Francesco di Filippo, although I was very much given to certain pleasures, on account of that cursed playing, I never let go of certain hours of the day or the night which I gave to my studies.

In this time I made a silver clasp of the kind they used to call a heart-key. It was a belt three fingers wide, of the sort that used to be made for newly married women, and it was worked in half relief with some little figures in the round among the rest. It was made for a man called Raffaello Lapaccini. Although I was very badly paid for it, the honour I drew out of it was worth far more than the price I could justly have got.

Having worked at this time with a good many different people in Florence, where I had come to know some decent men among the goldsmiths — like that Marcone, my first master — others who had the name of being very good men, having been undermined by them in my work as far as they could manage, robbed me grossly. Seeing this, I broke away from them and considered them scoundrels and thieves. One goldsmith among the rest, called Giovanbattista Sogliani, obligingly let me have part of his shop, which was at the corner of the Mercato Nuovo, next to what had been the Landi bank. There I made a number of beautiful little pieces and earned well: I was able to help my household very well indeed. This woke the envy of those bad masters I had had before, who were called Salvadore and Michele Guasconti. They had three big shops in the goldsmiths' trade and did a great deal of business; so that, seeing that they were injuring me, I complained to a decent man about it, saying that the thefts they had used on me under the cloak of their pretended goodness ought to have been enough for them. This came back to their ears, and they boasted that they would make me repent of such words; at which I, not knowing what colour fear was, thought nothing or little of them.

Chapter 16

One day it happened that I was leaning against the shop of one of these men, who called me over and partly scolded me and partly bullied me; to which I answered that if they had done right by me I should have said of them what one says of good and decent men; and that since they had done the opposite, they should complain of themselves and not of me. While I was talking, one of them, called Gherardo Guasconti, their cousin — put up to it, perhaps, by the rest of them together — waited for a load to pass. It was a load of bricks. When the load was opposite me, this Gherardo shoved it into me so hard that it hurt me badly. Turning round at once and seeing him laughing, I fetched him such a punch on the temple that he fell senseless like a dead man; then, turning to his cousins, I said: "That is how one treats thieving cowards of your sort." And when they wanted to make some demonstration, since there were a good many of them, I, being on fire, put my hand to a small knife I had, saying: "Whichever of you comes out of his shop, let the other run for the confessor, because the doctor will have nothing to do here." These words frightened them so much that not one of them moved to help his cousin.

As soon as I had gone, the fathers and the sons ran to the Eight, and there they said that I had attacked them in their own shops with weapons in my hand — a thing that had never been done in Florence before. The Eight had me summoned; and I appeared; and they gave me a great scolding and shouted at me, partly because they saw me in a cloak and them in gown and hood like respectable citizens; and also because my adversaries had been to speak to those gentlemen at home, each one separately, while I, not knowing how these things are done, had spoken to none of them, trusting in the great right I had on my side. And I said that, at the great injury and insult Gherardo had done me, moved by the greatest anger, and having given him nothing but a slap, it did not seem to me I deserved so stiff a reprimand.

Prinzivalle della Stufa, who was one of the Eight, hardly let me finish saying "slap" before he said: "You gave him a punch and not a slap." The little bell was rung and we were all sent outside, and in my defence Prinzivalle said to his colleagues: "Consider, gentlemen, the simplicity of this poor young man, who accuses himself of having given a slap, thinking it is a smaller offence than giving a punch; because for a slap in the Mercato Nuovo the penalty is twenty-five scudi, and for a punch little or nothing. This is a very able young man, and he keeps his poor household by his own labours, and keeps it in plenty; and would to God our city had abundance of that sort, seeing how short of them it is."

Chapter 17

There were among them certain scraggy little hoods who, moved by my adversaries' pleading and their false information, being of the party of Fra Girolamo, would have liked to put me in prison and sentence me by the shovelful; but the good Prinzivalle put a stop to all of it. So he had me fined a small fine of four bushels of flour, to be given as alms to the convent of the Murate. As soon as we were called back in, he ordered me not to speak a word, on pain of their displeasure, and to obey what I had been sentenced to. So, giving me a stiff shouting-at, they sent us to the chancellor; and I, still muttering "It was a slap and not a punch," left the Eight laughing.

The chancellor ordered us, on the magistrate's behalf, to stand surety for each other; and they sentenced me alone to those four bushels of flour. It seemed to me I had been robbed on the highway; and so I sent for a cousin of mine called Master Annibale, a surgeon, the father of Messer Librodoro Librodori, wanting him to give the promise for me. He would not come; at which I, in a rage, blew up like an asp and made a desperate decision. Here you can see how much the stars not only incline us but force us. Knowing what a great obligation this Annibale was under to my house, my anger grew so much that, being carried right over to the bad and being somewhat choleric by nature, I stayed and waited until the said office of the Eight had gone to dinner. Being left there alone, and seeing that none of the Eight's staff was watching me any more, on fire with anger I went out of the Palace and ran to my shop, where I found a good-sized dagger, and jumped into the house of my adversaries, who kept house and shop together.

I found them at table; and that young Gherardo, who had been the head of the quarrel, threw himself on me; at which I struck him a dagger blow in the chest that went through his doublet and his jerkin right through to the shirt, without having touched his flesh or done him any harm in the world. It seemed to me, from the way my hand went in and the noise of the clothes, that I had done him tremendous harm; and he fell to the ground with fright, and I said: "Oh, you traitors, today is the day I kill the lot of you." The father, the mother and the sisters, believing it was Judgement Day, threw themselves on their knees on the ground at once and called out for mercy at the top of their voices; and seeing that they made no defence against me at all, and him stretched on the ground like a dead man, it seemed to me too low a thing to touch them; but I ran furiously down the stairs.

When I got to the street I found the whole rest of the family, more than twelve of them; one of them had an iron shovel, some a great iron bar, others hammers and anvils, others sticks. Getting in among them like a poisoned bull, I threw four or five of them to the ground and fell down with them, always striking with the dagger, now at this one and now at that. Those who were left standing worked as hard as they could, hitting me two-handed with hammers, sticks and anvils; and because God sometimes mercifully steps in, he brought it about that neither they to me nor I to them did any harm in the world. All that was left there was my cap, which my adversaries, having pulled themselves together after running some way off from it, each of them struck with his weapon; and afterwards, looking round among themselves for the wounded and the dead, there was not one of them who had any hurt.

Chapter 18

I went off towards Santa Maria Novella, and ran straight into Fra Alesso Strozzi, whom I did not know; and I commended myself to this good friar for the love of God, to save my life, because I had done a great wrong. The good friar told me not to be afraid of anything, because whatever evils in the world I had done, in that little cell of his I was perfectly safe.

An hour later the Eight, meeting outside their usual order, had one of the most terrifying proclamations issued against me that anyone ever heard, with the heaviest penalties for whoever had me or knew where I was, without regard to the place or the quality of the person keeping me. My afflicted, poor, good father went in to the Eight and threw himself on his knees on the ground, asking mercy for his poor young son; at which one of those hot-headed men, shaking the crest of his scraggy hood and standing up, said to my poor father with some insulting words: "Get up from there, and get out at once, because tomorrow morning we shall send him out to your farm with the lances." My poor father, still bold, answered them, saying: "You will do what God has ordained, and no more than that." To which the same man answered that God had certainly ordained it. And my father said to him: "I take comfort in the fact that you certainly do not know it."

Leaving them, he came to find me, along with a young man of my own age called Piero di Giovanni Landi; we loved each other more than if we had been brothers. This young man had a marvellous sword under his cloak and a beautiful coat of mail; and when they came to me my spirited father told me what had happened, and what the lords of the Eight had said to him. Then he kissed me on the forehead and on both eyes; he blessed me from his heart, saying: "May the power of God be what helps you"; and handing me the sword and the armour, he helped me put them on with his own hands. Then he said: "Oh, my good son, with these in your hand, either you live or you die." Pier Landi, who was standing there, never stopped weeping; and when he had handed me ten gold scudi, I asked him to take off certain little hairs of my beard, which were the first down.

Fra Alesso dressed me as a friar and gave me a lay brother for company. Going out of the convent and out by the Prato gate, I went along the walls as far as the Piazza San Gallo; and climbing the slope of Montui, in one of the first houses I found a man called Il Grassuccio, own brother to Messer Benedetto da Montevarchi. I unfriared myself at once, and having turned back into a man, we mounted two horses that were there for us and went off that night to Siena. I sent Grassuccio back to Florence, and he greeted my father and told him I had got safely away.

My father was overjoyed, and thought a thousand years had gone by before he could find that man of the Eight who had insulted him; and when he found him he said: "You see, Antonio, that it was God who knew what was going to happen to my son, and not you?" To which he answered: "Let him come back here another time." My father to him: "I shall attend to thanking God, who has saved him from this."

Chapter 19

Being in Siena, I waited for the Rome courier and joined up with him. When we had passed the Paglia we met the messenger who was carrying the news of the new Pope, who was Pope Clement.

When I got to Rome I set to work in the shop of Master Santi the goldsmith. He was dead, but a son of his kept the shop. This man did not work himself, but had all the business of the shop done by a young man called Lucagnolo da Iesi. He was a countryman, and had come as a small boy to work with Master Santi. He was short, but very well built. This young man worked better than any man I had seen up to that time, with the greatest ease and with a great deal of design; he worked only in large pieces, that is, very beautiful vases and basins and things of that sort.

Having set myself to work in that shop, I undertook to make certain candlesticks for the Spanish bishop of Salamanca. These candlesticks were richly worked, as far as such a piece allows. A pupil of Raphael of Urbino, called Gianfrancesco, nicknamed Il Fattore, was a very able painter; and because he was a friend of the bishop, he put me very much in his favour, so that I had a great many pieces to do for this bishop, and earned very well.

At this time I went sometimes to draw in Michelangelo Buonarroti's chapel, and sometimes to the house of Agostino Chigi of Siena, in which house there were many beautiful paintings by the hand of that most excellent man Raphael of Urbino; and this was on holidays, because Messer Gismondo Chigi, brother of Messer Agostino, lived in that house. They were very proud when they saw young men like me coming to learn inside their houses. Messer Gismondo's wife, having seen me often in this house of hers — this lady was as well bred as could be and beautiful beyond measure — came up to me one day, looked at my drawings, and asked me whether I was a sculptor or a painter. I told the lady I was a goldsmith. She said I drew far too well for a goldsmith; and having a lily of the most beautiful diamonds set in gold brought to her by one of her waiting women, she showed it to me and wanted me to value it. I valued it at eight hundred scudi. Then she said I had valued it very well.

Then she asked me whether I had the confidence to set them well. I said very willingly; and in front of her I made a small drawing of it, and I made it all the better because I was enjoying spending time with this most beautiful and pleasant gentlewoman. When the drawing was finished, another very beautiful Roman lady came up, who had been upstairs and came down and asked Madonna Porzia what she was doing there; and she said, smiling: "I am amusing myself watching this good young man draw, who is both good and handsome." I, having got a little bold, but with a little honest shame mixed in, went red and said: "Whatever I am, madam, I shall always be entirely at your service." The gentlewoman, also blushing a little, said: "You know very well that I want you to serve me"; and handing me the lily, she said I should take it away with me. And more than that, she gave me twenty gold scudi that she had in her pocket, and said: "Set it for me in the way you have drawn it for me, and save me this old gold it is set in now." The Roman lady then said: "If I were that young man, I should be off with a good conscience." Madonna Porzia added that skill is rarely found with vice, and that if I did such a thing I should badly deceive that good look of an honest man that I showed. And turning, taking the Roman lady by the hand, she said to me with the pleasantest laugh: "Goodbye, Benvenuto."

I stayed a while longer over the drawing I was making, copying a certain figure of Jove by the hand of Raphael of Urbino. When I had finished it I left, and set myself to make a small wax model, showing by it how the finished piece would come out; and when I took it to show to Madonna Porzia, that Roman lady I spoke of being present, both of them were greatly satisfied with my work and did me so much honour that, moved by a little boldness, I promised them the piece would be half as good again as the model. So I set to work, and in twelve days I finished the jewel in the form of a lily, as I said above, ornamented with little masks, children and animals, and beautifully enamelled; so that the diamonds the lily was made of were improved more than half.

Chapter 20

While I was working on this piece, that able man Lucagnolo, whom I spoke of above, showed that he took it very badly, telling me more than once that I should make far more profit and more honour by helping him work large silver vases, as I had begun to do. To which I said that I would be able, whenever I wanted, to work on large silver vases; but that pieces like the one I was making did not come along every day; and that in such pieces there was no less honour than in large silver vases, and a great deal more profit.

This Lucagnolo laughed at me, saying: "You will see, Benvenuto; because by the time you have finished that piece of yours, I shall have hurried up and finished this vase, which I began when you began the jewel; and experience will show you what profit I shall get out of my vase and what you will get out of your jewel." To which I answered that I was very glad to make such a trial with so able a man as he was, because at the end of the pieces it would be seen which of us was deceived. And so both of us, with a little scornful laugh, bent our heads fiercely, each eager to finish what he had begun; so that in about ten days each of us had finished his piece with great polish and art.

Lucagnolo's was a very large vase which was used on Pope Clement's table, where he threw the little bones of meat and the peel of various fruits while he was at his meal; made more for show than for necessity. This vase was ornamented with two beautiful handles, with many little and large masks, and with much beautiful foliage, of as much beauty and grace of design as anyone could imagine; to which I said that it was the most beautiful vase I had ever seen. At this Lucagnolo, thinking he had convinced me, said: "Your piece seems no less beautiful to me, but we shall soon see the difference between the one and the other."

So he took his vase and carried it to the Pope, who was very well satisfied, and had it paid for at once at the usual rate of the trade for such large work. Meanwhile I carried my piece to the gentlewoman, Madonna Porzia, who told me with great astonishment that I had gone far beyond the promise I had made her; and then she added that I should ask whatever I liked for my labour, because it seemed to her that I deserved so much that if she gave me a castle she would hardly feel she had satisfied me; but since she could not do that, she said, laughing, that I should ask for what she could do. To which I said that the greatest reward I had wanted for my labour was to have satisfied her ladyship. And so, laughing as well, and having made her a bow, I left, saying that I wanted no other reward than that.

Then Madonna Porzia turned to that Roman lady and said: "Do you see that the qualities we judged were in him are these, and not the vices?" Both of them wondered at it, and Madonna Porzia said: "My Benvenuto, have you ever heard it said that when the poor man gives to the rich, the devil laughs at it?" To which I said: "And so, out of all his troubles, this time I want to see him laugh." And as I went off she said that this time she did not want to do him that favour.

When I got back to my shop, Lucagnolo had the money he had had for his vase in a paper twist; and when I came in he said: "Come and compare the price of your jewel here alongside the price of my vase." To which I said that he should keep it that way until the next day, because I hoped that, just as my piece in its own kind had been no less beautiful than his, so I expected to let him see the price of it.

Chapter 21

The next day came, and Madonna Porzia sent her steward to my shop, called me outside, and handed me a paper twist full of money on that lady's behalf, saying that she did not want the devil laughing at it altogether; giving me to understand that what she was sending me was not the full payment my labour deserved, with a great many other courteous words worthy of such a lady.

Lucagnolo, who thought a thousand years had gone by before he could put his twist next to mine, as soon as I came into the shop, in front of twelve workmen and other neighbours who had come up wanting to see the end of the contest, Lucagnolo took his twist with a scornful laugh, saying "Ho! Ho!" three or four times, and poured his money onto the counter with a great noise. There were twenty-five scudi in giulios, and he thought mine would be four or five scudi in small change. As for me, deafened by his shouting and by the looks and the laughter of the people standing round, after glancing a little way into my own twist and seeing that it was all gold, I stood at one end of the counter with my eyes down, and without a sound in the world lifted my twist high up in both hands and let it pour out like a mill hopper. My money was half as much again as his; so that all those eyes, which had been fixed on me with some scorn, turned at once to him and said: "Lucagnolo, Benvenuto's money, being gold, and being half as much again, makes a much finer show than yours."

I quite believed that out of envy, together with the shame Lucagnolo felt, he would drop dead on the spot; and although a third of that money of mine came to him, since I was the workman — for that is the custom: two thirds go to the workman and the other third to the masters of the shop — reckless envy had more power in him than greed, which ought to have worked entirely the other way, this Lucagnolo being the son of a peasant from Iesi. He cursed his art and the men who had taught it to him, saying that from then on he did not want to do that heavy work any more; he only wanted to attend to making those little bits of tomfoolery, since they were paid so well.

No less angry, I said that every bird sang its own song; that he was talking according to the caves he had come out of; but that I told him plainly, I should manage his rubbish perfectly well, and he would never manage that sort of tomfoolery. And going off in a rage, I told him I would soon let him see it. The people who were there told him out loud that he was in the wrong, holding him for the boor he was, and me for a man, as I had shown myself.

Chapter 22

The next day I went to thank Madonna Porzia, and told her that her ladyship had done the opposite of what she had said: that when I wanted to make the devil laugh, she had made him deny God again. We both laughed pleasantly, and she gave me other beautiful and good pieces to do.

In the meantime I arranged, through a pupil of Raphael of Urbino the painter, for the bishop of Salamanca to give me a large water vessel to make, called an ewer, of the kind that are kept on sideboards for ornament. And since the bishop wanted two of them made of equal size, he gave one to Lucagnolo to make and I had the other; and that painter Gianfrancesco gave us the design for the mouldings of the vessels.

So I set my hand to my vessel with a marvellous appetite, and was accommodated with a small part of a shop by a Milanese called Master Giovanpiero della Tacca. Having got myself in order, I reckoned up the money I might need for my own affairs, and sent all the rest to help my poor good father. While it was being paid to him in Florence, he happened to run into one of those hot-headed men who were of the Eight at the time I made that little bit of disorder, and who, insulting him, had told him that he would send me out to the farm with lances whatever happened. And because that hot-head had certain thoroughly bad sons, my father said, to the point: "Misfortunes can happen to anybody, and above all to hot-tempered men when they are in the right, as happened to my son; but let us look at the rest of his life, and see how well I have known how to bring him up. Please God, for your own sake, that your sons do you neither worse nor better than mine do to me; because just as God has made me able to bring them up, so, where my own powers could not reach, he himself has saved them for me, against your belief, out of your violent hands."

And going off, he wrote me all of this, begging me for the love of God to play sometimes, so as not to lose that fine skill which he had taught me with so much labour. The letter was full of the most loving fatherly words anyone could ever hear; so that they moved me to tears of pity, and I wanted to give him a good measure of contentment about the playing before he died — as God grants us all the lawful favours we faithfully ask him for.
