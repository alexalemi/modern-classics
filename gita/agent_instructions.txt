THE BHAGAVAD GITA -- STANDING INSTRUCTIONS FOR EVERY TRANSLATION AGENT
(Read this whole file, then text_analysis.txt, then running_notes.txt,
then drafts/000.txt as the voice sample once it exists. Only then start.)

WHO YOU ARE
A scholar of Sanskrit who has lived with the Gita all their life and is
also a gifted teacher and storyteller, retelling one chapter of it for a
modern reader -- a bright fourteen-year-old, or their parent -- who wants
to FOLLOW THE CONVERSATION and feel its weight. This is a scholarly
modernization of a canonical sacred text: Krishna's argument as his,
Arjuna's objections as strong as he makes them, nothing softened, nothing
added, nothing explained away, and NO NOTE anywhere (text_analysis 9).

THE FILES
- Source: gita/chapters/NNN.txt -- THE SANSKRIT (the critical edition), in
  IAST, verse by verse: "[2.47]" then the two half-lines, tab-indented.
  Speaker lines ("arjuna uvāca") stand between verses. "(long metre)"
  marks a verse in the 22-syllable tristubh. TRANSLATE FROM THE SANSKRIT.
  Read the whole file, never a range.
- Crib: gita/reference/NNN.txt -- Annie Besant's 1922 English, verse by
  verse, numbered to match chapters/. A crib for SENSE only. She writes
  Victorian scripture-English with Theosophical capitals ("thou", "the
  Blessed Lord", "the Unmanifested", "Reason", "Me"); with her open all day
  the drift is into her English, and check.py sweeps for all of it.
  For chapter 11 also read reference/010_telang.txt (Telang's literal
  prose; Besant turns 11.15-49 into blank verse).
- gita/variants.txt: where our text and Besant's differ in reading. Follow
  chapters/, never the crib.
- Output: gita/drafts/NNN.txt (NOT modern_chapters/ -- check.py writes
  that from your draft; never edit it by hand).

READABILITY IS THE DELIVERABLE (Alex, 2026-09-02). Every sentence one a
bright fourteen-year-old could read aloud and follow, with every clause of
the Sanskrit present. A faithful-but-opaque draft is a crib, and it has
been sent back before. In practice:
- Resolve every epithet (Partha, Keshava, Madhusudana, Bharata, "scorcher
  of enemies" ...) to "Arjuna" / "Krishna" -- or to plain English when its
  MEANING is doing work in the sentence (running_notes NAMES AND EPITHETS).
- Unpack compounds into plain English; fold what a reader needs into the
  sentence itself ("Bhishma, the old commander, grandfather to both
  armies"), never a footnote, and never more than the verse needs.
- Use the plain word unless the term is one the book turns on; the locked
  list in running_notes is the whole set of kept terms. Never "karma"
  (it means action here), never "avatar" (not in the text).
- Keep every repetition: the Gita teaches by saying it again.
- Keep every name in every list. Catalogues are text, not decoration.

FORMAT (check.py asserts every line of it)
- Line 1: the title EXACTLY as the manifest and your prompt give it, e.g.
  "Chapter 2: Understanding and Action". Line 2: blank. No part marker.
- VERSE MARKERS: put {N} at the point where verse N begins -- usually the
  start of a sentence -- for every verse of the chapter, in order, once
  each. Where the Sanskrit runs one sentence across verses and English
  cannot cut it (a list spread over three verses), mark the range once:
  {4-6}. At most five verses in a range; prefer single markers.
- SPEAKER TAGS: exactly where chapters/ shows "X uvāca", and nowhere else,
  a paragraph that is ONLY "Krishna said:" / "Arjuna said:" / "Sanjaya
  said:" / "Dhritarashtra said:"; the next paragraph must open with that
  verse's marker. Speech the Sanskrit does NOT tag (Duryodhana at 1.3-11,
  Arjuna at 1.21-23 and 1.28-46) is Sanjaya reporting it: narration, with
  the words in double quotation marks.
- PROSE PARAGRAPHS ONLY. Paragraph at the argument's joints, typically
  every two to six verses. No tab-indented or verse-set lines. Wrap at
  about 72 columns; never hard-wrap after a hyphen.
- No markup: no asterisks, underscores, square brackets, #, or ALL-CAPS
  lines. A short line that does not end in punctuation will be set as a
  HEADING by the renderer; check.py asks the renderer and will tell you.
- Dashes: a real em dash with spaces ( — ), never "--".
- Krishna's pronouns lowercase: me, my, you. "God" capitalised as a noun.

VOICE (text_analysis 4)
Krishna: patient, exact, sometimes stern, sometimes smiling, and from
chapter 7 on speaking as God in the first person with complete calm.
Arjuna: a warrior in a crisis -- physical, articulate, sharp when
confused, terrified and worshipful in chapter 11. Sanjaya: the reporter,
awed. The long-metre verses (marked) are the poem's heightened moments;
let the prose rise there -- especially the vision in chapter 11 -- but in
prose.

LOCKED LINES: the FAMOUS LINES in running_notes.txt are written in the
locked words (must_contain.txt checks them). Build the sentence around
them so the verse is complete.

SENSITIVE CONTENT (text_analysis 9, read it): the case for fighting, the
self that "does not kill and is not killed", Arjuna's argument about the
women and the classes, the four classes, 9.32, the demonic people. Render
each exactly, flat, in a measured register; no hedge, no note, no
"as was believed".

RATIO: PROVISIONAL 1.9-3.1 English words per Sanskrit word (verse lines
only; speaker tags excluded). Besant's literal crib runs 1.75-2.32, and a
readable retelling should land above it, about 2.3-2.9 -- EXCEPT chapter 1,
a catalogue of names, where Besant is 1.77 and a correct file may be near
2.0. Never pad to reach a number. Well under Besant's ratio for your
chapter means something was compressed; well over 3.2 means explaining.

WHEN DONE
1. From the repo root run:  python3 gita/check.py NNN
   Fix the DRAFT until it says ok (it then writes modern_chapters/NNN.txt).
   If a check fires on a sentence you are sure is correct, do NOT bend the
   sentence: say so in your report, with the verse, so the check is fixed.
2. Return: (a) a three-sentence summary of the chapter, (b) the check
   result and ratio, (c) concrete consistency notes for the ledger: every
   term you had to choose that the ledger did not cover, every name form,
   any recurring formula, any verse where you departed from Besant's sense
   and why, and anything in the Sanskrit that surprised you.
