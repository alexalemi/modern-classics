1. THE SOURCE, AND WHAT IS TRANSLATED
======================================================================

THE TEXT TRANSLATED is the Sanskrit of the CRITICAL EDITION: the Bhagavad
Gita as it stands in the Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute's
Mahabharata (Bhishma Parva, adhyayas 23-40), in the electronic text entered
by Muneo Tokunaga et al. and revised by John Smith (Cambridge), served by
GRETIL as bhgce__u.htm. IAST transliteration, one half-verse a line, every
line tagged with its Gita and Mahabharata number. 700 verses, 18 chapters,
counts 47 72 43 42 29 47 30 28 34 42 55 20 34 27 20 24 28 78 -- the standard
count, which prep.py hard-codes and asserts.
GRETIL's notice: "for reference purposes only; copyright and terms of usage
as for source file." What this edition publishes is a new English
translation of an ancient text; the Sanskrit itself is not reproduced on
the page.

THE SECOND SANSKRIT WITNESS is the Devanagari printed verse by verse in
Besant's 1922 edition (the vulgate, the text of Shankara's commentary and
of nearly every printed Gita). prep.py transliterates it and compares every
verse to the critical edition with spelling, sandhi spacing and nasal
spelling normalised away: 650 of 700 verses agree EXACTLY, and the 50 that
differ (variants.txt) are almost all single-letter orthography (klaibya /
klaivya, varttamana / vartamana). Two independent readings agreeing on 650
verses, in order, is the evidence that the parse is complete and right.
Real readings that differ: 1.28 (plural/singular "kinsmen"), 1.37
(sabandhavan / svabandhavan), 7.11, 12.14, 13.17. FOLLOW THE CRITICAL
EDITION; where Besant's English follows the other reading the crib is
wrong for this source.

THE ONE STRUCTURAL DIFFERENCE: the vulgate's chapter 13 opens with a verse
the critical edition omits -- Arjuna asking about "nature and spirit, the
field and the knower of the field, knowledge and what is to be known".
Besant prints it as 13.1, so her chapter 13 runs to 35 and every number is
one higher than the critical edition's. prep.py corrects her numbering
(asserting that her 13.2 transliterates to the CE's 13.1) and sets the
extra verse at the top of reference/012.txt as "13.0 -- VULGATE ONLY". ALEX:
include it or not? Recommendation: NOT in the body (it is not in the text
we translate, and the standard numbering every reader cites -- 13.1 "This
body is called the field" -- is the critical edition's), and say in the
editor's introduction that most printed Gitas have one more verse here.

THE COLOPHONS. The vulgate closes each chapter "Thus in the Upanishad of the
glorious Bhagavad Gita ... the chapter called The Yoga of ..."; the critical
edition does not print them, being later than the poem. They are dropped
from the body. They are, however, where the eighteen traditional chapter
NAMES come from, and the headings are English renderings of them (section 7).

THE CRIB (English): Annie Besant, The Bhagavad-Gita, 4th edition, G. A.
Natesan, Madras, 1922 -- Wikisource's proofread transcription, verse by
verse, with her footnotes, in reference/NNN.txt. She died in 1933; the
edition is public domain in the US (Wikisource: PD-old / PD-US). This is
NOT the 1905 Besant & Bhagavan Das word-by-word edition the brief hoped
for, which was not found as a clean text. Besant is literal enough to be a
crib and is clean and verse-aligned, which is what the method needs; she is
also a THEOSOPHIST, and her English is a costume (section 4).
In chapter 11, verses 15-49 (the hymn, in the long metre), Besant sets her
own BLANK VERSE, padded to the line. For that stretch reference/010_telang.txt
gives Telang's literal prose for the whole chapter.

TELANG (Sacred Books of the East 8, 1882; 2nd ed. 1898) was the brief's
first choice and is the most literal public-domain English. Both Archive.org
OCRs were fetched (_src/telang_1882_djvu.txt, _src/telang_1898_djvu.txt).
They are too damaged to be the per-verse crib: every diacritic is mangled
("Arguna", "Pazdavas", "Dhvvztardsh/ra"), footnotes are interleaved with the
text, the verse numbers survive only in running heads, and the chapter
headings for II-VIII, XII, XVII and XVIII are unrecognisable. Chapter 11 is
cut out by hand between asserted anchors (above); the rest is the
orchestrator's reference for cruxes, not given to agents.

======================================================================
2. WHAT THE BOOK IS
======================================================================

Eighteen chapters lifted out of the sixth book of the Mahabharata, the
world's longest poem. Two branches of one royal family -- the five sons of
Pandu (the Pandavas) and the hundred sons of the blind king Dhritarashtra
(the Kauravas, led by Duryodhana) -- have brought their armies to the plain
of Kurukshetra. The frame is double: the blind king asks his minister
Sanjaya, who has been given the power to see the distant battle, what is
happening; Sanjaya reports what he sees and hears. What he hears is a
conversation in a chariot between the two armies. Arjuna, the Pandavas'
greatest archer, sees his grandfather, his teacher, his cousins and his
friends lined up to be killed, puts down his bow and refuses to fight. His
charioteer Krishna -- his cousin and friend, and, as the poem reveals, God
-- answers him for seventeen chapters.

The argument, in order: grief rests on a mistake about what dies (2); you
cannot stop acting, so act without clinging to the result (2-3); action
offered as a sacrifice does not bind (3-4); renunciation means giving up
attachment, not deeds (5); the disciplined, meditating mind (6); God as the
source of everything, both higher and lower nature (7-9); God's glories,
listed (10); God SEEN, as everything that exists and everything that
destroys (11); devotion as the way open to anyone (12); the body as a field
and the self as its knower (13); the three strands of nature (14); the
world-tree and the Supreme Person (15); the divine and the demonic in
people (16); three kinds of faith, food, sacrifice, austerity and giving
(17); renunciation done right, duty by nature, and the last word: give up
everything and come to me (18). Arjuna picks up his bow.

======================================================================
3. FORM
======================================================================

PROSE, paragraphed. The metre was measured, not assumed (prep.py counts
syllables per half-verse): 645 verses are the 16-syllable sloka, the plain
narrative metre of the whole epic; 55 are the 22-syllable tristubh --
2.5-8, 2.20, 2.22, 2.29, 2.70, 8.9-11, 8.28, 9.20-21, 11.15-50 (the whole
hymn), 15.2-5, 15.15. The long metre marks heightened moments, but it is
not a different KIND of speech (the sung/spoken split of the drama shelf):
the same speakers use both, mid-speech. So it is carried by REGISTER, not
layout: the hymn of chapter 11 is the book's highest pitch and should read
that way. chapters/NNN.txt tags each long-metre verse "(long metre)" so the
translator knows. ALEX: the alternative is prose for the sloka and
tab-indented verse for the 55 tristubh (the sophocles pattern).
Recommendation: prose throughout -- the Gita is a dialogue whose reader
wants to follow an argument (the lucretius ruling), and a verse block
dropping into a speech mid-argument reads as a change of speaker it is not.

VERSE NUMBERS. The Gita is cited by verse ("Gita 2.47") more than almost
any book in the collection. The translation is written with a verse marker
-- {47} -- where each verse begins, in gita/drafts/NNN.txt; check.py proves
all 700 are there, once each, in order, and writes modern_chapters/NNN.txt
with the markers removed. This is the coverage check (a dropped catalogue
verse moves the word ratio by under one percent) and it keeps the citation
data. ALEX: ship without visible numbers (the lucretius/ovid precedent,
recommended for the reading page), or render them small in the margin,
which would need a renderer change in assemble.py and build_ebook.py; the
drafts make either possible without retranslating anything.

SPEAKERS. The critical edition marks 55 changes of speaker with "X said"
(uvaca). Each becomes its own paragraph, exactly "Krishna said:", "Arjuna
said:", "Sanjaya said:", "Dhritarashtra said:", in the critical edition's
places and nowhere else (check.py asserts sequence and position).
"bhagavan" is a title, "the Blessed One"; the tag uses the plain name a
fourteen-year-old can follow (ALEX may prefer "The Lord said:"). The vulgate
adds four tags the critical edition lacks, all in chapter 1 (Besant prints
"Arjuna said" at 1.21 and 1.28 and "Sanjaya said" at 1.24 and 1.47; checked
by script against her text): where the critical edition has none, the speech sits inside Sanjaya's
narration as reported speech in quotation marks ("... and Arjuna said to
Krishna, 'Bring my chariot out between the two armies ...'"). Arjuna's
whole lament at 1.28-46 is therefore Sanjaya quoting him, which is what
the Sanskrit says, and it makes chapter 1 the story it is.

======================================================================
4. STYLE, TONE AND VOICE
======================================================================

The Sanskrit is plain, clear and spare -- the sloka is the epic's working
metre, and the Gita's diction is simple beside the court poetry that came
later. Its difficulty is not the sentences but the ideas, the technical
words, and the density of address and epithet. A modern retelling should
therefore be EASY TO READ AND HARD TO FORGET: short clear sentences, a
teacher talking to a friend in a crisis.

Three voices.
- KRISHNA: a teacher with all the time in the world and none to waste;
  patient, sometimes stern ("Where did this weakness come from, at a moment
  like this?" 2.2), sometimes amused (he smiles before he starts, 2.10),
  and, from chapter 7 on, speaking as God in the first person -- "I am the
  taste in water" -- with complete calm. He repeats himself ON PURPOSE:
  repetition is how this book teaches. Keep every repetition.
- ARJUNA: a warrior, articulate, physically shaken (1.29-30: his limbs
  give way, his mouth is dry, his bow slips from his hand), sharp when
  confused ("You say one thing and then another," 3.2), awed and
  terrified in chapter 11, and apologetic for having treated God as a
  friend (11.41-44).
- SANJAYA: the reporter, a little breathless, and at the very end openly
  moved (18.74-78).

READABILITY IS THE DELIVERABLE (Alex on the Dogen drafts). Every sentence
one a bright fourteen-year-old could read aloud and follow, with every
clause of the Sanskrit present. Where the text leans on something its
hearers knew, tell it inside the sentence -- never a footnote: "Bhishma,
the old commander, grandfather to both sides"; "the conch Panchajanya,
made from the shell of a demon Krishna once killed" is TOO MUCH (not in
the verse); "his conch, Panchajanya" is enough. Fold in what the sentence
needs to be understood and nothing it does not.

WHOSE ARCHAISM IS IT? The Sanskrit has none: it uses the ordinary second
person, and Krishna and Arjuna are friends. "Thou", "thy", "the Blessed
Lord", "the SELF", "the Unmanifested", the capital "Reason" for buddhi and
reverential capitals on Krishna's pronouns ("Me", "My") are BESANT'S
costume -- Victorian scripture-English and Theosophical capitals -- and none
of it is in the Sanskrit (Devanagari has no capital letters). All of it
goes, and check.py sweeps for it with no exemption list. Lowercase "me",
"my", "you" for Krishna throughout; "God" capitalised only as the noun.

======================================================================
5. CHALLENGES
======================================================================

1. TECHNICAL VOCABULARY -- THE THREE OBSTRUCTION CLASSES AT ONCE. The Gita is
   a philosophical text and its crib is a philosopher's calque (the
   epictetus lesson: the archaism measure cannot see this). Worse, two of
   its words are English words that now mean something else (FALSE FRIENDS):
   - "karma" in the Gita means ACTION, deeds, work -- the thing you do. In
     modern English it means cosmic payback. Never write "karma".
   - "yoga" in the Gita is a discipline of mind and action, the yoking of
     yourself to a purpose; modern English means a class with mats. Keep
     the word -- the book's every chapter is named "the yoga of ..." and the
     word is its subject -- but gloss it in the sentence at its first
     working use (2.39) and keep it from meaning exercise.
   Locked list: section 6 and running_notes.txt.

2. EPITHETS AND VOCATIVES. Arjuna is addressed by a dozen names (Partha,
   Kaunteya, Bharata, Dhananjaya, Gudakesha, Parantapa, "mighty-armed",
   "best of the Kurus" ...), Krishna by a dozen more (Keshava, Madhava,
   Janardana, Madhusudana, Govinda, Hrishikesha, Achyuta, Varshneya ...).
   Many fill out the metre. Carried bare they are the single biggest
   obstacle to a new reader, who cannot tell they are all one person.
   RULE: resolve every one to "Arjuna" / "Krishna", or to plain English
   where the meaning is doing work -- "Madhusudana", "slayer of the demon
   Madhu", when Arjuna asks the killer of demons how he can kill his own
   teachers (2.4); "you who make your enemies tremble" when Krishna is
   shaming a warrior out of weakness (2.3). Not every vocative needs a
   name in English, but keep the address so the dialogue still sounds
   like two people talking. Dhritarashtra is "my king".

3. CATALOGUES. Chapter 1's roll-call of warriors and conches, chapter 10's
   glories ("of the Adityas I am Vishnu; of lights, the shining sun"),
   chapter 11's gods and the warriors going into the mouths. NEVER
   SUMMARISE A LIST: every name stays (check.py checks the names against
   the Sanskrit). Make each name intelligible in a few words where needed
   ("Bhrigu, greatest of the ancient seers"; "Ucchaihshravas, the horse
   that rose from the ocean when the gods churned it for the nectar of
   immortality" -- that one does need its story).

4. ENUMERATIONS AND DEFINITIONS (8.1-4, 13.5-6, 18.13-15): Arjuna asks
   what the technical terms MEAN (8.1: "What is Brahman? What is the
   adhyatma? What is action?"). Where the question is about the word,
   keep the word and give its meaning in the answer; elsewhere translate.

5. DOUBLE MEANINGS THE ARGUMENT USES. 2.47's "fruit" is both result and
   reward -- keep "fruit", it is plain English and the metaphor runs all
   book long. Kurukshetra is "the field of the Kurus" and in 1.1 "the field
   of dharma": the book opens on a pun that the whole poem unpacks; fold
   both in. kala (11.32) is both "time" and "death" (ALEX, section 8).

9. SENSITIVE CONTENT -- A SACRED TEXT, AND A WAR
======================================================================

This is scripture for over a billion people, and its setting is a god
urging a man to kill his relatives in battle. Scholarly modernization of a
canonical text: a measured register; KRISHNA'S ARGUMENT AS HIS ARGUMENT,
complete and in his terms; ARJUNA'S OBJECTIONS AS STRONG AS HE MAKES THEM;
no editorialising, no softening, no sensationalising, and NO NOTE (the
Verne rule: a note is the wink the rule forbids). Neither devotional nor
skeptical -- the translator is a scholar who has loved this book all his
life and does not need to defend it.
Passages to render flat and entire:
- The argument to fight: 2.31-38 (a warrior's duty; a just war is a door
  to heaven; dishonour is worse than death; "kill and win the earth, or die
  and win heaven"); 3.30; 11.32-34 ("they are already slain by me -- be
  only the instrument"); 18.59-60.
- 2.19-21: the self "does not kill and is not killed" -- the metaphysics
  as metaphysics. It has been quoted to justify violence; that is not the
  translator's business. Render it exactly.
- 1.36-44: Arjuna's case -- killing the family destroys its sacred laws;
  lawlessness corrupts the women of the family; the classes mix; the
  ancestors fall, deprived of their offerings of rice and water. Fold the
  ancestor rites into the sentence. It is Arjuna's argument and he means
  it; keep "corrupted" and keep the logic chain exactly.
- 4.13 and 18.41-48: the four classes created by God according to qualities
  and work, the duties of each, and "better your own duty done imperfectly". Say
  "class", not "caste"; no hedging.
- 9.32: "even those born of sinful wombs -- women, vaishyas, and shudras --
  reach the highest goal". The verse's point is INCLUSION and its premise
  is a hierarchy; the syntax lets the three be read as examples of "those
  born of sinful wombs" or as further categories, and commentators divide.
  Keep the Sanskrit's order and its ambiguity; do not resolve it either
  way. ALEX may want to rule on this one in advance.
- 16.6-20: the demonic people, thrown "again and again into demonic wombs".
- 18.67: do not tell this to anyone without discipline or devotion.
- Krishna's claim to be God, the source and end of all: stated as he
  states it. No "as he believed", no "the poem claims".

10. RATIO
======================================================================

8,474 Sanskrit words in the verse lines -- but a Sanskrit "word" here is a
sandhi-joined compound, so the English runs far above the Latin's 1.7.
Besant's literal English is 16,677 words, 1.97 overall and 1.75-2.32 per
chapter; chapter 1, with its catalogue of names, is the lowest. A readable
retelling resolves epithets (shorter), folds in allusions and unpacks
compounds (longer), and should land ABOVE the crib: expect about 2.3-2.9,
i.e. ~19,000-25,000 English words for the whole book. check.py's band
(English words, speaker tags excluded, per Sanskrit verse word) is set
PROVISIONALLY at 1.6-3.6 (the floor allows for chapter 1, a catalogue) and
is re-derived from file 000 plus one argumentative chapter (the dogen rule:
the predicted band for kanbun was wrong by half). verify.py counts the
chapter file whole (headings, tags and verse numbers included; 9,502
"words"), so its ratio runs about 0.89x check.py's: provisional
`python3 verify.py gita --min-ratio 1.6 --max-ratio 3.3`, re-derived with
the other.

11. PRECEDENTS USED
======================================================================

dogen/ and hojoki/ (readability correction; allusions folded in; record
where you looked); lucretius/ and ovid/ (verse as prose; per-file crib;
pins from the finished text); sophocles/euripides (form derived from the
source and checked; speaker parity as THE decisive check; a short line the
renderer would set as a heading); epictetus/ (technical calque; checks that
exit nonzero and mirror the renderer); nights/ and augustine/ (whose
archaism -- the translator's costume goes).
