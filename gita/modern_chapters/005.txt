Chapter 6: The Way of Meditation

Krishna said:

The person who does the work he has to do without leaning on the
fruit of action is a renouncer and a yogi. It is not the one who has
stopped lighting the sacred fire, or the one who has given up the rites.
What people call renunciation, Arjuna, know that it is yoga. No one
becomes a yogi without renouncing his intentions.

For a sage who wants to climb up to yoga, action is said to be the
means. For that same sage, once he has climbed up to yoga, calm is said
to be the means. When a person is no longer attached to the things
of the senses or to his actions, and has renounced every intention, then
he is said to have climbed up to yoga.

Lift yourself up by yourself. Don't let yourself sink. For you are
your own friend, and you are your own enemy. You are a friend to
yourself if you have conquered yourself by yourself. But if you have not
taken hold of yourself, your own self will turn against you like an
enemy.

When a person has conquered himself and is at peace, his supreme
self stays collected in cold and heat, in pleasure and pain, in honor
and in disgrace. The yogi who is satisfied with knowledge and
insight, who stands unchanging, who has conquered his senses, to whom a
lump of earth, a stone and a piece of gold are all the same, is called
disciplined. And the best of all is the one whose understanding is
the same toward those who wish him well, his friends and his enemies,
the indifferent and those who stand neutral between two sides, those he
cannot stand and his own relatives, good people and wicked ones alike.

A yogi should always be disciplining himself, staying somewhere
secluded, alone, his thoughts and his self under control, hoping for
nothing and owning nothing. In a clean place he should set up a
firm seat for himself, not too high and not too low, made of the sacred
kusha grass with a deerskin over it and a cloth over that. Sitting
on that seat, he should bring his mind to a single point, keep his
thoughts and the work of his senses under control, and practice yoga to
make himself pure.

He should hold his body, head and neck straight and still, and sit
steady, gazing at the tip of his own nose and not looking around in any
direction. Calm in himself, free of fear, firm in his vow of
chastity, like a young student of the Vedas, his mind under control, he
should sit disciplined, his thoughts on me, with me as his highest goal.
By always disciplining himself like this, the yogi whose mind is
under control reaches the peace that rests in me, the peace whose
highest point is nirvana.

But yoga is not for the person who eats too much, Arjuna, or for
the one who eats nothing at all, or for the one who sleeps too much, or
for the one who stays awake too long. Yoga destroys all suffering
for the person who is disciplined in eating and in play, disciplined in
the way he goes about his work, disciplined in sleeping and waking.

When his thinking is held in check and rests in the self alone, and
he no longer craves anything he might desire, then he is called
disciplined. A lamp in a place where no wind blows does not
flicker: that is the comparison people use for the yogi whose thinking
is held in check, as he practices the yoga of the self.

There is a state in which the mind, held still by the practice of
yoga, comes to rest, and in which a person sees the self by the self and
is content in the self. In it he knows a happiness without end,
which understanding can grasp and the senses cannot reach, and once he
stands in it he never wanders from the truth. Once he has it, he
thinks there is no gain greater than this, and standing in it he is not
shaken even by the heaviest sorrow. Know that this state is called
yoga: the unyoking of the yoke of pain. This yoga must be practiced with
determination, and with a heart that does not lose heart.

He should give up, completely and without exception, all the
desires that intention gives birth to, and use the mind itself to rein
in the whole crowd of senses on every side. Little by little he
should come to rest, with his understanding held firm by resolve. He
should settle his mind in the self and think of nothing at all.
Wherever the restless, unsteady mind wanders off, he should rein it
in from there and bring it back under the control of the self.

For the highest happiness comes to the yogi whose mind is at peace,
whose passion is stilled, who has become Brahman and is free from stain.
By always disciplining himself like this, the yogi, his stains
gone, easily enjoys the endless happiness of the touch of Brahman.

The person whose self is disciplined by yoga sees the self in all
beings and all beings in the self. He sees the same everywhere.
Whoever sees me everywhere and sees everything in me: I am never
lost to him, and he is never lost to me. Whoever stands firm in
oneness and loves me as the one who lives in all beings, that yogi lives
in me, however he lives. The one who measures everything by
himself, Arjuna, and sees the same in everything, whether it is pleasure
or pain, is thought to be the highest yogi.

Arjuna said:

Krishna, this yoga you have described as evenness of mind — I don't
see how it can ever stand steady, because the mind is so restless.
The mind is restless, Krishna, violent, strong and stubborn. I
think holding it still is as hard as holding the wind.

Krishna said:

No doubt about it, Arjuna, the mind is restless and hard to hold
still. But it can be held, by practice and by detachment. My view
is that yoga is hard to reach for a person who has not mastered himself.
But a person who has mastered himself and keeps working at it can reach
it, if he goes about it the right way.

Arjuna said:

What about someone who has faith but does not master himself, whose
mind wanders away from yoga, so that he never reaches perfection in
yoga? What becomes of him, Krishna? Fallen from both, with nothing
to stand on, lost on the path to Brahman, isn't he destroyed, like a
cloud torn apart in the sky? You must cut through this doubt of
mine completely, Krishna. There is no one but you who can cut through
it.

Krishna said:

Arjuna, there is no destruction for him, not in this world and not
in the next. No one who does good ever comes to a bad end, my friend.
The one who has fallen away from yoga goes to the worlds of those
who have done good, lives there for countless years, and then is born
again into the house of pure and prosperous people. Or else he is
born into a family of wise yogis, though a birth like that is even
harder to come by in this world. There he gets back the
understanding he had in his earlier body, and from there he strives
again, harder than before, for perfection, Arjuna.

The practice he did before carries him along, even without his
willing it. Even someone who only wants to know about yoga goes beyond
Brahman as it is set down in words, in the Vedas. But the yogi who
strives with all his effort, purified of his faults and perfected over
many births, then reaches the highest goal.

The yogi is greater than the ascetics. He is thought greater even
than the learned, and the yogi is greater than those who perform the
rites. So be a yogi, Arjuna. And of all yogis, the one who loves me
with faith, whose inmost self has gone to me — I count him the most
disciplined of all.
