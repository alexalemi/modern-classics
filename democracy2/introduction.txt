This is the complete Democracy in America, both volumes, translated for this collection directly from Alexis de Tocqueville's French. The collection also holds a second edition of the same work, made instead from Henry Reeve's Victorian English version, which appears as Democracy in America. Neither is an abridgement of the other and neither is volume one or volume two: they are two routes to the same book.

Tocqueville arrived in the United States in May 1831 with his friend Gustave de Beaumont. He was twenty-five, a junior magistrate at Versailles, and his official errand was the American prison system, on which the two men duly reported. They also spent about nine months travelling — New England, the Michigan frontier, Lower Canada, down the Ohio and the Mississippi to New Orleans, back through the South to Washington — asking everyone they met how the country ran itself.

America was not really his subject. His family had been wrecked by the French Revolution: his parents were jailed during the Terror and his great-grandfather was guillotined. He had concluded that the levelling of ranks which caused all that was still going on, that nothing would stop it, and that Europe was going to have it whether it liked the prospect or not. The United States was the one place where it had already arrived and could be examined. The book is therefore addressed to Frenchmen about France at least as much as it is about America.

The volume of 1835 deals with institutions — the township, the jury, the federal constitution, the courts, the press, the parties, and the tyranny of the majority, which for him is social pressure rather than law. The volume of 1840 asks a harder question: what equality does to the inside of a person, to their philosophy, religion, poetry, ambitions, families and manners. It ends with the passage everyone quotes, on a mild and orderly despotism that would leave people perfectly free except in the one matter of governing themselves.

Two of his words mislead. Democracy means equality of social conditions, not a voting system. Manners means the settled moral habits of a people, not etiquette.

Working from the French matters more here than it usually would. Reeve was a contemporary and knew Tocqueville, but his English is formal in a way the French is not, and Tocqueville wrote quickly, personally and in the first person, dropping into epigram whenever he had one. That voice is what this version is for. Tocqueville's own preface to the tenth French edition is included.
