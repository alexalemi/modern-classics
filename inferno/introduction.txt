Dante Alighieri was born in Florence in 1265 and thrown out of it in 1302. He had been away in Rome on an embassy when his faction lost the city; the new government fined him, exiled him, and ruled that if he ever came back he would be burned alive. He never came back. He wrote this poem in other men's houses in other men's towns, and died at Ravenna in 1321 without seeing Florence again.

The Inferno is the first of its three parts. A man of thirty-five, lost in a wood, is found by the ghost of Virgil, who has been sent to lead him down through Hell and out the other side. It is set in Holy Week of the year 1300, two years before the exile, which lets the dead keep telling Dante what is about to happen to him. He wrote it in Tuscan Italian instead of Latin, which was not what a serious poem was written in, and that decision is a large part of why Italians today speak Italian. He called the whole thing a Comedy. Divine was added by admirers later.

What makes it unlike other visions of the afterlife is that it is full of people the author knew or could have met. Popes, neighbours, bankers, his own former teacher, the man who betrayed the city — all named, all still arguing about Florentine politics, all certain they were in the right. Francesca explains how she and her husband's brother were caught, and blames the book they were reading. Ulysses describes sailing out past Gibraltar. Ugolino describes eight days locked in a tower with his sons and then stops.

The tone changes as the funnel narrows, and it is worth knowing that in advance. The upper circles are sad and courteous. The middle is slapstick — three cantos of demons who cheat each other and fall in the pitch, ending on a joke about a trumpet. The bottom is flat, cold and very quiet. Readers who expect one solemn register the whole way down will think something has gone wrong; nothing has.

This is a translation from the Italian, complete, keeping the shape Dante built: thirty-four cantos, 4,720 lines, three-line stanzas in the same number and order. The rhyme is not attempted, because his interlocking triple rhyme cannot be carried into English without padding, and padding is the one thing a poem this compressed cannot take. The Inferno is one third of one poem, and the other two thirds, the Purgatorio and the Paradiso, are in this collection as separate volumes. The last word of each of them is stars.
