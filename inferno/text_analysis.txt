TEXT ANALYSIS — Dante's Inferno
Read this before translating. The consistency ledger is running_notes.txt.


WHAT THIS EDITION IS
---------------------------------------------------------------------
From the Italian (Gutenberg #997, the Petrocchi text), with Longfellow's
1867 English as a per-canto crib under reference/ — the ovid/ and
de-officiis/ pattern. Longfellow is line-for-line faithful, which is
what makes him a good crib and a bad read: he keeps Italian word order
in English and scores 28.7 archaisms per 1,000 words, second only to
Pusey's Augustine in this project's screening pass. USE HIM TO CHECK A
LINE, NEVER TO BORROW A PHRASE. His endnotes are in reference/ too and
are drawn on only where a modern reader genuinely cannot follow.


THE FORM, AND IT IS THE WHOLE BOOK
---------------------------------------------------------------------
Terza rima — aba bcb cdc — cannot be reproduced in English without
padding, because English has a fraction of Italian's rhyming
vocabulary. Padding a poem famous for compression is the one thing
that must not happen. So:

  - VERSE STAYS VERSE. Every line is its own line, tab-indented.
    Dissolving a tercet into prose is this book's silent summarisation.
  - TERCETS STAY TERCETS, same number, same order, three lines each
    (and one line at the end of every canto, which is Dante's form).
    check.py compares the shape exactly against chapters/.
  - THE LINE COUNT PER CANTO IS EXACT and is asserted in prep.py
    against a written-out table, because it is a fact about the poem
    rather than about any translation of it.
  - NO RHYME, and no metre imposed on the English. What survives is
    the tercet's shape and its forward lean.
  - ENJAMBMENT ACROSS A TERCET BOUNDARY IS DANTE'S and is kept where he
    has it; do not tidy a sentence back inside its tercet.
  - AIM AT ONE LINE OF ENGLISH PER LINE OF ITALIAN in sense, not in
    syllables. A rendering that needs a line and a half has usually
    explained something Dante left compressed.


VOICE
---------------------------------------------------------------------
Dante is CONCRETE, FAST AND PHYSICAL. He is not solemn; that is the
translations. He notices weather, footing, smells, the exact way a
body moves. The famous lines are famous because they are plain: "e
quindi uscimmo a riveder le stelle" is "and from there we came out to
see the stars again", and no ornament improves it. THE hume RULE
GOVERNS: if a line is already clear, leave it alone.

He is also, constantly, IN THE SCENE — frightened, curious, faint,
weeping, ashamed, occasionally spiteful. Keep the pilgrim's nerves.
Virgil is patient, sometimes sharp, and once or twice genuinely angry.

REGISTER PER CIRCLE. The poem changes voice as it descends and the
translation must follow:
  - the upper circles are elegiac (Francesca, Brunetto, Ulysses);
  - the middle is grotesque and comic (the Malebranche cantos, XXI-XXIII,
    are slapstick with real cruelty in them, and end with the devils
    falling into their own pitch);
  - the lowest circles go flat, cold and quiet (Ugolino, Cocytus).
Do not flatten these together into one "epic" tone.

THE OBSCENE AND THE CRUEL STAY. Canto XXI ends with a devil making a
trumpet of his backside, and the line is exactly that. The thieves'
transformations in XXIV-XXV are anatomical and are meant to disgust.
Ugolino's last line is meant to be unresolvable. Render all of it
plainly, in full, and let it do its own work.


NAMES AND REFERENCES
---------------------------------------------------------------------
  - FAMILIAR ENGLISH FORMS for names that have them: Virgil, Ulysses,
    Charon, Minos, Beatrice, Florence, Lucifer. Italian forms stay for
    Italians: Farinata, Cavalcante, Ciacco, Vanni Fucci, Ugolino.
  - DANTE'S PERIPHRASIS IS OFTEN THE POINT and is kept where the poem
    withholds a name deliberately ("that one who made the great
    refusal"). Where it is merely learned shorthand for a name a
    thirteenth-century reader knew, resolve it.
  - NO FOOTNOTES ON THE PAGE. The Inferno is full of Florentine
    politics, and annotating it would double the book and turn a poem
    into an apparatus. Where a reference is genuinely unfollowable,
    let the line carry the smallest amount of identification it can
    ("Frederick, the Emperor") rather than adding a note. Any
    exception is logged in running_notes.txt.
  - LATIN STAYS LATIN where Dante writes Latin and means it to be
    heard as Latin ("Vexilla regis prodeunt inferni", the gate
    inscription's rhythm, "Papè Satàn, papè Satàn aleppe").


THE VERNE RULE, AND THIS BOOK NEEDS IT MORE THAN MOST
---------------------------------------------------------------------
The Inferno is a Christian poem that damns real people by name, most
of them the author's political enemies, and puts a Pope head-down in a
hole. It also damns the virtuous pagans to Limbo for not being
baptised, consigns Muhammad to the ninth pit of the eighth circle in
one of the most gruesome passages in European literature, and has
Virgil explain that the geography of Hell was rearranged by the
Crucifixion.

ALL OF IT STANDS, unhedged, with no note anywhere in the volume. These
are the author's claims and they are the subject of the book, not
incidental to it. Softening the Muhammad canto would replace Dante's
poem with one he did not write; a modern editorial note would be the
wink the rule forbids. Translate it in full, with gravity, and do not
sensationalise it either.

The same goes for the suicides of canto XIII, the sodomites of XV-XVI
(where Dante's tenderness towards Brunetto Latini is the most
interesting thing in the canto and must not be tidied), and the
usurers. Where a dated WORD is doing no work, it is modernised
silently, as everywhere in this project; where the dated CLAIM is the
point, it stays.


CONSISTENT VOCABULARY
---------------------------------------------------------------------
  Inferno / Hell            "Hell" in the body; "Inferno" is the title.
  duca, maestro, segnore    "guide", "master", "my lord" — Dante varies
                            them deliberately and so does this edition.
  ombra                     "shade" (not "ghost", not "spirit").
  peccato / colpa           "sin" / "fault".
  malebolge                 "Malebolge", kept, glossed once as "the evil
                            ditches" when the name is introduced.
  contrapasso               "counter-suffering", glossed once in XXVIII.
  gentile, cortese          "noble", "courteous" — thirteenth-century
                            social vocabulary, not compliments.
  pietà / pietoso           watch this one: it swings between "pity"
                            and "piety" and Dante uses the ambiguity.
