Jules Verne published Around the World in Eighty Days in 1872, and set it in that same year. Phileas Fogg, an English gentleman who has apparently never done anything, plays whist at the Reform Club every afternoon, dines there to the second, and walks home at midnight. An argument about a newspaper article ends with him betting four of his fellow members twenty thousand pounds — half of everything he has — that he can go round the world and be back in the same room eighty days later. He leaves that night with a valet he hired at half past eleven that morning.

The wager was topical arithmetic, not fantasy, and that is most of what made the book exciting in 1872. Three things had just been finished: the Suez Canal in 1869, the American transcontinental railroad in the same year, and the rail link across India. Between them they had cut the world down to a size where the trip could be planned on published timetables. Verne worked it out that way, and the plot is a clockwork of departures, connections and delays. Every figure in it is meant to be checked, and the figures are kept exactly here. So is the clock: what actually threatens Fogg is never distance but a connection missed by a few minutes.

It is a comedy, which its reputation as a boys' adventure story tends to hide. Fogg's servant Passepartout — the name means a key that opens everything — is a former circus gymnast and fireman who took the job because he wanted a quiet life, and he is the feeling in the book. Fix, the Scotland Yard detective who follows them from Suez onward, has decided that Fogg is the man who robbed the Bank of England of fifty-five thousand pounds, and every reasonable step he takes to stop him makes matters worse. Verne's chapter titles are jokes in their own right, and they are translated here rather than replaced.

The Victorian English translations of Verne were abridged and clumsy, and lost most of the wit along with a good deal of the geography. This one is made from Verne's French and is complete, as are the two other Verne novels in this collection. A warning about the ending: the last chapters turn on a point of fact about travel that Fogg has overlooked, and nothing here will say what it is. The book also contains a rescue from a forced funeral pyre in India, an attack on a train crossing the American plains, and a crossing of the Atlantic conducted in a way no shipowner would forgive. All of it is as Verne wrote it.
