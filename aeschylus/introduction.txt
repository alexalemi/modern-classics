Aeschylus is the oldest playwright whose work survives. He was born near Athens around 525 BC, fought the Persians at Marathon, and died in Sicily in 456. He is credited with something like eighty plays. Seven came through whole, and they are in this book, complete. Three of them are the Oresteia of 458 BC, the only complete trilogy left from the Greek theatre: a king comes home from Troy and his wife kills him in the bath, their son comes home and kills her, and the goddesses who hunt him for it are argued to a standstill in the first courtroom in literature. Prometheus Bound, the fourth play here, has been doubted since antiquity and may not be his; it is printed with the rest because the manuscripts carry it under his name.

These plays were written for a competition. Once a year, at the festival of Dionysus, three playwrights each put on a day of plays in an open-air theatre below the Acropolis, in front of some thousands of people, and judges awarded a prize. Every part was played by a man in a mask, and there were only two or three speaking actors, so one man took several roles. The rest of the cast was a chorus of twelve or fifteen who sang and danced. Aeschylus is said to have been the one who added the second actor, which means he was working close to the point where the form was still mostly singing, and it shows: more than half of what is here is chorus. The Agamemnon runs two hundred lines of it before anyone speaks.

That difference is on the page. The spoken scenes are set as prose and the sung parts as verse, indented, unrhymed, because in the Greek the metre says which is which and a reader ought to be able to see it. The rhyme that Victorian translators added has gone; none of it was ever there. This is one of three volumes here, with Sophocles and Euripides, and between them they hold every Greek tragedy that survives.

The Persians, from 472 BC, is the only surviving tragedy about a real event. It is about the sea battle at Salamis eight years earlier, which the author had lived through, and it is told entirely from the losing side, in the Persian court, waiting for news. The images are the argument in these plays, and the same English word is used for each of them every time it recurs: the net, the yoke, the lion cub raised in the house, the blood that soaks into the ground and will not come up. Nothing is softened. Iphigenia is gagged so she cannot curse her father. Apollo argues in court that a mother is not a parent, and he wins.
