The Libation Bearers
(Part 2 of 2)

Chorus.
	The earth breeds many things
	that are terrible, griefs that bring terror,
	and the arms of the sea are crowded
	with monsters that hate us.
	Between the sky and the ground
	lights hang high in the air and come flashing down,
	and every creature with wings, everything that walks,
	could tell you about the anger of the whirlwind,
	the storm that comes roaring.

Chorus.
	But the reckless pride of a man,
	who could put words to that?
	Or to the loves that stop at nothing
	in the hard hearts of women,
	loves that live side by side with the ruin of mortals?
	A loveless love, the kind that masters the female,
	wins its twisted victory
	over the yoked pairs who share one bed,
	beasts and human beings alike.

Chorus.
	Let anyone whose mind is not all feathers
	know this, and learn from it:
	the plan that Althaea lit with fire,
	Thestius's wretched daughter,
	the killer of her own child.
	She burned the red log, the one
	that was the same age as her son,
	that had shared his life from the day
	he came out of his mother crying,
	and would have measured him out, step for step,
	to the day the Fates had fixed.

Chorus.
	And there is another to hate in the old stories,
	a girl with blood on her hands, Scylla,
	who destroyed the man she should have loved
	because his enemies asked it,
	won over by the gift of Minos,
	a Cretan necklace worked in gold.
	While Nisus, her father, breathed in his sleep
	suspecting nothing, she cut away
	the one hair that kept him from dying,
	the bitch-hearted girl.
	And Hermes, who leads the dead, came for him.

Chorus.
	And since I have called to mind
	pains that nothing could soften,
	this is the moment for a marriage no one could love,
	a thing this house prays to be rid of,
	and the schemes a woman's mind laid
	against her husband in his armour,
	a husband even his enemies had reason to honour.
	I honour the hearth that no fire of passion heats,
	and in a woman a spear-point that dares nothing.

Chorus.
	Of all crimes the one from Lemnos comes first
	in the telling, the women who killed every man on the island.
	People still groan over it as a thing to spit at,
	and whenever a new horror comes
	they measure it against Lemnos.
	Because of a deed the gods loathed
	a whole race was dishonoured and is gone.
	No one respects what the gods hate.
	Which of these stories have I gathered without good reason?

Chorus.
	The sword is near the lungs.
	Sharp, bitter, it drives clean through,
	because Justice sends it.
	For what is not right
	is lying on the ground, kicked and trampled,
	when a man has stepped over the whole majesty of Zeus
	against everything that is lawful.

Chorus.
	The anvil of Justice is planted firm.
	Fate, who forges swords, is hammering the bronze ahead of time,
	and the Fury, famous in story, brooding in the deep,
	is bringing a child into the house
	to pay off, at last, the filth of old blood.

(Orestes and Pylades come to the palace door, dressed as travellers.
Orestes knocks.)

Orestes.
Boy! Boy! Do you hear me knocking at the courtyard door? Who is inside?
Boy! Boy, I say it again, is anyone at home? This is the third time I
have called for someone to come out of the house, if Aegisthus keeps a
house that welcomes strangers.

Servant.
All right, all right, I hear you. Where is the stranger from? What
country?

Orestes.
Announce me to the masters of the house. It is to them I have come, and
I bring news. And hurry, because the dark chariot of night is coming on
fast, and it is time for travellers to drop anchor in a house that takes
in guests. Let someone with authority come out, the woman in charge of
the place, though a man would be more fitting. Between men there is no
embarrassment to blur the words. A man speaks to a man with confidence
and shows plainly what he means.

(The Servant goes in. Clytemnestra comes to the door with a slave woman
behind her.)

Clytemnestra.
Strangers, tell me what you need. We have here everything a house like
this should have: hot baths, beds to charm away the tiredness of the
road, and the presence of honest faces. But if there is some other
business that needs more careful counsel, that is men's work, and we
will share it with them.

Orestes.
I am a stranger, a man of Daulis in Phocis. I was on the road to Argos,
carrying my own pack on my own back, and just as I unyoked my feet and
stopped here, a man fell in with me, a stranger to me as I was to him.
He asked where I was going and told me his own road. He was Strophius
the Phocian, I learned his name as we talked, and he said to me:
"Stranger, since you are going to Argos anyway, remember this carefully
and tell his parents that Orestes is dead. Do not, whatever happens, let
it slip your mind. And whether his people decide to bring him home or to
bury him where he died, a foreigner, a guest for ever, carry their
instructions back to me." For now the sides of a bronze urn hold the
ashes of a man who has been well mourned. That is what I heard, and that
is what I have told you. Whether I happen to be speaking to the people
whose business this is, his own family, I do not know. But his parent
ought to know the truth.

Clytemnestra.
Oh, no. Your words say we are sacked to the top of the walls. Curse on
this house, so hard to wrestle down, how far you see! Even what was laid
well out of your reach you bring down with a well-aimed arrow from a
long way off, and you strip me bare of the people I love, wretched woman
that I am. And now Orestes. He was being sensible, keeping his foot out
of the deadly mud. And now the one hope of a cure for this house, for
its fine wild revelling, write him down as a deserter.

Orestes.
For my part, with hosts as fortunate as you, I would have liked to make
myself known and be welcomed on account of good news. Where is there
more goodwill than between guest and host? But to my mind it would have
been an offence against the gods not to carry out a charge like this for
people I was bound to, when I had given my word and been taken in as a
guest.

Clytemnestra.
You will get nothing less than you deserve, and be no less a friend to
this house for it. Someone else would have come and brought this news
just the same. But it is the hour when strangers who have been on the
road all day on a long journey should get what is proper.

(She turns to the slave woman.)

Take him to the men's guest rooms, him and these attendants of his and
his fellow traveller, and let them be looked after there as this house
does things. I order you to see to it, and you will answer for it.
Meanwhile we will share this with the master of the house, and, since we
are not short of friends, we will take counsel together about what has
happened.

(All go into the house. The Chorus is left alone.)

Chorus.
	Well then, women of the house, slaves and friends,
	when will we show the strength
	that is in our mouths, for Orestes?
	Sacred earth, and sacred lip of the mound
	that lies now on the body of the king,
	the commander of the fleet,
	hear us now, help us now.
	Now is the moment for Persuasion, the cunning one,
	to go down into the fight beside him,
	and for Hermes of the underworld, Hermes of the night,
	to stand over this contest
	of the killing sword.

(The Nurse comes out of the house, crying.)

Chorus.
It looks as if the stranger is working some harm. There is Orestes'
nurse coming out in tears. Cilissa, where are you going, out through the
palace gates? And grief walking with you, a companion nobody hired?

Nurse.
The mistress orders me to call Aegisthus for the strangers, as fast as I
can, so that he can come and hear this fresh news more clearly, man from
man. In front of the servants she put on a gloomy face, but behind her
eyes she was hiding her laughter at how well things have turned out for
her. For this house it is disaster from top to bottom, and the strangers
told it plainly. And he, when he hears the story, he will be glad in his
heart all right. Oh, I am wretched. The old griefs, all mixed together,
the ones so hard to bear that happened in this house of Atreus, they
hurt my heart inside my chest badly enough. But I never had to hold up
under a blow like this. The other troubles I bailed out patiently, like
water from a boat. But my Orestes, my darling, the thing I wore my soul
out on, the one I took from his mother's arms and brought up. And the
shrill orders that had me walking the floor all night, all the drudgery
that got me nothing, and I put up with it. A thing with no sense in it
you have to feed like an animal, how else? By guessing what it wants. A
baby still in its nappies cannot tell you anything, whether it is
hungry, or thirsty, or needs to pee. A baby's little belly answers to
nobody. I had to be a prophet for all that, and many a time, I think, I
got it wrong, and then I was the one washing the baby's linen. Laundress
and nurse, it was the same job. I did both those trades with my two
hands, and that is how I took Orestes from his father to raise him. And
now, wretched woman, they tell me he is dead. And I am on my way to the
man who has done this house so much harm, and he will be glad enough to
hear it.

Chorus.
Then how did she tell him to come? With what, in what company?

Nurse.
How do you mean, how? Say it again so I understand you better.

Chorus.
With his bodyguard, or by himself?

Nurse.
She says to bring his spearmen with him.

Chorus.
Then don't give that message to the master we all loathe. Tell him to
come by himself, so he can hear it without being afraid, and tell him as
fast as you can, with a glad heart. In a messenger's mouth a crooked
message gets straightened out.

Nurse.
What? Are you pleased about the news that has just come?

Chorus.
Why not, if Zeus is ever going to turn the wind of our troubles?

Nurse.
And how? Orestes, the hope of the house, is gone.

Chorus.
Not yet. It would be a bad prophet who read it that way.

Nurse.
What are you saying? Do you know something beyond what has been told?

Chorus.
Go and give the message. Do what you were sent to do. The gods take care
of what the gods care about.

Nurse.
Well, I will go, and do as you say. And may it all turn out for the
best, with the gods' help.

(The Nurse goes out.)

Chorus.
	Now, Zeus, father of the Olympian gods,
	at my asking
	grant that the fortunes of this house be settled, rightly,
	for those who long to see good order.
	Every word I have cried out is for justice.
	Zeus, may you keep it safe.

Chorus.
	Ah, ah. Set the man inside the house
	in front of his enemies, Zeus,
	because if you raise him up high
	he will gladly pay you back
	twice over, and three times.

Chorus.
	Know that the orphaned colt of a man you loved
	is yoked to a chariot of troubles.
	Set a measure to his running,
	so that we can see him keep his stride
	safe over the ground,
	reaching, at full gallop, to the finish.

Chorus.
	And you gods inside the house,
	who keep the inner room that is glad with its wealth,
	hear us, you who feel with us.
	Come, wash out the blood of what was done long ago
	with fresh justice.
	Let old Murder breed no more children in this house.

Chorus.
	And you who live in the great cavern,
	the one so finely built, Apollo,
	grant that this man's house lifts up its eyes again,
	and looks with loving eyes on the bright light of freedom
	out from under its dark veil.

Chorus.
	And may Maia's son, Hermes, take a hand in it, rightly,
	since no one carries an action on a fairer wind
	when he wants to.
	Many hidden things he brings to light at need,
	and with a word no eye can follow
	he brings night and darkness over people's eyes,
	and by day he is no easier to see.

Chorus.
	And then at last, for the house set free,
	we will sing the famous song,
	the song women sing when the wind sits fair,
	not the shrill, high song of mourning:
	the ship sails well.
	This is my gain, mine, and it grows,
	and ruin stands far off from the people I love.

Chorus.
	And you, take heart when the moment for action comes.
	When she cries out to you "Son,"
	shout back "Father,"
	and carry through the ruin that no one can blame.

Chorus.
	Hold up the heart of Perseus in your chest,
	and for the ones you love under the earth
	and the ones above it,
	do this favour to their bitter anger:
	set bloody ruin inside the house,
	and wipe out the man who is guilty of the murder.

(Aegisthus comes in, alone.)

Aegisthus.
I have come, not uninvited but sent for. I hear there is fresh news,
that some strangers have arrived and are telling a story that is
anything but welcome: Orestes is dead. To take this on top of everything
else would be a load dripping with terror for a house that is still raw
and bleeding from the murder before it. How am I to judge whether this
is true, alive and real, or just women's talk, frightened out of them,
that jumps up into the air and dies for nothing? What can you tell me
about it that would make it plain to my mind?

Chorus.
We heard it, yes. But go in and ask the strangers yourself. There is no
strength in messengers compared with a man questioning the man himself.

Aegisthus.
I want to see this messenger and test him again, whether he was there
himself, close by, when the man died, or whether he is only repeating a
dim rumour he picked up. He will not steal past a mind with its eyes
open.

(Aegisthus goes into the house.)

Chorus.
	Zeus, Zeus, what am I to say?
	Where do I begin this prayer,
	this calling on the gods?
	How can I find words to match my goodwill
	and finish what I am saying?
	Now the blood-stained edges
	of the blades that cut men down
	are about to do one of two things:
	destroy the house of Agamemnon
	altogether, for good,
	or light fire and light for freedom,
	and win him the rule of the city
	and the great wealth of his fathers.
	That is the wrestling match godlike Orestes
	is about to join, one against two,
	the fresh man waiting at the edge of the ring.
	Let it be for victory.

(A cry comes from inside the house.)

Aegisthus.
Ah! Ah! Otototoi!

Chorus.
Listen. Listen, again. How is it? How has it been decided for the house?
Let us stand back while the thing is being finished, so that we seem to
have no part in these evils. The fight has been settled now, one way or
the other.

(The Chorus draws aside. A Servant of Aegisthus runs out of the house.)

Servant.
Oh, oh, oh, the master is struck down! Oh, again, a third time I cry it:
Aegisthus is no more. Open up, as fast as you can, and unbar the women's
doors. It will take a young man's strength, and not to help him, he is
finished. What use would that be? Help! Help! Am I shouting to the deaf,
wasting my voice for nothing on people who are asleep? Where is
Clytemnestra? What is she doing? Her own neck is on the razor's edge
now, I think, ready to fall, struck down by justice.

(Clytemnestra comes in, alone.)

Clytemnestra.
What is this? What is this shouting you are raising in the house?

Servant.
The dead are killing the living, I tell you.

Clytemnestra.
Oh. I understand the riddle. We are to die by treachery, exactly as we
killed. Someone give me an axe, a man-killing axe, quickly! Let us find
out whether we win or lose. That is where I have come to, in this whole
evil business.

(The Servant goes. The door opens. Aegisthus lies dead. Orestes stands
over him with a sword, Pylades behind him.)

Orestes.
You are the one I am looking for. He has had enough.

Clytemnestra.
Oh. You are dead, Aegisthus, my love, my strength.

Orestes.
You love the man? Then you will lie in the same grave with him, and
never desert him now that he is dead.

Clytemnestra.
Stop, my son. Have some respect, child, for this breast. Here is where
you dozed off so many times, sucking with your soft gums the milk that
made you grow.

Orestes.
Pylades, what do I do? Shall I be ashamed to kill my mother, and spare
her?

Pylades.
Then what becomes of the rest of Apollo's oracles, the ones given at
Delphi? What becomes of the oaths you swore? Count every man your enemy
sooner than the gods.

Orestes.
I judge you the winner. You advise me well.

(He turns to Clytemnestra.)

Come with me. I mean to cut your throat right next to him. While he was
alive you thought him a better man than my father. Sleep with him in
death, since you love this man and hate the one you should have loved.

Clytemnestra.
I brought you up. I want to grow old with you.

Orestes.
Kill my father and then live in the same house as me?

Clytemnestra.
Fate had a share in that, my child.

Orestes.
Then Fate has arranged this death too.

Clytemnestra.
Have you no fear of a parent's curse, child?

Orestes.
You gave birth to me and threw me out into misery.

Clytemnestra.
I did not throw you out. I sent you to the house of an ally.

Orestes.
I was sold, shamefully, and I was a free man's son.

Clytemnestra.
Then where is the price I got for you?

Orestes.
I am ashamed to throw that in your face outright.

Clytemnestra.
No, go on. And name your father's follies in the same breath.

Orestes.
Do not put the man who was out there suffering on trial, you who sat at
home.

Clytemnestra.
It is hard on a woman, child, to be kept from her husband.

Orestes.
Yes, but it is the husband's hard work that feeds the women sitting at
home.

Clytemnestra.
You seem determined, child, to kill your mother.

Orestes.
You are killing yourself. Not I.

Clytemnestra.
Look out. Beware the hounds of a mother's anger.

Orestes.
And my father's hounds, how do I escape them, if I leave this undone?

Clytemnestra.
I see it. I am alive, and wailing at a tomb, for nothing.

Orestes.
Yes. My father's fate marks out this death for you.

Clytemnestra.
Oh, no. This is the snake I gave birth to and fed.

Orestes.
The fear in your dream was a true prophet. You killed the man you should
not have; now suffer what you should not.

(He drives her into the house. Pylades follows.)

Chorus.
I grieve, even for these two, and the double blow that has fallen on
them. But since Orestes, who has suffered so much, has reached the crest
of all this blood, we choose this anyway: that the eye of the house
should not fall and be lost altogether.

Chorus.
	Justice came to the sons of Priam in time,
	a heavy punishment, and just.
	And to the house of Agamemnon
	came a double lion, a double War.
	The exile the oracle at Delphi sent
	has driven it through to the end,
	sped on his way by good counsel from the gods.

Chorus.
	Cry out, cry out for joy, for the house of our masters,
	its escape from evil, from the wasting of its wealth
	under two who fouled it,
	from a fortune that had lost its road.

Chorus.
	And he has come, the one whose business
	is the secret fight, the punishment that plots,
	and in the fight the true daughter of Zeus
	touched his hand.
	Justice, we mortals call her,
	and we hit the right name.
	She breathes deadly anger on her enemies.

Chorus.
	Cry out, cry out for joy, for the house of our masters,
	its escape from evil, from the wasting of its wealth
	under two who fouled it,
	from a fortune that had lost its road.

Chorus.
	What Apollo of Parnassus cried out,
	Apollo who holds the great hollow in the earth,
	comes now against the harm that had settled in so long,
	with a cunning that is no cunning.
	The god's word wins, somehow,
	so that I need not serve the wicked.
	The power that holds the sky deserves our worship.

Chorus.
	Look, the light is here.
	The great curb is off the mouth of the house.
	Rise up, house. Too long, far too long,
	you have lain flat on the ground.

Chorus.
	Soon time, that finishes everything,
	will cross the threshold of the house,
	when every stain is driven from the hearth
	by the cleansing rites that drive out ruin.
	And the dice of fortune will fall again
	and lie with their fair faces up,
	kind to see, kind to hear,
	for the people who live in this house.

Chorus.
	Look, the light is here.
	The great curb is off the mouth of the house.
	Rise up, house. Too long, far too long,
	you have lain flat on the ground.

(Orestes stands over the two bodies. Attendants spread out the robe in
which Agamemnon was killed.)

Orestes.
Look at them, the two tyrants of this land, who murdered my father and
sacked my house. They were majestic once, sitting on their thrones, and
they are lovers still, if you judge by what has happened to them, and
their oath holds good to its pledges. Together they swore death to my
poor father, and swore to die together, and they have kept their word
well. And look again, you who are hearing this tale of evils, at the
device, the thing that bound my poor father, the shackles for his hands,
the fetters for his feet, the pair of them. Spread it out. Stand round
it in a circle and show it, this thing that covered a man, so that the
Father can see it, not mine, but the one who watches over everything
here, the Sun, so that he can see my mother's unholy work and stand as
my witness one day in court that I hunted down this death, my mother's
death, justly. Aegisthus's death I do not even count. He has paid the
penalty for a seducer, as the law says. But she, who plotted this
hateful thing against her husband, the man whose children she carried as
a weight under her belt, a weight she loved once, and now, as it turns
out, a hateful evil, what do you think of her? If she had been born a
moray eel or a viper, she would have rotted anyone she touched without
even biting, by sheer daring, by the crookedness of her mind. And this
thing, what do I call it, however careful I am with my words? A trap for
a wild animal? A shroud for a corpse, wrapped round its feet in the
bath? No. A net. Call it a hunting net, or a robe to tangle a man's
feet. This is what a highway robber would own, a man who cheats
strangers and makes his living by stealing silver, and with this snare
he could kill men by the dozen and warm his heart at it. May I never
have a woman like that living in my house. Sooner let the gods kill me
childless.

Chorus.
	Aiai, aiai, for the miserable work.
	You were finished off by a hateful death.
	Ah, ah.
	And for the one who is left, suffering is coming into flower.

Orestes.
Did she do it, or did she not? This robe is my witness, dyed where
Aegisthus's sword went in. The ooze of blood has worked with time to
spoil the many dyes of the embroidery. Now I praise him. Now I am here
to mourn him, speaking to this woven thing that killed my father. I
grieve for the deed and the suffering and my whole family. The stain of
this victory is nothing anyone would envy.

Chorus.
	No one alive gets through a whole life unhurt,
	unharmed to the end.
	Ah, ah.
	One trouble is here now, and another is on its way.

Orestes.
But so that you know, for I do not know how this ends: I am like a
charioteer driving his horses wide of the course. My mind is hard to
rein in and it is carrying me off, beaten, and at my heart fear is ready
to sing and dance to the tune of anger. While I still have my wits, I
proclaim to the people I love, and I say it: I killed my mother, and not
without justice, the unclean murderer of my father, a thing the gods
hated. And as for what drugged me with courage for this deed, I name
above all Apollo, the prophet of Delphi, who told me that if I did this
I would be clear of all blame, and if I let it go, I will not say the
penalty. No bowshot could reach that height of pain. And now look at me,
how I go, with this branch and this wreath, a suppliant, to the seat at
the centre of the earth, Apollo's ground, and the fire they call
undying, in flight from this blood, my own family's blood. Apollo told
me to turn to no other hearth. And I charge all the people of Argos to
bear witness for me, in time to come, to how these evils were brought
about. I go out a wanderer, a stranger to this land, and living or dead
I leave this name behind me.

Chorus.
But you have done well. So do not yoke your mouth to an ill-omened
speech, or put evil on your tongue. You have set the whole city of Argos
free, and cut off the heads of two snakes with one lucky stroke.

Orestes.
Ah, ah! Women, slaves of this house, look at them, there, like Gorgons,
dressed in grey, wound about with thick, swarming snakes. I cannot stay
here any longer.

Chorus.
What are these visions that whirl you round, of all men the one your
father loved best? Hold on. Do not let fear beat you altogether.

Orestes.
These are no visions to me. These are real. They are the hounds of my
mother's anger.

Chorus.
It is the blood, still fresh on your hands. That is what is throwing
your mind into confusion.

Orestes.
Lord Apollo, look, there are more and more of them, and from their eyes
they are dripping blood, foul blood.

Chorus.
There is one cleansing for you. Apollo's touch will set you free from
these torments.

Orestes.
You do not see them. I see them. I am being driven. I cannot stay here
any longer.

(Orestes runs out.)

Chorus.
Then good luck go with you, and may the god look on you kindly, and
guard you, and bring you good fortune when you need it.

Chorus.
	Here, on the house of the kings,
	a third storm has blown itself out,
	a storm out of the family's blood.
	First came the eating of children,
	the wretched sufferings of Thyestes.
	Second, the suffering of a man, a king:
	the war leader of the Achaeans died,
	cut down in his bath.
	And now a third has come from somewhere,
	a saviour, or shall I say a death?
	Where will it end? Where will it come to rest,
	the fury of ruin, lulled to sleep at last?
