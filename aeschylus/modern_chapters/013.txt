Prometheus Bound
(Part 1 of 2)

(A bare mountainside at the edge of the world, in Scythia. Power and
Force, the servants of Zeus, drag in Prometheus. Hephaestus follows
them with his hammer and his chains.)

Power.
We have come to the last ground of the earth, the Scythian road, an
empty place where nobody walks. Hephaestus, the orders the Father gave
you are your business now: to fasten this criminal to the high cliff
rock in chains of adamant that nothing can break. Because it was your
flower he stole, the flash of fire that makes every craft possible,
and he handed it to mortals. For a crime like that he has to pay the
gods what he owes them, so that he learns to love the tyranny of Zeus
and gets out of the habit of loving mankind.

Hephaestus.
Power, and you, Force: as far as you two are concerned, the command of
Zeus is carried out, and nothing stands in your way any longer. But I
do not have the stomach to chain a god of my own blood by force to
this ravine where the winter storms hit. And yet I have to find the
stomach for it, whether I like it or not. It is a heavy thing to brush
aside the Father's word.

High-thinking son of Themis, whose counsel runs straight: against your
will and against mine I am going to nail you with bronze that no one
can undo to this rock where no human ever comes. You will never see a
mortal's face here or hear a mortal's voice. The sun's bright flame
will roast you and change the flower of your skin. You will be glad
when night in her embroidered dress hides the light, and glad again
when the sun scatters the morning frost. Always, the weight of
whatever pain is on you at the moment will grind you down, because the
one who is going to relieve you has not been born yet. That is what
you got for your love of mankind. You were a god, and you did not
cower at the gods' anger; you gave mortals honours that were more than
their due. For that you will stand guard over this joyless rock,
upright, sleepless, never bending your knee. You will cry out, many
times, groans and laments that do no good, because the mind of Zeus is
hard to move. Everyone is harsh whose power is new.

Power.
Well? Why are you dawdling and wasting pity? Why don't you hate a god
the gods hate most, the one who handed your own privilege over to
mortals?

Hephaestus.
Kinship is a terrible thing. So is friendship.

Power.
Agreed. But disobeying the Father's word, how is that possible? Aren't
you more afraid of that?

Hephaestus.
You never change. Pitiless, and full of nerve.

Power.
Because crying over this one cures nothing. Stop working at what is no
use to anyone.

Hephaestus.
Oh, my craft. How I hate it.

Power.
Why hate it? In plain words, your skill is not to blame for any of the
trouble in front of us.

Hephaestus.
All the same, I wish it had fallen to someone else.

Power.
Every job has its burden, except being king of the gods. Nobody is
free except Zeus.

Hephaestus.
I know that. This proves it. I have nothing to say back.

Power.
Then hurry up and throw the chains round him, before the Father sees
you loitering.

Hephaestus.
There. The cuffs are ready, you can see them.

Power.
Put them round his wrists and hit them with the hammer with all your
strength. Nail him to the rock.

Hephaestus.
It is getting done, and it is not being done badly.

Power.
Hit harder. Squeeze tight. Leave nothing slack. He is frightening at
finding a way out where there is no way out.

Hephaestus.
This arm is fixed, anyway. He will not get that one loose.

Power.
Now pin this one too, and make it safe, so that the clever one learns
he is slower than Zeus.

Hephaestus.
Nobody but him could fairly find fault with my work.

Power.
Now the wedge of adamant, the stubborn tooth of it. Drive it straight
through his chest and put your weight behind it.

Hephaestus.
Aiai, Prometheus. I groan for your pain.

Power.
Hanging back again? Groaning for the enemies of Zeus? Watch out, or
one day you will be pitying yourself.

Hephaestus.
You are looking at a sight that eyes can hardly bear to look at.

Power.
I am looking at a man getting what he deserves. Come on. Throw the
straps round his ribs.

Hephaestus.
I have to do it. You do not have to keep ordering me.

Power.
Oh, I will order you, and I will shout you on as well. Get down there
and ring his legs with iron. Use force.

Hephaestus.
There. Done, and it was not much work.

Power.
Now hammer the fetters right through. Hard. The one who inspects this
work is a severe judge.

Hephaestus.
Your tongue talks the way your face looks.

Power.
You be soft. Just do not lecture me for being stubborn and rough in
my temper.

Hephaestus.
Let us go. He has the net on his limbs.

(Hephaestus goes down the mountain.)

Power.
Now sit there and be outrageous. Rob the gods of their privileges and
give them to creatures that live for a day. Can mortals bail you out
of any of this pain? The gods call you Prometheus, the Forethinker,
and the name is a lie. You are the one who needs some forethought
now, to work out how you will wriggle out of this piece of
craftsmanship.

(Power and Force go out. Prometheus is alone on the rock.)

Prometheus.
Bright sky. Winds with your quick wings. Springs of the rivers, and
you, waves of the sea, laughing beyond counting. Earth, mother of
everything. And the circle of the sun that sees it all: I call on you.
Look at what I, a god, am suffering at the hands of gods. Look at the
torments that are tearing me apart, and that I have to wrestle with

Prometheus.
	for ten thousand years.
	This is the shameful chain
	the new commander of the blessed gods
	has invented for me.
	Oh, oh. I groan for the pain that is here
	and the pain that is coming.
	Where will an end to this labour ever rise?

Prometheus.
And yet, what am I saying? I know everything that is coming, exactly, in
advance. No pain will reach me that I did not expect. What is allotted
has to be carried as lightly as it can be, knowing that the strength of
Necessity cannot be fought. But I cannot keep silent about my luck, and
I cannot speak of it either. I gave gifts to mortals, and for that I am
yoked in these bonds, poor wretch. I hunted down the spring of fire, hid
it in a hollow fennel stalk, and stole it, and it has turned out to be
mortals' teacher in every craft and their great resource. That is the
crime I am paying for, nailed up in chains under the open sky.

Prometheus.
	Ah. Ah. What is that?
	What sound, what smell has flown to me, out of nowhere?
	A god's? A mortal's? Something of both?
	Has someone come to this rock at the end of the world
	to watch my pain? Or what does he want?
	Look at me, a god in chains, a god with no luck,
	the enemy of Zeus, whom every god

Prometheus.
	who walks through the court of Zeus has learned to hate,
	because I loved mortals too much.
	Oh, oh. What is that again? A whirring, close by,
	like birds. The air whistles
	under the light beating of wings.
	Everything that creeps toward me frightens me.

(The daughters of Oceanus arrive through the air in a winged chariot
and hover near the rock.)

Chorus.
	Do not be afraid. We come as friends,
	this flight of wings
	racing each other to your rock.
	It was hard to talk our father round.
	The quick winds carried me here.
	The clang of iron on iron rang
	down to the bottom of our caves
	and knocked the shyness out of me, for all my grave looks.
	I came without my sandals, in a winged car.

Prometheus.
	Aiai, aiai.
	Children of Tethys, mother of so many,
	daughters of the father who circles the whole earth
	with his stream that never sleeps, Oceanus:
	look at me, see the chain
	that pins me to the top of this ravine,
	the watch I keep
	that nobody would envy.

Chorus.
	I see you, Prometheus.
	A fog of fear, full of tears,
	came over my eyes when I saw your body
	drying out on the rock
	in these insults of adamant.
	New helmsmen are steering Olympus.
	Zeus rules by new laws, and there is no law in them.
	What was huge before, he is wiping out.

Prometheus.
	If only he had thrown me under the earth,
	under Hades who takes in the dead,
	into Tartarus that has no end,
	and chained me there, savagely, in bonds that cannot be undone,
	so that no god and nobody else
	could gloat over this.
	Instead I hang in the wind, a toy for the air,
	and my pain is my enemies' pleasure.

Chorus.
	Which of the gods has a heart hard enough
	to be pleased by this?
	Who does not feel your pain with you,
	except Zeus? He has set his mind
	and it will not bend. He is angry always,
	and he is crushing the children of Heaven.
	He will not stop until his heart is full,
	or until somebody, by some trick, seizes
	the power that is so hard to take.

Prometheus.
	Believe this: the day will come
	when the lord of the blessed gods will need me,
	me, tortured in these strong shackles,
	to show him the new plan
	that strips him of his sceptre and his honours.
	He will not charm me with the honeyed spells of persuasion.
	I will not cower at his hard threats
	and tell him what I know,
	not until he loosens these savage chains
	and agrees to pay
	for this outrage.

Chorus.
	You are bold. You give nothing
	to your bitter suffering,
	and your mouth is far too free.
	A piercing fear has stirred up my mind.
	I am afraid for what happens to you.
	Where will you ever bring this ship of pain to shore
	and see its end? The son of Cronus
	has ways nobody can reach
	and a heart nobody can talk to.

Prometheus.
	I know Zeus is harsh
	and keeps justice in his own hands.
	All the same, I think one day
	he will be soft-minded, when he is broken
	in the way I know.
	He will flatten that iron rage of his
	and come hurrying to me, to be friends and allies,
	and I will be hurrying to meet him.

Chorus.
Uncover the whole of it and tell us the story. What was the charge
Zeus caught you on, that he tortures you so cruelly and with such
dishonour? Teach us, unless the telling does you some harm.

Prometheus.
It hurts me to tell this, and it hurts to keep quiet. Either way it is
bad luck. As soon as the gods began to quarrel and faction broke out
among them, one side wanting to throw Cronus off his seat so that
Zeus, if you please, could be king, the other pushing the opposite
way, so that Zeus would never rule the gods, that was when I gave the
best advice there was and tried to persuade the Titans, the children
of Heaven and Earth, and could not. They despised clever schemes. In
the pride of their strength they thought they would be masters
without effort, by force. But my mother Themis, who is also Earth, one
shape with many names, had told me more than once how the future was
fated to fall out: that it was not by strength, not by main force, but
by cunning that the winners would win. I laid all this out for them in
words, and they did not think it worth a glance. So the best thing
open to me at the time, it seemed, was to take my mother with me and
stand beside Zeus, a willing helper of a willing god. And it is by my
plans that the black pit of Tartarus now covers old Cronus and his
allies with him. That is the help the tyrant of the gods had from me,
and this is how he paid me back, with these foul punishments. It is a
sickness that comes with tyranny somehow: it cannot trust the people
who are its own.

But you asked what he tortures me for, the charge against me, and I
will make that clear. The moment he sat down on his father's throne he
began handing out privileges to the gods, this to one and that to
another, and dividing up his kingdom. But of wretched mortals he took
no account at all. He wanted to wipe out the whole race and plant a
new one. Nobody stood against that except me. I dared to. I saved
mortals from being smashed to pieces and going down to Hades. That is
why I am bent under these agonies, painful to suffer and pitiful to
look at. I put mortals first in my pity, and I was not judged worthy
of any pity myself. Instead I have been disciplined like this, without
mercy, into a sight that shames the glory of Zeus.

Chorus.
Anyone who does not ache with you at your torments, Prometheus, has a
mind of iron and was carved out of rock. I would never have wanted to
see this, and now that I have seen it my heart hurts.

Prometheus.
Yes. To the people who are my own I am a pitiful thing to look at.

Chorus.
You did not go any further than this, did you?

Prometheus.
I did. I stopped mortals from seeing their own death coming.

Chorus.
What cure did you find for that sickness?

Prometheus.
I planted blind hopes inside them.

Chorus.
That was a great gift to give mortals.

Prometheus.
And on top of that, I gave them fire.

Chorus.
What? Creatures of a day have fire now, with its bright eye?

Prometheus.
Yes. And from it they will learn many crafts.

Chorus.
So these are the charges Zeus has against you, and he...

Prometheus.
And he tortures me and never once lets up.

Chorus.
And there is no end set to your ordeal?

Prometheus.
None. Only when he decides.

Chorus.
And how will he ever decide? What hope is there? Don't you see that
you did wrong? How you did wrong is not a pleasant thing for me to
say, and it is pain for you to hear, so let us leave it. Look for some
way out of this ordeal.

Prometheus.
It is easy, when your own foot is clear of trouble, to advise and
lecture someone who is in it. I knew all of this. I chose it, I chose
it, I did wrong, I will not deny it. I helped mortals and found pain
for myself. But I never thought the punishment would be like this:
withering on a cliff in mid-air, with this empty rock for my share and
no neighbour. Now stop crying over the pain in front of you. Come down
to the ground and hear what is creeping toward me, so that you learn
all of it to the end. Do it for me. Please. Share the burden with
someone who is labouring under it now. Pain wanders. It settles on one
of us today and on another tomorrow.

Chorus.
	You did not shout that to ears that were unwilling,
	Prometheus.
	Now, on a light foot, I will leave my racing seat
	and the clean air, the road of the birds,
	and come down to this jagged ground.
	I want to hear your troubles
	from beginning to end.

(The daughters of Oceanus come down from the chariot. Oceanus arrives
through the air, riding a winged four-legged creature.)

Oceanus.
	I have come to the end of a long road,
	Prometheus, to you,
	steering this fast-winged bird
	with my mind, and no bit in its mouth.
	Know this: what has happened to you hurts me.
	Kinship makes me feel it, I think,
	and blood aside, there is no one
	I would give a bigger share of my respect to than you.
	You will find that this is true.
	I do not have it in me to talk sweetly for nothing.
	Come, show me what I can do to help you.
	You will never say you had a firmer friend than Oceanus.

Prometheus.
What? What am I looking at? You have come too, to watch my pain? How
did you find the nerve to leave the stream that carries your name and
your caves, roofed with rock, that you hollowed out yourself, and come
to this country, the mother of iron? Did you come to look at what has
happened to me and to share my grief? Then look. Here is the sight:
the friend of Zeus, the one who helped him set up his tyranny, and
see what agonies he has bent me under.

Oceanus.
I see, Prometheus, and I want to give you the best advice there is,
clever as you are. Know yourself. Fit your ways to the new times.
There is a new tyrant among the gods too. If you keep throwing out
words as harsh and sharpened as these, Zeus may hear you, even sitting
far above you, and then the mass of trouble you have now will look
like a game. You poor wretch, let go of the anger you are holding and
look for a way out of this suffering. Maybe what I am saying sounds
old-fashioned to you. But this, Prometheus, is what a tongue that
talks too big earns. You are still not humble. You still do not give
way to trouble. You want to add fresh trouble to what you have. Take
me as your teacher and you will stop kicking against the spurs. You
can see that a harsh king rules, and he answers to nobody. Now I am
going to go and try, if I can, to get you released from this pain.
You keep quiet. Do not let your mouth run away with you. You are very
wise; surely you know exactly what an idle tongue gets for its
trouble?

Prometheus.
I envy you. You shared in all of this, you dared as much as I did, and
you are standing there with no blame on you. Now leave it alone. Do
not concern yourself. You will not persuade him, whatever you do. He
is not easy to persuade. And watch yourself, or the journey will do
you harm.

Oceanus.
You are far better at teaching your neighbours sense than yourself. I
judge by what I see done, not by talk. I am set on going; do not pull
me back. I am sure, I am sure, that Zeus will give me this gift, and
release you from this pain.

Prometheus.
For that, I thank you, and I will never stop thanking you. There is
nothing lacking in your goodwill. But do not trouble yourself. You
will trouble yourself for nothing and do me no good, if you really do
want to take the trouble. Keep quiet and keep yourself out of the way.
Because I am in trouble, that is no reason for me to want as many
others in it as possible. No. My brother Atlas's fate is grinding at
me enough already. He stands in the west, holding up the pillar of sky
and earth on his shoulders, a load no arms can hold comfortably. And I
pitied the one who lived in the Cilician caves, born of the earth,
when I saw him: the murderous monster with a hundred heads, Typhon the
furious, broken by force. He stood up against all the gods. Terror
hissed out of his hideous jaws, and from his eyes a glare like the
Gorgon's flashed, as if he would tear the tyranny of Zeus down by
force. But the sleepless missile of Zeus came for him, the thunderbolt
that comes down breathing flame, and it knocked him clean out of his
big talk. He was hit in the very seat of his mind, and burned to a
cinder, and the thunder blasted the strength out of him. And now he
lies there, a useless body, a sprawl, near the narrow strait of the
sea, pressed flat under the roots of Etna. Hephaestus sits on the
topmost peaks and hammers his hot iron. And out of there, one day,
rivers of fire will burst and eat the smooth fields of Sicily with
savage jaws, Sicily with her lovely crops: that is the rage Typhon
will boil up, in hot missiles of a fire-breathing storm nobody can
come near, even though the thunderbolt of Zeus burned him to charcoal.

You are not inexperienced. You do not need me to teach you. Save
yourself, the way you know how. I will drain the cup of what I have
until the mind of Zeus lets go of its anger.

Oceanus.
Then do you not know this, Prometheus, that words are the doctors for
a sick temper?

Prometheus.
If you soften the heart at the right moment, and do not use force to
shrink a rage while it is still swelling.

Oceanus.
And when a man is eager and takes a risk, what harm do you see in
that? Teach me.

Prometheus.
Wasted effort and light-headed foolishness.

Oceanus.
Let me be sick with that sickness. When you are really wise, it pays
best to seem a fool.

Prometheus.
That fault will look like mine.

Oceanus.
Plainly your words are sending me back home.

Prometheus.
Yes. So that grieving for me does not get you hated.

Oceanus.
By the one who has just sat down on the throne that rules everything?

Prometheus.
Watch him. Do not ever let that heart of his get angry with you.

Oceanus.
What has happened to you, Prometheus, is my teacher.

Prometheus.
Go. Get moving. Keep the mind you have right now.

Oceanus.
You shouted that at me as I was already starting. My four-footed bird
is brushing the smooth road of the air with his wings. He will be glad
to bend his knees in his own stall at home.

(Oceanus flies off.)

Chorus.
	I groan for your ruined luck, Prometheus.
	Tears drip from my eyes
	and run down my soft cheek
	and soak it in their wet stream.
	Zeus is ruling by laws of his own,
	and what he is doing is past envy;
	he shows the gods who came before him
	the spear-point of his pride.

Chorus.
	The whole country is crying out in grief already.
	They mourn the great, the ancient dignity
	that was yours and your brothers',
	all of them who live in the settled land
	of holy Asia:
	mortals who suffer along with you
	in your loud-groaning pain.

Chorus.
	And the girls who live in the land of Colchis,
	who do not flinch from a fight,
	and the crowd of Scythians who hold
	the last place on earth,
	round the marsh of Maeotis,

Chorus.
	and the warrior flower of Arabia,
	who hold the high cliff fortress
	near the Caucasus,
	a hostile army roaring
	behind sharp-pointed spears.

Chorus.
	One other Titan, a god, one only, have I seen before this
	broken in torment, in insults of adamant:
	Atlas, whose strength
	stood out above every other,
	who groans under the pole of the sky
	as he props it on his back.

Chorus.
	The sea's surf roars as it breaks.
	The deep groans.
	The black hollow of Hades rumbles under the earth,
	and the springs of the holy-flowing rivers
	groan for your pitiful pain.

Prometheus.
Do not think it is pride that keeps me silent, or stubbornness.
Thought is eating my heart, seeing myself abused like this. And yet,
who else but I marked out the privileges of these new gods, every one
of them, from start to finish? But I say nothing about that. I would
be telling you what you already know. Listen instead to the troubles
of mortals, and how I took them, who were children before, and gave
them minds and put them in possession of their wits. I say this with
no complaint against men. I am only explaining the goodwill behind
what I gave.

At first they had eyes and saw nothing; they had ears and did not
hear. Like the shapes in dreams they muddled through the length of
their lives, all of it at random. They did not know houses of brick
that face the sun. They did not know how to work wood. They lived
underground like restless ants, in the sunless corners of caves. They
had no reliable sign of winter, or of flowering spring, or of summer
with its harvest. They did everything without understanding, until I
showed them the risings of the stars and their settings, which are
hard to tell apart. And number, the cleverest of inventions, I found
for them, and the putting together of letters, which is the memory of
everything and the working mother of the Muses. I was the first to
yoke wild animals and make them slaves to the collar and the
pack-saddle, so that they could take over the heaviest labour from
mortal bodies, and I brought horses under the chariot and made them
love the rein, the pride and ornament of rich men's luxury. And nobody
but me invented the sailors' sea-roaming carriages with their wings of
linen. Those are the devices I found for mortals, poor wretch, and I
have no device of my own to get me out of the pain I am in now.

Chorus.
You have suffered a shameful thing. You have lost your wits and gone
astray. Like a bad doctor who has fallen ill himself, you are losing
heart, and you cannot find the drugs that would cure you.

Prometheus.
Hear the rest and you will marvel more, at the crafts and the
resources I worked out. The biggest of them first. If anyone fell sick
there was no defence at all, nothing to eat, nothing to rub on,
nothing to drink. For want of drugs they shrivelled away, until I
showed them how to mix gentle remedies, with which they now fight off
every disease. I laid out the many roads of prophecy. I was the first
to sort out which dreams are going to come true in waking life. I
taught them to read chance words, hard to interpret, and the omens
met on the road. The flight of birds with crooked talons I marked out
exactly: which are lucky by nature and which are on the wrong side,
what way of life each kind has, what feuds they have with each other
and what loves, and where they gather. The smoothness of the inner
organs, and what colour the gall must be to please the gods, and the
mottled beauty of the liver's lobe. The thigh-bones wrapped in fat,
and the long backbone, I burned, and set mortals on the road into an
art that is hard to read. And I opened their eyes to the signs in the
flame, which had a film over them before. So much for that. And the
things hidden under the earth to help mankind, bronze, iron, silver,
and gold: who could claim to have found them before I did? No one, I
know it for certain, unless he wants to babble for nothing. Take the
whole of it in one short word: every craft mortals have comes from
Prometheus, and from no one else.

Chorus.
Then do not go on helping mortals past all reason and neglect yourself
in your trouble. I have good hope that you will still be set free from
these chains and be no weaker than Zeus.

Prometheus.
That is not how Fate, who brings everything to its end, has decreed
this will finish. Only when I have been bent by ten thousand pains and
torments do I escape these chains. Craft is far weaker than Necessity.

Chorus.
Then who holds the tiller of Necessity?

Prometheus.
The three Fates, and the Furies, who forget nothing.

Chorus.
So Zeus is weaker than they are?

Prometheus.
He cannot escape what is fated, at any rate.

Chorus.
And what is fated for Zeus, except to rule for ever?

Prometheus.
That you will not learn yet. Do not press me.

Chorus.
It is something solemn, surely, that you are wrapping up.

Prometheus.
Think about something else. This is no time to speak of that. It has
to be kept covered, as far as it possibly can be. By keeping it safe I
escape these shameful chains and these torments.

Chorus.
	May Zeus, who allots everything,
	never set his power against my mind.
	May I never be slow to come to the gods
	with holy feasts of slaughtered oxen
	beside the stream of Oceanus, my father, that never fails.
	May I never sin in what I say.
	Let this stay fixed in me and never melt away.

Chorus.
	It is sweet to stretch out a long life
	on hopes that have courage in them,
	feeding the heart on bright good cheer.
	But I shudder when I look at you,
	torn by ten thousand torments.
	You do not tremble at Zeus.
	You go your own way, Prometheus,
	and you honour mortals too much.

Chorus.
	Come, my friend, what a thankless thanks it was.
	Tell me, where is the strength in it?
	What help can creatures of a day give you?
	Did you not see how feeble they are, how weak,
	no more than a dream,
	the blind race of men with their feet in fetters?
	Never will the plans of mortals
	step outside the order Zeus has made.

Chorus.
	I learned this from looking at what has destroyed you,
	Prometheus.
	And a very different song has flown to me now
	from the one I sang then,
	around your bath and your bed,
	the wedding song, when you won
	my sister Hesione with your gifts
	and led her home to share your bed as your wife.

(Io runs in. She has the horns of a cow on her head, and she is being
driven by something no one else can see.)

Io.
	What land is this? What people? Who is this
	I see, storm-beaten
	in a bridle of rock?
	What was your crime, that you are dying for it like this?
	Tell me where on earth
	my wretched wandering has brought me.

Io.
	Ah, ah. Eh, eh.
	It is stinging me again, the gadfly, poor wretch that I am,
	the ghost of Argus, the earth-born. Keep it off. Earth!
	I am afraid when I see him, the herdsman with ten thousand eyes.
	He comes on with his crafty eye,
	and even dead the earth will not hide him.
	He crosses over from the dead
	and hunts me, poor wretch, and drives me,
	starving, along the sand by the sea.

Io.
	And under it the reed pipe, stuck together with wax,
	drones its shrill tune that brings sleep.
	Oh, oh, popoi. Where are they taking me,
	these wanderings that carry me so far?
	What did I do, son of Cronus, what did you find me guilty of,
	that you yoked me in pain like this? Eh, eh.
	Why wear me down with a terror that drives me,
	a gadfly's terror, wretched and out of my mind?
	Burn me with fire, or hide me in the earth,
	or throw me to the beasts of the sea to eat,
	only do not begrudge me
	what I pray for, lord.
	These wanderings, all this wandering, have worked me hard enough.
	I cannot learn how to escape the pain.
	Do you hear the voice of the girl with the horns of a cow?

Prometheus.
How could I not hear the girl the gadfly spins round, Inachus's
daughter, who warms the heart of Zeus with love, and now, hated by Hera,
is forced to run these endless courses?

Io.
	How do you know my father's name to speak it?
	Tell me, wretched as I am, who are you,
	that you call out to a wretched girl, poor sufferer,
	and get it right?
	You have named the sickness the god sent me,
	that wastes me and stings me with goads
	that drive me on. Eh, eh.
	I came here leaping, starving, tormented,
	rushing, beaten down
	by the angry schemes of Hera.
	Who among the unlucky, eh, eh,
	suffers what I suffer?
	Show me plainly
	what is waiting for me to suffer.
	What remedy is there, what cure for the sickness?
	Tell me, if you know.
	Speak. Explain it to a girl who has wandered so badly.
