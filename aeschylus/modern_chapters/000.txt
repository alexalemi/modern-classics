The Persians
(Part 1 of 2)

(Susa, the Persian capital, before the council house, with the tomb of
Darius in view. The Elders of the kingdom come in, old men left in
charge while the army is away.)

Chorus.
	We are the ones they call the Faithful.
	The Persians have gone to the land of Greece
	and we were left to keep the seats,
	the rich seats, the seats heavy with gold.
	Xerxes the King, Darius's son,
	chose us himself, for our age and our rank,
	to watch over the country.
	But about the King's return,
	about the army and all its gold,
	something inside me has started
	to prophesy disaster.
	The whole strength of Asia is gone,
	and every house is muttering the young man's name,
	and no runner comes, no rider comes,
	to the city of the Persians.
	They left the walls of Susa, of Agbatana,
	the old stronghold of Cissia,
	and went, some on horses,
	some in ships, some on foot at the march,
	a solid mass of war.
	Men like Amistres and Artaphrenes,
	Megabates and Astaspes,
	marshals of Persia,
	kings themselves, and vassals of the Great King,
	are hurrying on at the head of that huge army,
	masters of the bow, masters of the horse,
	terrible to see and terrible in a fight,
	with hearts that have decided to endure.
	Artembares, who fights from the chariot,
	and Masistres, and Imaeus, the good archer,
	and Pharandaces,
	and Sosthanes, who drives the horses.
	And others the great Nile sent, the river that feeds so many:
	Susiscanes,
	Pegastagon, born in Egypt,
	great Arsames, lord of holy Memphis,
	Ariomardus, who governs ancient Thebes,
	and the rowers out of the marshes,
	frightening men, too many to count.

Chorus.
	Behind them come the soft-living Lydians in a crowd,
	and the men who hold down the whole mainland,
	whom Metrogathes and brave Arcteus, their commanding kings,
	and Sardis, heavy with gold, sent riding out
	in chariot after chariot,
	teams of two poles and of three,
	a sight to make you afraid to look.
	And the men who live by holy Tmolus swear
	they will throw the yoke of slavery over Greece:
	Mardon and Tharybis, anvils for any spear,
	and the Mysians, who throw the javelin. And Babylon,
	heavy with gold, sends a mixed crowd streaming out,
	men who ride the ships
	and men who trust in the pull of the bow.
	And the whole nation that wears the short sword
	follows out of every part of Asia
	at the King's terrible summons.
	That is the flower of the Persian land,
	the men who are gone,
	and all Asia, the country that nursed them,
	groans for them with a longing that burns,
	and parents and wives count the days
	and shake, as the time stretches out.

Chorus.
	The King's army, the sacker of cities,
	has already crossed
	to the neighbouring land on the far shore.
	It went over the strait of Helle, Athamas's daughter,
	on a raft of boats lashed with flax,
	a road bolted tight,
	a yoke thrown over the neck of the sea.

Chorus.
	The hot-headed ruler of Asia, land of so many men,
	is driving his marvellous flock,
	his herd of men, against the whole earth,
	in two divisions, on land and off the sea,
	trusting in his hard, rough commanders,
	he himself, a man like a god,
	of the race that was born from gold.

Chorus.
	With the dark blue glare
	of a deadly snake in his eyes,
	with his thousands of hands and thousands of sailors,
	whipping on his Syrian chariot,
	he brings a War of arrows
	against men famous for the spear.

Chorus.
	And no one has yet been found who can stand
	against that great river of men
	and hold back with solid walls
	the unfightable wave of the sea.
	The Persian army cannot be approached,
	and its people have brave hearts.

Chorus.
	For from the gods, long ago,
	Fate took hold and laid it on the Persians
	to carry through the wars
	that break down towers,
	the confusion of charging horsemen,
	the uprooting of cities.

Chorus.
	And they learned to look out
	on the sea's sacred grove,
	the broad roads of water
	whitening under the wild wind,
	trusting their thin-spun cables
	and the machines that carry a people across.

Chorus.
	But the god's trick, the cunning deception,
	what man alive can escape it?
	Who is so quick on his feet
	that he can leap clear of it lightly?

Chorus.
	For Ruin is friendly at first. She fawns
	and coaxes a man into her nets,
	and from there no mortal
	slips out above the trap and away.

Chorus.
	This is why my heart is dressed in black
	and torn with fear.
	Oh, for the Persian army!
	I am afraid the city will hear
	that the great town of Susa has no men left in it,

Chorus.
	and that the fortress of the Cissians
	will sing the answer back,
	Oh, a crowd of women
	crying that one word out loud,
	and the tearing will fall on the fine linen dresses.

Chorus.
	For every man who rides a horse
	and every man who walks the plain
	has left, like a swarm out of the hive
	when the bees go with the leader of the host,
	and crossed the headland that is yoked to both shores,
	the sea's own cape,
	the land that the two continents share.

Chorus.
	And the beds fill with tears
	for the men they miss.
	The women of Persia are soft in their grief. Each one
	sent off her fierce, spear-carrying husband
	with love and longing,
	and is left
	in a yoke made for two, alone.

Chorus.
	But come, Persians. Let us sit down here
	under this ancient roof
	and think carefully, think deep.
	The need has come for it.
	How is Xerxes the King doing,
	Darius's son,
	of the race that gave our people their name?
	Is it the drawn bow that has won,
	or has the strength of the spear
	with its iron head prevailed?
	But look. Here comes a light like the eyes of the gods:
	the King's mother, my Queen.
	I fall to the ground before her.
	And it is right that every one of us
	speaks to her with words of greeting.

(The Queen comes in on a chariot, richly dressed, with attendants. The
old men prostrate themselves and rise again.)

Chorus.
Queen, highest of the deep-belted women of Persia, aged mother of
Xerxes, wife of Darius, greetings. You were the wife of the Persians'
god and you are the mother of a god, unless the old luck of the army
has turned its back on it now.

Queen.
That is exactly why I have come, out of the palace with its gold
hangings, out of the room where Darius and I slept. Worry is clawing
at my heart too. I will tell you something, friends, and I am in no
way free of fear when I tell it: I am afraid that great wealth may
kick up the dust from the ground and trip up, with its own foot, the
prosperity that Darius built, and did not build without some god's
help. So there is a double anxiety in my mind, and it is hard to put
into words. A pile of money with no men to it is nothing to honour.
And men with no money never shine with the light their strength
deserves. We have wealth enough, and no one could fault it. My fear
is for the eye. The eye of a house, I think, is the presence of its
master. That is how things stand. So be my advisers in this, Persians,
old and faithful men. Every piece of good counsel I have is in you.

Chorus.
Be sure of this, Queen of this land: you will never have to ask twice
for anything, in word or deed, that it is in our power to guide you
to. You are calling on advisers who wish you well.

Queen.
Ever since my son fitted out his army and went off meaning to lay
waste the land of the Ionians, I have lived with dreams, night after
night. But I have never seen one as clear as last night's. I will tell
it to you.

Two women in beautiful clothes seemed to come before my eyes. One was
dressed in Persian robes, the other in Dorian dress. They were far
taller than women are now, flawless in their beauty, and sisters, of
one family. For a homeland, one had drawn Greece by lot, the other the
land of the barbarians. And the two of them, as I seemed to see it,
were starting some quarrel between themselves. My son noticed it, and
tried to hold them back and calm them down. He yoked them both under
his chariot and put the collar straps on their necks. One of them
towered up proud in that harness and kept her mouth obedient to the
reins. The other fought. She tore the chariot's tackle apart with her
hands, dragged it off with her by force, bit and all, and broke the
yoke in the middle. My son fell. And his father Darius stood there
beside him, pitying him. And when Xerxes saw him, he tore the clothes
off his own body.

That is what I say I saw in the night. When I got up and touched my
hands in the clear running water of the spring, I went to the altar
with an offering in my hand, meaning to sacrifice a cake to the gods
who turn evil away. That is their due. And I saw an eagle running for
refuge to the hearth of Apollo, and I stood there without a sound, my
friends, in fear. And then I saw a hawk come rushing in on its wings
and pluck at the eagle's head with its claws. And the eagle did
nothing but crouch down and offer up its body. Those were terrors for
me to see, and they are terrors for you to hear. For you know this
well: if my son does well, he will be a man everyone marvels at. And
if he does badly, he answers to no city. If he comes home safe, he
rules this land exactly as before.

Chorus.
Mother, we do not want to frighten you too much with what we say, or
to reassure you too much either. Go to the gods with your prayers. If
what you saw was bad, ask them to turn it aside and finish it off, and
to bring the good things to fulfilment for you and your children and
the city and everyone you love. Then you must pour offerings to the
Earth and the dead. And ask your husband Darius, whom you say you saw
in the night, ask him kindly to send good things up out of the earth
into the light for you and for your son, and to hold the opposite of
them down under the ground, fading in the dark. That is my advice,
and I mean it well. My heart is my prophet. Our judgement is that all
of this turns out well for you, in every way.

Queen.
Well, you have read the dream kindly, at least, as its first
interpreter, and with goodwill to my son and my house. May the good
things come true. Everything you ask, I will do for the gods and for
the dear ones under the earth, as soon as I go back into the house.
But there is something I want to find out, my friends. Where in the
world do they say Athens is?

Chorus.
Far away, towards the west, where our lord the Sun goes down and dies.

Queen.
And my son really wanted to hunt down this city?

Chorus.
Yes. Because then all Greece would be the King's subject.

Queen.
Do they have so many men in their army?

Chorus.
Enough of an army to have done the Medes a great deal of harm already.

Queen.
And what else do they have? Enough wealth in their houses?

Chorus.
They have a spring of silver, a treasure in their soil.

Queen.
Is it the arrow and the drawn bow that fits their hands?

Chorus.
Not at all. Spears for standing and fighting close, and shields for
armour.

Queen.
And who is the shepherd over them? Who is master of their army?

Chorus.
They are called nobody's slaves, and nobody's subjects.

Queen.
Then how can they stand against an invading enemy?

Chorus.
Well enough to have destroyed Darius's army, which was large and fine.

Queen.
What you say is terrible for the parents of the men who have gone
there to think about.

Chorus.
But I think you will soon know the whole story, and every word of it
true. That is a Persian running, you can tell by the way he moves, and
he brings some clear news, good or bad to hear.

Messenger.
Cities of all the land of Asia! Land of Persia, great harbour of
wealth! At one blow, all that prosperity is destroyed, and the flower
of the Persians has fallen and is gone. Oh, it is a bad thing to be
the first to bring bad news. But I have to unfold the whole of the
suffering, Persians. The whole barbarian army is lost.

Chorus.
	Pain, pain, strange new pain,
	and cruel. Aiai,
	be drenched, Persians,
	as you hear this grief.

Messenger.
Yes, because everything over there is finished. And I, past all hope, am
looking at the light of home.

Chorus.
	Too long.
	This life has shown itself too long for us,
	old men, to have to hear
	this pain we never expected.

Messenger.
I was there, Persians. I did not get it from other men's stories. I can
tell you what kind of disaster was worked.

Chorus.
	Otototoi. For nothing,
	all those weapons, all those mixed peoples,
	went out from the land of Asia
	against Greece, the enemy's country.

Messenger.
The shores of Salamis and all the coast around are full of the bodies of
men who died an ugly death.

Chorus.
	Otototoi. You are telling me the bodies of the men we love,
	rolled by the sea, soaked through,
	dead, are being carried
	back and forth, tossing in their long cloaks.

Messenger.
The bows were no use. The whole army was lost, broken by the ramming of
the ships.

Chorus.
	Raise the cry, the unlucky cry,
	the cry of misery for the Persians:
	the enemy has made an end of everything,
	utterly. Aiai for the army that is destroyed.

Messenger.
Salamis. The most hateful name there is to hear. Oh. How I groan when I
remember Athens.

Chorus.
	Hateful Athens, to her enemies.
	We have cause to remember
	how many Persian women she has made,
	for nothing, childless and without a man.

Queen.
I have been silent a long while, wretched, struck dumb by the
disaster. This calamity is too big to speak of, too big even to ask
about. But when the gods send suffering, mortals have to bear it.
Compose yourself, and unfold the whole of it, even if you are groaning
at our losses as you speak. Who is not dead? Which of the commanders
shall we mourn, who held a place of authority and left his post empty
of its man by dying?

Messenger.
Xerxes himself is alive and sees the light.

Queen.
You have spoken a great light for my house, and a white day after a
night black as pitch.

Messenger.
But Artembares, who commanded ten thousand horse, is being beaten now
against the rough shore of Sileniae. And Dadaces, captain of a
thousand, took a spear and made a light leap out of his ship. Tenagon,
the true-born chief of the Bactrians, is roaming the sea-beaten island
of Ajax. Lilaeus, Arsames, and Argestes, a third, were spun round the
island where the doves breed and butted the hard ground with their
heads. And from the springs of the Egyptian Nile, their neighbour,
Arcteus, Adeues, and a third, Pharnuchus of the great shield: these
fell out of one ship. Matallus of Chrysa, commander of ten thousand,
leader of the Black Horse, thirty thousand strong, died and soaked his
thick, bushy, tawny beard, changing its colour with a purple dye. And
Arabus the Magian, and Artabes the Bactrian, who has settled now in a
hard country, perished there. Amistris, and Amphistreus, who handled
a spear that gave a lot of trouble, and good Ariomardus, who has
brought grief to Sardis, and Seisames the Mysian, and Tharybis,
commander of five times fifty ships, a Lyrnaean by birth, a handsome
man, lies dead, poor wretch, not lucky at all. And Syennesis, first of
them all for courage, commander of the Cilicians, one man who gave
the enemy more trouble than any other, died with glory. Those are the
commanders I have called to mind. There were many losses. I am
reporting a few.

Queen.
Aiai. These are the highest evils I could hear: shame for the
Persians, and shrill wailing. But turn back and tell me this. How many
Greek ships were there, that they thought themselves fit to join
battle with the Persian fleet and ram it?

Messenger.
If it had gone by numbers, be sure of this, the barbarians would have
won with their ships. The Greeks had ten times thirty ships in all,
and a picked squadron of ten besides. Xerxes, and I know this, had a
thousand under his command, and the ones that were exceptional for
speed were two hundred and seven. That is the count. Do you think we
were outnumbered in that fight? No. It was some god that destroyed the
army, loading the scales with a luck that was not evenly weighed. The
gods keep the goddess Pallas's city safe.

Queen.
Then the city of Athens is still unsacked?

Messenger.
While she has her men, her wall is secure.

Queen.
Tell me how the ships came to engage. Who began the fighting? The
Greeks? Or my son, glorying in the number of his ships?

Messenger.
What began it all, my lady, was a fiend, or some evil spirit, that
appeared from somewhere. A Greek came from the Athenian army to your
son Xerxes and told him this: that when the darkness of black night
came on, the Greeks would not stay, but would jump to the rowing
benches of their ships and save their lives by slipping away, each one
where he could. Xerxes heard it, and he did not see the Greek's trick,
or the gods' jealousy. He gave every ship's captain this order at
once. When the sun stopped burning the earth with its rays and dark
took hold of the precinct of the sky, they were to draw up the main
body of the fleet in three lines to guard the exits and the sounding
channels, and post other ships in a ring around the island of Ajax.
And if the Greeks escaped their fate and found some way to slip off
with their ships, it was laid down that every captain would lose his
head. He said all this with a very confident heart. He did not know
what was coming from the gods.

Our men, in good order and with obedient spirits, got their supper
ready, and every sailor looped his oar to the thole-pin so it ran
well. When the light of the sun had gone and night came on, every
master of an oar and every man in charge of arms went aboard. Line
called out to line along the long ships, and they sailed each to the
station he was given, and all night long the captains kept the whole
fleet cruising back and forth across the strait. Night wore on, and
the Greek fleet made no attempt anywhere to slip out. But when day
with her white horses had spread over all the land, bright to see, the
first thing was a noise from the Greeks, a shout like a song of
triumph, and at the same instant the echo came back high and clear
from the rocks of the island. Terror took hold of all the barbarians.
They had been wrong. The Greeks were not singing their solemn hymn
for flight. They were going into battle, with brave hearts.

Messenger.
Then the trumpet set the whole of their side ablaze. At the word of
command they struck the deep brine together, oars roaring, and in a
moment all of them were there to be seen. The right wing led off
first, well marshalled and in order, and then the whole fleet came on
behind, and at the same time we could hear a great shout: "Forward,
sons of the Greeks! Free your country! Free your children, your wives,
the seats of your fathers' gods and the tombs of your ancestors! Now
the fight is for everything!" And from our side a roar of Persian
speech went out to meet it, and there was no more time to wait. Ship
struck ship, bronze beak on bronze. A Greek ship began the ramming
and sheared off the whole stern of a Phoenician, and after that every
captain aimed his ship at another. At first the flood of the Persian
fleet held its own. But when the mass of the ships was jammed in the
narrows, and no one could help anyone, and they were striking each
other with their own bronze-mouthed rams and splintering the whole
bank of oars, then the Greek ships, who knew exactly what they were
doing, hemmed them in and battered them all round. Hulls rolled over.
You could no longer see the sea for wrecks and slaughtered men. The
beaches and the reefs were full of corpses, and every ship in the
barbarian fleet was rowing away in disorder. And the Greeks kept
hitting them, hacking at them with broken oars and pieces of wreckage,
as if they were tuna or some other haul of fish. Groaning and
shrieking together filled the open sea, until the face of black night
took it away. As for the full extent of the losses, if I talked in
order for ten days I could not get to the end of them for you. But be
sure of this: never before did so great a number of men die in a
single day.

Queen.
Aiai. A great sea of evils has broken over the Persians and the whole
barbarian race.

Messenger.
And know this: the evil is not yet at the halfway mark. Such a
disaster came on them after it as to outweigh all this twice over.

Queen.
What worse luck than this could there be? Say what this other blow
was, that you say fell on the army and tipped the scale further down
into evil.

Messenger.
Those Persians who were in the prime of their strength, the bravest
in spirit, the best born, and always first in loyalty to the King
himself: they are dead, shamefully, by the most inglorious death of
all.

Queen.
Oh, I am wretched, friends, wretched at this disaster. By what death
do you say they died?

Messenger.
There is an island in front of Salamis, a small one, a bad anchorage
for ships, where Pan, who loves the dance, walks the shore by the sea.
Xerxes sent these men there, so that when the enemy were wrecked from
their ships and got away to the island, they could kill the Greek
force easily and rescue their own men out of the channels of the sea.
He read the future badly. For when a god gave the glory of the sea
battle to the Greeks, that same day they fenced their bodies in good
bronze armour and jumped down from their ships and ringed the whole
island, so that our men had no idea where to turn. They were hit
again and again with stones thrown by hand, and arrows flying from
the bowstring kept falling on them and killing. In the end the Greeks
charged with a single roar, and struck, and hacked the poor wretches
limb from limb, until they had put out the life of every one. Xerxes
cried aloud when he saw the depth of the disaster. He had a seat with
a clear view of the whole army, a high hill by the open sea. He tore
his robes and shrieked, gave his orders at once to the land army, and
let it go in disorderly flight. That is the disaster you have to mourn
on top of the first.

Queen.
Hateful god. How you have cheated the Persians of their hopes. My son
has found a bitter revenge on famous Athens. The barbarians that
Marathon destroyed before were not enough. My son thought he would
make them pay for those, and he has drawn this whole mass of suffering
on himself. But the ships that escaped their fate, tell me: where did
you leave them? Can you give a clear account?

Messenger.
The captains of the ships that were left put up sail and fled in a
rush, in no order, wherever the wind took them. And the rest of the
army was dying in the land of the Boeotians, some of them struggling
with thirst beside the sparkling springs, and the rest of us, empty
and gasping, came through to the country of the Phocians and the land
of Doris and the Malian gulf, where the Spercheus waters the plain
with its kindly stream. From there the plain of Achaea and the cities
of Thessaly took us in, starving. There most of us died, of thirst and
hunger, because we had both. We reached the Magnesian country and the
land of the Macedonians, the crossing of the Axius, the reedy marsh of
Bolbe, Mount Pangaeus, the Edonian land. And that night the god raised
winter out of season and froze the whole stream of the holy Strymon
from bank to bank. Men who had never thought anything of the gods
before prayed then, and bowed to earth and sky. And when the army had
finished calling on the gods, it crossed over the frozen ford. Every
one of us who set out before the god scattered his rays got across
safe. But the sun's bright disc, blazing, heated the middle of the
crossing with its flame and cut it through. And they fell in on top of
one another, and the lucky man was the one whose breath broke off
soonest. The rest, the ones who happened to survive, struggled across
Thrace with great hardship and have come home, not many of them, to
the land of their hearths. So the city of the Persians may mourn, and
long for the dearest youth of the land. That is the truth. And I have
left out many of the evils the god hurled down on the Persians.

(The Messenger goes out.)

Chorus.
Terrible god, god of hard labours, how heavily you have jumped with
both feet on the whole Persian race.

Queen.
Oh, I am wretched. The army is destroyed. Vision of my dreams, so
plain in the night, how clearly you showed me the evil. And you, you
read it far too lightly. Still, since your word has settled it so,
first I want to pray to the gods, and then I will come back with gifts
for the Earth and the dead, a cake from my own house. I know it is for
what is already done. But for whatever is still to come, if there is
anything better in it. And you, after what has happened, must bring
faithful counsel to faithful men. And my son, if he comes here before
I get back: comfort him, and see him into the house, so that he does
not add some further evil to the evils we have.

(The Queen goes out.)
