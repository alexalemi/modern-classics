Agamemnon
(Part 3 of 3)

Cassandra.
Now my prophecy will no longer look out at you from behind a veil, like
a bride on her wedding morning. It is going to come clear and rushing,
like a wind that blows into the sunrise, so that a grief far bigger than
mine breaks like a wave against the light. I will not teach you in
riddles any more. And be my witnesses. Run beside me while I follow the
scent of crimes done long ago. There is a choir that never leaves this
roof. It sings in unison, and the sound is ugly, because what it sings
is not good. And it has been drinking, this gang of revellers that stays
in the house and cannot be got out of the door: it has drunk human blood
to make itself bolder, this troop of Furies born with the family. They
sit in the house and sing their song, the song of the first ruin that
began it all, and each in turn they spit in disgust at a brother's bed
and hate the man who trampled it. Did I miss? Or did I hit something,
like an archer? Or am I a lying prophet, a babbler who knocks on doors?
Swear it first, and then be my witness: I know the story of this house's
old sins.

Chorus.
And how could an oath, however honestly the pledge was made, cure
anything? But I am amazed at you. You were raised across the sea, and
you speak of a city that talks another language as if you had been
standing right there.

Cassandra.
The prophet Apollo set me to this work.

Chorus.
Surely not a god, struck with desire?

Cassandra.
Before now I was ashamed to talk about this.

Chorus.
Yes. Everyone is more delicate when things are going well.

Cassandra.
But he was a wrestler, and he breathed his favour on me hard.

Chorus.
And did the two of you come to the making of children, in the usual way?

Cassandra.
I gave Apollo my consent, and then I cheated him.

Chorus.
Were you already taken by the god's art by then?

Cassandra.
Already I was prophesying to my people every suffering that was coming.

Chorus.
Then how did Apollo's anger leave you unharmed?

Cassandra.
After that fault I could persuade no one of anything.

Chorus.
To us, at least, what you prophesy seems worth believing.

Cassandra.
Iou, iou! Oh, oh, the pain! Again the terrible labour of true prophecy
spins me and shakes me with its opening notes, and the opening is
dreadful. Do you see them, these children sitting by the house, young,
like the shapes in dreams? Children killed, it seems, by the people who
should have loved them, their hands full of meat, their own flesh for
food, holding up their guts and innards, a load that would make you
weep, plain to see. And their father tasted them. For that, I tell you,
someone is planning to make them pay: a lion with no courage, rolling in
the bed, keeping the house, oh, against my master when he comes home. My
master, yes. I have to wear the yoke of slavery now. And the commander
of the ships, the man who turned Troy upside down, does not know what
that hateful bitch's tongue is going to bring him, by an evil stroke of
luck. She licked his hand. She talked, and stretched her ears out in
gladness, like a Ruin that works in secret. That is her daring: the
female is the killer of the male. What hateful monster can I call her
and hit the mark? A two-headed snake? Some Scylla living in the rocks,
the wreck of sailors? A raging mother out of Hades, breathing a war
without truce against her own family? And how she shouted for joy, that
woman who dares anything, as if the battle had turned, while she
pretended to be glad he had come home safe. Whether I convince you of
any of this or not makes no difference. Why should it? What is coming
will come. And soon you will stand here and see it, and pity me, and say
I was a prophet who told too much truth.

Chorus.
Thyestes' feast on his children's flesh: that I understood, and I
shudder, and fear has hold of me, hearing it told true, with nothing
made up to look like truth. But the rest of it I heard and fell off the
track, and now I am running blind.

Cassandra.
I say you will see Agamemnon dead.

Chorus.
Poor girl, put your mouth to sleep. Say something lucky.

Cassandra.
There is no healing god standing over these words.

Chorus.
No, not if it is really going to happen. But let it not, somehow.

Cassandra.
You pray. Their business is killing.

Chorus.
What man is getting this pollution ready?

Cassandra.
Then you have wandered a very long way off the track of my prophecies.

Chorus.
I do not understand how the one who does it will do it.

Cassandra.
And yet I know Greek. I know it far too well.

Chorus.
So do the oracles from Delphi, and they are still hard to understand.

Cassandra.
Papai! What fire! It is coming over me. Otototoi! Apollo, Wolf-god, oh,
me, me! This two-footed lioness, who sleeps with a wolf while the noble
lion is away, is going to kill me, poor wretch that I am. She is brewing
a drug, and she swears she will mix my wages into her anger too. She is
sharpening the sword for the man, to pay back murder for bringing me
here. So why do I still wear these things that make a joke of me, the
staff and the prophet's wreaths around my neck? You, at least, I will
destroy before my own hour comes.

(She breaks the staff and throws it down with the wreaths and the rest
of her prophet's dress, and tramples them.)

Go to ruin! There. You are on the ground, and that is how I pay you
back. Make some other woman rich in ruin instead of me. Look, Apollo
himself is stripping me of the prophet's dress. He watched me in this
finery being laughed at, laughed at hard, by friends who were enemies,
all of them of one mind, and all for nothing. They called me a wandering
beggar-woman, a fortune-teller at the door, a starving wretch, and I
bore it. And now the prophet has finished with his prophetess and led me
away to this, to a death like this. Instead of my father's altar a
butcher's block is waiting for me, hot with the red blood of the victim
cut down before the sacrifice, and the victim is me. But we will not die
unhonoured by the gods. Someone else will come, another avenger for us,
a child who kills his mother, a son who makes men pay for his father. An
exile, a wanderer, a stranger to this land, he will come back and set
the coping-stone on these ruins for his family. For the gods have sworn
a great oath: the body of his father, lying on its back, will bring him
home. Why do I stand here wailing and pitying myself? I saw the city of
Troy fare as it fared, and the men who took the city are ending like
this, by the gods' judgement. I will go and do it. I will bear dying. I
greet these doors as the gates of Hades. And I pray for a clean stroke,
so that I close these eyes without a struggle, with the blood running
out and death coming easy.

Chorus.
Woman, you have suffered so much and you know so much, and you have gone
on a long time. But if you really know your own death, how can you walk
to the altar so bravely, like a cow the god drives?

Cassandra.
There is no escape, friends. No. Not with any more time.

Chorus.
But the last of the time is worth the most.

Cassandra.
The day is here. I would gain very little by running.

Chorus.
Then know this: you are bearing it with a brave heart.

Cassandra.
Nobody says that to the happy ones.

Chorus.
But to die with honour is a grace for a mortal.

Cassandra.
Oh, father. Oh, for you and your noble children.

(She stops at the door and turns back.)

Chorus.
What is it? What fear is turning you back?

Cassandra.
Oh. Oh.

Chorus.
Why that cry? Unless it is some horror in your mind.

Cassandra.
The house breathes murder. It drips blood.

Chorus.
And what of it? That is the smell of the sacrifices at the hearth.

Cassandra.
It is the same kind of breath that comes off a grave.

Chorus.
That is not Syrian incense you are describing, to make the house
splendid.

Cassandra.
Well, I will go in, and wail inside the house too, for my own fate and
Agamemnon's. Enough of life. Oh, friends. I am not shrinking in fear
like a bird at a bush for no reason. When I am dead, be my witnesses to
this: when a woman dies for me, a woman, and a man falls for the man who
married badly. I ask this of you as a guest's gift, now that I am going
to die.

Chorus.
Poor woman, I pity you for the death you have foretold.

Cassandra.
Once more I want to speak, one speech, not a dirge, my own, for myself.
I pray to the sun, in the last of his light I will see, that my enemies,
my murderers, pay my avengers in the same coin for a slave killed, an
easy thing to overpower. Oh, the affairs of men. When they go well, a
shadow can turn them over. When they go badly, a wet sponge, a few
strokes, and the picture is gone. And of the two, it is the second I
pity far more.

(She goes into the house.)

Chorus.
	Doing well is a thing no mortal
	ever gets enough of.
	Nobody points at a great house
	and turns it away from the door
	with the words, Come in no more.
	And to this man the blessed gods
	gave Priam's city to take,
	and he comes home honoured by heaven.
	But now, if he must pay for the blood of those before him,
	and by dying for the dead
	bring other deaths to pass in payment,
	what mortal, hearing this, could boast
	he was born to a fate that does him no harm?

(A cry comes from inside the house.)

Agamemnon.
Oh! I am hit. A deep blow, a deadly one, in here.

Chorus.
Quiet. Who is that crying out, hit with a deadly blow?

Agamemnon.
Oh! Again. I am hit a second time.

Chorus.
The deed is done, I think, by the king's groaning. But let us put our
heads together, in case there is some safe plan.

(The old men speak one after another, each giving his own view.)

Chorus.
I tell you my opinion: shout for the townspeople, call them here to the
house.

I say we break in as fast as we can and prove the deed while the sword
is still dripping.

I share that opinion, and I vote we do something. This is no time to
hang back.

It is plain to see. Their first move announces what they mean for the
city: a tyranny.

Yes, because we are wasting time. And they are trampling the fine name
of Delay into the dirt, and their hands are not asleep.

I do not know what plan I could hit on to propose. The man who acts
should be the man who plans it too.

I am the same. I cannot find a way to raise a dead man again with words.

What, are we to drag our lives out like this and bow to these men who
defile the house, and let them lead us?

No. It cannot be borne. Better to die. Death is a gentler fate than
tyranny.

Are we really going to take groans as evidence and prophesy that the man
is dead?

We should know for certain before we get angry about it. Guessing is one
thing and knowing is another.

That is the view I find myself pushed into from every side: to find out
clearly how it stands with the son of Atreus.

(The doors of the house open. Agamemnon lies dead in a silver bath,
tangled in the robe, and Cassandra's body lies beside his. Clytemnestra
stands over them with the sword in her hand.)

Clytemnestra.
I said a great deal before to suit the moment, and I will not be ashamed
to say the opposite now. How else could a woman, working out hatred
against people she hated, people who pretended to be her friends, fence
in the nets of ruin too high to jump out of? This contest is an old one,
from an old quarrel. I have been thinking about it for a long time, and
it has come at last, late as it is. I stand where I struck, over the
work as I finished it. And I did it like this, and I will not deny it:
so that he could neither run from his death nor fight it off, I threw a
net around him with no way out, the kind you throw for fish, a robe, a
rich and deadly robe. I struck him twice, and with two groans his legs
went from under him. And when he was down I gave him a third blow, a
thank-offering to the Zeus under the earth who keeps the dead safe, a
prayer fulfilled. So he lay there and coughed his life out, and as he
blew out the sharp spurt of his blood he hit me with a dark spray of
bloody dew. And I was glad of it. It fell on me like rain from heaven on
the sprouting corn, and I was as glad as the corn is, when the buds are
being born. Since that is how things are, old men of Argos, be glad of
it, if you can be glad. I exult in it. And if it were the right thing to
pour a libation over a dead body, over this one it would be just. It
would be more than just. He filled a great bowl in this house with so
many cursed evils, and now he has come home and drunk it down himself.

Chorus.
We are amazed at your tongue, how bold it is in your mouth, to boast a
speech like that over your husband.

Clytemnestra.
You are testing me as if I were a woman with no sense. My heart does not
tremble, and I say this to people who know it already. Praise me or
blame me, it is all the same. This is Agamemnon, my husband, and a
corpse, the work of this right hand, a craftsman who did justice. That
is how things stand.

Chorus.
	Woman, what evil thing
	did you eat that grew out of the earth,
	what did you drink that welled up from the running sea,
	that you took this sacrifice on yourself, and the curses the people roar?
	You threw him off. You cut him off. And you will be cut off from the city,
	a huge hatred to its people.

Clytemnestra.
Now you sentence me to exile from the city, to the hatred of the
townspeople and the curses the people roar. But then you brought nothing
against this man. He thought no more of it than the death of a sheep,
when his flocks were thick with fleece: he sacrificed his own child, the
child I bore, my dearest labour, to charm the winds of Thrace. Was it
not him you should have driven out of this land, to pay for the filth he
brought on it? But when you hear what I have done, you are a hard judge.
Well, I tell you this. Threaten me like that, and understand that I am
ready: if you beat me by force, on equal terms, you rule me. But if the
god decides otherwise, you will learn, late as the lesson comes, to keep
yourselves in hand.

Chorus.
	Your plans are big
	and your talk is arrogant.
	Just as your mind is mad
	with the luck that drips with slaughter,
	so the smear of blood on your eyes shows plain.
	Unhonoured yet, stripped of your friends,
	you will pay blow for blow.

Clytemnestra.
Then hear this too, the right I swear by: by Justice, who brought my
child's case to its end, by Ruin, and by the Fury, to whom I cut this
man's throat, hope does not walk the halls of fear for me, so long as
Aegisthus lights the fire on my hearth and keeps faith with me as he has
before. He is no small shield for my confidence. Here lies the man who
wronged his wife, the pet of every Chryseis under the walls of Troy. And
here she lies, his prisoner, his seer, his bedfellow, his faithful whore
who talked oracles and knew the sailors' benches as well as he did. The
two have got what they deserved. He, like that. She, like a swan, sang
her last dying lament and lies here, his lover. And to me she has
brought a side dish for my bed, an extra to my pleasure.

Chorus.
	Oh, if only some fate would come, quickly,
	without too much pain,
	without long lying in bed,
	and bring us the sleep that never ends, that lasts for ever,
	now that our kindest guardian is brought down,
	who suffered so much for a woman's sake
	and has lost his life at a woman's hand.

Chorus.
	Oh, oh, Helen, out of your mind,
	one woman who destroyed so many,
	so very many lives under Troy.
	Now you have put on your last crown, the one they will remember,
	for blood that cannot be washed out.
	Truly there was a spirit of strife in this house then,
	strong-built, and a man's misery.

Clytemnestra.
	Do not pray for a share of death
	because these things weigh on you,
	and do not turn your anger on Helen
	as a killer of men, as if she alone
	destroyed the lives of so many Greeks
	and worked a pain that nothing can heal.

Chorus.
	Spirit that falls on this house
	and on the two grandsons of Tantalus,
	you rule through women with a strength that matches theirs,
	and it bites at my heart.
	She stands over the body like a hateful crow,
	against all law,
	and glories in the song she sings.

Clytemnestra.
	Now you have set your mouth's judgement straight,
	calling on the spirit of this family,
	the one fattened three times over.
	He is the one who feeds the lust for lapping blood
	in the belly of this house: before the old wound
	stops hurting, there is new blood.

Chorus.
	Yes, a great spirit you speak of,
	a heavy anger that keeps this house.
	Oh, oh, an evil tale to tell,
	of a ruinous fate that is never full.
	Ie, ie, through Zeus,
	the cause of all, the worker of all.
	For what is brought to pass for mortals without Zeus?
	Which of these things is not the gods' doing?

Chorus.
	Oh, oh, my King, my King,
	how shall I weep for you?
	What can I say, out of a heart that loved you?
	You lie in this spider's web,
	breathing your life out in a godless death.
	Oh, oh, this bed no free man should lie on,
	brought down by a treacherous death
	from your wife's hand, with a two-edged weapon.

Clytemnestra.
	You claim this deed is mine?
	Do not even count me
	as Agamemnon's wife.
	It was the old, bitter avenger
	of Atreus, that cruel host at the feast,
	who took the shape of this dead man's wife
	and paid him back with this,
	a grown man sacrificed on top of the children.

Chorus.
	That you are guiltless of this murder,
	who will witness that? How? How?
	And yet the avenger from his father's side
	could have been your helper.
	Ares, the black god of war, forces his way on
	through streams of kindred blood,
	and wherever he goes forward he will grant justice
	for the clotted gore of children eaten.

Chorus.
	Oh, oh, my King, my King,
	how shall I weep for you?
	What can I say, out of a heart that loved you?
	You lie in this spider's web,
	breathing your life out in a godless death.
	Oh, oh, this bed no free man should lie on,
	brought down by a treacherous death
	from your wife's hand, with a two-edged weapon.

Clytemnestra.
	I do not think his death
	was one no free man should die.
	Did he not bring a treacherous ruin
	on this house himself?
	He did to the child I grew from him,
	Iphigenia, so much wept for,
	what he deserved to suffer for. Let him not boast
	in Hades: killed by the sword,
	he has paid with death for what he began.

Chorus.
	I have no plan. I am stripped
	of the ready thought that could tell me
	where to turn, and the house is falling.
	I am afraid of the beat of the rain of blood
	that shakes the house: it is not drops any more.
	And Fate is sharpening justice for another deed of harm
	on other whetstones.

Chorus.
	Oh, earth, earth, if only you had taken me
	before I saw this man lying low
	in the silver-sided bath.
	Who will bury him? Who will sing his dirge?
	Will you dare do it, you who killed
	your own husband, to wail over him
	and pay his soul a graceless grace
	for great things done, and wrong him again?
	Who will speak the praise over the godlike man's tomb,
	with tears falling,
	and labour at it with truth in his heart?

Clytemnestra.
	That care is not yours to trouble about.
	By our hands
	he fell, he died, and we will bury him,
	and not with weeping from the house.
	No: Iphigenia, his daughter,
	as she should, will meet her father gladly
	at the fast-running ferry of sorrows,
	and throw her arms around him, and kiss him.

Chorus.
	Here comes reproach to answer reproach.
	It is hard to judge.
	The robber is robbed. The killer pays.
	And it stays, while Zeus stays on his throne:
	the doer must suffer. That is the law.
	Who could throw the seed of the curse out of the house?
	The family is glued to ruin.

Clytemnestra.
	You have walked into that prophecy with the truth on your side.
	And I, for my part, am willing
	to swear an oath to the spirit of the house of Pleisthenes:
	I will be content with this, hard as it is to bear,
	and for the rest, let him go out of this house
	and wear down some other family
	with deaths inside itself.
	A small share of the goods is enough for me, all I want,
	if I can take the madness of kin killing kin
	out of these halls.

(Aegisthus comes in with armed men behind him.)

Aegisthus.
Kind light of the day that brings justice! Now at last I can say that
the gods who avenge men look down from above on the sufferings of the
earth, now that I see this man lying here, to my delight, in the woven
robe the Furies made, paying in full for the schemes of his father's
hand. For Atreus, who ruled this land, this man's father, when his right
to rule was challenged, drove my father Thyestes, to say it plainly, his
own brother, out of the city and out of his home. And when the wretched
Thyestes came back as a suppliant to the hearth, he found a fate safe
enough for himself: he did not die and stain his native ground with his
own blood. But as his guest-gift, this man's godless father Atreus, more
eager than friendly, pretending to keep a feast-day cheerfully with a
meal of meat, served my father a dinner of his own children's flesh. The
feet, and the fingers spread out to their tips, he broke off and hid
underneath, and the men sat each at his own table. My father took the
unrecognisable pieces at once, not knowing, and ate a meal that has
been, as you see, the ruin of the family. Then he realised the unholy
thing that had been done and screamed, and fell back vomiting the
butchered flesh, and called down a fate too heavy to bear on the house
of Pelops, kicking the table over to go with his curse: so perish the
whole race of Pleisthenes. That is why you see this man fallen here. And
I am the one who stitched this murder together, and justly. He drove me
out too, with my miserable father, the third child, a baby still in
swaddling clothes. But I grew up, and Justice brought me home again. I
laid hands on this man from outside the house, and I put together every
piece of the plan that undid him. So now even death would be a fine
thing for me, having seen him in the net of Justice.

Chorus.
Aegisthus, I have no respect for outrage in the middle of misery. You
say you killed this man on purpose, and that you alone planned this
pitiful murder. Then I say your head will not escape, in a court of
justice, the curses the people throw, and the stones. Be sure of it.

Aegisthus.
You talk like that, you, sitting at the lower oar, when the men on the
upper bench hold the ship? Old as you are, you will learn how heavy a
lesson is for a man your age, when the lesson set is to keep yourself in
hand. Chains and the pangs of hunger, even in old age, are the very best
prophet-doctors for teaching the mind. You have eyes. Do you not see
this? Do not kick against the goad. You will hit it and hurt yourself.

Chorus.
Woman. You stayed at home and waited for the men to come back from the
fighting, disgracing a man's bed all the while, and you plotted this
death for the general of the army?

Aegisthus.
Those words too will be the start of your tears. You have a tongue that
is the opposite of Orpheus's. He led everything after him with joy at
his voice. You have stirred us up with your childish yelping, and you
will be led away. When you are mastered you will look tamer.

Chorus.
So you are going to be tyrant over the men of Argos, you, who planned
this man's death and then did not have the courage to do the deed with
your own hand?

Aegisthus.
Because the trapping was clearly the woman's job. I was an old enemy,
and suspected. But now I will try to rule the citizens with this man's
money. And the one who does not obey, I will yoke him under a heavy
yoke, and he will be no well-fed trace-horse on barley for me. No.
Hunger, that hateful lodger who lives with darkness, will see him
gentle.

Chorus.
Then why, out of the cowardice of your soul, did you not kill the man
yourself? Why did a woman kill him, a pollution on the land and on the
gods of the land? Does Orestes still see the light somewhere, so that he
can come home with luck on his side and kill this pair, and win
outright?

Aegisthus.
Since you mean to act and talk like this, you will learn soon. Come on,
my men, my guard: your work is not far off.

Chorus.
Come on, then. Every man get his sword ready, hand on the hilt.

Aegisthus.
I have my hand on my hilt too, and I do not refuse to die.

Chorus.
Die, you say. We take the omen. We choose that luck.

Clytemnestra.
No, dearest of men. Let us not do any more harm. Even this is a great
deal to reap, a wretched harvest. There is enough suffering already. Let
us not have more blood. Go home, honoured old men, and give way to what
is fated before you get hurt. What we did had to be done. And if this
trouble turns out to be enough, we will accept it, struck hard as we
have been by the heavy claw of the spirit. That is a woman's counsel, if
anyone thinks it worth learning.

Aegisthus.
But that these men should pick the flowers of empty talk and throw them
at me, and fling out words like that, and put their luck to the test,
and miss the sensible line, and insult the man in power!

Chorus.
It would not be like men of Argos to fawn on a coward.

Aegisthus.
I will come after you yet, in the days to come.

Chorus.
Not if the god steers Orestes home.

Aegisthus.
I know how exiles feed on hope.

Chorus.
Go on. Get fat, and dirty justice, while you can.

Aegisthus.
Know that you will pay me for this stupidity.

Chorus.
Crow away, brave, like a cock beside his hen.

Clytemnestra.
Pay no attention to their empty yelping. You and I are masters of this
house, and you and I will set this house in order.
