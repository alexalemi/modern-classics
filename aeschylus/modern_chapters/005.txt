The Suppliants
(Part 2 of 2)

Chorus.
	Lord of lords, most blessed of the blessed,
	power most perfect of all perfect powers,
	Zeus, the fortunate one: hear us.
	Keep the outrage of these men away from your own blood.
	Hate it well, and keep it far off.
	Throw their ruin, their black-benched ships,
	into the purple sea.

Chorus.
	Look at the women's side of this.
	Look at our race, old in the story,
	and at the woman you loved, our ancestor,
	and renew the kind old tale.
	Remember it all, you who laid your hand on Io.
	We say we are the children of Zeus,
	settlers who went out from this land.

Chorus.
	I have come back to the old tracks,
	to the meadow where my mother was watched
	as she grazed among the flowers,
	the pasture rich for cattle, where Io,
	stung to madness by the gadfly,
	ran with her wits gone,
	passing through tribe after tribe of men,
	and, as fate had cut it, split the surging strait in two
	and marked out the land on the other shore.

Chorus.
	She races on through the land of Asia,
	straight through Phrygia, where the sheep graze,
	past the Mysian city of Teuthras,
	through the hollows of Lydia,
	over the mountains of the Cilicians
	and the tribes of the Pamphylians,
	over rivers that never run dry,
	over deep, rich earth,
	and the land of Aphrodite, heavy with wheat.

Chorus.
	Driven on by the sting of the winged herdsman,
	she comes at last to the holy grove of Zeus
	that feeds every living thing,
	to the meadow fed by snow that Typhon's fury attacks,
	to the water of the Nile that no sickness can touch,
	maddened by torments no one should have to suffer
	and by the pain of the goad that pierced her,
	Hera's frenzied dancer.

Chorus.
	And the mortals who lived in that land then
	shook with green fear in their hearts
	at a sight they had never seen,
	a creature hard to look at, half beast and half human,
	one part cow
	and one part woman. They stared at the monster.
	And then, who was it who soothed her,
	the far wanderer, the wretched one,
	Io, driven by the gadfly?

Chorus.
	Zeus, who rules through endless time.
	Zeus.
	By the strength of his hand, which does no harm,
	and by his divine breath,
	she was stilled, and she wept out
	the grief and the shame in tears.
	She took Zeus into herself, and the story is true:
	she bore a child without a fault,

Chorus.
	blessed through the long ages.
	And so the whole earth shouts,
	This truly is the child of Zeus,
	who gives life to everything.
	For who else could have stopped
	the sickness Hera plotted against her?
	Say it was the work of Zeus, say this race
	comes from Epaphus, and you have the truth.

Chorus.
	Which of the gods has done deeds
	that give me a better right to call on him?
	The father himself, the planter, who made us with his own hand,
	the lord, the great, old-minded builder of our race,
	who devises everything, Zeus, the fair wind behind us.

Chorus.
	He does not sit under anyone's command,
	a lesser power ruling at a greater one's pleasure.
	No one is enthroned above him whose strength he bows to.
	His deed is as quick as his word.
	Whatever his counselling mind carries, he hurries to do it.

(Danaus comes back from the city.)

Danaus.
Take heart, children. All is well with the people of this land. The
people have voted, and the decrees they passed carry full authority.

Chorus.
Welcome, old man, welcome, dearest of all messengers to me. Tell us
how it was settled, how the people's ruling hand fell, which way the
weight of the vote came down.

Danaus.
The Argives decided, and not by a split vote. They decided in a way
that made my old heart young again. The air bristled with right
hands, the whole people at once, as they ratified this proposal into
law: that we are to live in this land as settlers, free, not to be
seized, and safe from any man's violence; that nobody, native or
foreigner, may carry us off; and that if anyone uses force, any
landholder who fails to come to our help loses his rights and is
driven out of the country by the people.

That is the speech the King of the Pelasgians made on our behalf, and
it persuaded them. He warned them of the great anger of Zeus, the
protector of suppliants, and said the city must never, in time to
come, fatten that anger. He told them that a double pollution, from
strangers and from citizens both at once, appearing before the city,
would be a feeding-ground for grief past all cure. When the Argive
people heard that, they decided by a show of hands, without waiting
for the crier's call, that so it should be. The Pelasgian people
listened to the windings of his speech and were easily persuaded. But
it was Zeus who brought it to its end.

Chorus.
	Come now, let us speak good prayers
	over the Argives, good returned for good.
	And may Zeus, god of strangers, look on
	this thanks from a stranger's mouth
	and see it true, and see it reach its goal with nothing lacking.

Chorus.
	Now, gods born of Zeus,
	hear me as I pour out
	prayers for our kin.
	Never may the Pelasgian land,
	this city, be gutted by fire
	at the hands of Ares, who never has enough of the war-cry,
	the lecher of battle,
	who reaps his harvest of men
	in fields other men have ploughed.
	Because they pitied us
	and cast a kind vote,
	and respect the suppliants of Zeus,
	this poor flock of ours.

Chorus.
	They did not cast their votes
	with the males and trample down
	the women's quarrel.
	They looked to Zeus,
	the avenger, the watcher
	no one can fight, whom no house
	would want to have
	settling on its roof
	and fouling it. He sits heavy.
	They honour their own blood,
	suppliants of holy Zeus.
	And so, with clean altars,
	they will please the gods.

Chorus.
	So let a prayer fly up,
	eager to honour them,
	out of mouths in the shadow of these branches.
	Never may plague
	empty this city of its men.
	Never may civil strife
	bloody the ground of the land with the bodies of its own people.
	Let the flower of its youth
	stand unplucked. Let Ares,
	who shares Aphrodite's bed and kills men,
	never cut off its bloom.

Chorus.
	And let the altars where the old men gather
	be crowded with old men
	and blaze with their offerings.
	So may the city be well governed,
	its people honouring great Zeus,
	and above all Zeus, god of strangers,
	who by his grey old law sets destiny straight.
	We pray that guardians of the land
	will always be born to take the others' place,
	and that Artemis, who is Hecate too,
	will watch over the women when they give birth.

Chorus.
	Let no man-killing slaughter
	come on this city
	to tear it apart,
	arming Ares, who has no dance and no lyre
	and is the father of tears,
	and arming the shout of civil war.
	Let the joyless swarm of diseases
	settle far from the heads of its people.
	And may Apollo the Wolf God be kind
	to all the young.

Chorus.
	May Zeus bring the earth to its full fruit,
	with the produce of every season.
	May the grazing herds in the fields
	breed and multiply.
	May they have everything from the gods.
	At the altars, let singers set
	a music of good omen,
	and out of clean mouths let the song
	that loves the lyre go up.

Chorus.
	May the people, who hold the city's power,
	guard its rights without fear,
	a government that thinks ahead and counsels for the common good.
	And to strangers, before they arm Ares,
	may they give fair terms of settlement,
	justice without grief.

Chorus.
	And the gods who hold this land forever,
	may they always honour them, in the way of their fathers,
	with the laurel branch carried high and the ox slaughtered.
	For respect for parents
	stands written third
	among the laws of Justice, who is honoured above all.

Danaus.
I approve these prayers, dear children; they are sensible. But do not
be frightened when you hear from your father news you did not expect,
and news that is new. From this look-out, here on the shrine that
shelters suppliants, I can see the ship. It is well marked, and there
is no mistaking it: the trimming of the sail, the leather screens
along the sides, the prow whose painted eyes look out on the road
ahead, obeying the steering-oar at the stern all too well, for those
who are not its friends. The men on board are plain to see, their
black limbs against their white linen clothes. And the other ships,
and the whole supporting fleet, are in clear view. The lead ship
herself has furled her sail close under the land and is rowing in
with every oar beating.

But you must look at this calmly and with your wits about you, and
not forget these gods. I will go, and come back with helpers and
advocates for our case. Perhaps some herald or embassy will come,
wanting to take you away, to lay hands on you as if you were their
property. None of that will happen. Do not be afraid of them. Still,
in case we are slow to answer your cry, it is better never for a
moment to forget the help you have right here. Take courage. In time,
on the appointed day, the man who dishonours the gods pays the
penalty.

Chorus.
Father, I am afraid. The ships come on such quick wings. There is no
time left in between.

Chorus.
	Fear has me all round. Terror. So it is true.
	Was all my long running any use to me at all?
	Father, I am dying of fright.

Danaus.
The Argives' vote is final, children. Take heart. They will fight for
you. I know it for certain.

Chorus.
The lustful sons of Aegyptus are a race that should be wiped out, and
they never have enough of a fight. I am telling a man who knows.

Chorus.
	In ships bolted tight with timber, dark-eyed at the prow,
	they have sailed here, and their anger has found its mark,
	with a great dark army at their backs.

Danaus.
And they will find plenty of men here whose arms are filed hard by the
noon heat.

Chorus.
Do not leave me alone. I beg you, father. A woman alone is nothing,
and there is no Ares in her.

Chorus.
	They are murderous-minded and full of tricks,
	unclean in their hearts, like ravens,
	caring nothing for altars.

Danaus.
That would work out well for us, children, if the gods hated them as
much as you do.

Chorus.
No, father. No fear of these tridents or the holy things of the gods
will ever keep their hands off us.

Chorus.
	They are too proud, mad with an unholy rage,
	shameless as dogs, and they hear
	nothing the gods say.

Danaus.
But there is a saying that wolves are stronger than dogs. And the
fruit of the papyrus does not beat the ear of wheat.

Chorus.
They have the tempers of lewd and godless beasts. We must guard
against them, and fast.

Danaus.
A fleet is not quick to get itself ashore, nor quick to moor, when
the cables that keep it safe have to be carried up onto the land.
Even at anchor, the shepherds of ships do not feel safe straight
away, especially when they have come to a coast with no harbour and
the sun is going down into night. Night tends to breed worry in a
careful pilot. And an army cannot be landed properly before the ship
trusts her mooring. You keep your mind on this: for all your fear, do
not neglect the gods. I will get help and come back. The city will
not fault its messenger for being old, when his mind and his tongue
are young.

(Danaus goes out toward the city.)

Chorus.
	Oh, land of hills, land we rightly worship,
	what will become of us? Where in this land of Apis can we run,
	if there is any dark hiding-hole anywhere?
	Let me turn into black smoke
	and neighbour the clouds of Zeus.
	Let me lift, without wings, and vanish altogether,
	unseen, like dust,
	and be gone.

Chorus.
	There is no escaping the evil now.
	My heart is black and it shudders.
	My father's look-out has caught me. I am going out of my mind with fear.
	I would rather find my death
	in a noose, in the knotted rope,
	before a man I loathe
	comes near this skin of mine.
	Let Hades be my master. I will die first.

Chorus.
	Where could I find a seat high in the air,
	where the wet clouds turn into snow,
	or some smooth cliff no goat can climb,
	out of everyone's sight, brooding alone,
	overhanging, a haunt of vultures,
	to witness my long fall,
	before a marriage that would tear my heart
	is forced on me?

Chorus.
	After that, I would not refuse to be
	carrion for dogs and a feast for the birds of this country.
	Death sets you free
	from evils that love a wail. Let it come,
	let death come and find me
	before the marriage bed.
	What other road of escape can I cut,
	one that frees me from marriage?

Chorus.
	Shriek it to the sky,
	songs, sad songs of prayer to the gods.
	Father, see that somehow they reach their end
	and set me free, and set me at peace.
	Look on this violence with no kindness in your eyes,
	your just eyes.
	Respect your suppliants,
	Zeus, who holds the earth and has all the power.

Chorus.
	For the race of Aegyptus, the male race,
	unbearable in their outrage,
	are running me down, a fugitive,
	with their loud, shouting lust,
	and they want to take me by force.
	But yours, and only yours, is the beam of the balance.
	Without you, what is ever brought to its end
	for mortals?

(An Egyptian ship has landed. The Herald of the sons of Aegyptus
comes up from the shore with armed men behind him.)

Chorus.
	Oh, oh, oh. Ah, ah, ah.
	Here he is, the snatcher, off the ship and onto the land.
	Before that, snatcher, may you drop dead.
	I cry out, I cry out the pain.
	I see the start of it, the violence I am going to suffer.
	Eh, eh.
	Run for shelter, run.
	He is savage in his heart and he swaggers,
	unbearable on ship and on land.
	Lord of the land, stand in front of us.

Herald.
	Move, move, to the boat,
	as fast as your feet can carry you.
	Or else, or else,
	hair torn out, hair torn out, and the goad,
	and bloody, a bloody
	cutting off of heads.
	Move, move, damn you, damn you, to the boat.

Chorus.
	I wish you had drowned on the wide salt road
	with all your masters' outrage
	and your ship bolted with nails.
	You men of blood, with your boat, your boat.

Herald.
	I order you to stop your shrieking.
	Give up the madness in your heart.
	Iou, iou.
	Leave your seats. Go to the ship.
	You have no honour and no city, and I do not respect you.

Chorus.
	Never again let me see
	the water that fattens the cattle,
	where the blood that gives life to men
	grows and thrives.
	I belong to this land, I am of old, deep stock here,
	old man, of the deep-rooted stock.

Herald.
	You will go on board, on board,
	fast, whether you want to or not,
	by force, by force, and plenty of it.
	Go, before you suffer the worst of it
	and die under our hands.

Chorus.
	Aiai, aiai.
	May you die past all help,
	driven off course across the sea's salt grove,
	along the sandy mound of Sarpedon's tomb,
	wandering under the east winds.

Herald.
	Shriek and scream and call on the gods.
	You will not jump clear of the Egyptian boat.
	Shriek and shout.
	Pour out a song more bitter than grief.

Chorus.
	Oioi, oioi,
	the outrage. You bark ahead of the land
	and swagger over the sea, you crocodile.
	May the great Nile, who watches you,
	turn you back from your outrage,
	you and your unseen violence, and blot you out.

Herald.
I order you to the boat that turns both ways. As fast as you can. Let no
one hang back. Dragging has no respect for a head of hair.

Chorus.
	Oioi, father. The help of the holy image
	is ruining me. He is dragging me to the sea,
	step by step, like a spider.
	A dream, a black dream.
	Otototoi.
	Mother Earth, Mother Earth, turn away
	his terrible cry.
	Oh father, son of Earth, Zeus.

Herald.
I am not afraid of the gods of this place. They did not rear me. They
never fed me into old age.

Chorus.
	He is close, he is raging, the two-footed snake.
	Like a viper he has me,
	he has bitten my foot and holds on.
	Otototoi.
	Mother Earth, Mother Earth, turn away
	his terrible cry.
	Oh father, son of Earth, Zeus.

Herald.
If none of you agrees to this and goes to the ship, the tearing will
have no pity on the cloth of your dresses.

Chorus.
We are lost. King, we are suffering things no god allows...

Herald.
Kings? You will soon see plenty of kings, the sons of Aegyptus. Take
heart. You will not be able to complain that no one is in charge.

Chorus.
Io, leaders of the city, chiefs, I am being overpowered!

Herald.
It looks as if I will have to grab you by the hair and drag you,
since you do not listen sharply to what I say.

(The King comes in with armed men.)

King.
You there. What do you think you are doing? What kind of arrogance
made you dishonour this land of Pelasgian men? Do you think you have
come to a city of women? For a barbarian dealing with Greeks you take
far too many liberties. You have got a great deal wrong and nothing
right.

Herald.
And what have I done wrong here, that goes against my rights?

King.
First of all, you do not know how to be a stranger.

Herald.
How do I not? I found what I had lost and I am taking it.

King.
Which of our citizens did you tell, whose duty it is to look after
strangers?

Herald.
Hermes the Searcher, greatest of all who look after strangers.

King.
You name the gods and you show them no respect.

Herald.
The gods I respect are the gods by the Nile.

King.
And ours are nothing, from what I hear you say?

Herald.
I will take these women, unless someone tears them out of my hands.

King.
You will be sorry if you touch them, and not in the long run either.

Herald.
I hear you. And what you say is no way to treat a guest.

King.
No. I do not host men who rob the gods.

Herald.
I will go and tell this to the sons of Aegyptus.

King.
That is a thought my pride will not graze on.

Herald.
But so that I can know it and report it more clearly, since it is a
herald's duty to report every detail exactly, what am I to say? Who
am I to say took this band of women, their own cousins, away from me,
when I come back? Ares does not judge a case like this by calling
witnesses. He does not settle the quarrel by taking silver. Before it
is over, many men fall and kick their lives away.

King.
Why should I tell you my name? You will learn it in time, you and
your shipmates. As for these women: if they are willing, if their
hearts are in it, you may take them, if a god-fearing argument
persuades them. But the people of this city have passed a single
decree, all of one mind, never to give up this company of women to
force. The bolt is driven clean through it, to hold and stay fixed.
This is not written on tablets or sealed up in the folds of a book.
You hear it plainly from a tongue that speaks free. Now get out of my
sight, and fast.

Herald.
It seems we are about to start a new war. May victory and power go to
the males.

King.
Males? You will find that the men who live in this land are males
too, and they do not drink beer brewed from barley.

(The Herald goes back down to the ships.)

But you, all of you, with your dear serving women, take heart and go
into our well-walled city, closed in by a deep engineering of towers.
There are plenty of public houses, and I am not housed with a small
hand myself. There you can live in well-prepared rooms with many
others, or, if it pleases you more, you may live in houses of your
own. Choose the best of these, whatever pleases your hearts most. I
am your protector, I and all the citizens, whose vote this is that is
now taking effect. Why wait for anyone with more authority than that?

Chorus.
	Good things for good things. May you overflow with them,
	godlike son of the Pelasgians.
	And be kind, and send our father here,
	brave Danaus, who thinks ahead
	and leads our counsels. It is for him first
	to plan where we should live
	and what quarter is friendly. Everyone is quick
	to find fault with people who speak a foreign tongue.
	May the best come of it.
	With a good name, and with no angry word
	from the people of this land,
	take your places, dear slaves, just as
	Danaus allotted to each of you
	her share of service, as her dowry.

(The King goes out toward the city. Danaus comes back with a
bodyguard of Argive spearmen.)

Danaus.
Children, we should pray to the Argives, and sacrifice to them, and
pour libations to them as if to Olympian gods, because they are our
saviours, and not by a split vote. They heard from me what happened,
what your cousins did to their own kin, and it made them bitter
against them. And they assigned me these spearmen as an escort, so
that I should have rank and honour, and should not be killed unawares
by a spear from an ambush and become a burden on the land that never
dies. When people receive favours like these, gratitude has to be
honoured all the more, from the bottom of the heart.

And write this too, beside the many other wise rules of your father
already written in your minds: an unknown company is tested by time.
Everyone has an evil tongue ready for an immigrant, and it is easy to
say something filthy about him. So I urge you: do not shame me, now
that you have in you the ripeness that turns men's heads. Tender
summer fruit is never easy to guard. Beasts spoil it, and men, why
not, and creatures that fly and creatures that walk the earth.
Aphrodite cries out the news of fruit that drips with ripeness, and
every man who passes by, overcome by desire, shoots the eye's
bewitching arrow at the soft beauty of virgins. So let us not suffer
the very thing we took so much trouble to avoid, ploughing so much
sea with our ship, and let us not bring shame on ourselves and
pleasure to my enemies. We have two kinds of housing offered, one
from Pelasgus and one from the city, to live in and pay nothing.
These terms are easy. Only keep your father's instructions, and value
your chastity above your life.

Chorus.
For all the rest, may the Olympian gods give us good luck. As for the
ripeness of my body, father, be easy. Unless the gods have planned
something new, I will not turn my mind off the track it has followed.

Danaids.
	Come now, go on, and glorify
	the blessed gods who rule the city and keep it,
	and those who live around
	the ancient stream of Erasinus.
	And you, our attendants, take up the song.
	Let our praise be for this city of the Pelasgians,
	and let us no longer honour with our hymns
	the mouths of the Nile,

Danaids.
	but the rivers that pour their gentle drink
	through this land and give it many children,
	soothing its soil
	with their fat streams.
	May pure Artemis look on this company
	with pity, and may marriage never come
	by Aphrodite's compulsion.
	Let that prize go to the people I hate.

Serving Women.
	But this kindly song of ours does not neglect Aphrodite,
	for with Hera she has the power nearest to Zeus,
	and the goddess of many wiles is honoured
	for her solemn work.
	At their dear mother's side stand her partners,
	Desire, and the one who is refused nothing,
	Persuasion, who enchants.
	And Harmony has her share of Aphrodite,
	and so do the whispering paths of the Loves.

Serving Women.
	But for the fugitives I am afraid of what is blowing in:
	bitter pain and bloody wars.
	Why did they have such fair sailing,
	with the pursuit so quick behind them?
	What is fated will happen.
	The great mind of Zeus cannot be stepped over.
	It has no bounds.
	Many women before you have ended
	in marriage. May it be so for you.

A Danaid.
	May great Zeus keep marriage
	with the sons of Aegyptus away from me.

A Serving Woman.
	That would be best.

A Danaid.
	You would charm what cannot be charmed.

A Serving Woman.
	And you do not know what is to come.

A Danaid.
	How could I look into the mind of Zeus,
	a sight with no bottom to it?

A Serving Woman.
	Then pray in measured words.

A Danaid.
	What is this measure you are teaching me?

A Serving Woman.
	Not to ask too much of the gods.

Chorus.
	May Zeus the lord keep from me
	a marriage to a man I hate,
	a deadly marriage: Zeus, who set Io free
	from her pain, holding her still
	with his healing hand,
	making his force a kindness.

Chorus.
	And may he give the victory to the women.
	I praise what is better than evil,
	two parts good to one part bad,
	and I praise this: that by my prayers, justice
	should follow justice, by the means of release
	that come from a god.

(They all go out together toward the city.)
