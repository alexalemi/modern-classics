Augustine was born in 354 in Thagaste, a small inland town in Roman North Africa, in what is now Algeria. He taught rhetoric for a living — at Carthage, then Rome, then the imperial court at Milan — spent nine years as a Manichee, lived for about fifteen years with a woman whose name he never gives and had a son by her, and was baptised in Milan in 387, at thirty-two. His mother Monica, who had followed him across the sea, died a few months later at Ostia. He was made bishop of Hippo, a port on the same North African coast, in his early forties, and wrote this book at about that time, somewhere between 397 and 400.

The first thing to know about it is who it is talking to. Every sentence of the thirteen books is addressed to God — not about him, to him. Augustine is telling the story of his own life to someone who already knows all of it, and asking questions of him in the middle of the telling. That is why the book reads the way it does, and a reader who expects to be addressed will spend the first pages confused. Nobody is explaining anything to us. We are overhearing.

The pronoun for God here is "you", capitalised. Pusey's 1838 translation, which this edition works from, used "Thou" throughout, and that has been the English voice of the book ever since. But Latin tu is the intimate singular — the pronoun for a friend or a child — so the Victorian "Thou" makes the book considerably more formal than Augustine wrote it. The capital is kept for a practical reason: he breaks off from addressing God to address his readers directly, sometimes in the same paragraph, and the capital is the cheapest way to say which "you" is which.

Nine of the thirteen books are memoir, ending with Monica's death. Book Ten is about memory. Books Eleven to Thirteen are about time, creation, and the opening verses of Genesis. Most modern editions cut or append those last four, which turns the Confessions into a conversion story, which is a thing it invented and then refused to be. All thirteen are here. Book Eleven is the one people come back for; it contains the best-known admission of ignorance in philosophy, about what time is.

He is faster and funnier than his reputation. He steals pears he does not want and cannot stop thinking about it thirty years later. He prays for chastity, and adds, but not yet, and knows exactly how funny that is. He is jealous of a cheerful drunk in a Milan street. He hated learning Greek.
