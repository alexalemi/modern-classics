AUGUSTINE'S CONFESSIONS — TRANSLATION STRATEGY
==============================================

THE WORK

Written c. 397-400, thirteen books. Nine of memoir, running from infancy
to his mother's death at Ostia; then Book Ten on memory; then Books
Eleven to Thirteen on time, creation and the opening verses of Genesis.
It is the first autobiography in the Western tradition and it invented
most of what we mean by an inner life — the divided will, the memory as
a place you walk through, the self that is a problem to itself.

Source: E. B. Pusey's translation of 1838 (Gutenberg #3296), the
classic English Confessions and the one that made "Thou" the voice of
the book in English.

THIS IS NOT A HARD BOOK. IT IS A HARD SURFACE.

Measured at 35.9 archaisms per 1,000 words — higher than anything else
in this collection, higher than Burton's Nights, far higher than Bunyan.
But almost all of it is one thing: the second person. Thou, Thee, Thy,
Thine, hast, art, doth, wert, knowest, madest, gavest. Strip that and
Pusey is a lucid Victorian prose stylist and Augustine is a fast,
funny, self-interrupting writer.

So the archaism density is real and is also concentrated in a single
grammatical feature. Clearing it is nearly mechanical. What is NOT
mechanical is everything the "Thou" was carrying, which is the next
section and is the whole job.


THE CENTRAL PROBLEM: THE BOOK IS A PRAYER

Every sentence of thirteen books is addressed to God. Not "about" God —
TO God. Augustine narrates his own life to someone who already knows it,
which is a strange and deliberate rhetorical position and it is the
reason the book feels the way it does. He is not explaining himself to
us. We are overhearing.

Pusey's "Thou" did two jobs at once, and modern English has no single
word that does both:
  1. It marked the ADDRESSEE. A reader always knows who is being
     spoken to.
  2. It marked REVERENCE — or so a modern reader assumes. In fact in
     1838 "thou" was already archaic-liturgical; in Augustine's Latin
     "tu" is simply the intimate singular, the pronoun you use with a
     friend, a child, or a lover. The Latin is CLOSER, not grander.
     Pusey's "Thou" is a Victorian addition and it makes the book more
     formal than Augustine wrote it.

DECISION: render as "you", and take the reverence out of the pronoun and
put it back in the RHYTHM. This is the single most important call in the
book and everything else follows from it. Augustine's Latin is intimate
and urgent; a modern "you" is the faithful choice, and it recovers
something Pusey's readers never had.

DECISION: CAPITALISE "You / Your / Yours" WHEN GOD IS MEANT. Not for
piety — for disambiguation, and the need is real and frequent. Augustine
breaks off from addressing God to address his readers directly, often in
the same paragraph (Book Ten opens by asking what business it is of
other people to hear his confessions, and speaks to them as "you" for
several pages). Lowercase throughout would make those passages genuinely
ambiguous. The capital is the cheapest possible signal and it costs the
prose nothing. Same for "He / Him / His" where a sentence has both God
and a man in it.

Note the asymmetry this produces and keep it: God is "You", the reader
is "you". That is the correct hierarchy of attention for this book.


VOICE

Augustine is:
  - FAST. Short questions in bursts. "What then is time? If no one asks
    me, I know; if I want to explain it to someone who asks, I don't."
    Do not smooth these into statements.
  - SELF-INTERRUPTING. He argues with himself mid-sentence, corrects
    himself, asks whether he has just said something stupid. Keep every
    one of these turns.
  - RHETORICALLY TRAINED, and he was a professor of rhetoric who is
    ashamed of having been one. The antitheses and the piled clauses are
    professional equipment ("most hidden, yet most present; most
    beautiful, yet most strong"). KEEP THE FIGURES. They are not padding
    and unstacking them into plain sentences guts the passage. This is
    the one place where the usual instruction is reversed.
  - FUNNY, more often than his reputation allows. The pear theft is
    played as absurd. The prayer for chastity "but not yet" is a joke he
    is making at his own expense. His account of learning to talk, of
    hating Greek homework, of the drunk beggar in Milan who was happier
    than he was — all comic.

THE MODEL IS NOT A SERMON. Read it as a brilliant, restless, verbally
gifted man talking at three in the morning about the worst things he has
done, to the one listener who cannot be shocked.


SCRIPTURE

He quotes constantly — the Psalms most of all — and he does NOT mark the
quotations. They are woven into his own syntax, half-clause by
half-clause, because the Psalms are the language he thinks in. Pusey
italicised some of them; the plain-text source has lost the italics, and
they are not being restored.

RULE: render a quoted phrase in the SAME modern register as the prose
around it, so the seam stays exactly where Augustine put it — invisible.
Do not lift them into King James English; that would put a frame around
something he deliberately left unframed, and would make the book look
like a book of proofs rather than a man whose sentences have the Psalms
in them the way ours have song lyrics.

EXCEPTION: where a phrase is famous enough that a reader will recognise
it and the recognition is the point, keep the familiar wording ("I
believe; help my unbelief", "Give me chastity", "Take up and read").


THE VERNE RULE APPLIES THROUGHOUT

Everything Augustine argues stands as HIS argument, unhedged, with no
correction and no wink:
  - INFANT SIN. He says babies are jealous, greedy and would do harm if
    they were strong enough, and that this is guilt. A modern reader will
    disagree. It is one of the most famous passages in the book and it
    is argued, not asserted.
  - THE MANICHEES. Nine years of his life and he renounces them
    completely. Render his account of them as his — he is not a neutral
    witness and does not pretend to be.
  - ASTROLOGY, which he demolishes, and the twins argument he uses.
  - UNBAPTISED INFANTS, and his grief over a friend's death.
  - THE ALLEGORICAL GENESIS of Book Thirteen. He reads the firmament as
    Scripture, the sea creatures as sacraments, and he KNOWS other
    readings are possible and says so. Keep the reading and keep the
    modesty; do not turn it into a claim he is not making.
  - The physics and cosmology, kept as written.


THE PASSAGE ABOUT THE WOMAN

Book Six. Augustine lived with a woman for about fifteen years, had a
son by her whom he loved, and dismissed her so he could make a
respectable marriage. She is never named in thirteen books. He records
that she went back to Africa vowing never to know another man, that his
heart was "torn and wounded and trailing blood" — and then that he took
another mistress while waiting for his fiancée to come of age, because
he could not wait.

Render this FLAT, IN FULL AND UNREMARKED. No note. The Sindbad precedent
from nights/: a modern reader will notice what Augustine does not
notice, and that noticing is the reading experience. Doing it for them
destroys it. The one thing to get right in the translation is that his
grief is entirely about his own pain — do not let a sympathetic phrase
slip in on her behalf that he did not write.

Same handling for the concubine's replacement, for the sale of Adeodatus'
mother's household, and for his account of his mother's endurance of a
violent husband, which he presents with admiration.


BOOKS ELEVEN TO THIRTEEN

Most modern editions cut them or print them as an appendix. They are not
being cut. Book Eleven is the greatest sustained piece of writing about
time in the language, Book Ten is why anyone still reads the book in a
psychology department, and dropping the last three turns the Confessions
into a conversion memoir, which is a genre it invented and then refused.

They ARE a different register: sustained argument, not narrative, and
much closer to Hume's problem than to Books One to Nine. Handle them the
way hume/ is handled — clear the technical apparatus, keep the
argument's joints visible, never flatten a distinction he is making. Do
NOT import the memoir's warmth into them, and do not let them go cold
either; he prays in the middle of the argument and that is the point.


VOCABULARY

  Thou/Thee/Thy/Thine        -> You/Your (God), you/your (people)
  hast, art, doth, wert      -> have, are, does, were
  whither, whence, thither   -> where, from where, there
  aught, naught              -> anything, nothing
  suffer me                  -> let me
  wont                       -> used to / in the habit of
  presently                  -> at once  [FALSE FRIEND — never "soon"]
  conversation               -> way of life / conduct  [FALSE FRIEND]
  want                       -> lack  [FALSE FRIEND]
  prevent                    -> go before  [FALSE FRIEND]
  sensible                   -> perceptible by the senses  [FALSE FRIEND]
  concupiscence              -> keep in the Romans quotation; elsewhere
                                "craving" or "lust" as the sense demands
  continency                 -> self-control (and see must_contain: the
                                famous prayer keeps "chastity")
  inebriate                  -> keep the metaphor; "flood it", "make it
                                drunk" — he means it
  repose                     -> rest  (INCLUDING the opening sentence:
                                "our heart is restless until it rests in
                                you" is the settled modern English and
                                is pinned in must_contain)
  charity                    -> love, where it means caritas
  the flesh                  -> kept as "the flesh"; it is a technical
                                term of his and "the body" is wrong
  cleave                     -> hold fast to / cling to
  bowels (of mercy)          -> heart
  Manichees                  -> Manichees (glossed once, at first use)
  Academics                  -> the Academics, glossed once as the
                                sceptical school
  Punic, Getulian            -> kept; they are real places and peoples


NAMES

Monica (Pusey has "Monnica" in places — normalise to Monica), Alypius,
Nebridius, Adeodatus, Ambrose, Faustus, Patricius, Verecundus,
Romanianus, Ponticianus. Places in modern English form: Thagaste,
Carthage, Rome, Milan, Ostia. "Hippo" only appears retrospectively.


THE STRUCTURE

Thirteen books, no chapters — see prep.py for why the standard chapter
and section numbers are NOT in this transcription and are NOT invented
here. Each book has a descriptive title written for this edition, on the
soap-bubbles precedent (new writing, clearly editorial): "Book Eight:
The Garden at Milan" tells a reader what they have reached in a way that
"Book Eight" cannot.

Files: 36, largest 3,781 words, cut at paragraph boundaries. Multi-part
files take the standard form — heading, "(Part n of k)", blank, body;
parts 2+ never re-introduce the book, non-final parts never conclude it.

RATIO: EN->EN modernization, so target 0.95-1.15. Verify with
--min-ratio 0.85 --max-ratio 1.3. Removing the thou-forms costs nothing
in length; unstacking is minimal because Pusey's sentences are already
short. If a file comes in under 0.9, something has been summarised —
most likely one of the piled-antithesis passages, which look like
padding and are not.
