Book One: Infancy, Boyhood, and the Beginnings of Sin

(Part 1 of 2)

You are great, Lord, and greatly to be praised; great is Your power, and Your wisdom has no limit. And a human being wants to praise You — a human being, one small piece of what You have made, carrying his own mortality about with him, carrying the evidence of his sin and the evidence that You resist the proud. Still he wants to praise You, this small piece of what You have made. You stir us to take delight in praising You, because You made us for Yourself, and our heart is restless until it rests in You.

Grant me, Lord, to know and understand which comes first: calling on You or praising You? And again, knowing You or calling on You? For how can anyone call on You without knowing You? Not knowing You, he might call on something else instead. Or is it rather that we call on You in order to know You? But how shall they call on him in whom they have not believed? And how shall they believe without a preacher? And they that seek the Lord shall praise him — for those who seek will find him, and those who find will praise him. So let me seek You, Lord, by calling on You, and call on You by believing in You; for You have been preached to us. My faith calls on You, Lord — the faith You gave me, the faith You breathed into me through the humanity of Your Son, through the work of the man who preached.

And how shall I call on my God, my God and Lord, when calling for him means calling him into myself? What room is there in me for my God to come into? Where can God come into me — God, who made heaven and earth? Is there really anything in me, Lord my God, that can hold You? Do heaven and earth hold You, which You made, and in which You made me? Or is it that, since nothing that exists could exist without You, whatever exists holds You? Then since I exist too, why do I ask You to come into me, when I would not be at all if You were not in me already? Why? Because I have not gone down to hell — and yet You are there too. For if I go down into hell, You are there. I could not exist, my God, could not exist at all, if You were not in me — or rather, unless I were in You, from whom are all things, by whom are all things, in whom are all things. Just so, Lord. Just so. Where am I calling You to, when I am already in You? Or where can You come into me from? Where could I go, beyond heaven and earth, for my God to come to me from there — my God, who has said, I fill heaven and earth?

Do heaven and earth hold You, then, since You fill them? Or do You fill them and overflow, since they cannot hold You? And when heaven and earth are full, where do You pour out the rest of Yourself? Or do You have no need of anything to hold You, since You hold all things — filling what You fill by holding it? The vessels You fill do not hold You up: they could all be smashed and You would not spill out. And when You are poured out over us, You are not brought down but lift us up; You are not scattered but gather us together. But You, who fill all things: do You fill them with the whole of Yourself? Or, since nothing can hold all of You, does each thing hold a part? And the same part, all at once? Or each its own part, the larger things more and the smaller less? Is one part of You larger, then, and another smaller? Or are You wholly everywhere, while nothing holds the whole of You?

What are You, then, my God? What, except the Lord God? For who is Lord but the Lord? Who is God but our God? Highest, best, most powerful, most all-powerful; most merciful and most just; most hidden and most present; most beautiful and most strong; steady and beyond comprehension; unchangeable and changing everything; never new, never old, and making all things new, while bringing the proud into old age without their noticing; always working, always at rest; still gathering and lacking nothing; supporting, filling, sheltering; creating, feeding, ripening; searching, though You have everything. You love, without being agitated by it; You are jealous, without anxiety; You repent, but You do not grieve; You are angry, and You are calm; You change Your work, and Your purpose does not change; You take back what You find, and never lost it; You are never in need, and You are glad of what You gain; You are never greedy, and You demand interest. We give You more than we owe, so that You may be in our debt — and who has anything that is not Yours? You pay debts, owing nothing; You cancel debts, losing nothing. And what have I said now, my God, my life, my holy joy? What does anyone say when he speaks about You? And yet woe to the man who says nothing, since even the most eloquent are struck dumb.

Oh, that I might rest in You! Oh, that You would come into my heart and flood it, so that I might forget my troubles and take hold of You, my one good! What are You to me? Have pity, and teach me how to say it. What am I to You, that You demand my love and are angry with me if I do not give it, and threaten me with terrible miseries? Is it a small misery, then, not to love You? Oh, for Your mercy's sake, Lord my God, tell me what You are to me. Say to my soul, I am your salvation. Say it so that I hear it. Look: my heart is in front of You, Lord; open its ears and say to my soul, I am your salvation. Let me run after that voice and take hold of You. Do not hide Your face from me. Let me die — so that I do not die — only let me see Your face.

The house of my soul is too narrow for You to come into; enlarge it. It is falling down; repair it. There are things in it that must offend Your eyes; I confess it and I know it. But who is going to clean it out? Whom should I cry to but You? Lord, cleanse me from my secret faults, and spare Your servant from the power of the enemy. I believe, and so I speak. Lord, You know. Have I not confessed my sins against myself to You, and have You not, my God, forgiven the wickedness of my heart? I will not go to law with You, who are the truth. I am afraid of deceiving myself, of letting my own wrongdoing lie to itself. So I will not go to law with You — for if You, Lord, should keep account of our sins, Lord, who could stand it?

Yet let me speak to Your mercy, dust and ashes that I am. Let me speak, since it is Your mercy I am speaking to, and not to some man who would laugh at me. Perhaps You laugh at me too — but You will turn back and have compassion on me. For what would I say, Lord my God, except that I do not know where I came from into this dying life, or should I call it living death? And straight away the comforts of Your compassion took me up, as I have heard from the parents of my flesh, out of whose substance You once shaped me — I do not remember it myself. So I was met by the comfort of a woman's milk. My mother and my nurses did not fill their own breasts for me; it was You who gave me the food of my infancy through them, according to Your arrangement, by which You distribute Your riches through the hidden depths of things. You also gave me not to want more than You gave, and gave my nurses the willingness to give me what You had given them. They gave me what they had from You, and they gave it gladly, out of a love You had taught them. What was good for me from them was good for them too. And in fact it was not from them but through them — because all good things are from You, God, and all my health is from my God. I learned this only later, when You called out to me through these gifts of Yours, inside me and outside me. In those days all I knew was how to suck, to be content with what pleased me and cry at what hurt me; nothing more.

Later I began to smile, first in my sleep and then awake — so I was told about myself, and I believed it, because we see other babies do the same, though I remember nothing of my own. Bit by bit I began to notice where I was, and to want to make my wants known to the people who could satisfy them; and I could not, because the wants were inside me and the people were outside, and no sense of theirs could get into my mind. So I flung my arms and legs and voice about at random, making the few signs I could — signs very little like what I actually meant. And when I was not obeyed at once, either because my wants were harmful or because nobody understood them, I was outraged: outraged that my elders would not do as they were told, that free people would not serve me, that people who owed me nothing would not wait on me — and I punished them for it by crying. That is what babies are like; I have watched them and learned it. And that I was like that myself, they have shown me better, without knowing it, than my nurses could who did know it.

And now my infancy is long dead, and I am alive. But You, Lord, live for ever, and nothing in You dies. Before the foundation of the world, before anything that can be called "before", You are, and You are God and Lord of everything You created. In You stand fixed for ever the first causes of everything that does not stand; in You, unchanging, are the sources of everything that changes; in You live the eternal reasons of everything unreasoning and temporary. Tell me, Lord, tell Your suppliant; have pity, and tell me who need pity: did my infancy come after some earlier age of mine that had already died? Was that the time I spent inside my mother? I have heard something about that, and I have seen pregnant women myself. But before that again, God my joy — was I anywhere, was I anybody? There is nobody to tell me. Not my father, not my mother, not other people's experience, not my own memory. Are You laughing at me for asking, and telling me to praise You and acknowledge You for what I do know?

I acknowledge You, Lord of heaven and earth, and praise You for the first rudiments of my being and for my infancy, which I remember nothing of; because You have arranged that a man should guess much about himself from other people, and believe a great deal of it on the word of mere women. Even then I had being and life, and by the end of my infancy I could look for signs by which to make my feelings known to others. Where could such a creature come from, Lord, if not from You? Can anyone be his own maker? Is there some other channel anywhere that could pour existence and life into us, except from You, Lord, in whom existing and living are one thing — since You Yourself are supremely existence and supremely life? For You are most high, and You do not change, and today does not come to an end in You. And yet it does come to an end in You, since all such things are in You too; they would have no way of passing at all unless You held them up. And since Your years do not run out, Your years are a single today. How many of our years, and our fathers' years, have flowed away through Your "today", taking from it the measure and the shape of whatever being they had? And still others will flow away and take their shape in turn. But You are still the same, and everything of tomorrow and beyond, and everything of yesterday and behind it, You have done today. What is it to me if somebody cannot follow this? Let him be glad and say, What is this? Let him be glad even so, and be content to find You by not finding You, rather than to miss You by finding.

Hear me, God. How terrible human sin is! So says a man, and You pity him, because You made him and did not make the sin in him. Who will remind me of the sins of my infancy? For in Your sight nobody is free of sin, not even a baby whose life on earth is one day long. Who will remind me? Does not every small child, in whom I can see what I do not remember of myself? What was my sin, then? Was it that I cried for the breast? If I cried now for food suitable to my age, I should rightly be laughed at and told off. What I did then deserved telling off; but since I could not have understood it, custom and reason forbade anyone to tell me off. Those are habits we dig out and throw away once we have grown — and no one who prunes a tree knowingly throws away the good wood. So was it good, even for a time, to cry for what would have hurt me if I got it? To be bitterly angry that free people, my own elders, the very people who gave me life, did not wait on me? That plenty of others, wiser than I was, did not jump at my nod? To do my best to hit and hurt them, because they would not obey orders that it would have harmed me to have obeyed? It is the weakness of a baby's limbs that is innocent, not its will. I have seen a baby jealous myself. It could not talk yet, and it went pale and glared at the other child at the breast. Everybody knows this. Mothers and nurses will tell you they have their own remedies for it. But is that innocence — when the fountain of milk is running rich and full, to refuse to let another share it, though he needs it desperately and his whole life depends on it? We put up with all this gently, not because it is no evil, or a small one, but because the child will grow out of it. It is tolerated now, and exactly the same temper is completely intolerable in an older person.

You then, Lord my God, who gave life to this infancy of mine, and fitted out the body You gave me with senses, as we can see, and joined its limbs together, and shaped it well, and planted in it all the functions of life for its safety and good — You command me to praise You for these things, to confess to You and sing to Your name, most high. For You are God, almighty and good, even if You had done nothing but this, which nobody but You could do: You whose oneness is the pattern of all things; who out of Your own beauty make all things beautiful; who order all things by Your law. This age, then, Lord, which I have no memory of, which I take on other people's word and guess at from other babies — true though the guess may be — I am still reluctant to count as part of this life of mine that I live in this world. It is hidden from me in the shadows of forgetting, exactly as the time I spent in my mother's womb is. But if I was shaped in wickedness, and my mother conceived me in sin, then where, I beg You, my God — where, Lord, or when, was I, Your servant, ever innocent? Still, I pass that period by. What have I to do now with a time I can recall no trace of?

Leaving infancy behind, I came to boyhood — or rather it came to me, and pushed infancy out. Not that infancy went away, for where would it have gone? And yet it was no longer there. I was no longer a speechless infant but a talking boy. This I do remember, and I have worked out since how I learned to talk. It was not that my elders taught me words by any set method, the way they taught me other things later. It was that I longed to express what I was thinking, by cries and broken noises and various movements of my limbs, so as to get my own way; and being unable to express everything I wanted, or to the person I wanted, I practised the sounds in my memory with the mind You had given me, my God. When people named something and turned towards it as they spoke, I saw it and understood that the thing they were pointing out was called by the sound they made. And that they meant this thing and not another was obvious from the movement of their bodies — the natural language of all nations, made up of expression, glances, gestures and tone of voice, which shows what the mind is feeling as it goes after something, or holds it, or refuses it, or runs from it. So by hearing words over and over as they turned up in different sentences, I gradually collected what they stood for; and having broken my mouth in to these signs, I used them to say what I wanted. In this way I traded the current signs of our wants with the people around me, and launched further out into the stormy business of human life — still depending, of course, on the authority of my parents and the nod of my elders.

My God, my God, what miseries and mockeries I met with then, when as a boy I was told that the right thing was to obey my teachers, so that I might get on in this world and excel in the science of the tongue, which serves to win the praise of men and money that deceives. Next I was sent to school to get an education, though I had no idea, poor wretch, what it was good for; and yet if I was lazy at my lessons I was beaten. Our forefathers thought this the right way, and plenty of people had gone the same road before us and laid out these weary paths, which we were made to walk, multiplying toil and misery for the sons of Adam. But, Lord, we found that people called on You, and we learned from them to think of You — as far as we were able — as somebody great, who could hear us and help us, though hidden from our senses. So as a boy I began to pray to You, my help and my refuge, and I loosened my tongue to call on You, and I prayed, small as I was, with no small earnestness, not to be beaten at school. And when You did not hear me — which was not abandoning me to my folly — my elders, and even my parents, who wished me no harm at all, laughed at my beatings, which were my great and grievous trouble.

Is there anyone, Lord, of a soul so large, so passionately attached to You — for a kind of stupidity will do it after a fashion, but I mean anyone made so great-hearted by devotion to You — that he can think as lightly of the racks and hooks and other tortures that people all over the world beg You in terror to be spared, and laugh at those who dread them most bitterly, as our parents laughed at the torments we suffered from our schoolmasters as boys? For we were no less afraid of ours, and prayed to You no less to escape them. And still we sinned, by writing or reading or studying less than we were set. It was not that we lacked memory or ability, Lord; Your will had given us plenty for our age. It was that our whole delight was in play — and for that we were punished by people who were doing exactly the same. But an adult's idleness is called business; a boy's, which is the same thing, gets him punished by those adults, and nobody feels sorry for either the boys or the men. For would anyone with any sense approve of my being beaten as a boy because playing ball slowed my progress in studies that were to fit me, as a man, for playing at something less becoming? And what else was the man doing who beat me? If he came off worse in some trivial argument with a fellow teacher, he was more embittered and jealous than I was on losing a ball game to a playmate.

And yet I did sin in this, Lord God, orderer and creator of everything in nature — orderer only, of sin. I sinned, Lord my God, by disobeying the commands of my parents and my teachers. Whatever their motive, what they wanted me to learn I might have put to good use later. I disobeyed, not because I had chosen something better, but out of love of play: I loved the pride of winning my contests, and having my ears tickled with lying stories so that they itched all the more; and the same curiosity kept flashing brighter in my eyes for the shows and games of my elders. And those who put on such shows are held in such esteem that almost everyone wants the same for their children — while being perfectly willing to have them beaten if the games get in the way of the studies by which they hope the children will grow up to put such shows on. Look on all this with pity, Lord; and rescue us who call on You now; and rescue those too who do not yet call on You, so that they may call on You, and You may rescue them.
