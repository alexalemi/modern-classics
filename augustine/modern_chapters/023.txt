Book Ten: Memory

(Part 3 of 5)

But what about when the memory itself loses something, as happens when we forget and go looking so as to recollect? Where, in the end, do we search, but in the memory itself? And there, if one thing happens to be offered instead of another, we reject it until what we are looking for comes to meet us; and when it does, we say, "This is it" — which we could not say unless we recognised it, or recognise it unless we remembered it. So we certainly had forgotten it. Or had the whole not escaped us, but the lost part was being looked for by the part we still had hold of, because the memory felt it was not carrying along everything it usually did, and, crippled by having its old habit cut short, demanded back what it was missing? For instance, if we see or think of somebody we know, and having forgotten his name try to recover it, then whatever else occurs to us will not connect with him, because it was not in the habit of being thought of along with him, and so it is rejected — until the right one presents itself, and our knowledge settles on it evenly as its accustomed object. And where does that come from, if not out of the memory itself? Even when we recognise it on being reminded by somebody else, that is where it comes from; for we do not accept it as something new, but on recollecting it we agree that the name given is the right one. If it had been completely rubbed out of the mind, we would not remember it even when reminded. For we have not yet completely forgotten what we remember having forgotten. And what we have completely forgotten, being lost, we cannot even look for.

How then do I look for You, Lord? For when I look for You, my God, I am looking for a happy life. I will look for You, so that my soul may live; for my body lives by my soul and my soul lives by You. How then do I look for a happy life, seeing that I do not have it until I can say, where I ought to say it, "This is enough"? How do I look for it? By remembering, as though I had forgotten it and remembered that I had forgotten it? Or by wanting to learn it as something unknown, either never having known it, or having forgotten it so thoroughly that I do not even remember forgetting it? Is a happy life not what everybody wants, and nobody at all does not want? Where have they known it, that they want it so? Where have they seen it, that they love it so? Certainly we have it, though how I do not know.

And there is another way of having it, by which a man is happy when he has it; and there are people who are blessed in hope. These have it in a lesser sense than those who have it in fact — and yet they are better off than those who are happy neither in fact nor in hope. And even these last, if they did not have it in some way, would not want to be happy as they do; and that they do want it is quite certain. So they have known it somehow, and have it by some sort of knowledge; what sort, I do not know, and I am puzzled whether it is in the memory. If it is, then we were once happy — whether each of us separately, or in the man who first sinned, in whom we all died and from whom we are all born into misery, I am not asking now. I only ask whether the happy life is in the memory. For we could not love it unless we knew it. We hear the name, and we all admit we want the thing; it is not the mere sound that pleases us. When a Greek hears the word in Latin he is not pleased, since he does not know what has been said; but we Latin speakers are pleased, and so would he be if he heard it in Greek — because the thing itself is neither Greek nor Latin, and Greeks and Latins and speakers of every other language long for it just as eagerly. So it is known to everybody; for if they could all be asked with one voice, "Would you like to be happy?" they would answer without hesitation that they would. And that could not be, unless the thing the name stands for were kept in their memory.

But is it kept there in the way a man who has seen Carthage remembers Carthage? No; a happy life is not seen with the eye, because it is not a body. Then as we remember numbers? No; a man who has those in his knowledge does not go on trying to attain them, whereas a happy life we have in our knowledge and therefore love, and still want to attain, so as to be happy. Then as we remember eloquence? No; for although on hearing that word too some people call the thing to mind who are not yet eloquent, and many want to be, from which it appears that it is in their knowledge — even so, they have observed other people being eloquent with their bodily senses, and have been delighted, and want to be like them. Though indeed they would not be delighted without some inward knowledge of it, or want to be like them unless they were delighted. But a happy life is something we do not experience in others by any bodily sense. Then as we remember joy? Perhaps; for I remember my joy even when I am sad, as I remember a happy life when I am unhappy. And I never saw, heard, smelled, tasted or touched my joy with any bodily sense; I experienced it in my mind when I rejoiced, and the knowledge of it stuck in my memory, so that I can recall it sometimes with disgust and sometimes with longing, depending on what sort of thing it was I remember having rejoiced in. For I have been steeped in a kind of joy even by foul things, which I now detest and curse when I recall them; and at other times by good and honourable things, which I recall with longing, though perhaps they are no longer here — and so I recall a former joy with sadness.

Where then, and when, did I experience my happy life, that I should remember it and love it and long for it? And it is not only I, or a few others; all of us would gladly be happy. And unless we knew it by some sort of definite knowledge, we would not want it with so definite a will. But how is it that if two men are asked whether they want to go to the wars, one might answer that he does and the other that he does not; but if they were asked whether they wanted to be happy, both would say at once and without hesitation that they did? And the only reason the one goes to the wars and the other does not is to be happy. Is it perhaps that one looks for his joy in this and another in that, and they all agree in wanting to be happy, as they would agree, if asked, that they wanted to have joy — and this joy is what they call a happy life? So although one gets this joy by one means and another by another, they all have one end they are trying to reach: joy. And this being something everybody must say they have experienced, it is therefore found in the memory and recognised whenever the phrase "a happy life" is mentioned.

Far be it, Lord, far be it from the heart of Your servant who is confessing to You here, far be it that whatever the joy might be, I should therefore think myself happy. For there is a joy that is not given to the ungodly but to those who love You for Your own sake, whose joy You Yourself are. And this is the happy life: to rejoice towards You, from You, for You. This is it, and there is no other. Those who think there is another are chasing some other joy, and not the true one — though even their will is not turned away from some semblance of joy.

So it is not certain that everybody wants to be happy, since those who do not want to rejoice in You, which is the only happy life, do not truly want a happy life. Or does everybody want it, but, because the flesh craves against the Spirit and the Spirit against the flesh so that they cannot do what they want, they fall back on what they can do and are satisfied with that — because what they cannot do, they do not want strongly enough to make themselves able? For if I ask anybody whether he would rather have his joy in truth or in falsehood, he will hesitate as little to say "in the truth" as to say that he wants to be happy. For a happy life is joy in the truth; and that is a joy in You, who are the truth, God my light, health of my face, my God. This is the happy life that everybody wants; this life, which alone is happy, everybody wants; joy in the truth is what everybody wants. I have met plenty of people who wanted to deceive; not one who wanted to be deceived. So where did they come to know this happy life, except where they also know the truth? For they love the truth too, since they do not want to be deceived. And when they love a happy life, which is nothing but rejoicing in the truth, then they love the truth as well — which they would not love if there were not some trace of it in their memory. Why then do they not rejoice in it? Why are they not happy? Because they are more strongly taken up with other things, which have more power to make them miserable than the thing they so faintly remember has to make them happy. There is still a little light in men; let them walk, let them walk, in case the darkness overtakes them.

But why does truth breed hatred, and why does Your man, preaching the truth, become an enemy to them, when a happy life is loved, which is nothing but rejoicing in the truth? It must be that truth is loved in such a way that people who love something else would like the thing they love to be the truth; and since they do not want to be deceived, they will not be convinced that they are. So they hate the truth for the sake of the thing they loved instead of it. They love the truth when it shows them light and hate it when it shows them up. Since they do not want to be deceived and do want to deceive, they love it when it reveals itself to them and hate it when it reveals them. And truth will pay them back for this: those who did not want to be shown up by it, it shows up against their will, and does not show itself to them. That is how the human mind is — blind and sick, foul and ugly, wanting to be hidden, and refusing to let anything be hidden from itself. And it gets the opposite of what it wants: it is not hidden from the truth, and the truth is hidden from it. And yet even in this misery it would rather rejoice in truths than in falsehoods. It will be happy, then, when it rejoices, with no distraction interrupting, in that one truth by whom everything true is true.

See what a space I have crossed in my memory looking for You, Lord; and I have not found You outside it. Nor have I found anything about You except what I have kept in my memory since I first learned You. For since I learned You, I have not forgotten You. Where I found truth, there I found my God, who is the truth itself; and since I learned that, I have not forgotten it. So since I learned You, You have lived in my memory, and there I find You when I call You to mind and take delight in You. These are my holy delights, which You have given me in Your mercy, having regard to my poverty.

But where in my memory do You live, Lord? Where do You live there? What kind of lodging have You made for Yourself? What kind of sanctuary have You built for Yourself? You have given my memory this honour, that You live in it — but in what quarter of it You live is what I am now considering. For in thinking about You I went past the parts of it that animals have too, since I did not find You there among the images of bodily things. And I came to the parts where I put the feelings of my mind, and did not find You there. And I went into the very seat of my mind, which it has in my memory, since the mind remembers itself too; and You were not there either. For as You are not a bodily image, and not the feeling of a living creature, as when we rejoice or grieve or desire or fear or remember or forget or the like, so neither are You the mind itself — because You are the Lord God of the mind. And all these things change, while You remain unchangeable above them all; and yet You have consented to live in my memory ever since I learned You. And why am I now asking in what part of it You live, as though there were parts in it? I am sure that You live in it, since I have remembered You ever since I learned You, and it is there that I find You when I call You to mind.

Where then did I find You, that I might learn You? For You were not in my memory before I learned You. Where then did I find You, that I might learn You, but in Yourself above me? There is no place; we go backwards and forwards, and there is no place. Everywhere, truth, You give audience to everyone who asks Your advice, and answer them all at once, though they ask about all sorts of different things. Clearly You answer, though not everybody clearly hears. Everyone consults You about what they want, though they do not always hear what they want. Your best servant is the one who looks not so much to hear from You what he himself wants, as rather to want what he hears from You.

Late have I loved You, beauty so old and so new: late have I loved You! And look, You were within me, and I was outside, and it was out there that I looked for You; deformed as I was, I flung myself on the beautiful things You have made. You were with me, and I was not with You. Those things kept me far away from You, things which would not exist at all unless they existed in You. You called and shouted and shattered my deafness. You flashed and blazed and scattered my blindness. You breathed out fragrance, and I drew in my breath and I pant for You. I tasted, and now I hunger and thirst. You touched me, and I burned for Your peace.

When I hold fast to You with the whole of myself, I shall nowhere have grief or labour, and my life shall wholly live, being wholly full of You. But since now You lift up whoever You fill, and I am not full of You, I am a burden to myself. My deplorable joys fight with my joyful sorrows, and which side has won I do not know. I am wretched: Lord, have pity on me. My bad sorrows fight with my good joys, and which side has won I do not know. I am wretched: Lord, have pity on me. I am wretched: look, I am not hiding my wounds. You are the doctor and I am the patient; You are merciful and I am miserable. Is not a man's life on earth all trial? Who wants troubles and difficulties? You command them to be endured, not to be loved. Nobody loves what he endures, though he may love enduring; for although he is glad that he endures, he would rather there were nothing for him to endure. In hard times I long for good ones; in good times I dread the hard ones. What middle ground is there between the two, where a man's life is not all trial? A curse on the world's prosperity, twice over: for fear of adversity, and for the corruption of joy. A curse on the world's adversity, twice over, and a third time: for the longing for prosperity, and because adversity itself is a hard thing, and because it may shatter our endurance. Is a man's life on earth not all trial, without a single break?

And all my hope is nowhere but in Your exceedingly great mercy. Give what You command, and command what You will. You command us self-control; and when I knew, says one, that nobody can be self-controlled unless God gives it, this itself was part of wisdom, to know whose gift it is. By self-control we are indeed gathered up and brought back into one, out of the many we were scattered into. For a man loves You too little if he loves anything alongside You that he does not love for Your sake. Love, always burning and never burning out! Charity, my God, set me alight. You command self-control: give what You command, and command what You will.

You command me self-control from the appetite of the flesh, the appetite of the eyes, and the ambition of the world. You command self-control from concubinage; and as for marriage itself, You have counselled something better than what You have permitted. And since You gave it, it was done, even before I became a minister of Your sacrament. But there still live in my memory, which I have said so much about, the images of such things as my bad habit fixed there. They come at me powerless while I am awake; but in sleep they come not only to give pleasure but even to win consent and something very like reality. So far does the illusion of an image prevail, in my soul and in my flesh, that when I am asleep, false visions persuade me to what true ones cannot when I am awake. Am I not then myself, Lord my God? And yet how much difference there is between myself and myself, in the moment when I pass from waking to sleeping, or come back from sleeping to waking! Where is reason then, which resists such suggestions when I am awake, and stands unshaken even if the things themselves are pressed on it? Is it shut up along with my eyes? Is it lulled asleep along with the senses of the body? And how is it that even in sleep we often do resist, and, remembering our resolve and staying chastely in it, give no consent to such enticements? And yet there is enough difference that when it goes the other way, we come back on waking to a clear conscience — and by that very difference we discover that we did not do what we are nevertheless sorry was in some way done in us.

Are You not mighty enough, God almighty, to heal all the diseases of my soul, and by Your more abundant grace to put out even the impure motions of my sleep? You will increase Your gifts in me more and more, Lord, so that my soul may follow me to You, disentangled from the birdlime of craving; so that it does not rebel against itself, and even in dreams does not consent to those debasing corruptions through images of sense — let alone commit them to the point of the body's pollution. It is not hard for the Almighty, who is able to do more than we ask or think, to bring it about that nothing of this sort should have the very slightest influence over the pure affections even of a sleeper, not even the sort of influence that a thought could hold back: and to bring it about not only over a lifetime but at my present age. But what I still am in this kind of evil of mine I have confessed to my good Lord, rejoicing with trembling in what You have given me, and mourning over what is still unfinished in me; and hoping that You will bring Your mercies to completion in me, right up to that perfect peace which my outward and inward man will have with You, when death is swallowed up in victory.
