Book Seven: The Platonists and the Problem of Evil

(Part 1 of 3)

My evil and disgusting youth was dead by now and I was passing into early manhood — and the older I grew the dirtier I got with empty things, since I could not imagine any substance except the kind that can be seen with these eyes. I did not think of You, God, in the shape of a human body; from the time I first heard anything about wisdom, I had always avoided that, and I was glad to find the same view in the faith of our spiritual mother, Your Catholic Church. But what else to conceive of You I did not know. And I, a man, and such a man, tried to conceive of You, the sovereign, only, true God. In the depths of my soul I did believe that You were incorruptible and injury-proof and unchangeable; because although I did not know how or where the idea came from, I saw plainly and was certain that what can be corrupted must be inferior to what cannot, and I unhesitatingly preferred what could not be damaged to what could take damage, and the unchangeable to things subject to change. My heart cried out passionately against all my phantoms, and with this one blow I tried to beat away from my mind's eye the whole unclean swarm buzzing round it. And barely had they been driven off when, in the blink of an eye, they crowded thickly back round me, flew at my face and blotted it out; so that although I was not conceiving of You in the shape of a human body, I was still forced to conceive of You — that incorruptible, injury-proof, unchangeable thing which I preferred to the corruptible and damageable and changeable — as existing in space, whether poured into the world or spread infinitely outside it. Because anything I conceived without this space seemed to me to be nothing; utterly nothing, not even a void. It was as though, if a body were taken out of its place and the place remained empty of everything — of earth and water, air and sky — it would still remain an empty place, a spacious kind of nothing.

Being so gross-hearted, then, and not clear even to myself, I thought that whatever was not spread out over some space, or thinned or thickened or swollen out, and did not or could not take on some of these dimensions, was altogether nothing. For my heart ranged over the same sorts of shape my eyes were used to ranging over. And I did not see that the very act of the mind by which I was shaping those images was not of that kind at all — and yet it could not have shaped them unless it were itself something great. So I tried to conceive of You, life of my life, as vast, going through infinite spaces on every side, penetrating the whole mass of the universe, and beyond it in every direction through immeasurable, boundless space; so that the earth had You, and the sky had You, and everything had You, and everything was bounded within You, while You were bounded nowhere. For just as the body of the air above the earth does not stop the light of the sun from passing through it, penetrating it not by bursting it or cutting it but by filling it completely, so I thought that the substance not only of the sky and air and sea but of the earth as well was pervious to You, so that in all its parts, the largest and the smallest, it took in Your presence, by a secret breathing that directed everything You have created from within and from without. That was my guess, only because I could not conceive of anything else, and it was false. For on this account a larger part of the earth would contain a larger portion of You, and a smaller part a smaller portion; and everything would be full of You in such a way that an elephant's body would contain more of You than a sparrow's, in proportion to its being bigger and taking up more room; and so You would be making the several portions of Yourself present to the several portions of the world in fragments — large ones to the large, tiny ones to the tiny. But that is not what You are. And You had not yet lit up my darkness.

It was enough for me, Lord, to set against those deceived deceivers and dumb chatterers — since Your word never sounded out of them — the argument Nebridius used to put forward long ago, when we were still at Carthage, which staggered all of us who heard it. "That nation of darkness which the Manichees are in the habit of setting up as an opposing mass over against You: what could it have done to You, if You had refused to fight it? If they answer that it could have done You some harm, then You would be subject to injury and corruption. But if it could do You no harm, then no reason has been given for Your fighting it — and fighting in such a way that a certain portion or limb of You, an offspring of Your very substance, should be mixed with opposing powers and with natures You did not create, and be corrupted by them and changed for the worse to the point of being turned from happiness into misery, and need help to get it out and clean it up. And this offspring of Your substance was supposed to be the soul, which, being enslaved and defiled and corrupted, Your Word — free, pure and whole — might rescue; while that Word itself would still be corruptible, since it was of one and the same substance. So if they say that You, whatever You are, that is, the substance by which You are what You are, is incorruptible, then all these claims are false and to be cursed; and if they say it is corruptible, then the statement shows itself false and disgusting on the face of it." So this argument of Nebridius' was enough against people who deserved to be vomited up altogether from a stomach that could not hold them; because there was no way out for them, without a horrible blasphemy of heart and tongue, thinking and speaking of You like that.

But I still did not clearly and easily understand the cause of evil, though I held and was firmly convinced that You, our Lord the true God, who made not only our souls but our bodies, and not only our souls and bodies but every being and every thing, were undefilable and unalterable and in no degree changeable. And yet whatever the cause was, I saw that it must be looked for in a way that did not force me to believe the unchangeable God changeable — in case I became the very evil I was looking for. So I set about the search free from that anxiety at least, being certain of the falsity of what the people I recoiled from with my whole heart maintained. For I could see that in enquiring into the origin of evil they were filled with evil, since they preferred to think that Your substance suffered evil rather than that their own committed it.

And I strained to grasp what I now heard: that free will is the cause of our doing evil, and Your just judgement the cause of our suffering it. But I could not make it out clearly. So when I tried to draw my soul's vision up out of that deep pit, I was plunged back into it; and trying often, I was plunged back as often. But one thing did lift me a little way into Your light: I knew as surely that I had a will as that I was alive. When I willed or refused something, I was quite certain that it was nobody but myself who willed and refused; and I very nearly saw that this was where the cause of my sin lay. As for what I did against my will, I saw that I was undergoing it rather than doing it, and I judged that to be not my fault but my punishment — by which, since I held You to be just, I quickly admitted I was not being punished unjustly. But then again I said: who made me? Was it not my God, who is not only good but goodness itself? So where did it come from, my willing evil and refusing good, so that I am justly punished for it? Who put this in me and grafted this bitter plant into me, when I was made entirely by my sweetest God? If the devil is the author, then where does the devil come from? And if he too, by his own perverse will, turned from a good angel into a devil, where did the evil will come from in him by which he became a devil, seeing the whole nature of angels was made by that best of creators? By these thoughts I was pushed under and choked again — though not brought down to that hell of error, where nobody confesses to You, of thinking that You suffer evil rather than that man does it.

For I was trying to work out the rest as a man who had already found that the incorruptible must be better than the corruptible; and I therefore confessed that You, whatever You are, are incorruptible. For no soul ever was or ever will be able to conceive anything better than You, who are the sovereign and the best good. And since it is completely true and certain that the incorruptible is preferable to the corruptible, as I now preferred it, then if You were not incorruptible I could have arrived in thought at something better than my God. So where I saw the incorruptible to be preferable to the corruptible, there I ought to look for You, and there notice where evil itself was — that is, where corruption comes from, by which Your substance can by no means be damaged. For corruption does not damage our God in any way: not by any will, not by any necessity, not by any unforeseen accident. Because he is God, and what he wills is good, and he himself is that good; and being corrupted is not good. Nor are You forced into anything against Your will, since Your will is not greater than Your power. It would have to be greater if You were greater than Yourself; for the will and the power of God are God himself. And what can be unforeseen by You, who know everything? There is no nature among things that You do not know. And what more need we say about why the substance that God is should not be corruptible, seeing that if it were, he would not be God?

And I looked for where evil comes from, and looked in an evil way, and did not see the evil in the very way I was looking. I set before the sight of my spirit the whole of creation, everything we can see in it — sea, earth, air, stars, trees, mortal creatures — and everything in it we do not see, the vault of the sky, and all the angels too, and all its spiritual inhabitants. But my imagination arranged even these beings in places, as though they were bodies. And I made one great mass out of Your creation, sorted by the kinds of bodies in it: some real bodies, and some the ones I had made up for spirits. I made this mass huge — not as it actually was, which I could not know, but as I found convenient, and finite in every direction. And You, Lord, I imagined surrounding and penetrating it on every side, but infinite in every direction: as though there were a sea, everywhere and on every side, through unmeasured space, one single boundless sea, and it held inside it a sponge, huge but bounded, and that sponge would have to be filled in every part from that unmeasurable sea. That was how I conceived of Your creation: finite itself, and full of You, the infinite. And I said, Look, this is God, and this is what God has created; and God is good, mightily and incomparably better than all of this; but still he, the Good, created them good, and see how he surrounds and fills them. Where then is evil, and where does it come from, and how did it creep in here? What is its root and what is its seed? Or has it no being at all? Then why do we fear and avoid what does not exist? Or if we fear it for nothing, then that very fear is an evil, by which the soul is goaded and racked for nothing — and so much the greater an evil, in that we have nothing to fear and are still afraid. So either what we fear is evil, or else the evil is that we fear. Where does it come from, then, seeing God, the Good, created all these things good? He indeed, the greater and chief good, created these lesser goods; but creator and created, all of them are good. Where does evil come from?

Or was there some evil matter which he made and shaped and ordered, and yet left something in it that he did not turn into good? But why? Had he no power to turn and change the whole of it, so that no evil should be left in it, seeing he is almighty? Last of all, why would he make anything at all out of it, instead of using that same almightiness to make it not exist? Or could it exist against his will? Or if it existed from eternity, why did he allow it to be so through infinite stretches of past time, and only decide so long afterwards to make something out of it? Or if he suddenly decided to do something now, then this is what the almighty should have done rather: that this evil matter should not exist, and he alone should be, the whole, true, sovereign and infinite good. Or if it was not good that he who was good should not also frame and create something good, then he might have taken that evil matter away and reduced it to nothing, and formed good matter to create everything out of. For he would not be almighty if he could not create something good without the help of matter he had not created himself. These thoughts I turned over in my miserable heart, weighed down with the most gnawing anxiety in case I should die before I had found the truth. And yet the faith of Your Christ, our Lord and saviour, as professed in the Catholic Church, was firmly fixed in my heart — on many points still unformed, certainly, and drifting away from the rule of doctrine, but my mind did not leave it entirely; rather it took in more and more of it every day.

By this time I had also rejected the lying divinations and irreligious ravings of the astrologers. Let Your own mercies confess to You for this too, God, out of the very depths of my soul. For it was You, entirely You — since who else calls us back from the death of all errors, but the Life that cannot die and the Wisdom that needs no light and lights up the minds that need it, by which the universe is steered, right down to the spinning leaves on the trees? You made provision for the obstinacy with which I resisted Vindicianus, a sharp old man, and Nebridius, a young man of admirable ability. The first asserted vehemently, and the second often said, though with some hesitation, that there is no such art for foreseeing what is to come, and that men's guesses are a kind of lottery: out of the many things they say will happen, some actually do, without the speakers knowing why, since they stumble on it by talking so much. So You provided me with a friend who was no negligent consulter of astrologers — and not much skilled in those arts either, but, as I say, a curious consulter of them, who nevertheless knew one thing which he said he had heard from his father, and had no idea how far it went towards overturning any respect for that art.

This man, Firminus by name, having had a liberal education and been well taught in rhetoric, consulted me as a very dear friend about what I thought, according to his so-called horoscope, of certain business of his in which his worldly hopes had risen. And I, having by now begun to lean towards Nebridius' opinion, did not altogether refuse to guess and tell him what came into my unsettled mind, though I added that I was now almost convinced these were empty and ridiculous follies. At that he told me his father had been very keen on such books, and had a friend as enthusiastic about them as himself; and by studying and discussing together they fanned their affection for these toys into a flame, to the point that they would note the exact moments at which even the dumb animals bred about their houses gave birth, and then note the position of the heavens, so as to make fresh experiments in this so-called art. He said, then, that he had heard from his father that at the time when his mother was about to give birth to him, Firminus, a maidservant of his father's friend was also pregnant — which could not escape her master, who took the most exact care to know the births even of his puppies. And so it happened that, the one for his wife and the other for his servant, with the most careful observation, counting days, hours and even the smaller divisions of hours, both women were delivered at the same instant; so that both men were obliged to allow the same horoscope, down to the smallest points, the one for his son and the other for his newborn slave. For as soon as the women went into labour, each man sent word to the other of what was happening in his house, and had messengers ready to send off the moment there was news of the actual birth — which each had easily arranged for in his own household. And the two sets of messengers, he said, met at such an equal distance from each house that neither man could make out any difference in the position of the stars, or in any other minute detail. And yet Firminus, born to a high position in his parents' house, ran his course along the gilded roads of life, grew rich and rose to honours; while that slave went on serving his masters with his yoke never loosened at all, as Firminus, who knew him, told me.
