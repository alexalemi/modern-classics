Book Nine: Baptism, Ostia, and the Death of Monica

(Part 2 of 3)

When the vintage holiday was over I gave notice to the people of Milan to find their students another master to sell them words, since I had both chosen to serve You and was no longer up to the professorship, with my difficulty in breathing and the pain in my chest. And by letter I told Your bishop, the holy man Ambrose, about my former errors and my present desires, asking his advice on which of Your Scriptures I had best read to become readier and fitter to receive so great a grace. He recommended the prophet Isaiah — I think because Isaiah more than the rest foreshadows the gospel and the calling of the Gentiles. But I did not understand the first passage of it, and imagining the whole book was like that, I put it aside to take up again when I had more practice in our Lord's own words.

Then, when the time came for me to give in my name, we left the country and went back to Milan. Alypius wanted to be born again in You along with me, being already clothed in the humility that suits Your sacraments; and he was a most valiant tamer of the body, to the point of walking barefoot over the frozen ground of Italy, which was an unusual venture. With us we took the boy Adeodatus, born in the flesh of my sin. You had made him excellently. He was not quite fifteen, and in intelligence he was ahead of many serious and learned men. I confess Your gifts to You, Lord my God, creator of everything and abundantly able to reshape our deformities; for I had no part in that boy except the sin. That we brought him up in Your discipline was inspired by You and by nobody else. I confess Your gifts to You. There is a book of ours called The Teacher; it is a dialogue between him and me. You know that everything given there to the person talking with me was his own thinking, in his sixteenth year. I found much else in him, more admirable still. That talent frightened me. And who but You could be the maker of such wonders? You soon took his life from the earth; and I now remember him without anxiety, with nothing to fear for his childhood or his youth or for the whole of him. Him we took with us, our contemporary in grace, to be brought up in Your discipline; and we were baptised, and anxiety about our past life left us. Nor could I get enough, in those days, of the marvellous sweetness of considering the depth of Your plans for the salvation of mankind. How I wept at Your hymns and canticles, cut to the quick by the voices of Your sweet-singing church! The voices flowed into my ears and the truth was distilled into my heart, and the feelings of my devotion overflowed, and the tears ran down, and I was happy in them.

It was not long since the church at Milan had begun to use this kind of consolation and encouragement, with the brothers joining in eagerly with harmony of voice and heart. For it had been a year, or not much more, since Justina, mother of the boy emperor Valentinian, had persecuted Your servant Ambrose in favour of her heresy, which the Arians had seduced her into. The devout people kept watch in the church, ready to die with their bishop, Your servant. There my mother, Your handmaid, taking a leading part in those anxieties and vigils, lived for prayer. We ourselves, not yet warmed by the heat of Your Spirit, were still stirred by the sight of the astonished and disturbed city. It was then that it was first laid down that hymns and psalms should be sung after the manner of the Eastern churches, so that the people should not grow faint through the tedium of their grief; and from that day to this the custom has been kept, with many, indeed almost all, of Your congregations in other parts of the world following it.

Then in a vision You revealed to that bishop of Yours where the bodies of the martyrs Gervasius and Protasius lay hidden, whom You had kept uncorrupted in Your secret store for so many years, so that You might bring them out at the right moment to check the fury of a woman, though she was an empress. For when they were found and dug up and carried with the proper honour to the Ambrosian basilica, not only were people troubled by unclean spirits cured, with the devils confessing themselves, but a certain man who had been blind for many years, a citizen well known in the town, asked what all the confused rejoicing was about; and hearing the answer, he leapt up and asked his guide to take him there. Once there, he begged to be allowed to touch the bier of Your saints with his handkerchief — Your saints, whose death is precious in Your sight. When he had done it and put the handkerchief to his eyes, they were opened at once. From there the story spread; from there Your praises glowed and shone; and from there the mind of that enemy of Yours, though not turned to a sound faith, was at least turned back from her fury of persecution. Thanks be to You, my God. Where have You brought my memory from and to, that I should confess these things to You as well, which, great as they are, I had passed over and forgotten? And even then, when the scent of Your ointments was so strong, we did not run after You. That is why I wept the more at the singing of Your hymns — having sighed after You for so long, and at last breathing You in, as far as breath can enter this house of grass we live in.

You, who make men live of one mind in one house, joined Euodius to us as well, a young man from our own town. Being an officer at court, he had been converted to You and baptised before us, and had given up his secular service and buckled himself to Yours. We were together, and about to live together in our devout purpose. We looked for where we might serve You most usefully, and were on our way back to Africa together — and having got as far as Ostia, my mother left this life. I am leaving out a great deal, because I am hurrying on. Accept my confessions and thanksgivings, my God, for the countless things I say nothing about. But I shall not leave out whatever my soul can bring forth about that handmaid of Yours who brought me forth: in the flesh, so that I might be born into this temporary light, and in her heart, so that I might be born into the eternal one. It is not her gifts I want to speak of but Yours in her; for she neither made nor educated herself. You created her, and her father and mother had no idea what would come of them. And it was the sceptre of Your Christ, the discipline of Your only Son, in a Christian household, a good member of Your church, that brought her up in reverence for You.

And yet, for her good upbringing, she used to give credit not so much to her mother's diligence as to that of a certain very old servant, who had carried her father as a child the way small children get carried on the backs of older girls. For that reason, and for her great age and excellent way of life, she was well respected in that Christian household by the heads of it. So the charge of her master's daughters was entrusted to her, and she took it seriously, restraining them firmly when necessary with a holy severity and teaching them with a grave good sense. Except at the hours when they were fed very temperately at their parents' table, she would not let them drink even water, however parched they were — heading off a bad habit, and adding this wholesome advice: "You drink water now because you have no power to get wine. But when you are married and made mistresses of cellars and storerooms, you will despise water, and the habit of drinking will still be there." By this method of teaching, and by the authority she had, she held back the greediness of childhood and shaped the girls' very thirst to such an excellent moderation that they no longer wanted what they should not have.

And yet, as Your handmaid told me, her son, a love of wine had crept up on her. For when, as the custom was, she was told by her parents to draw wine from the cask, being a sober girl, she would hold the jug under the tap and, before pouring the wine into the flagon, sip a little with the tip of her lips; more than that her instincts refused. She did this not out of any craving for drink but out of the exuberance of youth, which boils over into playful mischief and is usually kept down in the young by the seriousness of their elders. And so, adding to that little a daily little more — for whoever despises small things will fall little by little — she had fallen into the habit of greedily draining her small cup nearly full of wine. Where was that discreet old woman then, and her firm prohibitions? Would anything have helped against a secret disease, if Your healing hand, Lord, had not been watching over us? With her father and mother and guardians away and You present, You who create, who call, and who work something towards the salvation of our souls even through the people set over us — what did You do then, my God? How did You cure her? How did You heal her? Did You not bring out of another soul a hard, sharp taunt, like a surgeon's knife out of Your secret store, and remove all that foulness with one cut?

For a maidservant who used to go down to the cellar with her fell into an argument, as happens, with her young mistress when they were alone, and taunted her with this fault in the most bitter and insulting terms, calling her a wine-swiller. Stung to the quick by that taunt, she saw how foul her fault was, and immediately condemned it and gave it up. As flattering friends corrupt us, so reproachful enemies mostly correct us. And yet You repay them not for what You do through them but for what they intended themselves. For that girl in her anger meant to hurt her young mistress, not to reform her; and she did it in private, either because that was where and when the quarrel happened to find them, or in case she should get into trouble herself for bringing it up so late. But You, Lord, governor of everything in heaven and earth, who turn the deepest currents to Your own purposes and direct the turbulent tide of the ages, healed one soul by the very unhealthiness of another — so that nobody who notices this should credit it to his own power, even when somebody he wanted to see reformed is reformed by words of his.

Brought up like this, modestly and soberly, and made subject to her parents by You rather than to You by her parents, as soon as she was of marriageable age she was given to a husband, and served him as her lord. And she did her best to win him over to You, preaching You to him by the way she lived; by which You adorned her, and made her reverently lovable and admirable to her husband. And she put up with his unfaithfulness in such a way as never to have a single quarrel with him about it, because she looked for Your mercy on him — that believing in You he might be made chaste. Besides this, he was as hot in his temper as he was in his affections; but she had learned not to resist an angry husband, not only in action but not even in words. Only when he had smoothed down and was calm and in a state to take it would she explain what she had done, if he had happened to take offence too hastily. In fact, while many women with gentler husbands still carried the marks of beatings on their faces, and would criticise their husbands' behaviour in casual conversation, she would criticise their tongues, giving them serious advice in the form of a joke: that from the moment they heard the marriage contract read to them they should count it as an indenture making them servants, and so, remembering their position, should not set themselves up against their masters. And when they, knowing what a bad-tempered husband she put up with, marvelled that it had never been heard or noticed by any sign that Patricius had beaten his wife, or that there had been so much as a day's domestic quarrel between them, and asked her the reason in confidence, she taught them the practice I have described. Those wives who followed it found the good of it and thanked her; those who did not found no relief, and suffered.

Her mother-in-law, too, was at first set against her by the whispering of malicious servants; and she so overcame her by attentiveness and persistent endurance and mildness that the older woman of her own accord told her son about the meddling tongues that had disturbed the domestic peace between herself and her daughter-in-law, and asked him to correct them. And when, in obedience to his mother and for the good order of the household, he had corrected the offenders with a beating, at the wish of the woman who had reported them, she promised the same reward to anyone else who spoke ill of her daughter-in-law to please her; and after that nobody dared, and they lived together with a remarkable sweetness of mutual kindness.

You also gave this great gift, my God, my mercy, to that good handmaid of Yours in whose womb You created me: that between any people who were quarrelling, wherever she could, she showed herself such a peacemaker that although she heard the bitterest things from both sides — the kind of thing that swollen and undigested anger breaks out into, when the raw material of hatred is breathed out in sour talk to a friend who is there about an enemy who is not — she would never disclose anything from one to the other except what might tend towards their reconciliation. This might seem a small good to me, if I did not know to my sorrow countless people who, through some horrible and widely spreading infection of sin, not only report to angry people what was said about them in anger, but add things that were never said at all. Whereas ordinary humanity ought to think it a small thing not to feed or increase ill will by bad words, unless one also works to put it out with good ones. That is what she was like, with You, her most inward teacher, teaching her in the school of the heart.

In the end she won her own husband over to You, at the very end of his earthly life; and she had no cause to complain of anything in him as a believer that she had put up with from him before he was one. She was also the servant of Your servants. Whichever of them knew her found much in her to praise and honour and love You for, because through the testimony of the fruits of a holy life they could feel Your presence in her heart. For she had been the wife of one man, had repaid her parents, had run her household devoutly, was well spoken of for her good works, had brought up children, going into labour over them again each time she saw them straying from You. And last of all, Lord, of all of us Your servants — whom by Your own gift You allow to speak — us who before she fell asleep in You lived united together, having received the grace of Your baptism, she took care of as though she had been mother of us all, and served as though she had been the child of us all.

The day was now coming on when she was to leave this life — a day You knew well and we did not — and it happened, arranged, as I believe, by Your own secret ways, that she and I were standing alone, leaning in a window that looked into the garden of the house where we were staying at Ostia. There, far from the noise of people, we were recovering from the exhaustion of a long journey and preparing for the voyage. So we were talking together, alone, very sweetly; and forgetting the things behind and reaching forward to the things ahead, we were asking each other, in the presence of the truth, which is You, what the eternal life of the saints would be like, which no eye has seen and no ear has heard and which has not entered into any human heart. But we gasped with the mouth of our heart after those heavenly streams of Your fountain, the fountain of life which is with You, so that being sprinkled from it according to our capacity, we might somehow think about so high a mystery.

And when our conversation had reached the point that the highest delight of the bodily senses, in the very purest material light, was, compared with the sweetness of that life, not only not worth comparing but not worth mentioning, we lifted ourselves up with a still more glowing love towards the Selfsame, and passed step by step through all bodily things, even the sky itself, from which the sun and moon and stars shine on the earth. And we soared higher still, by inward thought and talk and wonder at Your works; and we came to our own minds, and went beyond them, to arrive at that region of never-failing plenty where You feed Israel for ever on the food of truth, and where life is the Wisdom by whom all these things are made, and everything that has been and shall be. And she is not made, but is, as she has been, and so shall be for ever — or rather, "has been" and "shall be" are not in her, only "is", since she is eternal; for "has been" and "shall be" are not eternal. And while we were talking and panting after her, with one whole effort of our heart we just touched her; and we sighed, and left there, bound to her, the first fruits of the Spirit, and came back down to the noises our mouths make, where a spoken word has a beginning and an end. And what is like Your Word, our Lord, who remains in himself without growing old, and makes all things new?
