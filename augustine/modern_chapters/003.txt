Book Three: Carthage, the Theatre, and the Manichees

(Part 1 of 2)

I came to Carthage, where a cauldron of unholy loves sang all around my ears. I was not yet in love, but I was in love with being in love, and out of a deep-seated need I hated myself for not needing more. I looked for something to love, being in love with loving; and I hated safety, and a road without traps. Inside me there was a famine of that inward food which is You, my God — and yet the famine did not make me hungry. I had no appetite at all for food that does not go bad, not because I was full of it but because the emptier I got the more I loathed it. And so my soul was sick and covered in sores, and it flung itself outside, desperate to be scratched by the touch of things it could feel. Yet if these things had no souls in them, they would not be objects of love. To love and to be loved was sweet to me, and sweeter still when I got to enjoy the person I loved. So I dirtied the spring of friendship with the filth of lust, and clouded its brightness over with the hell of appetite; and foul and ugly as I was, I still wanted, out of enormous vanity, to be thought elegant and sophisticated. I fell headlong into the love I had been longing to be caught by. My God, my mercy, out of Your great goodness how much bitterness You sprinkled over that sweetness for me! For I was loved in return, and I secretly reached the point of enjoying it; and I was joyfully tied down with chains that brought misery, so that I could be beaten with the red-hot rods of jealousy and suspicion and fear and anger and quarrelling.

The theatre carried me away as well, full of pictures of my own miseries and fuel for my fire. Why is it that a man wants to be made sad by watching tragic and terrible things that he would not put up with for a moment himself? And yet he wants to feel grief at them as a spectator, and that very grief is his pleasure. What is this but a miserable madness? For the less free a man is from such feelings himself, the more these performances affect him. When he suffers in his own person we usually call it misery; when he feels for other people we call it compassion. But what sort of compassion is this, for staged and invented sufferings? The listener is not being called on to help, only to grieve — and the more he grieves the more he applauds the actor who is inventing it. And if the disasters of those characters, whether ancient or made up, are acted so badly that the spectator is not moved to tears, he leaves disgusted and complaining; whereas if he is moved, he stays riveted and weeps with enjoyment.

So griefs are loved as well? Surely everybody wants joy. Or is it that although nobody likes being miserable himself, he does like being merciful — and since mercy cannot exist without feeling pain, that is the only reason such pains are loved? This too springs from that same vein of friendship. But where does that vein go? Where does it flow to? Why does it run off into a boiling torrent of pitch, throwing up those monstrous tides of foul appetite, into which it deliberately changes itself, flinging itself down of its own will from its heavenly clarity? Should compassion be got rid of, then? By no means. So griefs may sometimes be loved. But beware of uncleanness, my soul, under the protection of my God, the God of our fathers, who is to be praised and lifted high above everything for ever — beware of uncleanness. I have not stopped pitying people now. But in those days, in the theatres, I rejoiced with lovers when they wickedly enjoyed one another, though it was only make-believe on stage; and when they lost each other I grieved with them, as though full of compassion — and enjoyed myself either way. Now I pity far more the man who is delighted with his own wickedness than the man who is supposed to be suffering hardship by missing out on some destructive pleasure and losing some wretched happiness. That is certainly the truer mercy, but there is no delight in the grief of it. For although a man who grieves for the miserable is to be commended for his charity, a genuinely compassionate person would much rather there were nothing to grieve about. If goodwill could be ill-willed, which it never can, then a man who truly pities others might wish there were some miserable people about for him to pity. So some grief may be allowed; none may be loved. That is how You do it, Lord God, who love souls far more purely than we do and pity them more incorruptibly, and are yet wounded by no grief. And who is equal to that?

But I in my misery loved grieving in those days and went looking for things to grieve at; and in somebody else's suffering, invented and acted, the performance that pleased me best and drew me hardest was the one that made me cry. What wonder that an unhappy sheep, straying from Your flock and impatient of Your keeping, should catch a foul disease? Hence the love of griefs — not the kind that sink deep into me, since I did not want to suffer what I liked to look at, but the kind that scratched the surface lightly when I heard the story. And as when nails are poisoned, what followed the scratching was inflammation and swelling and an infected sore. My life being like that, was it a life at all, my God?

And Your faithful mercy hovered over me from far off. On what dreadful wickednesses I wore myself out, pursuing a sacrilegious curiosity which, now that it had abandoned You, was to bring me down to the treacherous depths and the cheating service of devils, to whom I sacrificed my bad actions — and in all this You were whipping me. I even dared, while Your solemn services were being celebrated inside the walls of Your church, to want and to arrange a piece of business whose fruits deserved death; and for that You whipped me with heavy punishments, though nothing next to what I deserved, my enormous mercy, my God, my refuge from those terrible destroyers among whom I wandered with a stiff neck, going further and further from You, loving my own ways and not Yours — loving a runaway liberty.

The studies that were reckoned respectable had their eye on excelling in the law courts: the more praised, the more crooked. Such is human blindness, glorying even in its blindness. By now I was top of the school of rhetoric, and I was proudly delighted with it and swollen with arrogance — though, Lord, You know, I was far quieter and had nothing at all to do with the wreckings of the "Wreckers", as that ill-omened and devilish name was the very badge of style among the people I lived with. I lived among them with a shameless shame at not being like them. I lived with them and sometimes enjoyed their friendship, while always detesting what they did — their "wreckings", by which they wantonly persecuted the modesty of newcomers, breaking in on them with gratuitous mockery and feeding their own malicious nature on it. Nothing could be more like the actual behaviour of devils. So what could they more truly be called than "Wreckers"? They were wrecked themselves and completely perverted first, with the deceiving spirits secretly laughing at them and leading them on, in the very thing they enjoy — mocking and deceiving others.

Among people like this, at that unsettled time of my life, I studied books of rhetoric, in which I wanted to be outstanding, for a damnable and boastful purpose: delight in human vanity. In the ordinary course of study I came across a book of Cicero's, whose language nearly everybody admires, though not his heart. That book of his is an exhortation to philosophy, and is called the Hortensius. But this book changed what I felt. It turned my prayers towards You, Lord, and gave me different aims and desires. Every empty hope became worthless to me at a stroke, and I longed with an incredible burning of the heart for the immortality that wisdom gives, and began to get up and go back to You. It was not to sharpen my tongue that I took up that book — which sharpening seemed to be what I was buying with my mother's allowance, in my nineteenth year, my father having died two years before. It was not to sharpen my tongue: what got into me from it was not its style but its content.

How I burned then, my God, how I burned to fly back up from earthly things to You — and I did not know what You meant to do with me. For with You is wisdom. But love of wisdom is called philosophy in Greek, and it was philosophy this book set me on fire for. There are people who use philosophy to lead others astray, colouring and disguising their own errors with a great, smooth, respectable name; and almost everyone of that kind, in Cicero's time and before it, is criticised and exposed in that book. It also makes plain that wholesome warning of Your Spirit, given through Your good and devoted servant: Beware lest any man spoil you through philosophy and empty deceit, after the tradition of men, after the rudiments of the world, and not after Christ. For in him dwells all the fullness of the Godhead bodily. And since at that time — You know it, light of my heart — the writings of the Apostles were unknown to me, my delight in that exhortation went only so far as this: that it strongly roused and kindled and inflamed me to love and seek and win and hold and embrace, not this school or that, but wisdom itself, whatever it might be. And one thing alone checked me in the middle of this blaze: that the name of Christ was not in it. For by Your mercy, Lord, this name of my saviour, Your Son, my young heart had devoutly drunk in with my mother's milk and stored up deep; and whatever lacked that name, however learned and polished and true, could not take complete hold of me.

So I decided to turn my mind to the holy Scriptures, to see what they were like. And look: I see something the proud do not understand and that is not laid open to children — humble to approach, lofty in its inner rooms, and veiled in mystery. And I was not the sort who could get inside it, or bend my neck to follow where it went. For I did not feel then as I speak now when I turned to those Scriptures. They struck me as unworthy to be set beside the grandeur of Cicero. My swollen pride recoiled from their plainness, and my sharp wit could not get through to what was inside. Yet they were the kind of thing that grows up along with a little child. But I disdained to be a little child, and being puffed up with pride I took myself for a great man.

So I fell in among men who were proud and delirious, extremely fleshly and full of chatter, whose mouths were traps of the devil, limed with a mixture of the syllables of Your name and of our Lord Jesus Christ and of the Holy Spirit, our Comforter. These names never left their mouths — but only as sound and the noise of the tongue, for their hearts were empty of truth. Still they cried out "Truth! Truth!" and said a great deal to me about it, and it was not in them: they told lies, and not only about You, who truly are Truth, but even about the elements of this world, which are Your creatures. And I ought to have passed over even philosophers who told the truth about those elements, for love of You, my Father, supremely good, beauty of everything beautiful. Truth, Truth — how the marrow of my soul panted after You even then, when they kept sounding Your name at me in all sorts of ways, in many enormous books, and it was only an echo! And these were the dishes on which, while I was starving for You, they served me the sun and moon instead — beautiful works of Yours, but Your works and not You, and not even Your first works. For Your spiritual works come before these bodily ones, however heavenly and shining they are. But it was not even those first works of Yours I hungered and thirsted for; it was You Yourself, the Truth, in whom is no variableness, neither shadow of turning. And still they set before me on those dishes a set of glittering fantasies — and it would have been better to love this actual sun, which is at least real to our sight, than the fantasies that fool the mind through the eyes. But because I took them for You, I fed on them; not eagerly, since You did not taste to me in them as You are, because You were not those empty things; and they did not nourish me but wore me out. Food in a dream looks very like our food when awake, and yet sleepers get no nourishment from it, because they are asleep. But these things were not even in any way like You, as You have now spoken to me, for they were bodily fantasies, false bodies — and the true bodies we see with our physical sight, in the sky or on the earth, are far more certain than they are. Animals and birds can make out those as well as we can, and they are more certain than when we imagine them. And again, we imagine them more certainly than we can guess from them at other vaster and infinite bodies that have no existence at all. Those were the empty husks I was fed on then, and I was not fed. But You, love of my soul, in looking for whom I fail so that I may grow strong, are neither the bodies we see, even in heaven, nor the ones we do not see there; for You created them, and do not count them among the greatest of Your works. How far, then, are You from those fantasies of mine, fantasies of bodies that do not exist at all — when the images of bodies that do exist are far more certain, and the bodies themselves more certain still, which even so You are not; nor are You the soul, which is the life of bodies. So the life of bodies is better and more certain than the bodies. But You are the life of souls, the life of lives, having life in Yourself, and You do not change, life of my soul.

Where were You then, in relation to me, and how far off? Far indeed I was straying from You, shut out even from the husks of the pigs I was feeding with husks. For how much better are the fables of the poets and grammarians than these traps! Verses and poems and "Medea flying" are truly more useful than these men's five elements, variously disguised to answer to five dens of darkness, which do not exist and yet kill the man who believes in them. Verses and poems I can turn into real food; and as for "Medea flying", I might sing it but I did not maintain it, and I heard it sung but I did not believe it — whereas those other things I did believe. What steps I went down by into the depths of hell, toiling and churning for want of truth! For I sought You, my God — I confess it to You, who had mercy on me before I confessed anything — not with the understanding of the mind, by which You wanted me to be above the animals, but with the senses of the flesh. And You were more inward to me than my most inward part, and higher than my highest. I ran into that brazen woman, foolish and knowing nothing, who is pictured in Solomon, sitting at the door and saying, Eat bread of secrecy gladly, and drink stolen waters, which are sweet. She seduced me, because she found my soul living outdoors, in the eye of my flesh, chewing over the sort of food I had swallowed through it.

For I did not know anything else, anything that really is; and I was persuaded, by what felt like sharpness of wit, to agree with foolish deceivers when they asked me, "Where does evil come from?" and "Is God confined to a bodily shape, and does he have hair and nails?" and "Are men to be counted righteous who had several wives at once, and killed people, and sacrificed animals?" I was badly troubled by these questions in my ignorance, and while walking away from the truth I thought I was walking towards it — because I did not yet know that evil is nothing but the absence of good, right down to the point where a thing stops existing altogether. And how was I to see that, when the sight of my eyes reached only to bodies and the sight of my mind only to a phantom? And I did not know God to be a Spirit, not something with parts stretched out in length and breadth, or whose being was bulk; because every bulk is less in a part than in the whole, and if it is infinite it must be less in a part marked off by a certain space than in its infinity, and so is not wholly everywhere, as Spirit is, as God is. And what it was in us by which we resembled God, and could rightly be said to be made in the image of God, I had no idea at all.
