John Locke published this book anonymously in 1689, a few months after William of Orange had taken the English throne from James II, and the preface presents it as a defence of that revolution. He denied writing it for the rest of his life and admitted it only in his will. The denial was not mere modesty. Most of the book was written years earlier, around 1680, when Locke was working for the Earl of Shaftesbury and the question was not how to justify a change of king that had already happened but whether subjects might lawfully rise against one who was still on the throne. Men were executed for less. Locke fled to Holland in 1683 and stayed there six years.

The book is in two parts and this edition has both. The First Treatise, eleven chapters, is a line-by-line demolition of Sir Robert Filmer's Patriarcha, which argued that kings inherit their authority from the fatherly power God gave Adam, and that every man is therefore born a subject. Filmer is unread now, which is why most editions print only the Second Treatise, and the First is often tedious. It is also where Locke is funniest, and where the opening sentence declares slavery so vile that no Englishman should be found arguing for it. Locke says in his preface that the middle of the manuscript was lost and that he could not face doing it again, which is why the First Treatise stops where it does.

The Second Treatise, nineteen chapters, builds the replacement. People begin free and equal under a law of nature; they set up government by consent, and only to protect what Locke calls their property, meaning their lives and liberties as well as their goods; the government holds its power in trust; and when it breaks that trust the people may dissolve it. Thomas Jefferson's argument in the Declaration of Independence is this argument.

Two of his words carry more weight than they look. Property does not mean real estate. Labour is the thing that makes property in the first place: the world was given to everyone in common, and a man makes a part of it his by mixing his work with it, so long as he leaves enough and as good for others. That proviso, and the question of what money does to it, is chapter five, and it is the most argued-over chapter in political philosophy.

Chapter four is on slavery. Locke defines it as a state of war continued between a lawful conqueror and a captive, and holds that nobody can sell himself into it. He was at the same time an investor in the Royal African Company, which traded in slaves, and had a hand in drafting the Fundamental Constitutions of Carolina, which gave every freeman absolute power over his slaves. Both things are true and the book does not reconcile them.

Hobbes' Leviathan, Rousseau's The Social Contract, Paine's Common Sense and the Federalist Papers are in this collection.
