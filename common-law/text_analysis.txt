THE COMMON LAW — TEXT ANALYSIS AND TRANSLATION STRATEGY

THE BOOK
Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. (1841–1935) gave these lectures at the Lowell
Institute in Boston in the winter of 1880, when he was a Boston lawyer and
an editor of the American Law Review, and published them in 1881. A year later he was on the
Supreme Judicial Court of Massachusetts; in 1902 he joined the United States
Supreme Court. The book is the founding text of American legal realism and
the most famous book an American lawyer has written.

ITS ARGUMENT
The law is not a system deduced from principles but a deposit of history:
"The life of the law has not been logic: it has been experience." Rules
that began in one need (vengeance, ritual, the procedure of a writ)
survive after the need is gone and are given new reasons. Holmes traces
this through liability, crime, torts, bailment, possession, contract and
succession, and argues one thesis throughout: the law moves from
standards of personal moral fault toward EXTERNAL standards — what a man
of ordinary prudence would have foreseen, not what this defendant felt.

WHY IT NEEDS A RETELLING (the calque class, and more)
Archaism is low (0.10 per 1,000) but the calque score is high (32.1),
and the obstruction is real: dense Victorian periodic prose; an
apparatus of Year Book citations, Latin, law French and German legal
scholarship; and technical terms used with total precision (trespass,
case, assumpsit, bailment, consideration, seisin, noxal, deodand,
possession, animus domini). A general reader cannot follow the argument
through the apparatus. The ARGUMENT is not hard; the prose around it is.

THE GOVERNING RULE (the hume/mill rule): WHERE HOLMES IS ALREADY CLEAR,
LEAVE HIM ALONE. He is one of the great sentence-makers in American
prose, and his aphorisms are the reason the book is read. They are
pinned verbatim in must_contain.txt. The work is unstacking the long
expository paragraphs, glossing the terms once, and making the historical
examples followable.

VOCABULARY — keep the legal term, gloss it ONCE at first use, then use it
plainly (locked in running_notes.txt as the agents meet them):
  - trespass (a direct, forcible wrong; the old action for it)
  - action on the case / case (the action for indirect harm)
  - negligence; the "prudent man" / "man of ordinary prudence" (Holmes's
    external standard: keep his phrases, do not substitute "reasonable
    person")
  - bailment, bailor, bailee (goods held by one person for another)
  - consideration (what is given in return for a promise)
  - assumpsit, covenant, debt (the old forms of action for promises)
  - deodand (the object forfeited for causing a death), noxal surrender
  - possession, animus domini (the intent to hold as owner)
  - seisin, heir, executor, feoffment, easement, covenant running with land
  - Year Books (the medieval law reports) — gloss once
Latin, law French and German quoted in the body: give the English
meaning in the sentence; keep a short Latin term of art (animus domini,
pro hac vice) with its meaning at first use.

THE FOOTNOTES
Holmes's 833 notes are overwhelmingly bare citations ("Y.B. 6 Ed. IV. 7,
pl. 18"). The source files carry every note, as "Footnote: ..." after the
paragraph that cites it. THE RETELLING DROPS THE BARE CITATIONS and
retells only what Holmes SAYS in a note: an argument, an explanation, a
qualification, a quoted passage that matters to the text. Such a note is
kept as a "Footnote: ..." paragraph in the same place, in Holmes's own
first-person voice, WITHOUT its citations. A note that is only a quoted
authority (a Latin maxim with its source) may be dropped unless the text
depends on it. The original-text companion page carries every note.

CASE NAMES IN THE BODY are Holmes's evidence and are kept (Weaver v.
Ward, Coggs v. Bernard); the reporter citations, where they appear in the
body, are dropped. Page cross-references ("see p. 92") become the lecture
("in Lecture III") — resolve them from the manifest.

REGISTER
Lectures to an educated Boston audience: confident, ironic, compressed,
occasionally grand ("the felt necessities of the time"). The retelling is
in clear, contemporary American English at an eighth-grade-to-high-school
level WITHOUT losing that voice — no chattiness, no invented jokes. Holmes
is dry; stay dry.

SENSITIVE CONTENT — the Verne rule throughout, no notes: the book treats
slaves as property in Roman and Germanic law, the killing and forfeiture
of animals, early vengeance, and Holmes's own hard-edged views (that the
law does not care about a man's inner state; that the criminal law may
sacrifice an individual to the general good). These are his arguments and
stand as his. "Savage"/"barbarian" appear a handful of times in
comparative-history passages: render plainly as "early peoples" only
where the word carries no claim; where Holmes's point IS a claim about
early societies, keep his claim.

FAMOUS PASSAGES (pinned verbatim — see must_contain.txt):
  - "The life of the law has not been logic: it has been experience."
  - "even a dog distinguishes between being stumbled over and being kicked"
  - "In the course of centuries the custom, belief, or necessity disappears,
    but the rule remains."
  - "The truth is, that the law is always approaching, and never reaching,
    consistency."
  - "The general principle of our law is that loss from accident must lie
    where it falls"
  - "The first requirement of a sound body of law is, that it should
    correspond with the actual feelings and demands of the community,
    whether right or wrong."
