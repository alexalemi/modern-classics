Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. was born in Boston in 1841, the son of the doctor and poet of the same name. He left Harvard in 1861 to fight in the Civil War and was wounded three times, at Ball's Bluff, at Antietam and in the Chancellorsville campaign. After the war he read law, practised in Boston, edited the American Law Review and brought out a new edition of Kent's Commentaries. In the winter of 1880 the Lowell Institute asked him to give a course of public lectures, and this book, published in 1881, is those lectures written up. It made his name. Within two years he was a judge of the Supreme Judicial Court of Massachusetts, and from 1902 to 1932 he sat on the Supreme Court of the United States.

The first paragraph is the most quoted thing he ever wrote: the life of the law has not been logic, it has been experience. The rest of the book is the evidence. Holmes takes the main branches of the common law in turn (crime, tort, the liability of carriers and innkeepers, possession, contract, inheritance) and traces each rule back to where it came from. Again and again the origin is something nobody believes any more: the ox that gored a man was surrendered to be punished, a ship was sued as if it were the wrongdoer, a buyer stood in the seller's shoes because an heir was thought to carry on the dead man's person. The reason disappears and the rule stays, and a later age invents a new reason for it.

His second argument is that the law's standards are external. The criminal law and the law of negligence ask what a reasonable man would have foreseen, not what this defendant felt, and the law does not care, in most cases, what a contracting party privately meant. That idea runs through modern American law, and much of what is still argued about in it starts from here.

The book is harder than its reputation, because Holmes wrote for lawyers. He cites year books, Roman jurists and German professors without introducing them, and he uses Latin and Law French as working vocabulary. This edition retells it in modern English. Terms of art are explained the first time they appear. The Latin and French are put into English or glossed where they stand. References to pages of the 1881 book now point to the Lecture. The original has more than eight hundred footnotes, almost all of them bare citations to the law reports; they have been dropped, and the 97 notes in which Holmes argues a point are kept in place as his. One slip of his own in Lecture Eight, where both printings name the plaintiff and the sense needs the promisor, has been corrected. The text as he published it, footnotes and all, is on the companion page.
