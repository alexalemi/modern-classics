Lecture VIII: Contract—II. Elements

The general method for analyzing contract is the same one already explained for possession. Wherever the law gives special rights to one person, or lays special burdens on another, it does so because certain special facts are true of those persons. In every such case, then, the task is twofold. First, find out what the facts are to which the special consequences are attached; second, find out what the consequences are. The first is the main field of legal argument. With contracts, the facts are not always the same. They may be that a certain person has signed, sealed, and delivered a writing to a certain effect. They may be that he has made a spoken promise, and that the person he promised, the promisee, has furnished him a consideration.

The element common to all contracts might be said to be a promise—although even a promise was not necessary for liability in debt, the old action for a fixed sum owed, as that action was formerly understood. But since it will not be possible to discuss covenants (promises under seal) any further, and since consideration was the main topic of the last Lecture, I will take consideration up first. And because consideration in debt differs historically from consideration in assumpsit, I shall confine myself to assumpsit, which is the later and more philosophical form.

It is said that any benefit the promisee confers on the promisor, or any detriment the promisee incurs, may be a consideration. It is also thought that every consideration can be reduced to a case of the second sort, if the word "detriment" is used in a somewhat broad sense.

To illustrate the general doctrine, suppose a man wants a cask of brandy carried from Boston to Cambridge, and a truckman—a man who hauls goods by wagon—says he will carry it, whether out of kindness or from some other motive, and the cask is delivered to him accordingly. If he carelessly staves the cask in, there would perhaps be no need to allege that he undertook to carry it; and on principle, and according to the older cases, if an undertaking was alleged, no consideration for it needed to be stated. The complaint in that case would be of a wrong, apart from any contract. But if the complaint was that he did not carry the cask as agreed, the plaintiff's difficulty would be that the truckman was not bound to carry it unless there was a consideration for his promise. Suppose, then, it was alleged that he promised to carry it in consideration of its being delivered to him. Would that be a sufficient consideration? The oldest cases, working from the notion of benefit to the promisor, said it could not be: the delivery was a trouble to him, not a benefit. Then look at it from the side of detriment. The delivery is a necessary condition of the promisor's doing the kindness at all, and if he does it, the delivery, far from being a detriment to the promisee, is a clear benefit to him.

But this argument is a fallacy. Plainly the delivery would be consideration enough to let the owner sue in assumpsit for the breach of those duties which arose, apart from contract, from the defendant's having undertaken to deal with the thing. It would be consideration enough for any promise whose performance did not involve dealing with the thing at all—a promise to pay a thousand dollars, for instance. And the law has never pronounced a consideration good or bad according to the nature of the promise built on it. The delivery is a sufficient consideration for any promise.

The argument on the other side loses sight of the moment at which the sufficiency of the consideration is to be judged. That moment is when the consideration is furnished. At that moment the delivery of the cask is a detriment in the strictest sense. The owner has given up a present control over the cask, which he had a right to keep, and what he has got in return is not a performance for which the delivery was necessary, but a mere promise of performance. The performance is still in the future.

But it will be seen that, although the delivery may be a consideration, it need not be one. A promise to carry might be made and accepted on the understanding that it was merely a favor, without consideration and not legally binding. In that case the promisee would incur the detriment of delivery just as before, but obviously he would incur it for the sole purpose of enabling the promisor to carry the cask as agreed.

It seems to me that it has not always been kept sufficiently in mind that the same thing may or may not be a consideration, depending on how the parties deal with it. The popular explanation of Coggs v. Bernard is that the delivery was a consideration for a promise to carry the casks safely. I gave what I believe is the true explanation, and the one I think Lord Holt had in view, in the fifth Lecture. But whether mine is right or not, there is a serious objection to the commonly accepted one: the declaration does not allege that the delivery was the consideration.

The same caution should be used in construing the terms of an agreement. It is hard to see why any detriment an instrument happens to reveal or provide for should be raised into a consideration, unless the parties have dealt with it on that footing. In many cases a promisee may incur a detriment without thereby furnishing a consideration. The detriment may be nothing but a condition precedent—something that must happen before the promise has to be performed—as where a man promises to pay another five hundred dollars if he breaks his leg.

The courts, however, have gone a long way toward wiping out this distinction. Acts which, on a fair reading of the language, seem to have been meant only as the fulfilling of a condition have been treated as the consideration for the promise. So have counter-promises in an agreement which expressly named other things as the consideration. It should also be mentioned—leaving open whether there may be some special explanation for the doctrine—that an assignment of a lease is said not to be "voluntary," that is, a gift, under the statute of 27 Elizabeth, chapter 4, because the assignee takes on the obligations of the tenant. Yet the assignee's taking on that detriment may not have been meant as the inducement for the assignment at all. In many cases it only amounts to a deduction from the benefit conferred, as a right of way over the land would, especially if the only obligation is to pay rent, which in legal theory issues out of the land itself.

But although the courts may sometimes have gone a little far in their eagerness to uphold agreements, there can be no doubt of the principle I have laid down: that the same thing may or may not be a consideration, depending on how the parties deal with it. This raises the question how a thing must be dealt with in order to make it a consideration.

It is said that consideration must not be confused with motive. It is true that it must not be confused with what may in actual fact be the prevailing or chief motive. A man may promise to paint a picture for five hundred dollars while his chief motive is a desire for fame. A consideration may in fact be given and accepted solely for the purpose of making a promise binding. But, all the same, it is the essence of a consideration that, by the terms of the agreement, it is given and accepted as the motive or inducement of the promise. And the other way round: the promise must be made and accepted as the conventional motive or inducement for furnishing the consideration—conventional in the sense of agreed upon, whatever the real motives may be. The root of the whole matter is the relation of reciprocal conventional inducement, each for the other, between consideration and promise.

A good example of the first half of this proposition is a Massachusetts case. A man had made a spoken bargain to buy some wood standing on the plaintiff's land and had given his promissory note for it, but the plaintiff refused to let the wood be taken away unless he got additional security. So the buyer and the plaintiff went to the defendant, and the defendant put his name on the note. The plaintiff then let the buyer carry off the wood. But according to the testimony, the defendant signed without knowing that the plaintiff was going to change his position in any way on the strength of the signature, and it was held that, if that account was believed, there was no consideration.

An illustration of the other half of the rule is found in the cases where a reward is offered for doing something, and the thing is afterward done by a person who does not know of the offer. In such a case the reward cannot be claimed, because the supposed consideration was not furnished on the faith of the offer. The promise that was held out did not induce the furnishing of the consideration. A promise cannot be set up as the conventional motive for an act when nobody knew of it until after the act was done.

Footnote: The New York court criticized the English case of Williams v. Carwardine on this point. But in that case it does not appear that the plaintiff did not know of the offer of a reward; it appears only that the jury found she was in fact moved by other motives—a finding wholly beside the mark.

Both sides of the relation between consideration and promise, and the conventional nature of that relation, can be illustrated by the case of the cask. Suppose the truckman is willing to carry the cask, and the owner willing to let him, without any bargain, and each knows the other's state of mind. But the truckman, seeing some advantage to himself in the matter, says to the owner, "In consideration of your delivering me the cask, and letting me carry it, I promise to carry it," and the owner thereupon delivers it. I suppose the promise would be binding. The promise is offered in terms as the inducement for the delivery, and the delivery is made in terms as the inducement for the promise. It may be very likely that the owner would have delivered the cask without any promise, and that the truckman would have made his promise as a free favor if it had not been accepted on consideration; but that is only a guess, after all. The owner need not have delivered the cask unless he chose to, and since he delivered it as a term of a bargain, the promisor cannot set up what might have happened in order to destroy the effect of what did happen. It seems, then, that the same transaction, in substance and spirit, may be voluntary or binding according to the form of words the parties choose to use for the purpose of fixing its legal consequences.

If these principles are accepted, they will be seen to explain a doctrine which the courts have had some trouble in establishing: the doctrine that an executed consideration—something already done and over with—will not support a later promise. It has been said, to be sure, that such a consideration was enough if the thing had been done at the promisor's request. But the objections to that view are plain. If the request was of such a kind, and so put, as reasonably to imply that the other person was to be rewarded, then there was an express promise, even though not put into words, and that promise was made at the same time the consideration was given, not afterward. If, on the other hand, the words did not justify the understanding that the service was to be paid for, the service was a gift; and a past gift can no more be a consideration than any other act of the promisee that was not induced by the promise.

The source of the error can be traced, at least in part, in history. Some suggestions about it were made in the last Lecture; a few words should be added here. In the old cases of debt, where there was some question whether the plaintiff had shown enough to maintain his action, a "contract precedent"—a bargain made beforehand—was mentioned several times as what raised the duty. Thus, where a man had bound himself in one hundred shillings to pay his servant on a certain day for his services and for payments the servant had made on his account, it was argued that there was no contract precedent, and that a party is not bound by mere words; and further, that for all that appeared, the servant had made the payments on his own initiative and at no one's request, and from that no duty could arise.

So, too, when debt was brought on a deed to pay the plaintiff ten marks if he would marry the defendant's daughter, and it was objected that the action should have been covenant, the answer was that the plaintiff had a contract precedent, which gave him debt.

The first case in assumpsit meant only to adopt this long-familiar idea. A man went bail for his friend's servant, who had been arrested. Afterward the master promised to indemnify him—to make good any loss the bail might cost him—and when the master failed to do so, the man sued him in assumpsit. It was held that there was no consideration for which the defendant should be charged, unless the master had promised to indemnify the plaintiff before the servant was bailed; "for the master did never make request to the plaintiff for his servant to do so much, but he did it of his own head." This is perfectly plain sailing, and means no more than the case in the Year Books. The report, however, also mentions a case in which it was held that a later promise, made in consideration that the plaintiff had married the defendant's cousin at the defendant's special request, was binding, and that the marriage was "good cause... because [it] ensued the request of the defendant." Whether this was meant to establish a general principle, or was decided with an eye to the peculiar consideration of marriage, it was soon read in the broader sense, as was shown in the last Lecture. It was held several times that a past and executed matter was a sufficient consideration for a promise made at a later day, provided only that the thing relied on had been done or furnished at the promisor's request.

It is now time to analyze the nature of a promise, which is the second and most conspicuous element in a simple contract. The Indian Contract Act of 1872, in Section 2, says:

"(a.) When one person signifies to another his willingness to do or to abstain from doing anything, with a view to obtaining the assent of that other to such act or abstinence, he is said to make a proposal:

"(b.) When the person to whom the proposal is made signifies his assent thereto, the proposal is said to be accepted. A proposal when accepted becomes a promise."

By this definition, what may be promised is limited to the conduct of the promisor himself. If that only meant that the promisor alone must bear whatever legal burden his promise may create, it would be true. But that is not what it means. For this is a definition of a promise, not of a legally binding promise. We are not looking for the legal effects of a contract, but for the possible contents of a promise which the law may or may not enforce. So we must consider only what can possibly be promised in a legal sense, not what the secondary consequences will be of a promise that is binding but not performed.

An assurance that it shall rain tomorrow, or that some third person shall paint a picture, may just as well be a promise as an assurance that the promisee shall receive a hundred bales of cotton from some source, or that the promisor will pay the promisee a hundred dollars. What is the difference between these cases? It lies only in how much power the promisor has over the event. In the first case he has none. He has just as little legal authority to make a man paint a picture, although he may have greater means of persuasion. He will probably be able to make sure that the promisee gets the cotton. And being a rich man, he is certain to be able to pay the hundred dollars, barring some most improbable accident.

But the law does not, as a general rule, ask how far the fulfilment of an assurance about the future lies within the promisor's power. In the moral world it may be that the obligation of a promise is confined to what lies within reach of the promisor's will (except so far as the limit is unknown to one side and misrepresented by the other). But unless some consideration of public policy intervenes, I take it that a man may bind himself at law that any future event shall happen. He can therefore promise it in a legal sense. It may be said that when a man covenants that it shall rain tomorrow, or that A shall paint a picture, he is only saying, in short form, "I will pay if it does not rain, or if A does not paint a picture." But that is not necessarily so. A promise could easily be framed which would be broken by fair weather, or by A's not painting. A promise, then, is simply an accepted assurance that a certain event or state of things shall come to pass.

But if this is true, it matters for more than just enlarging the definition of the word promise. It bears on the theory of contract. At common law, the consequences of a binding promise do not depend on how much power the promisor has over the promised event. If the promised event does not come to pass, the promisor's property is sold to satisfy the damages—within certain limits—that the promisee has suffered by the failure. The consequences are the same in kind whether the promise is that it shall rain, or that another man shall paint a picture, or that the promisor will deliver a bale of cotton.

If the legal consequence is the same in every case, it seems right that all contracts should be looked at from the same legal point of view. In the case of a binding promise that it shall rain tomorrow, the immediate legal effect of what the promisor does is that he takes the risk of the event, within certain defined limits, as between himself and the promisee. He does no more when he promises to deliver a bale of cotton.

If it is proper to state the common-law meaning of promise and contract in this way, it has the advantage of freeing the subject from the needless theory that contract is a qualified subjection of one will to another, a kind of limited slavery. It might be seen that way if the law compelled men to perform their contracts, or allowed promisees to compel them. If, when a man promised to work for another, the law made him do it, his relation to the promisee might with some truth be called a servitude ad hoc—a bondage for that one purpose. But that is what the law never does. It never steps in until a promise has been broken, and therefore can no longer be performed according to its terms. It is true that in some instances equity does what is called compelling specific performance—ordering the promisor to do the very thing he promised. But, in the first place, I am speaking of the common law; and, in the second, this only means that equity compels performance of those parts of the whole promise which can still be performed. Take, for instance, a promise to convey land within a certain time. A court of equity does not usually interfere until the time has gone by, so that the promise can no longer be performed as it was made. But if the conveyance matters more than the time, and the promisee would rather have it late than never, the law may compel performance of that much. Even then it does not literally compel it; it puts the promisor in prison unless he will convey. This remedy is an exceptional one. The only universal consequence of a legally binding promise is, that the law makes the promisor pay damages if the promised event does not come to pass. In every case it leaves him free from interference until the time for fulfilment has gone by, and therefore free to break his contract if he chooses.

A more practical advantage of looking at a contract as the taking of a risk is the light it throws on the measure of damages—the rule for how much a plaintiff can recover. If a breach of contract were regarded in the same light as a tort, it would seem to follow that if, while the contract was being performed, the promisor were told of some particular consequence that would result from its not being performed, he should be held liable for that consequence if he failed to perform. Such a suggestion has been made. But it has not been accepted as the law. On the contrary, according to the opinion of a very able judge, which seems to be generally followed, notice of special circumstances out of which special damages would arise in case of breach—even notice given at the time the contract is made—is not enough, unless the assumption of that risk is to be taken as having fairly entered into the contract. If a carrier undertook to carry the machinery of a sawmill from Liverpool to Vancouver Island and failed to do so, he probably would not be held liable for the hire value of the machinery during the unavoidable delay, even though he knew it could not be replaced without sending to England—unless he was fairly understood to accept "the contract with the special condition attached to it."

It is true that when people make contracts they usually have the performance in mind rather than the breach. The express language they use does not generally go further than to define what will happen if the contract is carried out. A statute requiring a written memorandum of the contract would be satisfied by a written statement of the promise as it was made. That is so because to require more would run against the ordinary habits of mankind; and also because the statement that the effect of a contract is the assumption of the risk of a future event does not mean that there is a second, subsidiary promise to assume that risk. It means that the assumption follows as a consequence enforced directly by the law, without the promisor's co-operation. So parol evidence—evidence of what was said outside the writing—would no doubt be admissible to enlarge or narrow the extent of the liability assumed for non-performance, where it would not be admissible to change the scope of the promise.

But these concessions do not affect the view taken here. Since the relation between the parties to a contract is voluntary, the consequences attached to that relation must be voluntary too. What event the promise contemplates—in other words, what will amount to a breach of contract—is a matter of interpretation and construction. Which consequences of the breach are assumed is likewise, though more remotely, a matter of construction, taking into account the circumstances in which the contract is made. Knowledge of what depends on performance is one of those circumstances. It is not necessarily conclusive, but it may have the effect of enlarging the risk assumed.

The very business of construction is to work out, from what was expressly said and done, what would have been said about events not definitely before the parties' minds, if those events had been considered. In mercantile contracts the price paid generally rules out the reading that exceptional risks were meant to be assumed. The foregoing analysis is believed to show that the result the courts have reached on grounds of practical good sense falls in with the true theory of contract under the common law.

The discussion of the nature of a promise has led me to analyze contract and its consequences somewhat ahead of their place. I must say a word more about the facts that make up a promise. It is laid down, with theoretical truth, that besides the assurance or offer on one side, there must be an acceptance on the other. But I find it hard to think of a case in which a simple contract fails to be made that could not be explained on other grounds—generally by the lack of the relation between offer and consideration as reciprocal inducements, each of the other. Acceptance of an offer usually follows by mere implication from the furnishing of the consideration. And since by our law an accepted offer, or promise, stands on no different footing from an offer not yet accepted until the consideration is furnished—each being revocable until then, and each continuing until then unless it has expired or been revoked—the question of acceptance is rarely of practical importance.

Assuming that the general nature of consideration and promise is now understood, some questions peculiar to bilateral contracts—contracts in which each side makes a promise to the other—remain to be considered. They concern the sufficiency of the consideration, and the moment when the contract is made.

A promise may be a consideration for a promise, though not every promise for every other. It may be doubted whether a promise to make someone a gift of a hundred dollars would be supported by his promise to accept it. But where there were mutual promises, one to transfer and the other to accept unpaid shares in a railway company, it has been held that a binding contract was made. Here one party agrees to part with something that may prove valuable, and the other to take on a liability that may prove burdensome.

But now suppose there is no element of uncertainty except in the minds of the parties. Take, for instance, a bet on a horse race that has already been run. It has been thought that this would amount to an absolute promise on one side and no promise at all on the other. But this does not seem to me sound. Contracts are dealings between men by which they make arrangements for the future. In making such arrangements, what matters is not what is objectively true but what the parties know. Any present fact unknown to the parties is just as uncertain, for the purpose of making an arrangement at this moment, as any future fact. It is therefore a detriment to undertake to be ready to pay if the event turns out not to have been as expected. This seems to be the true explanation of why forbearing to sue on a claim the plaintiff believes to be good is a sufficient consideration, even though the claim was in fact bad and the defendant knew it was bad. If this view were unsound, it is hard to see how bets on any future event, short of a miracle, could be upheld. For if the happening or not happening of the event is subject to the law of cause and effect, the only uncertainty about it lies in our foresight, not in its happening.

The question when a contract is made arises for the most part with bilateral contracts made by letter. The doubt is whether the contract is complete at the moment the return promise is put into the mail, or at the moment it is received. If convenience weighs more heavily on the side of either view, that is reason enough to adopt it. As far as purely logical grounds go, the most ingenious argument for the later moment is Professor Langdell's. According to him, the conclusion follows from the fact that the consideration which makes the offer binding is itself a promise. Every promise, he says, is an offer before it is a promise, and the essence of an offer is that it should be communicated. But this reasoning seems unsound. When, as in the case supposed, the consideration for the return promise has been put within the offeree's power, and the return promise has been accepted in advance, there is not an instant, either in time or in logic, when the return promise is an offer. It is a promise, and a term of a binding contract, as soon as it is anything. An offer is a revocable and unaccepted communication of willingness to promise. When an offer of a certain bilateral contract has been made, the same contract cannot be offered by the other side. The so-called offer would be neither revocable nor unaccepted. It would complete the contract the moment it was made.

If it is said that it is of the essence of a promise to be communicated, whether or not it passes through the stage of offer—meaning by "communicated" brought to the actual knowledge of the promisee—the law is believed to be otherwise. A covenant is binding when it is delivered and accepted, whether it is read or not. On the same principle, it is believed that whenever the obligation is to be entered into by a tangible sign—as, in the case supposed, by a letter containing the return promise—and the consideration for the promise and the assent to it have already been given, the only question is when the tangible sign is sufficiently put into the promisee's power. I cannot believe that, if the letter had been delivered to the promisee and then snatched from his hands before he had read it, there would be no contract. If I am right, it matters little whether the post office is regarded as the offerer's agent, or as his bailee, or as a mere box to which he has access. The offeree, when he drops the letter containing the counter-promise into the letter box, does an overt act which, by general understanding, gives up control over the letter and puts it into a third hand for the benefit of the offerer, who is at liberty to take it at any moment afterward.

The principles governing revocation are wholly different. A person to whom an offer is made has a right to assume that it stays open according to its terms until he has actual notice to the contrary. The effect of one communication must be undone by a counter-communication. But the making of a contract does not depend on the state of the parties' minds; it depends on their overt acts. When the sign of the counter-promise is a tangible object, the contract is complete when control over that object changes hands.
