Lecture VII: Contract—I. History
(Part 1 of 3)

The doctrine of contract has been so thoroughly remodelled to meet the needs of modern times that there is less need here than elsewhere for historical research. It has been so ably discussed that there is less room here than elsewhere for essentially new analysis. But a short account of how the modern doctrines grew up, whether necessary or not, will at least be interesting. And an analysis of their main characteristics cannot be left out, and may show some new features.

It is popularly supposed that the oldest forms of contract known to our law are covenant, the action on a promise made under seal, and debt, the action for a fixed sum owed. They are of early date, no doubt. But there are other contracts still in use which, although they have in some degree put on modern dress, at least raise the question whether they did not appear just as early.

One of these, the promissory oath, is no longer the foundation of any rights in private law. It is still used, but mainly as a solemnity that goes with entering upon a public office. The judge swears that he will do justice according to law; the juryman, that he will find his verdict according to law and the evidence; the newly naturalized citizen, that he will bear true faith and allegiance to the government of his choice.

But there is another contract which plays a more important part. It may sound paradoxical to name the contract of suretyship—the promise of one person to answer for another's debt or default. Suretyship nowadays is only an accessory obligation. It presupposes a principal undertaking by someone else, and so far as the nature of the contract goes, it is just like any other. But, as Laferrière has pointed out, and very likely earlier writers too, the surety of ancient law was the hostage. And the giving of hostages was by no means confined to dealings between nations.

In the old verse romance of Huon of Bordeaux, Huon has killed the son of Charlemagne, and the Emperor requires him, as the price of forgiveness, to perform a series of seeming impossibilities. Huon sets out on the task, leaving twelve of his knights behind as hostages. He comes back successful, but at first the Emperor is made to believe that his orders have been disobeyed. At that Charlemagne cries out, "I summon hither the pledges for Huon. I will hang them, and they shall have no ransom." In the same way, when Huon is to fight a duel to establish the truth or falsehood of a charge against him, each side begins by producing some of his friends as hostages.

When hostages are given for a duel that is to settle the truth of an accusation, the transaction comes very close to giving the same kind of security in the trial of a case in court. That was in fact the usual course of Germanic procedure. It will be remembered that law first appeared as a substitute for the private feuds between families or clans. A defendant who would not peaceably submit to the court's jurisdiction might be put outside the protection of the law, so that any man might kill him on sight. But at first there was no way to secure the compensation the plaintiff was entitled to unless the defendant chose to give security for it.

The English customs that have come down to us are somewhat more advanced, but one of the noticeable features of their procedure is the giving of security at every step. All lawyers will remember a trace of this in the fiction of John Doe and Richard Roe, the imaginary pledges who stood surety for the plaintiff that he would prosecute his action. A more significant example is the rule, repeated in many of the early laws, that a defendant accused of a wrong must either find security or go to prison. This security was the hostage of earlier days. Later, when the actions for punishment and the actions for redress were separated, it became the bail of the criminal law—the persons who answer for a prisoner's appearance in return for his release. And the liability was still conceived of in the same way as when the bail actually put his own body into the power of the party he secured.

One of Charlemagne's additions to the Salic Law speaks of a freeman who has committed himself to the power of another by way of surety. The very phrase is copied in the English laws of Henry I. We have seen what this meant in the story of Huon of Bordeaux. The Mirror of Justices says that King Canute used to judge the mainprisors—the sureties who took a man out of custody on their undertaking for him—in the same way as their principals when the principals failed to appear in court. King Henry I, it says, confined Canute's rule to mainprisors who had consented to the crime.

As late as the reign of Edward III, Shard, an English judge, first states the law as it still is—that bail are a prisoner's keepers, and shall be charged if he escapes—and then observes that some say the bail shall be hanged in his place. That was the law in the parallel case of a jailer. The old notion can still be traced in the form modern writers give for the undertaking of bail in a case of felony. The bail are bound "body for body," and modern law-books find it necessary to explain that this does not make them liable to the principal offender's punishment if he fails to appear, but only to a fine. The contract also differed from our modern ideas in the way it was made. It was simply a solemn admission of liability in the presence of an officer authorized to take it. The bail did not have to sign, and the person bailed did not have to bind himself as a party.

But these peculiarities have been modified or abolished by statute. I have dwelt on the case not so much because it is a special form of contract unlike all others, as because the history of its origin shows one of the first appearances of contract in our law. It can be traced to a gradual growth of faith that the hostage would honorably surrender himself if the occasion arose, and to the consequent relaxing of actual imprisonment. An illustration may be found in the parallel way of dealing with the prisoner himself. His bail, into whose hands his body is supposed to be delivered, have a right to seize him at any time and anywhere, but he is allowed to go at large until he is surrendered. It will be noticed that this form of contract looked to the body of the contracting party as the satisfaction. So did debt as the Roman law of the Twelve Tables dealt with it, and for the same motive, although by a different process.

Debt is another and more popular candidate for the honor of being first. Since the time of Savigny, the first appearance of contract in both Roman and German law has often been traced to a sale that by some accident was left incomplete. The question does not seem to have much philosophical significance. To explain how mankind first learned to promise, we would have to go to metaphysics and find out how it ever came to frame a future tense. The nature of the particular promise that happened to be enforced first in a given system can hardly lead to any truth of general importance. But the history of the action of debt is instructive, if in a humbler way. One must know something about it to understand the enlightened rules that make up the law of contract today.

In Glanvill's treatise the action of debt is already one of the well-known remedies. But the law of those days was still in a somewhat primitive state, and it is easy to imagine that a form of action going back that far was not founded on any very delicate distinctions. It was, as I shall try to show directly, simply the general form in which any money claim was collected. The one exception was unliquidated claims—claims whose amount had not been fixed—for damage done by force, and for those there was the equally general remedy of trespass.

Some have thought the action was adopted from the procedure of the Roman law, which was then more civilized. It is a natural opinion, since all the early English law-writers take their phrasing and their classifications from Rome. Still, it seems much more probable that the action is of pure German descent. It has the features of the primitive procedure found on the Continent, as Laband describes it.

The substance of the plaintiff's claim, as set out in the writ of debt, is that the defendant owes him so much and wrongfully withholds it. For a claim framed like that, it does not matter how the defendant's duty arises. It is not confined to contract. It is satisfied if there is a duty to pay on any ground at all. It states a bare conclusion of law, not the facts from which the conclusion follows and the liability arises. The old German complaint was the same: "A owes me so much."

It was characteristic of German procedure that the defendant could meet that complaint by answering, in an equally general form, that he did not owe the plaintiff anything. If the plaintiff wanted to keep the defendant from escaping in that way, he had to do more than simply allege a debt. In England, if the plaintiff had nothing to show for his debt, the defendant's denial put him out of court. And even if he had something, he could still be defeated by the defendant's swearing, with some of his friends to back him, that he owed nothing. The chief reason why debt was pushed aside for centuries by a later remedy, assumpsit, was the survival of this relic of early days.

Finally, in England as in Germany, debt for withholding money was the twin brother of the action for wrongfully withholding any other kind of chattel. In either case the gist of the complaint was the same.

It seems strange that this crude product of the infancy of law should matter to us today. Yet whenever we trace a leading doctrine of substantive law far enough back, we are very likely to find some forgotten circumstance of procedure at its source. Illustrations of this truth have been given already. The action of debt and the other actions of contract will furnish more. Debt throws the most light on the doctrine of consideration.

Our law does not enforce every promise a man may make. Promises made as ninety-nine out of a hundred are made—by word of mouth or by simple writing—are not binding unless there is a consideration for them. That is, as it is commonly explained, unless the promisee has either conferred a benefit on the promisor or incurred a detriment as the inducement to the promise.

It has been thought that this rule was borrowed from the Roman law by the Chancery, and that after some modification there it passed into the common law.

But this account of the matter is at least questionable. So far as the words go, I do not know of consideration being plainly called "cause" before the reign of Elizabeth; in the earlier reports it always appears as quid pro quo. Its first appearance, so far as I know, is in Fleta's account of the action of debt. I am inclined to think Fleta's statement is not to be trusted. But a careful look at the cases in the Year Books, in their chronological order, will show, I think, that the doctrine was fully developed in debt before any mention of it in equity can be found. One of the earliest references to what a promisor was to get for his undertaking was in the action of assumpsit. But the doctrine certainly did not start there. The first mention of consideration in connection with equity that I have seen takes the form of quid pro quo, and it comes after the requirement had been thoroughly established in debt.

Footnote: Glanvill's phrase "justa debendi causa," a just cause of owing, seems to me remote from consideration.

Consideration was never required for contracts under seal—unless Fleta is to be trusted against the great weight of nearly contemporary evidence. That single fact goes far to show that the rule cannot have begun as a rule of substantive law, adopted on grounds of policy. And the other way round, the fact that the doctrine coincides with a peculiar mode of procedure points very strongly to the likelihood that the peculiar requirement and the peculiar procedure were connected. It will throw light on the question to put together a few undisputed facts and consider what consequences naturally followed from them. So it will be worth examining the action of debt a little further. But it is only fair to admit at the outset that I offer the explanation which follows with great hesitation, and, I think, with a full appreciation of the objections that might be raised against it.

I said a moment ago that, to recover against a defendant who denied his debt, the plaintiff had to show something for it. Otherwise he was turned over to the limited jurisdiction of the spiritual courts, the courts of the Church. This requirement did not mean evidence in the modern sense. It meant simply that he must maintain his case in one of the ways the law then recognized. There were three: the duel, a writing, and witnesses. The duel need not be discussed; it soon ceased to be used in debt, and it has no bearing on what I have to say. Trial by writing and trial by witnesses, on the other hand, must both be carefully studied. It will be convenient to take the witnesses first and find out what they were.

One thing we know from the start: they were not witnesses as we understand the word. They were not brought before a jury to be examined and cross-examined, and their testimony did not depend for its effect on being believed by the court that heard it. Nowadays a case is not decided by the evidence, but by a verdict, or a finding of facts, followed by a judgment. The oath of a witness has no effect unless it is believed. But in the time of Henry II our trial by jury did not exist. When an oath was allowed to be sworn, it had the same effect whether it was believed or not. There was no second body to sift it. In the cases where trial by witnesses was possible, if the party called on to go forward could find a certain number of men willing to swear in a certain form, that was the end of the matter.

Now this seems a more primitive way of proving a debt than producing the defendant's written acknowledgment, and it is important to discover where it came from.

The early books and reports show that the cases in which this mode of trial was used were almost wholly confined to claims arising out of a sale or a loan. And the question at once arises whether we are not on the traces of an institution that was already ancient when Glanvill wrote. For centuries before the Conquest, Anglo-Saxon law had required the choosing of a certain number of official witnesses, two or three of whom were to be called in to every bargain of sale. The purpose of these witnesses is not commonly supposed to have been the proof of debts. They go back to a time when theft and similar offenses were the chief ground of lawsuits, and they were appointed to give a means of deciding whether a person charged with holding stolen property had come by it rightfully or not. A defendant could clear himself of the felony by their oath that he had bought or received the thing openly, in the way the law appointed.

Having been present at the bargain, the witnesses were able to swear to what they had seen and heard if any question arose between the parties. So their use was not confined to disposing of a charge of felony. But that particular service is what identifies the transaction witnesses of the Saxon period. Now we know that these witnesses did not disappear at once under Norman influence. They are found with their old function in the laws of William the Conqueror. And the language of Glanvill seems to prove that they were still known under Henry II. He says that a purchaser may be able to summon in the man he bought from, to warrant the property to him and defend the suit—in which case the danger shifts to the seller. But if he cannot, then if he has sufficient proof that he lawfully bought the thing, de legittimo marcatu suo, "of his lawful market," it will clear him of felony. If he has not sufficient suit, he will be in danger. This is the law of William over again. It follows that purchasers still used the transaction witnesses.

Footnote: Suit, secta, was the term for the persons whose oath a party offered in support of his case.

But Glanvill also seems to allow the use of witnesses to prove debts. Since the transaction witnesses had formerly served this purpose, I see no reason to doubt that they still did, and that he is speaking of them here as well. Moreover, for a long time after Henry II, whenever an action was brought for a debt of which there was no written evidence, the plaintiff, when asked what he had to show for it, always answered "good suit" and offered his witnesses, who were sometimes examined by the court. I think it is not straining the evidence to infer that the "good suit" of the later reports was the descendant of the Saxon transaction witnesses, just as Glanvill's secta has been shown to be.

Footnote: It was no doubt true, as Glanvill says, that the usual mode of proof was by a writing or by duel, and that the King's Court did not generally give protection to private agreements made anywhere but in the King's Court itself. But it can hardly be that debts were never proved by witnesses in his time, in view of the continuous evidence from Bracton onward.

Footnote: I do not go so far as to say that the transaction witnesses were still a living institution. However that may be, tradition must at least have modelled itself on what had been the function of the former official body.

With this step in the argument taken, it will be well to recall once more the original nature of the witness oath. It was confined to facts the witnesses knew by sight and hearing. And since the purposes for which witnesses were provided required their presence only when property changed hands, the main case in which they could be of use between the parties to a bargain was when a debt was claimed because property had been delivered. The purpose did not reach agreements that were executory on both sides—where neither side had yet done anything—because there no question of theft could arise. And Glanvill shows that in his time the King's Court did not enforce such agreements. Now, if the oath of the secta could only be used to prove a debt where the transaction witnesses could have sworn, it is easy enough to see how an accident of procedure may have led to a most important rule of substantive law.

Footnote: It is possible that Glanvill means no more here than his often repeated statement that the King's Court did not, generally speaking, take notice of private agreements. The substantive law was perhaps still limited by traditions from the infancy of contract, as discussed elsewhere in this Lecture. The proposition in its broadest form may have rested on the impossibility of trying such agreements in any way but those which have been named.

Put the pieces together. Witnesses could only swear to facts within their knowledge. By accident, these witnesses were not used in transactions that might create a debt, except for one particular fact, the delivery of property. And by a further accident, this delivery was quid pro quo. Taken together, these came to the same thing as a rule that when a debt was proved by witnesses, there must be quid pro quo. But debts proved by witnesses instead of by deed are what we call simple contract debts—debts resting on a promise not under seal. And so, beginning with debt and later spreading to other contracts, our peculiar and most important doctrine is established: that every simple contract must have a consideration. This was never the law for debts or contracts proved in the usual way, by the defendant's seal. And the fact that it applied only to obligations which were formerly proved by a procedure of limited use goes far to show that its connection with procedure was not accidental.

The mode of proof soon changed, but as late as the reign of Queen Elizabeth we find a trace of this original connection. It is said, "But the common law requires that there should be a new cause (i. e. consideration), whereof the country may have intelligence or knowledge for the trial of it, if need be, so that it is necessary for the Public-weal." The "country" there is the jury, who must be able to know of the cause if the case is tried. Lord Mansfield showed his intuition of the historical grounds of our law when he said, "I take it that the ancient notion about the want of consideration was for the sake of evidence only; for when it is reduced into writing, as in covenants, specialties, bonds, etc., there was no objection to the want of consideration."

It may be objected that the argument so far is necessarily confined to debt, whereas the requirement of consideration applies equally to all simple contracts. The answer is that in all probability the rule began with debt and spread from debt to other contracts.

But it may be asked again whether there were no contracts proved by witnesses besides those already mentioned. Were there none proved in that way which lacked the accidental consideration? To this, too, there is an easy answer. The contracts enforced by the civil courts, even as late as Henry II, were few and simple. The witness procedure was no doubt broad enough for all the contracts made in early times. Besides sale, loan, and the like, which have been mentioned, I find only two contractual obligations. These were the warranties that went with a sale, and the suretyship referred to at the beginning of the Lecture. Of the first, the warranty of title was regarded rather as an obligation the law raised out of the relation of buyer and seller than as a contract. Other express warranties were matters within the knowledge of the transaction witnesses, and in Saxon times the witnesses swore to them.

But in the Norman period warranty is very little heard of except with regard to land, and then it was decided by the duel. Apart from warranties set down in a deed, it disappeared so completely that it can have had no influence on the law of consideration. I shall therefore assume, without more detail, that it does not bear on the question.

Then there is the pledge, or surety. He no longer paid with his body, except in very rare cases. His liability was translated into money and enforced in an action of debt. This time-honored contract, like the other debts of Glanvill's time, could be proved by witnesses without a writing. And here there was no consideration of the kind the law required when the doctrine was first stated—no benefit to the promisor. But this too is unimportant, because the surety's liability on the oath of witnesses came to an end, as did the warrantor's, before the foundations were laid for the rule I am trying to explain. A writing soon came to be required, as will be seen in a moment.

The result so far is this. The only action of contract in Glanvill's time was debt. The only debts recovered without a writing were those that have been described. And the only one of these for which there was no quid pro quo had ceased to be recoverable in that way by the reign of Edward III.

But great changes were beginning in the reign of Henry II. More varied and complex contracts soon came to be enforced. It may be asked why the scope of the witness oath was not enlarged. Or, if some better proof was to be had, why the secta was not done away with and other oral testimony admitted. And in any case, what can the law of Henry II's time have to do with consideration, which was not heard of until centuries later?
