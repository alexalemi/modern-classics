Lecture X: Successions after Death
(Part 2 of 2)

The obvious analogy between purchaser and heir seems to have been used in the folk-laws of the Germanic peoples, but mainly for a different purpose from those we shall have to consider in the English law. The purpose was to widen the range of things that could be alienated—given or sold away. It will be remembered that there are many traces of family ownership in early German law, as there are in early Roman law. And it seems that property which originally could not be given outside the family came to be transferred by the device of making the grantee an heir.

The history of language points to this conclusion. The Latin word heres, as Beseler and others have remarked, first meant a successor to the property of a dead person. It was then extended to the donee mortis causa—the person who receives a gift made in contemplation of death—and more broadly still to grantees in general. The verb hereditare was used in the same way for the transfer of land. Laferrière quotes Hevin as pointing out that the ancient usage in French was to say heriter, "to inherit," for buying, heritier, "heir," for the buyer, and desheriter, "to disinherit," for selling.

The texts of the Salic Law give us evidence that cannot be disputed. A man could transfer the whole or any part of his property by delivering possession of it to a trustee, who within twelve months handed it over to the beneficiaries—to those, the text reads, "whom the donor has named heirs" (quos heredes appellavit). Here, then, was a voluntary transfer of as much or as little property as the owner pleased, to persons freely chosen, who were not necessarily universal successors, if they ever were, and who nevertheless took under the name of heirs. The word, which must at first have meant persons taking by descent, was extended to persons taking by purchase. If the word grew in meaning, it was probably because the thought it carried was being turned to new uses. The transaction seems to have stood halfway between the naming of an heir and a sale. The later law of the Ripuarian Franks treats it more plainly as the naming of an heir. It allows a man who has no sons to give all his property to whomever he chooses, relatives or strangers, as an inheritance—either by adfathamire, as the Salic form was called, or by writing, or by delivery.

The Lombards had a similar transfer, in which the donee was not only called heres but was made liable, like an heir, for the donor's debts when he received the property after the donor's death. By the Salic Law, a man who could not pay the wergeld—the price of a life, owed by a killer to the dead man's kin—was allowed to make a formal transfer of his house-lot, and the liability went with it. But that transfer was to the next of kin.

Footnote: This, no doubt, was due to Roman influence. But it recalls what Sir Henry Maine quotes from Elphinstone's History of India about a sale by a member of one of the village communities: "The purchaser steps exactly into his place, and takes up all his obligations."

The house-lot, or family curtilage—the enclosed ground around the dwelling—at first passed strictly within the family. Here again, at least in England, freedom to alienate seems to have grown up by a gradual widening of the choice of successors. The early charters are few, but if we may trust the order of development they seem to show—and it is hard to believe the order is accidental—royal grants at first allowed the grantee to choose his heirs from among his kindred, and then extended the choice beyond them. In a deed of the year 679 the language is, "as it is granted so do you hold it and your posterity." One of a century later reads, "which let him always possess, and after his death leave to which of his heirs he will." Another says, "and after him with free power (of choice) leave to the man of his kin to whom he wishes to" (leave it). A charter of 736, somewhat earlier, goes a step further: "So that as long as he lives he shall have the power of holding and possessing (and) of leaving it to whomsoever he choose, either in his lifetime, or certainly after his death." At the beginning of the ninth century the donee has power to leave the property to whomever he will; or, in still broader terms, to exchange or grant it in his lifetime and after his death to leave it to whom he chooses; or to sell, exchange, and leave it to whatever heir he chooses. This choice of heirs recalls the "whom he has named heirs" of the Salic Law just mentioned. It may be compared with the language of a Norman charter of about the year 1190: "To W. and his heirs, to wit those whom he may constitute his heirs."

Footnote: The phrase "let him leave it to whichever heir he will" is very common in the later charters. It may be broader than "to whichever of his heirs he will."

A perfect example of a singular succession—a succession to one specific thing rather than to a whole estate—worked out by the fiction of kinship is found in the story of Burnt Njal. This Icelandic saga gives us a living picture of a society hardly more advanced than the Salian Franks as we see them in the Salic Law. A lawsuit was to be transferred by the proper plaintiff to another man who knew the laws better and was better able to carry it on—in fact, to an attorney. But a lawsuit at that time was the alternative to a feud, and both were the private affair of the family concerned. So when a suit for the killing of a member of the family was to be handed over to a stranger, the innovation had to be reconciled with the theory that such a suit belonged only to the next of kin. Mord is to take over Thorgeir's suit against Flosi for killing Helgi, and the form of transfer is described as follows.

Footnote: Under the Welsh laws, the champion in a cause decided by combat acquired the rights of the next of kin, the next of kin being the proper champion.

"Then Mord took Thorgeir by the hand and named two witnesses to bear witness, 'that Thorgeir Thofir's son hands me over a suit for manslaughter against Flosi Thord's son, to plead it for the slaying of Helgi Njal's son, with all those proofs which have to follow the suit. Thou handest over to me this suit to plead and to settle, and to enjoy all rights in it, as though I were the rightful next of kin. Thou handest it over to me by law; and I take it from thee by law.'" Afterwards these witnesses come before the court and bear witness to the transfer in the same words: "He handed over to him then this suit, with all the proofs and proceedings which belonged to the suit, he handed it over to him to plead and to settle, and to make use of all rights, as though he were the rightful next of kin. Thorgeir handed it over lawfully, and Mord took it lawfully." The suit went on, despite the change of hands, as if the next of kin were the plaintiff. A later step in the proceedings shows this. The defendant challenges two members of the court on the ground of their connection with Mord, the transferee, by blood and by baptism. But Mord answers that this is no good challenge; for "he challenged them not for their kinship to the true plaintiff, the next of kin, but for their kinship to him who pleaded the suit." And the other side had to admit that Mord was right in his law.

I now turn from the German sources to the Roman. These bear most closely on the argument, because much of the doctrine found there has been transplanted unchanged into modern law.

The early Roman law recognized as relatives only those who would have been members of the same patriarchal family, under the same patriarchal authority, if the common ancestor had still been alive. Since wives passed into their husbands' families and lost all connection with the family they were born into, relationship through women was excluded altogether. The heir was someone who traced his relationship to the dead man through males alone. As civilization advanced, this rule was changed. The praetor—the Roman magistrate in charge of the courts—gave the benefits of the inheritance to the blood relations, even though they were not heirs and could not be admitted to the succession under the ancient law. But the change was not made by repealing the old law, which lived on under the name of the jus civile, the civil law proper. The new principle was fitted to the old forms by a fiction. The blood relation could sue on the fiction that he was an heir, although in fact he was not one.

One of the early ways of naming an heir was a sale of the familia—the headship of the family, with all its rights and duties—to the intended heir. This sale of the universitas, the whole bundle of rights and duties taken together, was later extended beyond inheritance to bankruptcy, when it was desired to put the bankrupt's property into the hands of a trustee for distribution. This trustee, too, could use the fiction, and sue as if he had been the bankrupt's heir. One of the great jurists tells us that, in general, universal successors stand in the place of heirs.

The Roman heir, with one or two exceptions, was always a universal successor; and the fiction of heirship, as such, could hardly be used with propriety except to widen the range of universal successions. But as far as it went, every consequence attached to the original fiction of identity between heir and ancestor followed as a matter of course.

To return to the case of rights acquired by prescription: every universal successor could add the time of his predecessor's adverse use to his own in order to make out the right. Legally speaking there was no adding at all, but one continuous possession.

The express fiction of inheritance perhaps stopped there. But when a similar joining of times was allowed between a legatee or devisee (legatarius)—one who takes a specific thing under a will—and his testator, the same explanation was given. It was said that when a specific thing was left to a person by will, the legatee was in a certain sense quasi an heir—"as if" an heir—so far as having the benefit of the testator's time in possession toward acquiring a title. Yet a legatarius was not a universal successor, and for most purposes stood in sharp contrast with such successors.

Thus the strict law of inheritance had made familiar the notion that one man might have the advantage of a position filled by another, even though he had not filled it himself, or had filled it only in part. And the second fiction, by which a legal heir's privileges, in this respect and in others, had been extended to other persons, broke down the walls that might otherwise have confined those privileges to a single case. A new conception had entered the law, and nothing stood in the way of applying it further. As has been shown, it was applied in so many words to a sale of the universitas for business purposes, and to at least one case in which the succession was limited to a single specific thing. Why, then, might not every gift or sale be treated as a succession, far enough to secure the same advantages?

The joining of times to make out a title was soon allowed between buyer and seller. From the language the Roman lawyers always used, I have no doubt that it was reached in the way I have suggested. A passage from Scaevola (B.C. 30) will be proof enough. Joinder of possessions, he says—that is, the right to add the time of one's predecessor's holding to one's own—plainly belongs to those who succeed to the place of others, whether by contract or by will. For heirs, and those who are treated as holding the place of successors, are allowed to add their testator's possession to their own. Accordingly, if you sell me a slave, I shall have the benefit of your holding.

The joining of times is given to those who succeed to the place of another. Ulpian quotes a similar phrase from a jurist of the time of the Antonines: "to whose place I have succeeded by inheritance, or purchase, or any other right." Succedere in locum aliorum, "to succeed to the place of others," like sustinere personam, "to sustain the persona," is the Roman lawyers' expression for those continuations of one man's legal position by another of which the model was the succession of heir to ancestor. Succedere by itself is used to mean "inherit," and successio to mean "inheritance." The succession above all others was the inheritance; and it is believed that hardly an instance will be found in the Roman sources where "succession" does not carry that analogy and point to the taking over, at least in part, of a persona formerly sustained by someone else. It plainly does so in the passage before us.

But the succession that allows a joining of times is not hereditary succession alone. In the passage just cited, Scaevola says that it may be by contract or purchase as well as by inheritance or will. It may be singular as well as universal. The jurists often set universal successions against those confined to a single specific thing. Ulpian says that a man succeeds to another's place whether his succession is universal or to the single object.

If further evidence were needed for the present argument, it would be found in another expression of Ulpian's. He speaks of the benefit of joinder as derived from the persona of the grantor: "He to whom a thing is granted shall have the benefit of joinder from the persona of his grantor." A benefit cannot be derived from a persona except by sustaining it.

Footnote: Other cases Ulpian puts may rest on a different fiction. After a precarium ends, for instance—a holding at the owner's pleasure, revocable whenever he likes—the land is treated by a fiction as never having been possessed by the holder at all.

It further appears fairly plainly from Justinian's Institutes and the Digest that the benefit was not extended to purchasers in all cases until quite a late period.

Savigny came very near the truth when he said, somewhat broadly, that "every accessio, for whatever purpose, presupposes nothing else than a relation of juridical succession between the previous and present possessor. For succession does not apply to possession by itself." The accessio is the joining of times. And I may add, by way of further explanation, that every relation of juridical succession presupposes either an inheritance or a relation to which, as far as it goes, the analogies of the inheritance may be applied.

The way of thinking that led to the accessio, or joining of times, is equally visible elsewhere. The time during which a former owner failed to use an easement was charged to the person who had succeeded to his place. The defense that the plaintiff had sold and delivered the thing in dispute was open not only to the purchaser, but to his heirs, or to a second purchaser even before delivery to him, against the seller's successors, whether universal or successors only to the thing in question. If someone used a way wrongfully as against the predecessor in title, the use was wrongful as against the successor too, whether by inheritance, purchase, or any other right. The formal oath of a party to an action was conclusive in favor of his successors, universal or singular. Successors by purchase or gift had the benefit of agreements made with the seller. A multitude of general expressions show that for most purposes, whether in attack or defense, the buyer stood in the shoes of the seller, to use the metaphor of our own law. And what matters more than the result, which could often have been reached by other routes, is that the language and the analogies are drawn throughout from succession to the inheritance.

Footnote: The passage about the unused easement seems to be written of a rural servitude—a right in a neighbor's land, here a right to water—which was lost by mere disuse, without any adverse use by the owner of the burdened land.

Footnote: Savigny argued for reading actore in the passage about the wrongful use of a way; Mommsen, in his edition of the Digest, rejects that reading—rightly, it seems to me.

Understood this way, there could be no succession between a person dispossessed of a thing against his will and the wrongful possessor. Without the element of consent, there is no room for the analogy just explained. Accordingly, it is laid down that there is no joining of times when the possession is wrongful, and the only listed means of succeeding to a thing are by will, sale, gift, or some other right.

The argument now returns to the English law, strengthened by some general conclusions. It has been shown that in both of the systems whose union produced our law, the rules governing conveyance—the transfer of specific things between living persons—were deeply affected by notions drawn from inheritance. It was shown earlier that in England the principles of inheritance applied directly to the singular succession of the heir to a specific fee, as well as to the universal succession of the executor. Given their history, it would be remarkable if the same principles had not affected other singular successions too. It will soon appear that they have. Without being too careful about the order of proof, I will take up first the joining of times in prescription, since that has just been discussed so fully. The English law on the subject turns out, on examination, to be the same as the Roman in extent, in reason, and in expression. Indeed it is largely copied from the Roman. For servitudes—rights of way, of light, and the like—make up the chief class of prescriptive rights, and our law of servitudes is mainly Roman. Prescriptions, it is said, "are properly personal, and therefore are always alleged in the person of him who prescribes, viz. that he and all those whose estate he hath, etc.; therefore, a bishop or a parson may prescribe,... for there is a perpetual estate, and a perpetual succession and the successor hath the very same estate which his predecessor had, for that continues, though the person alters, like the case of the ancestor and the heir." So too in a modern case, where by statute twenty years' dispossession extinguished the owner's title, the Court of Queen's Bench said the right would probably pass to the possessor "if the same person, or several persons, claiming one from the other by descent, will or conveyance, had been in possession for the twenty years." "But.... such twenty years' possession must be either by the same person, or several persons claiming one from the other, which is not the case here."

In a word, it is equally clear that the continuous possession of privies in title—persons linked one to the next in a single chain of title, successors in the Roman phrase—has all the effect of the continuous possession of one person, and that no such effect is given to the continuous possession of different persons who are not in the same chain of title. One who dispossesses another of land cannot add the time during which his disseisee used a way to the period of his own use, while one who bought the land can.

Footnote: Of course, if a right had already been acquired before the disseisin, different considerations would apply. If the right claimed is one of those regarded as incident to land, as explained in the following Lecture, the disseisor will have it.

The authorities quoted make it plain that the English law proceeds on the same theory as the Roman. One who buys land from another gets the very same estate his seller had. He is in of the same fee, or hereditas, which means, as I have shown, that he sustains the same persona. On the other hand, one who wrongfully dispossesses another—a disseisor—gets a different estate. He is in of a new fee, although the land is the same; and a good deal of technical reasoning rests on this doctrine.

In the matter of prescription, therefore, buyer and seller were identified, like heir and ancestor. But the question remains whether this identification bore fruit in other parts of the law as well, or whether it stayed confined to one branch, where the Roman law was grafted onto the English stock.

There can be no doubt which answer is more probable, but it cannot be proved without difficulty. As has been said, the heir ceased at an early date to be his ancestor's general representative. And how far even he was identified with the ancestor came to be a matter of dispute. Common sense kept fiction in check here, as elsewhere in the common law. But there can be no doubt that in matters directly concerning the estate, the identification of heir and ancestor has continued to the present day. And since an estate in fee simple has been shown to be a distinct persona, we should expect to find a similar identification of buyer and seller in this part of the law, if anywhere.

Where the land was devised—left by will—the analogy applied with particular ease. There is no difference in principle between a devise of a piece of land by will and a conveyance of it by deed; but the dramatic resemblance of a devisee to an heir is stronger than that of a grantee. It will be remembered that one of the Roman jurists said a legatarius (legatee or devisee) was in a certain sense quasi heres, "as if an heir." The English courts have now and then used similar expressions. In one case a testator owned a rent and divided it by will among his sons, and one of the sons then brought debt for his share. Two of the judges admitted that the testator could not have divided the tenant's liability by a grant or deed in his lifetime, but thought it was otherwise with a division by will. Their reasoning was that "the devise is quasi an act of law, which shall inure without attornment, and shall make a sufficient privity, and so it may well be apportioned by this means." (Attornment was the tenant's formal acknowledgment of a new landlord; privity is the legal relation that lets one person sue another.) So too Lord Ellenborough said, in a case where a lessor and his heirs were entitled to end a lease on notice, that a devisee of the land, as heres factus—an heir made by the will—would be understood to have the same right.

But wills of land were allowed only exceptionally, by local custom, until the reign of Henry VIII; and since the main doctrines of conveyancing had been settled long before then, we must look further back, and to other sources, for their explanation. We shall find it in the history of warranty. That, and the modern law of covenants running with the land, will be treated in the next Lecture.
