Lecture VII: Contract—I. History
(Part 3 of 3)

An example like this raises no difficulty. It is as much an action of tort for a wrong as trespass itself. No contract was stated, and on principle none was necessary. But it does not belong to the class of cases we have to consider. The problem before us is to trace the origin of assumpsit, which is an action of contract. Assumpsit, however, began as an action of trespass on the case, and what has to be discovered is how trespass on the case ever came to be available for a mere breach of agreement.

It will be well to examine some of the earliest cases in which an undertaking (in Latin, assumpsit, "he undertook") was alleged. The first one reported in the books comes from the reign of Edward III. The plaintiff alleged that the defendant had undertaken to carry the plaintiff's horse safely across the Humber, but had overloaded the boat, and because of that the horse perished. It was objected that the action should have been either covenant, for breach of the agreement, or else trespass. But the answer was that the defendant committed a wrongful act when he overloaded the boat, and the objection was overruled. So this case, although an undertaking was stated, hardly introduced a new principle. The force did not come directly from the defendant, to be sure. But it was brought to bear by the combination of his overloading the boat and then pushing it out into the stream.

The next case is from the same reign, and it goes further. The writ set out that the defendant undertook to cure the plaintiff's horse of a sickness, and did his work so negligently that the horse died. This differs in two ways from the case of laming the horse with a nail. It does not charge any forcible act—indeed, it charges no act at all, only an omission. On the other hand, it states an undertaking, which the other case did not. The defendant at once objected that this was an action for breach of an undertaking, and that the plaintiff should have brought covenant. The plaintiff replied that he could not bring covenant without a deed, and that his action was for negligently causing the death of the horse—that is, for a tort, not for a breach of contract. Then, said the defendant, you could have had trespass. The plaintiff answered that the horse was not killed by force, but died through the defendant's failure in his cure. On this argument the writ was held good, and Justice Thorpe said that he had seen a man indicted for killing, by want of care, a patient whom he had undertaken to cure.

Both these cases, you will see, were treated by the court as pure actions of tort, even though an undertaking on the defendant's part was alleged. But you will also see that each is further than the last from an ordinary case of trespass. In the second case especially, the force that destroyed the horse did not come from the defendant in any sense. And so we are faced with the question: What possible analogy could have been found between a wrongful act that produces harm and a failure to act at all?

Before I attempt an answer, let me illustrate a little further with examples of somewhat later date. Suppose a man undertook to work on another's house, and by his lack of skill spoiled his employer's timbers. That would be like a trespass, though not one, and the employer would sue in trespass on the case. One of the judges stated this as clear law in the reign of Henry IV. But suppose that, instead of spoiling the materials directly, the carpenter had simply left a hole in the roof, and the rain had come in through it and done the damage. The likeness to the first case is marked, but we are a step farther from trespass, because the force does not come from the defendant. Yet here too the judges thought that trespass on the case would lie. In the time of Henry IV the action could not have been brought for a simple refusal to build as agreed. But the court suggested that if the writ had said "that the thing had been commenced and then by not done, it would have been otherwise."

I come back now to the question: What likeness could there have been between an omission and a trespass, close enough to justify a writ of trespass on the case? To find the answer, we must notice that in all the earlier cases the omission happened in the course of dealing with the plaintiff's person or property, and caused damage to one or the other. In view of this, two things are full of meaning: Thorpe's reference to indictments for killing a patient by want of care, and the later distinction between neglect before the task is begun and neglect after. The first is all the more suggestive when we remember that it is the earliest argument or analogy to be found on the subject.

The meaning of that analogy is plain. A man has a perfect right to stand by and watch his neighbor's property destroyed—or, for that matter, to watch his neighbor die for want of his help. But once he meddles, he no longer has the same freedom. He cannot withdraw at will. To take a more specific example: if a surgeon, out of kindness, cuts the umbilical cord of a newborn child, he cannot stop there and watch the baby bleed to death. To let death come about in that way on purpose would be murder, just as much as if he had meant it when he cut the cord. It would not matter whether the wickedness began with the act or with the omission that followed.

The same reasoning applies to civil liability. A carpenter need not go to work on another man's house at all. But if he accepts the other's confidence and takes the work in hand, he cannot stop when he pleases and leave the roof open to the weather. So with the farrier: once he had taken charge of the horse, he could not stop at the critical moment and leave the consequences to chance. And still more clearly with the ferryman who undertook to carry a horse across the Humber. It was the water that drowned the horse. But his earlier acts, overloading his boat and pushing it into the stream in that condition, brought about the loss, and he was answerable for it.

In these cases the duty did not depend on contract, or at least the judges who decided them did not think it did. It rested on the general rules that apply to human conduct, rules that even the criminal law applies. The immediate cause of the damage may have been a mere omission, which let natural forces do their work. But connect that omission, as in fact it was connected, with what the defendant had done before, and you have a course of action and conduct which, taken as a whole, has caused the harm.

It may be objected, to be sure, that there is a considerable step between two things. One is holding a man liable for the consequences of his own acts, consequences he might have prevented. The other is making him answerable for not interfering with the course of nature, when he neither set it going nor opened the door for it to do harm. And it may be said that exactly this difference separates making a hole in a roof and leaving it open, or cutting the cord and letting the child bleed, on one side, from the farrier who takes in a sick horse and fails to take proper precautions, on the other.

There seem to be two answers to this. First, it is not clear that the court which decided the farrier's case ever turned its mind to such a distinction. The allegation was that the defendant carried out his cure so negligently that the horse died. It may not have occurred to the judges that the defendant's conduct possibly went no further than leaving out a series of helpful measures. They probably assumed that it was a combination of acts and neglects which, taken together, amounted to an improper dealing with the thing.

Second, it is doubtful whether the distinction is sound on practical grounds. It may well be that so long as a man allows a trust to be placed in him, he is bound to use the precautions he knows of. This would be so even though he has made no contract, and is free to give up the trust in any reasonable way. The view gets some support from the issue on which the parties went to trial. It was that the defendant performed the cure as well as he knew how, and denied that the horse died for default of his care (cure?).

But it cannot be denied that the allegation of an undertaking carried the idea of a promise, as well as the idea of entering upon the business in hand. Indeed, the second idea is perhaps sufficiently conveyed without it. So it may be asked whether the promise did not count for something in raising a duty to act. If that means that the action was really for breach of a contract, the answer has already been given, and too great a weight of authority supports it for it to be doubted. To bind the defendant by a contract, an instrument under seal was essential. As has already been shown, even the ancient territory of debt had been narrowed by this requirement, and in the time of Edward III a deed was needed even to bind a surety—a person who answers for another's debt. It was all the more necessary, then, for bringing in a liability on promises that the ancient law did not enforce. Nevertheless, the suggestion was made at an early date that an action on the case for damage by negligence—that is, by leaving out proper precautions—which alleged an undertaking by way of inducement, was in fact an action of contract.

Five years after the action for negligence in curing a horse, an action was brought in similar form against a surgeon. It alleged that he undertook to cure the plaintiff's hand, and that by his negligence the hand was maimed. There was one difference, however: the writ set out that the plaintiff's hand had been wounded by a certain T. B. So it appeared that, however much the bad treatment may have made matters worse, the maiming was properly to be laid at T. B.'s door, and the plaintiff had an action against him. This may be what led the defendant to take the course he did, because he was unsure whether any action of tort would lie. He took issue on the undertaking, assuming it to be essential to the plaintiff's case. Then he objected that the writ did not show where the undertaking was made, and so was bad, because it did not show from what place the inquest should be summoned to speak to that point. The writ was held bad on that ground, which looks as if the court accepted the defendant's view. Indeed, one of the judges called it an action of covenant, and said that "of necessity it was maintainable without specialty"—that is, without a sealed writing—"because for so small a matter a man cannot always have a clerk at hand to write a deed." At the same time, the earlier cases I have mentioned were cited and relied on, and it is evident that the court was not prepared to go beyond them, or to hold that the action could be maintained on its merits apart from the technical objection. In another connection it seems to have looked at the action from the point of view of trespass.

Whatever questions this case may raise, the class of actions alleging an undertaking by the defendant went on being treated as actions of tort for a long time after Edward III. Liability was limited to damage to person or property that arose after the defendant had entered upon the employment. And it was mainly through reasoning drawn from the law of tort that the liability was later extended, as we shall see.

At the beginning of the reign of Henry VI it was probably still the law that the action would not lie for a simple failure to keep a promise. But, as has been shown, it had been suggested several times that it would be otherwise if the omission or neglect happened in the course of performance and the defendant's conduct had been followed by physical damage. This suggestion took its most striking form in the early years of Henry VI, when the case of the carpenter who left a hole in the roof was put. Once the courts had got that far, it was easy to go one step farther, and give the same effect to an omission at any stage, followed by similar damage.

A few years later the question was asked: What is the difference in principle between the cases where the action is admitted to lie, and the case of a smith who undertakes to shoe a horse and does not, so that the horse goes lame? Or the case of a lawyer who undertakes to argue your case, and then, having led you to rely on him, fails to appear, so that you lose it? It was said that in the earlier cases the duty depended on the covenant, as an accessory to it, and that if the action would lie on the accessory matter, it would lie on the principal. On demurrer it was held that an action would lie for failing to procure certain releases that the defendant had undertaken to get.

Five years later another case came up, very like the farrier's case in the reign of Edward III. It was alleged that the defendant undertook to cure the plaintiff's horse, and applied medicine so negligently that the horse died. Here, as in the earlier case, issue was taken on the assumpsit. But now the difference between an omission and an act was clearly stated. The declaration was held not necessarily to mean anything more than an omission, and it was said that, but for the undertaking, the defendant would have owed no duty to act. So the allegation of the defendant's promise was material, and an issue could properly be taken on it.

This decision plainly separated out, from the mass of actions on the case, a special class in which the source of the defendant's obligation was a promise. It was only a matter of time before that class became a new and distinct action of contract. Had the change happened at once, the doctrine of consideration, which was first definitely stated at about the same time, would no doubt have been applied, and a quid pro quo would have been required for the undertaking. But the notion of tort was not at once given up. At the beginning of the reign of Henry VII the law was laid down in line with the earlier decisions: the action would not lie for a failure to keep a promise, but only for negligence after the defendant had entered upon his undertaking.

So long as the action stayed within the true limits of tort, it made no difference whether there was a consideration for the undertaking or not. But then the mistake was made of supposing that all cases in which an assumpsit was alleged, whether they were proper torts or not, rested equally on the promise. From that mistake one of two wrong conclusions naturally seemed to follow. Either no assumpsit needed any quid pro quo, since there was clearly none in the older precedents (they being cases of pure tort), or else those precedents were wrong, and a quid pro quo ought to be alleged in every case. It was long recognized, with more or less understanding of the true limit, that where the heart of the action was negligent damage to property, no consideration was necessary. And there are traces of the notion that consideration was always unnecessary as late as the reign of Charles I.

In a case of that reign, the defendant hired an attorney to act in a lawsuit for a third person, and promised to pay him all his fees and expenses. The attorney did the work and then brought debt. It was objected that debt would not lie, because there was no contract between the parties and the defendant had received no quid pro quo. The court accepted the argument. It said there was no contract or consideration to ground this action, but that the plaintiff might have sued in assumpsit.

It was perhaps the lingering of this idea, together with the often repeated notion that an assumpsit was not a contract, that produced a wider theory of consideration in assumpsit than prevailed in debt. It was settled that assumpsit would lie for a mere omission, or nonfeasance. The cases of Henry VI's reign that I have mentioned were followed by others in the later years of Henry VII, and the point was never doubted again. An action on such a ground was plainly for a breach of promise, as had been recognized since the time of Edward III. If so, a consideration was necessary. Despite occasional wanderings, that too had been settled, or taken for granted, in many cases of Queen Elizabeth's time. But the bastard origin of the action had given rise to the doubt whether any consideration at all was necessary, and that made it possible to hold considerations sufficient in assumpsit which had been held insufficient in debt.

Another circumstance may have had some influence. It seems that in the period when assumpsit was just growing to its full size, there was some slight inclination to identify consideration with the Roman causa, the ground or reason of an obligation, taken in its broadest sense. The word "cause" was used for consideration in the early years of Elizabeth, in connection with a covenant to stand seized to uses—a promise by an owner of land to hold it for the benefit of a relative. It was used in the same sense in the action of assumpsit. In the report last referred to, the main case laid down only a doctrine that would be followed today. But the report also gave an anonymous case, which was read to mean that a consideration already performed at the defendant's request, with no promise of any kind at the time, would support a later promise to pay for it. Starting from this authority and the word "cause," the courts soon reached the conclusion that there was a great difference between a contract and an assumpsit. In a contract, they said, "everything which is requisite ought to concur and meet together, viz. the consideration of the one side, and the sale or the promise on the other side,... to maintain an action upon an assumpsit, the same is not requisite, for it is sufficient if there be a moving cause or consideration precedent; for which cause or consideration the promise was made."

But the case he cites for this statement was not decided until 1603, while the implication of the case of 1568 that the later cases followed as their authority is all the other way.

So where the defendant engaged the plaintiff to serve his aunt at ten shillings a week, it was held that assumpsit would lie. The service did the defendant no good, but it was a charge, or detriment, to the plaintiff. The old questions were argued over again, and views that had very nearly prevailed in debt under Henry VI prevailed in assumpsit under Elizabeth and James.

A surety could be sued in assumpsit, although he was no longer liable in debt. There was the same remedy on a promise made in consideration of the plaintiff's marrying the defendant's daughter. Once assumpsit had been extended this far, the illusion that it did not mean contract could not be kept up. With this admitted, and with the ancient precedents in view, the law swung for a time toward reward as the true essence of consideration. But the other view won, and in doing so it actually changed the substantive law. For the courts of Henry VI to recognize a simple contract as binding, it had to rest on a benefit to the debtor. Now a promise could be enforced in consideration of a detriment to the promisee, the person to whom it was made. But in the true archaic spirit, the doctrine was not separated or distinguished from the remedy that brought it in. And so in modern times debt has taken on the altered look of a duty limited to cases where the consideration was of a special kind.

The later fortunes of assumpsit can be told briefly. It brought in bilateral contracts—contracts of a promise for a promise—because a promise was a detriment, and therefore a sufficient consideration for another promise. It pushed out debt. That was because the existence of the duty to pay was a sufficient consideration for a promise to pay; or rather because, even before a consideration was required, and as soon as assumpsit would lie for a nonfeasance, this action was used to escape the defendant's wager of law. It enormously enlarged the number of contracts that could be sued on, which had formerly been confined to debts and covenants, whereas nearly any promise could be sued on in assumpsit. And it brought in a theory that has had great influence on modern law—that all the liabilities of a bailee are founded on contract. Whether the prominence thus given to contract as the foundation of legal rights and duties had anything to do with the similar prominence it soon gained in political speculation, it is beyond my province to inquire.
