Lecture IX: Contract—III. Void and Voidable
(Part 1 of 2)

We have now discussed the facts that must be present to bring a contract into existence, and the legal consequences of a contract once it is formed. It remains to consider, one after the other, the cases in which a contract is said to be void, and those in which it is said to be voidable. A void contract is one that fails to be made when it seems to have been made. A voidable contract is one that has been made, but can be rescinded—undone—by one side or the other, and treated as if it had never existed. I take up the first class of cases first.

When a contract fails to be made, although the usual forms have been gone through, the reason for the failure is commonly said to be mistake, misrepresentation, or fraud. But I shall try to show that these are merely dramatic circumstances. The true reason is the absence of one or more of the primary elements which have been shown, or are seen at once, to be necessary to the existence of a contract.

Suppose a man goes through the form of making a contract with A through B, as A's agent, and B is not in fact A's agent. There is no contract, because there is only one party. The promise offered to A has not been accepted by him, and no consideration has moved from him. In such a case there is generally mistake on one side and fraud on the other. But it is very clear that no special doctrine is needed, because the primary elements of a contract explained in the last Lecture are not yet present.

Take next a different case. The defendant agreed to buy, and the plaintiff agreed to sell, a cargo of cotton "to arrive ex Peerless from Bombay"—that is, to arrive on the ship Peerless. There were two ships of that name sailing from Bombay, one in October, the other in December. The plaintiff meant the December ship, the defendant the October one. It was held that the defendant was not bound to accept the cotton. It is commonly said that such a contract is void because of mutual mistake as to the subject-matter, and because the parties therefore did not consent to the same thing. But this way of putting it seems to me misleading. The law has nothing to do with the actual state of the parties' minds. In contract, as elsewhere, it must go by externals, and judge parties by their conduct. If there had been only one "Peerless," and the defendant had said "Peerless" by mistake, meaning "Peri," he would have been bound. The true ground of the decision was not that each party meant a different thing from the other, as the usual explanation implies, but that each said a different thing. The plaintiff offered one thing; the defendant expressed his assent to another.

A proper name, when used in business or in pleading, means one individual thing and no other, as everyone knows. Therefore a person to whom such a name is used must find out at his peril what object it designates. If there are no circumstances that make the use of the name deceptive on either side, each party is entitled to insist on the meaning favorable to him for the word as he used it, and neither is entitled to insist on that meaning for the word as the other used it. So far from mistake, as mistake, being the ground of the decision, its only bearing, it seems to me, was to establish that neither party knew that the other understood him to use the word "Peerless" in the sense the other gave it. Had one of them known that, there would perhaps have been a binding contract. For if a man uses a word to which he knows the other party attaches a certain meaning, and understands him to attach it, he may be held to that meaning, and not be allowed to give it any other.

Next, suppose a case in which the offer and the acceptance do not differ, and both parties have used the same words in the same sense. Suppose that A agreed to buy, and B agreed to sell, "these barrels of mackerel," and that the barrels in question turn out to contain salt. There is mutual mistake as to what is in the barrels, and no fraud on either side. I suppose the contract would be void.

It is commonly said that the contract fails in such a case because the actual subject-matter differs in kind from the thing the parties intended. It is perhaps more instructive to say that the terms of the supposed contract, although they seemed consistent, were in fact contradictory, in matters that went to the root of the bargain. For by one of the essential terms, the subject-matter of the agreement was the contents of certain barrels, and nothing else. By another, equally important, it was mackerel, and nothing else. But as a matter of fact it could not be both, because the contents of the barrels were salt. Neither term could be left out without forcing on the parties a contract they did not make. It follows that A cannot be required to accept, nor B to deliver, either these barrels of salt or other barrels of mackerel; and unless one term is left out, the promise is meaningless.

If there had been fraud on the seller's part, or if he had known what the barrels really contained, the buyer might have had a right to insist on delivery of the inferior article. Fraud would perhaps have made the contract valid at the buyer's option. For when a man qualifies words that make sense with other words which he knows, for reasons he keeps to himself, make no sense when applied to them, he may fairly be taken to authorize his promisee to insist on the possible part of the promise being performed, if the promisee is willing to give up the rest.

Take one more illustration like the last. A policy of insurance is issued on a certain building, which the policy describes as a machine-shop. In fact the building is not a machine-shop but an organ factory, which is a greater risk. The contract is void—not because of any misrepresentation, but, as before, because two of its essential terms are repugnant, that is, contradict each other, and put together they make no sense.

Of course, the principle of repugnancy just explained might be stretched to cover any inconsistency between the different terms of a contract. It might be said, for instance, that if a piece of gold is sold as eighteen-carat gold and is in fact less pure, or if a cow is sold as giving an average of twelve quarts of milk a day and in fact gives only six, then by the explanation just offered there is no logical difference between those cases and that of the barrel of salt sold as mackerel. Yet those bargains would not be void. At most, they would only be voidable, if the buyer chose to throw them up.

The distinctions of the law are founded on experience, not on logic. The law therefore does not make men's dealings depend on mathematical accuracy. Whatever is promised, a man has a right to be paid for if it is not given. But it does not follow that the absence of some insignificant detail will entitle him to throw up the contract, still less that it will prevent a contract from being formed, which is the question now before us. The repugnant terms must both be very important—so important that the court thinks that, if either were left out, the contract would be different in substance from the one the parties' words seemed to express.

A term that refers directly to an identification by the senses always has this degree of importance. If a promise is made to sell this cow, or this mackerel, to this man, then whatever else may be struck from the contract, it can never be enforced except as to this object and by this man. If this barrel of salt is fraudulently sold as a barrel of mackerel, the buyer may perhaps choose to take this barrel of salt if he likes, but he cannot choose to take another barrel of mackerel. Suppose the seller is introduced by the name B, and the buyer takes him for another person of the same name, and under that impression hands him a written promise to buy from B. The B to whom the writing is handed is the contractee, if anyone is; and notwithstanding what has been said about the use of proper names, I should suppose that a contract would be made. For it must further be said that, so far as one of the terms of a contract identifies the thing promised, or the promisee, by sight and hearing, that term so far outweighs all the others that it is very rare for the failure of any other part of the description to prevent a contract from being made. The most obvious of the seeming exceptions is where it is not the object itself that is so identified, but only its covering or wrapper.

Footnote: The maxim is that the presence of the thing itself cures a mistake in its name. It would seem that a proper name, or any other identification of an object or person as a specific one, may have the same effect as an actual identification by the senses, because it refers to such an identification, although less directly.

Of course, the performance of a promise may be made conditional on the other side's complying with all the terms it has agreed to. But conditions attached to performance can never come into question until a contract has been made, and so far the question has been whether a contract exists in the first place.

A case different from any yet considered may be suggested. Instead of a contradiction between offer and assent, which prevents an agreement, or between the terms of an agreement, which makes it meaningless on its face, there may be a similar contradiction between a term of the contract and an earlier representation of fact that was not expressly made part of the contract. The representation may have been the chief inducement to the bargain and its very foundation. It may be more important than any of the terms expressed, and yet the contract may have been put into writing in words that cannot fairly be read to include it. A seller may have stated that barrels filled with salt contain mackerel, while the contract is only for the barrels and their contents. An applicant for insurance may have misstated facts essential to the risk, while the policy simply insures a certain building or a certain life. It may be asked whether these contracts are not void as well.

There might conceivably be cases in which, taking into account the nature of the contract, the words used could be said to embody the representation as a term by construction—that is, by the court's reading of what the words mean. For instance, it might be said that the true and well-understood purpose of a contract of insurance is not, as the words seem to say, to take the risk of any loss by fire or perils of the sea, however great the risk may be. It is to take a risk of a certain size, and no other, a risk that has been calculated mathematically from the statements of the insured party. The extent of the risk is not stated in the policy, because the old forms and established usage do not state it, but the meaning is perfectly understood.

If this reasoning were adopted, there would be the same contradiction in the terms of the contract whether the nature of the risk was written in the policy or fixed by an earlier description. But, subject to possible exceptions of this kind, it would seem that a contract would be made, and that the most that could be claimed would be a right to rescind. Where parties who have the power to bind themselves do acts and use words that are fit to create an obligation, I take it that an obligation arises. If there is a mistake about a fact not mentioned in the contract, it goes only to the motives for making the contract. But a contract is not prevented from being made by the mere fact that one party would not have made it if he had known the truth. In what cases a mistake affecting motives only is a ground for avoiding a contract does not concern this discussion. The subject now before us is when a contract is made, and the question of avoiding or rescinding it assumes that it has been made.

I think it may now be taken as settled that, when fraud, misrepresentation, or mistake is said to make a contract void, no new principle comes in to set aside an otherwise perfect obligation. In every such case one or more of the first elements explained in the last Lecture is missing. Either there is no second party, or the two parties say different things, or essential terms that seem consistent are really inconsistent as used.

When a contract is said to be voidable, it is assumed that a contract has been made, but that it may be unmade at the choice—the election, as lawyers say—of one party. This must be because of the breach of some condition attached to its existence, either expressly or by implication.

If a condition is attached to the contract's coming into being, there is as yet no contract. Either party may withdraw at will until the condition is settled one way or the other. There is no obligation, although there may be an offer or a promise, and so there is no relation between the parties that needs discussing here. But some conditions that seem to arise out of a contract already made are really of this sort. That is always the case if the condition of a promise lies within the control of the promisor's own will. For instance, a promise to pay for clothes if they are made to the customer's satisfaction has been held in Massachusetts to make the promisor his own final judge. So interpreted, it seems to me to be no contract at all until the promisor declares himself satisfied. His promise is only to pay if he sees fit, and such a promise cannot be a contract, because it cannot impose any obligation. If the promise were read to mean that the clothes should be paid for provided they were such as ought to satisfy the promisor, and so to make the jury the judge, there would be a contract, because the promisor gives up control over the outcome. But it would be subject to a condition in the sense of the present analysis.

Theorists have divided the conditions a contract may contain into conditions precedent and conditions subsequent—conditions that must be met before a duty arises, and conditions whose happening ends a duty already in being. The distinction has even been declared to be of great importance. It must be admitted that it is, if the course of pleading is taken as the test. In some cases the plaintiff must state that a condition has been performed before the defendant is called on to answer; in others it is left to the defendant to set up that a condition has been broken.

In one sense all conditions are subsequent; in another, all are precedent. All are subsequent to the first stage of the obligation. Take, for instance, a promise to pay for work if it is done to the satisfaction of an architect. The condition is a clear case of what is called a condition precedent: there can be no duty to pay until the architect is satisfied. But there can be a contract before that moment, because the decision whether the promisor shall pay or not is no longer within his control. So the condition is subsequent to the existence of the obligation.

On the other hand, every condition subsequent is precedent to the moment when the burden of the law actually falls. Look at the law as it would be seen by a man with no scruples against doing anything he could do without legal consequences. To him it is obvious that the main consequence the law attaches to a contract is a greater or smaller chance of having to pay money. From the purely legal point of view, the only question is whether the promisor will be made to pay, and the important moment is the one at which that is settled. All conditions are precedent to that.

But all conditions are precedent not only in this extreme sense but also to the existence of the plaintiff's cause of action—his right to sue. As strong a case as can be put is that of an insurance policy which is to be void if it is not sued on within one year after a failure to pay as agreed. The condition does not come into play until a loss has happened, the duty to pay has been neglected, and a cause of action has arisen. Nevertheless, it is precedent to the plaintiff's cause of action. When a man sues, the question is not whether he has had a cause of action in the past, but whether he has one now. He has not, unless the year is still running. If it were left to the defendant to raise the lapse of the year, that would be because the order of pleading does not require a plaintiff to meet every possible defense and set out a case that can be answered only by denying it. The point at which the law calls on the defendant for an answer varies from case to case. Sometimes it seems to be governed simply by convenience of proof, requiring the party who asserts a fact to plead and prove it. Sometimes there seems to be a reference to the usual course of events, and matters belong to the defense because they are true only exceptionally.

The most logical distinction would be between conditions that must be satisfied before a promise can be broken, and conditions which, like the last, discharge the liability after a breach has occurred. But this is of the slightest possible importance, and it may be doubted whether another case like the last could be found.

It is much more important to mark the difference between a stipulation that merely confines a promise to certain cases and a condition properly so called. Every condition, it is true, has this confining effect on the promise it is attached to. Whatever the rule of pleading may be, a promise is as truly kept and performed by doing nothing, when the condition of the stipulated act has been broken, as it would have been by doing the act had the condition been fulfilled. But if that were all, every clause in a contract that showed what the promisor did not promise would be a condition, and the word would be worse than useless. The characteristic feature is quite different.

A condition properly so called is an event whose happening entitles the person in whose favor the condition is reserved to treat the contract as if it had not been made—to avoid it, as it is commonly put—that is, to insist that both parties be put back where they stood before the contract was made. When a condition works as a condition, it lets in an outside force to destroy the existing state of things. For although its existence is due to the consent of the parties, its operation depends on the choice of one of them. When a condition is broken, the person entitled to insist on it may do so if he chooses; but he may, if he prefers, choose to keep the contract alive. He gets his right to avoid it from the agreement, but the avoidance comes from him.

So it is important to distinguish the stipulations that have this extreme effect from those that only interpret the extent of a promise, or define the events to which it applies. And since it has just been shown that a condition need not be insisted on as such, we must further distinguish between its working by way of avoidance, which belongs to it alone, and its incidental working by way of interpretation and definition, which it shares with other clauses that are not conditions.

This is best illustrated by a bilateral contract between A and B, where A's undertaking is conditional on B's doing what he promises, and where, after A has gone a certain distance in his task, B breaks his half of the bargain. For instance, A is employed by B as a clerk, and is wrongfully dismissed in the middle of a quarter. In A's favor, the contract is conditional on B's keeping his agreement to employ him. Whether A insists on the condition or not, he is not bound to do any more. So far, the condition works simply by way of definition: it establishes that A has not promised to act in the case that has happened. But beyond this, for which no condition was needed, A may choose between two courses. First, he may elect to avoid the contract. Then the parties stand as if no contract had been made. A has done work for B which was understood not to be free, and for which no rate of pay has been fixed, so he can recover what the jury thinks his services were reasonably worth. The contract no longer fixes the quid pro quo. Alternatively, A may stand by the contract if he prefers, and sue B for breaking it. In that case he can recover, as part of his damages, pay at the contract rate for what he had done, and also compensation for losing the chance to finish. But the points that matter for the present discussion are these. The two remedies exclude each other, one relying on the contract, the other setting it aside. And A's stopping work and doing no more after B's breach is equally consistent with either choice, and in fact has nothing to do with the matter.

One word should be added to prevent a misunderstanding. When it is said that A has done all he promised to do in the case that has happened, it is not meant that he is necessarily entitled to the same pay as if he had done the larger amount of work. B's promise in the case supposed was to pay so much a quarter for services. Although the consideration for that promise was A's promise to perform them, its scope was limited to the case of their actually being performed. So A could not simply wait until the end of his term and then recover the full amount he would have had if the employment had continued. Nor is he any more entitled to do so because it was B's fault that the services were not rendered. B's answer to any such claim is perfect. He is liable only on a promise, and he in his turn only promised to pay in a case that has not happened. He did promise to employ A, however, and for not doing that he is liable in damages.

One or two more illustrations will be useful. A promises to deliver certain goods, and B promises to accept and pay for them, at a certain time and place. When the time comes, neither party is there. Neither would be liable to an action, and, according to what has been said, each has done all he promised to do in the event that has happened—namely, nothing. It might be objected that if A has done all he is bound to do, he ought to be able to sue B, since performance or readiness to perform was all that was needed to give him that right; and the same might be said the other way round of B. On the other hand, taking either B or A as defendant, the same facts would be a complete defense. The puzzle is largely one of words.

It is true that A and B have each performed all they promised to do at the present stage, because each promised only to act in the event of the other being ready and willing to act at the same time. But that readiness and willingness, although not necessary to the performance of either promise, and therefore not a duty, was necessary to present a case to which the promise of action on the other side would apply. So although A and B have each performed their own promise, they have not performed the condition of their right to demand more from the other side. Performing that condition is purely optional until one side has brought it within the scope of the other's undertaking by performing it himself. But it is performance in this second sense—satisfying all the conditions, as well as keeping one's own promises—that is necessary to give A or B a right of action.

Conditions may be created by the very words of a contract. Of such cases there is nothing to say, for parties may agree to whatever they choose. But conditions may also be held to arise by construction, where the contract makes no provision in terms for rescinding or avoiding it in any case. The nature of the conditions the law reads in this way needs explaining. Speaking generally, they are aimed at the existence of the evident grounds, on the side of the rescinding party, for making the bargain, or at the achievement of its evident purposes. But that is not enough. As a rule, the disappointment must be caused by wrongdoing on the other side; and the most obvious kinds of such wrongdoing are fraud and misrepresentation, or failure to perform one's own part of the contract.

Fraud and misrepresentation therefore have to be considered once more in this connection. I take misrepresentation first. The first question it raises is whether the representation is or is not part of the contract. If the contract is in writing and the representation is set out on the face of the paper, it may be material or immaterial, but the effect of its being untrue will be decided on much the same principles as govern a failure to perform a promise on the same side. If the contract is made by word of mouth, there may be wide latitude in connecting words of representation with later words of promise; but once they are found to be part of the contract, the same principles apply as if the whole were in writing.

The question now before us is the effect of a misrepresentation that leads to the contract but is not part of it. Suppose the contract is in writing but does not contain the representation. Does such an earlier misrepresentation ever authorize rescission? And if so, does it ever do so except where it rises to the level of fraud? The promisor might say: It does not matter to me whether you knew your representation was false or not; the only thing I care about is whether it was true. If it is untrue, I suffer equally whether you knew it or not. But it was shown in an earlier Lecture that the law does not go on the principle that a man is answerable for all the consequences of all his acts. An act is indifferent in itself. It takes its character from the surrounding facts known to the actor at the time. If a man states a thing reasonably believing that he is speaking from knowledge, it is contrary to the analogies of the law to throw the risk of its truth on him unless he agrees to take that risk. In the case supposed he did not, since the representation was not made part of the contract.

It is very different when there is fraud. Fraud may lead to the making of a contract through a statement outside the contract just as well as through one contained in it. And the law would hold the contract no less conditional on good faith in the one case than in the other.

To illustrate, we may take a somewhat extreme case. A says to B, "I have not opened these barrels myself, but they contain No. 1 mackerel. I paid so much for them to so-and-so," naming a well-known dealer. Afterward A writes to B, "I will sell the barrels you saw, and their contents, for so much," and B accepts. The barrels turn out to contain salt. I suppose the contract would be binding if the statements about the contents were honest, and voidable if they were fraudulent.
