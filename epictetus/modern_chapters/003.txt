Chapter 2: How to Keep Your Own Character in Every Situation

Only to a rational creature is the irrational unbearable. What is rational, he can bear. Blows are not naturally unbearable. How so? Look at the Spartans, who take a whipping calmly once they have learned that the whipping makes sense. Hanging yourself is not unbearable either: the moment you form the judgment that it is the rational thing, off you go and hang yourself. In short, if we look, we shall find that nothing gives a human being so much pain as what he finds irrational, and nothing draws him so strongly as what he finds rational.

But the rational and the irrational look different to different people, exactly as the good and the bad do, and the profitable and the unprofitable. That, above all, is why we need training: to learn how to fit our preconception of the rational and the irrational to particular cases, in line with nature. And in settling what is rational and what is not, we do not only weigh up externals; we also take into account what suits the particular person.

For one man it is perfectly rational to hold a chamber pot for someone else, looking no further than this: that if he does not hold it he will be beaten and go without food, and if he does hold it nothing hard or unpleasant will happen to him. To another man, holding the pot is unbearable for himself — and unbearable, too, that anybody else should do that service for him. So if you ask me whether you should hold the pot, I shall tell you that getting your food is worth more than not getting it, and being flogged is a worse indignity than not being flogged; so if that is the scale you measure your interests by, go and hold the pot.

"But it would be beneath me."

Then it is you who must bring that consideration into the reckoning, not I. You are the one who knows yourself — how much you are worth to yourself, and the price you sell yourself at. Men sell themselves at very different prices.

That is why, when Florus was deliberating whether to go down to Nero's shows and perform in them himself, Agrippinus told him: "Go down."

"Why don't you go down yourself?" Florus asked.

"Because I do not even deliberate about it. Once a man brings himself to deliberate about such things, and to start pricing up externals, he is very close to the people who have forgotten who they are. Why ask me whether death is preferable, or life? I say life. Pain or pleasure? I say pleasure. — But if I don't act in the tragedy, my head comes off. — Then go and act. I will not. — Why not? — Because you count yourself as one thread among the threads of a tunic. Well, then it is proper for you to see to it that you look like everyone else, just as a thread has no ambition to be anything more than the other threads. But I want to be the purple — that small bright part which makes all the rest look handsome and fine. So why tell me to make myself like the crowd? And if I do, how am I still purple?"

Priscus Helvidius saw this too, and acted on it. When Vespasian sent word forbidding him to go into the senate, he answered: "It is in your power not to let me be a senator. But as long as I am one, I have to go in."

"Go in, then," said the emperor, "but say nothing."

"Do not ask my opinion, and I will be silent."

"But I have to ask your opinion."

"And I have to say what I think is right."

"But if you do, I shall have you killed."

"When did I ever tell you I was immortal? You will do your part and I will do mine. Yours is to kill; mine is to die, and not in fear. Yours is to banish; mine is to go without grieving."

And what good did Priscus do, one man on his own? What good does the purple do for the toga? Why, just this: that it stands out in the toga as purple, and is set there as a fine example for everything else. In the same circumstances another man would have answered Caesar's ban with, "Thank you for sparing me." But Vespasian would never have troubled to forbid such a man the senate at all. He knew he would either sit there like a clay pot, or, if he did speak, would say what Caesar wanted — and throw in a little extra.

An athlete behaved the same way when he was in danger of dying unless his genitals were amputated. His brother, who was a philosopher, came to him and said: "Come, brother, what are you going to do? Are we going to cut this away and go back to the gymnasium?" The athlete would not be moved, and died.

When somebody asked Epictetus whether he had done it as an athlete or as a philosopher: As a man, he replied — a man who had been announced among the competitors at Olympia and had fought there, a man at home in such a place, and not merely rubbed with oil in Baton's training school. Another man would have let even his head be cut off, if he could have lived without it.

That is what regard for your own character amounts to, in people who are used to bringing it into their deliberations of their own accord, along with everything else.

"Come on then, Epictetus, shave off your beard."

If I am a philosopher, I say, I will not shave.

"Then I will have your head off."

If it will do you any good, take it off.

Somebody asked: how is each of us to work out what suits his own character?

How is it, he replied, that when the lion attacks, the bull alone discovers what he can do and puts himself in front of the whole herd? Plainly, having the power and knowing you have it come together; so any of us who has such power will not be in the dark about it. But a bull is not made all at once, and neither is a brave man. We have to train through the winter for the summer's campaign, and not go rushing at what is none of our business.

Only think what price you are selling your own will at. If for no other reason, then at least for this one: do not sell it cheap. The great and superior sort of thing may belong to Socrates and his like. Then why, if this is our nature too, are there not a great many more of us like him? Do all horses turn out fast? Are all dogs good on a scent? Well then — since I am naturally slow, am I to take no trouble on that account? I hope not. Epictetus is no better than Socrates; but if he is not worse, that is enough for me. I shall never be a Milo, and yet I do not neglect my body. I shall never be a Croesus, and yet I do not neglect my property. And in general we do not give up looking after anything just because we despair of being the best at it.
