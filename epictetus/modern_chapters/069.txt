Chapter 12: On Training

We ought not to make our training consist in means that are against nature and calculated to astonish people; if we do, we who call ourselves philosophers will be no different from street performers. It is difficult to walk a rope — difficult and dangerous too. Ought we to practise rope-walking for that reason, or setting up a palm tree, or throwing our arms round statues? Not at all.

Not everything difficult and dangerous is fit for practice. What is fit is whatever helps toward working out the thing set before us. And what is set before us to work out? To live with desire and avoidance unrestrained. And what is that? Neither to be disappointed of what you desire nor to fall into what you would avoid. That is what training ought to aim at.

Since it is not possible to have your desire undisappointed and your avoidance free of falling into what it flees without great and constant practice, you must understand that if you let your desire and avoidance turn toward things outside the power of the will, your desire will not be able to reach its object and your avoidance will not be able to escape what it flees.

And since strong habit is what prevails, and we are used to employing desire and avoidance only on things outside the will, we ought to set a contrary habit against this one; and where impressions are most slippery, there we should set the habit of training.

I am rather inclined toward pleasure: I will lean to the opposite side beyond measure, for training. I shrink from pain: I will rub and exercise against the impressions that come to me, for the purpose of drawing my avoidance away from every such thing.

For who is a man in training? The man who practises not using desire, and applies his avoidance only to things within the power of his will, and practises hardest at what is hardest to beat. So one man must train himself more against one thing and another against another.

What is the use, then, of setting up a palm tree, or carrying about a leather tent, or a mortar and pestle? Practise, man, if you are quick-tempered, to put up with being abused, and not to be upset at being treated with contempt. Then you will make so much progress that even if a man strikes you, you will say to yourself: imagine you have thrown your arms round a statue.

Then train yourself to use wine properly, so as not to drink much — for there are men who foolishly train themselves at this too. But first of all you should keep off it altogether, and keep off young girls and rich cakes. Then at last, if the occasion offers, you will go down into the arena at a proper time to test yourself, and find out whether impressions overpower you as they used to. But at first run a long way from what is stronger than you. The contest between a charming young girl and a beginner in philosophy is not an equal one. The earthenware jar and the rock, as the saying goes, do not agree.

After desire and avoidance comes the second topic, of impulses toward action and away from it: that you may be obedient to reason, and do nothing out of season or out of place, or contrary to any propriety of that kind.

The third topic concerns assent, and has to do with what is persuasive and attractive. As Socrates said we ought not to live a life without examination, so we ought not to accept an impression without examination, but should say: wait, let me see what you are and where you come from — like the sentry at night who says, show me your password. Have you the signal from nature that an impression must have to be accepted?

And finally, whatever means men use on the body when they train it, if these tend in any way toward desire and avoidance, they too may be fit means of training. But if they are for show, they mark a man who has turned toward something external, who is hunting for something else and looking for spectators to say, "What a great man."

This is why Apollonius put it well: when you mean to train yourself for your own benefit, and you are thirsty in hot weather, take a mouthful of cold water, spit it out, and tell nobody.
