Chapter 6: Miscellaneous Remarks

Somebody asked how it was that, although reason is more cultivated now than it used to be, greater progress was made in earlier times.

In what respect, he answered, is it more cultivated now, and in what respect was the progress greater then? Because in whatever respect it is more cultivated now, in that respect the progress will be found now too.

At present it is cultivated for the purpose of resolving syllogisms, and progress is being made. In earlier times it was cultivated for the purpose of keeping the governing part in a condition in line with nature, and progress was made in that.

So do not mix up different things, and do not expect to make progress in one thing while you are labouring at another. But see whether any man among us who is intent on this — on keeping himself in a state that is in line with nature, and living that way always — fails to make progress. You will not find one.

The good man is unbeatable, because he does not enter a contest where he is not the stronger. If you want his land and everything on it, take the land; take his slaves, take his public office, take his poor body. But you will not make his desire fail of what it goes after, nor his avoidance fall into what he would escape. The only contest he enters is over things within the power of his will. How can he not be unbeatable?

Somebody asked what common sense is, and Epictetus replied: There is a kind of common hearing that only tells one vocal sound from another, while the hearing that tells musical sounds apart is not common but trained. In the same way there are certain things that men who are not altogether twisted see by the common notions everybody has. A mind constituted that way is called common sense.

It is not easy to encourage weak young men — no easier than holding soft cheese with a hook. But men with a good natural disposition cling to reason all the harder when you try to turn them away from it. This is why Rufus generally tried to discourage his pupils: he used it as a test of who had a good natural disposition and who had not. He used to say, "As a stone thrown upward will be brought down to earth by its own nature, so the man whose mind is naturally good turns all the more toward what he is naturally inclined to, the harder you push him away."
