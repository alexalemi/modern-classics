Chapter 19: Against Those Who Take Up Philosophy Only in Words

The argument called the Master Argument seems to have been put forward from principles of this sort. There is a general contradiction between these three propositions, any two of them being in contradiction with the third. The propositions are: that everything past must necessarily be true; that an impossibility does not follow from a possibility; and that something is possible which neither is true nor ever will be.

Diodorus, noticing this contradiction, used the force of the first two to prove that nothing is possible which is not true and never will be. Another man will hold the other two — that something is possible which is neither true nor ever will be, and that an impossibility does not follow from a possibility — but will not allow that everything past is necessarily true; which is what the followers of Cleanthes seem to think, and Antipater defended them at length. Others again maintain the other pair — that something is possible which is neither true nor will be, and that everything past is necessarily true — and then have to maintain that an impossibility can follow from a possibility. But holding all three is impossible, because of the contradiction between them.

So if anybody asks me which of these propositions I maintain, I shall answer that I do not know; all I have is the story that Diodorus held one opinion, and the followers of Panthoides, I think, and Cleanthes another, and Chrysippus' people a third.

"And what is your own opinion?"

I was not made for this purpose — to examine my own impressions, compare what other men say, and form an opinion of my own on the question. So I am no different from a grammarian. Who was Hector's father? Priam. Who were his brothers? Paris and Deiphobus. Who was their mother? Hecuba. That is the story I have heard. Heard from whom? From Homer. And Hellanicus writes about the same things too, I think, and perhaps others like him.

And what more have I to say about the Master Argument? Nothing. But if I am a vain man, especially at a dinner party, I can astonish the guests by listing everybody who has written on the subject. Chrysippus has written marvellously in his first book On Possibilities; and Cleanthes has written a special treatise on it, and so has Archedemus. Antipater has written on it not only in his work On Possibilities but separately in his work on the Master Argument.

"Have you not read the book?"

I have not.

"Read it."

And what good will it do a man? He will be more trifling and more of a nuisance than he already is. What else have you gained by reading it? What opinion have you formed on the question? None. But you will tell us about Helen and Priam and the island of Calypso, which never was and never will be.

In that case it does not much matter if you keep the story and have formed no opinion of your own. But in questions of how to live, this happens to us far more than in the matters we are talking about.

"Talk to me about good and evil." Listen:

	The wind from Ilium carried me
	to the shores of the Cicones.
	— Odyssey, ix 39.

Of things, some are good, some bad, and others indifferent. The good are the virtues and what shares in the virtues; the bad are the vices and what shares in them; the indifferent are the things lying between the virtues and the vices — wealth, health, life, death, pleasure, pain.

"And how do you know this?"

Hellanicus says so in his Egyptian History. And what difference does it make whether I say that, or say that Diogenes has it in his Ethics, or Chrysippus, or Cleanthes? Have you examined any of it and formed your own opinion?

Show me how you behave in a storm at sea. Do you remember this distinction when the sail is rattling and you are screaming, and some man who knows nothing about timing stands beside you and says: tell me, in the gods' name, what you were saying just now — is being shipwrecked a vice? Does it have any share in vice? Will you not pick up a stick and lay it across his head? "What have we to do with you, man? We are dying and you come to make fun of us."

And if Caesar sends for you to answer a charge, do you remember the distinction then? If, as you are going in pale and trembling, somebody comes up and says: why are you trembling, man? What is this business you are engaged in? Does Caesar, sitting in there, hand out virtue and vice to the men who go in to him? — you answer, "Why do you mock me too, and add to my troubles?"

"Still, tell me, philosopher, why are you trembling? Is it not death that you are risking, or prison, or bodily pain, or exile, or disgrace? What else is there? Is any of it a vice, or does any of it share in vice? And what did you use to say about these things?"

"What have you to do with me, man? My own evils are enough for me."

And you are right. Your own evils are enough for you: your meanness, your cowardice, the boasting you did sitting in the school. Why did you decorate yourself with what belonged to other men? Why did you call yourself a Stoic?

Watch yourselves like this in your actions and you will find out which school you really belong to. You will find that most of you are Epicureans, and a few Peripatetics — and feeble ones at that. Where will you show that you really regard virtue as equal to everything else, or superior to it?

Show me a Stoic, if you can. Where? How? You can show me any number of men who recite the little arguments of the Stoics. But do the same men recite the Epicurean opinions any worse? And the Peripatetic ones — do they not handle those just as exactly?

So who is a Stoic? We call a statue Phidian when it is made according to the art of Phidias; so show me a man made according to the doctrines he recites. Show me a man who is sick and happy, in danger and happy, dying and happy, exiled and happy, in disgrace and happy. Show me. By the gods, I want to see a Stoic.

You cannot show me one finished; then show me at least one being formed, one who has turned in that direction. Do me this favour. Do not grudge an old man the sight of something he has never yet seen. Do you suppose you have to show me the Zeus of Phidias, or the Athena, a work of gold and ivory? Let one of you show me a human soul that is ready to think as God thinks; that does not blame God or man; that is not disappointed of anything; that does not count itself damaged by anything; that is not angry, not envious, not jealous — and why should I not say it straight out? — that wants to change from a man into a god, and that in this poor dead body is thinking of its fellowship with Zeus.

Show me the man. But you cannot. Then why do you deceive yourselves and cheat other people? Why do you put on a costume that is not yours, and walk about as thieves and pilferers of names and things that do not belong to you?

And now I am your teacher, and you are being taught in my school. And I have this purpose: to make you free of restraint, compulsion and blocking; to make you free, prosperous, happy, looking to God in everything great and small. And you are here to learn these things and practise them.

Why do you not finish the work, then, if you also have the purpose you ought to have, and if I, besides the purpose, have the qualifications I ought to have? What is missing?

When I see a craftsman with his material lying beside him, I expect the work. Well, here is the craftsman and here is the material — what are we short of? Is the thing not teachable? It is. Is it not in our power, then? It is the only thing of all things that is in our power. Wealth is not in our power, nor health, nor reputation, nor in a word anything else except the right use of impressions. That alone is by nature free of restraint; that alone is free of obstruction.

So why do you not finish the work? Tell me the reason. It is either my fault that you do not finish it, or your own, or it lies in the nature of the thing. The thing itself is possible, and the only thing in our power. So it remains that the fault is in me, or in you — or, nearer the truth, in both.

Well then: are you willing that we should at last start bringing such a purpose into this school, and take no notice of the past? Only let us make a beginning. Trust me, and you will see.
