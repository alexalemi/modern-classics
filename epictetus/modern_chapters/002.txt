Chapter 1: What Is Up to Us and What Is Not

Of all our capacities, you will find not one that can examine itself — and therefore not one that can approve or disapprove of anything. Except one. I will come to it.

Take the art of grammar. How far does its power of examination reach? As far as judging what is written and spoken. And music? As far as judging melody. Does either of them examine itself? Not at all. If you have to write to your friend, grammar will tell you which words to use; whether to write at all, grammar will not tell you. The same with music and musical sound: whether this is the moment to sing and play the lyre, or to do neither, music will not tell you.

What will tell you, then? The one capacity that examines both itself and everything else. And what is that? Reason — the rational faculty, to give it its name. It is the only thing we were given that examines itself: what it is, what it can do, what such a gift is worth. And it examines every other capacity as well. What else tells us that gold is beautiful? Gold does not say so itself. Plainly it is the thing in us that judges impressions. What else judges music and grammar and the rest, works out what they are good for, and points out the moment to use them? Nothing else does.

And so, as was fitting, the one thing the gods put in our power is the thing that is best of all and rules everything else: the right use of impressions. The rest they did not put in our power. Some things are up to us, and some are not. Was that because they did not want to? For my part, I think that if they had been able to, they would have handed us the rest as well — but they could not. We are here on the earth, tied to a body like this and to companions like these. How could we possibly not be blocked by externals?

But listen to what Zeus says. "Epictetus, if it had been possible I would have made this little body of yours and this little property of yours free, and beyond anyone's reach. But do not fool yourself about this: the body is not yours. It is clay, finely mixed. Since I could not give you that, I have given you a portion of myself — this capacity to go after a thing and to refuse it, to want and to decline; in a word, the capacity to use impressions. Look after that, treat it as the only thing you own, and you will never be blocked, never stopped. You will not moan. You will not blame anyone. You will not flatter anyone."

Well? Does that strike you as a small thing? I hope not. Then be content with it, and pray to the gods.

But as it is, when we could look after one thing and attach ourselves to that alone, we would rather look after a dozen and be tied to every one of them — to the body, to property, to a brother, a friend, a child, a slave. Tied to so many things, we are weighed down and dragged under. That is why, when the weather is wrong for sailing, we sit there tormenting ourselves, forever going out to see which way the wind is blowing. North. And what is that to us? When will the west wind blow? When it decides to, my good man — or when Aeolus decides. God did not make you manager of the winds. He made Aeolus.

So what follows? That we make the best use we can of what is up to us, and take everything else as it comes. And how does it come? However God pleases.

"So am I the only man who has to lose his head?"

Why — would you have the whole world beheaded, to console you? Will you not stretch out your neck the way Lateranus did at Rome, when Nero ordered his execution? He stretched out his neck, took a feeble blow, flinched back for a moment, and stretched it out again. And a little before that, when Nero's freedman Epaphroditus came to ask him what his offense had been, he said: "If I decide to say anything, I will say it to your master."

What should a man have ready for a time like that? What else but this: what is mine and what is not mine; what I am allowed and what I am not.

I have to die. Do I have to die groaning as well? I have to be put in chains. Do I have to whine about it too? I have to go into exile. Is anyone stopping me from going with a smile, cheerful and content?

"Tell me the secret you are keeping."

I will not. That much is up to me.

"Then I will put you in chains."

Man, what are you talking about? Chain me? You can chain my leg, but not even Zeus can conquer my will.

"I will throw you in prison."

My poor body, you mean.

"I will cut your head off."

When did I ever tell you that mine was the one head that could not be cut off?

These are the things philosophers ought to be turning over. These are what they should be writing down every day. This is what they should be training on.

Thrasea used to say: I would rather be killed today than banished tomorrow. And what did Rufus say to him? "If you choose death as the heavier of the two, what a foolish choice that is. And if as the lighter — who gave you the choice at all? Why not train yourself to be content with what you have been given?"

And Agrippinus — what did he say? "I am not going to get in my own way." Word came that his trial was going on in the Senate. "I hope it turns out well," he said. "But it is the fifth hour" — the hour when he always took his exercise and then a cold bath — "let us go and take our exercise."

When he had finished, a man came and told him: you have been condemned.

"To exile," he said, "or to death?"

To exile.

"And my property?"

It has not been taken.

"Then let us go to Aricia," he said, "and have dinner."

This is what it means to have studied what a man ought to study: to have made desire and avoidance proof against being blocked, proof against everything you would rather not meet. I have to die. If now, then I am dying now. If in a little while, then I will dine, since it is the dinner hour, and die afterwards. How? Like a man handing back what was never his.
