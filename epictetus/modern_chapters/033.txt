Chapter 2: On Peace of Mind

Consider, you who are going into court, what it is you want to keep hold of and what you want to succeed in. If you want to keep a will that is in line with nature, you have complete security, complete ease, no troubles at all. If you want to keep what is in your own power and is naturally free, and you are content with that, what else is there to worry about? Who is master of such things? Who can take them away? If you choose to be self-respecting and trustworthy, who will not allow it? If you choose not to be restrained or compelled, who is going to force you to desire what you think you should not desire, or to avoid what you do not think fit to avoid?

But what do you say? The judge will decide something against you that looks frightening. But how can he make you suffer in trying to avoid it as well? So when the pursuit of things and the avoiding of them is in your power, what else is there to worry about? Let this be your opening, your statement of the case, your proof, your victory, your closing speech, and your applause.

This is why Socrates said to the man who was reminding him to prepare for his trial: "Do you not think I have been preparing for it all my life?"

With what kind of preparation?

"I have kept hold of what was in my own power."

And how was that?

"I have never done anything unjust, in private life or in public."

But if you want to hold on to externals as well — your poor body, your little property, your little reputation — then I advise you to start making every possible preparation this minute, and then to study both the character of your judge and the character of your opponent. If you have to clasp his knees, clasp his knees; if you have to weep, weep; if you have to groan, groan. When you have made what is your own subject to externals, then be a slave and stop resisting. Do not choose to be a slave one moment and not the next; be one thing or the other with your whole mind — free or a slave, educated or uneducated, a well-bred fighting cock or a cheap one. Either take the beating until you die, or give in at once; do not let it happen that you take a great many blows and then give in anyway.

But if that is shameful, then settle the question now. Where is the nature of good and evil? Where truth is. Where truth is and where nature is, there is caution; where truth is, there is courage.

What do you think? If Socrates had wanted to preserve externals, would he have come forward and said, "Anytus and Meletus can certainly kill me, but they cannot harm me"? Was he such a fool that he could not see this road leads somewhere other than to the preservation of life and property? Why does he take no account of his opponents, and even provoke them?

Just as my friend Heraclitus did. He had a small suit at Rhodes about a piece of land, and when he had proved to the judges that his case was just, he said in his closing speech: "I shall not beg you, and I do not care what verdict you give — and it is you who are on trial rather than I." And that was the end of that.

What need was there for it? Simply do not beg. But do not say "I do not beg" either, unless there is a proper occasion for deliberately provoking the judges, as there was with Socrates. And you — if you are preparing a closing speech like that, why are you waiting? Why do you obey the summons to stand trial at all? If you want to be crucified, wait and the cross will come. But if you choose to submit and plead your case as well as you can, then you must do what is consistent with that aim — provided you keep hold of what is your own.

For the same reason it is ridiculous to say, "Suggest something to me."

What should I suggest? "Well, shape my mind so that it can adapt to any event." That is exactly as if a man who could not read said, "Tell me what to write when a name is put to me." If I tell him to write Dion, and then somebody comes along and puts to him not Dion but Theon, what will happen? What will he write? But if you have practised writing, you are ready to write whatever is called for. If you have not, what can I suggest now? If the circumstances call for something else, what will you say, or what will you do?

Remember this general rule, then, and you will need no suggestions. But if you stand gaping after externals, you must of necessity wander up and down at your master's will. And who is the master? Whoever has power over the things you are trying to get or trying to avoid.
