Chapter 27: The Ways Impressions Come at Us, and What Defenses We Have

Impressions come to us in four ways. Either things are as they appear; or they are not, and do not appear to be; or they are, and do not appear to be; or they are not, and yet appear to be. And in all four cases hitting the mark is the business of an educated man.

Whatever it is that troubles us, that is what we must apply the remedy to. If the sophisms of Pyrrho and the Academics are what trouble us, we must apply the remedy there. If it is the persuasiveness of impressions, by which some things look good when they are not, then let us look for a remedy for that. If it is habit that troubles us, we must try to find help against habit. And what help is there against habit? The opposite habit.

You hear the ignorant say: "That poor man is dead. His father and mother are overwhelmed with grief. He was cut off before his time, and in a foreign country." Listen to the opposite way of talking. Tear yourself away from these phrases. Set one habit against the other. Against sophistry set reason, and the exercise and drilling of reason. Against persuasive impressions we ought to have plain preconceptions ready to hand, cleaned of every impurity.

When death appears an evil, we ought to have this rule ready: that it is proper to avoid evil things, and that death is a necessary thing. What am I to do, and where shall I escape it? Suppose I am not Sarpedon, son of Zeus, and cannot speak in that noble way: I will go, and I am resolved either to act bravely myself or to give another man the chance of doing so; and if I cannot manage anything myself, I will not grudge another man the doing of something fine.

Suppose acting that way is beyond our power. Is reasoning that way beyond it? Tell me where I can escape death. Find me the country, show me the men I must go to, whom death never visits. Find me a charm against death. If I have none, what do you want me to do? I cannot escape death. Am I not to escape the fear of it either? Am I to die moaning and trembling?

For this is where being thrown comes from: wanting something, and that thing not happening. So if I can change externals to suit my wish, I change them; and if I cannot, I am ready to tear out the eyes of the man in my way. It is not in a man's nature to bear being deprived of the good, or to bear falling into the bad. Then at last, when I can neither change the circumstances nor tear out the eyes of the man in my way, I sit down and groan and abuse whoever I can — Zeus and the rest of the gods. If they take no care of me, what are they to me?

"Yes, but you will be an irreverent man."

And how will I be worse off than I am now?

To sum up: remember this, that unless reverence and your own interest lie in the same place, reverence cannot be kept up in any man. Do these things not seem necessarily true?

Let the followers of Pyrrho and the Academics come and raise their objections. For my own part I have no leisure for these disputes, and I am not able to undertake the defence of common consent. If I had a lawsuit about so much as a bit of land, I would call in somebody else to defend my interests.

What evidence satisfies me, then? The evidence that belongs to the matter in hand. How perception works — whether through the whole body or through a part — I perhaps cannot explain; both opinions perplex me. But that you and I are not the same, I know with perfect certainty.

"How do you know it?"

When I mean to swallow something I never carry it to your mouth, always to my own. When I mean to take bread I never take hold of a broom; I go straight to the bread as to a target. And you yourselves, who do away with the evidence of the senses — do you behave any differently? Which of you, meaning to go into a bath, ever walked into a mill?

What follows, then? Ought we not to hold on with all our strength to this too — maintaining the common view, and fortifying ourselves against the arguments aimed at it? Who denies that we ought? Well, the man who is able should do it, the man who has the leisure. But the man who trembles and is thrown and is broken inside had better spend his time on something else.
