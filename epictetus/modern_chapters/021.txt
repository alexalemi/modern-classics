Chapter 20: On Reason, and How It Examines Itself

Every art and every capacity has certain things it particularly examines. When the capacity is of the same kind as the objects it examines, it necessarily examines itself as well; when it is of a different kind, it cannot. The shoemaker's art, for instance, works on leather, but is itself something entirely distinct from leather, and so it does not examine itself. The grammarian's art works on speech; is the art itself speech? Not at all — and that is why it cannot examine itself.

Now reason: what has nature given it for? For the right use of impressions. And what is reason itself? A system built out of certain impressions. So by its own nature it also has the capacity of examining itself.

And good sense — what does it belong to us to examine with it? Good and evil, and things that are neither. And what is good sense itself? Good. And the want of sense? Evil. Do you see, then, that good sense necessarily examines both itself and its opposite?

This is why the first and chief work of a philosopher is to test impressions, and tell them apart, and let none in untested. Look at coin, where our interests seem to be somewhat involved: see what an art we have invented, and how many ways the assayer uses to test the value of a coin — sight, touch, smell, and finally hearing. He throws the coin down and listens to the ring, and is not satisfied with hearing it ring once; by sheer attention he turns himself into a musician. So where we think it makes a great difference whether we are mistaken or not, there we apply great attention to spotting whatever can deceive us. But over our poor governing part we yawn and doze, and let in every impression without question — because the harm goes unnoticed.

So when you want to know how careless you are about good and evil, and how energetic about things that are neither, look at how you feel about losing your eyesight, and how you feel about being deceived. You will find you are a long way from feeling as you ought about good and evil.

But this is a matter that needs much preparation and much labour and study. Well — did you expect to acquire the greatest of all the arts with a small effort? And yet the chief doctrine of the philosophers is very short. Read Zeno's writings if you want to see. How few words it takes to say that man's end is to follow the gods, and that the nature of the good is the right use of impressions.

But if you say, "What is God? What is an impression? What is particular nature, and what is universal nature?" — then a great many words are needed. And if Epicurus turns up and says the good must be in the body, then again many words are needed, and we have to be taught what the leading thing in us is, the fundamental and substantial thing. As it is not likely that a snail's good lies in its shell, is it likely that a man's good lies in his body?

But you yourself, Epicurus, possess something better than that. What is it in you that deliberates? What is it that examines everything? What is it that forms the judgment about the body itself, that the body is the principal part? And why do you light your lamp and work for us and write so many books? Is it so that we shall not be ignorant of the truth — who we are and what we are in relation to you? So the discussion takes a great many words.
