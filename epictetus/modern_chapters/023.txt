Chapter 22: On Preconceptions

Preconceptions are common to all men, and one preconception does not contradict another. Which of us does not take it that the good is useful and worth choosing, and that in all circumstances we ought to follow it and go after it? Which of us does not take it that justice is beautiful and fitting?

So where does the contradiction come in? It comes in fitting the preconceptions to particular cases. When one man says, "He did well, he is a brave man," and another says, "Not at all — he acted like a fool," that is where the quarrels between men begin.

This is the quarrel among the Jews and the Syrians and the Egyptians and the Romans: not whether holiness should be preferred to everything and pursued in every case, but whether eating pork is holy or unholy.

You will find the same quarrel between Agamemnon and Achilles. Call them up. What do you say, Agamemnon — ought the proper and right thing not to be done?

"Certainly."

And you, Achilles: do you not agree that what is good ought to be done?

"I do, most certainly."

Then fit your preconceptions to the case in front of you. And there the quarrel starts. Agamemnon says, "I ought not to give Chryseis back to her father." Achilles says, "You ought." Plainly one of the two is fitting the preconception of "ought" to the case wrongly. Then Agamemnon says, "Well, if I have to give Chryseis back, it is only right that I take one of your prizes." Achilles answers, "You would take the girl I love?" "Yes — the one you love." "And am I to be the only man who goes without? The only one with no prize at all?" And so the quarrel begins.

What is education, then? Education is learning how to fit our natural preconceptions to particular things in line with nature; and then to distinguish that some things are in our power and others are not. In our power are the will and every act that depends on the will. Not in our power are the body, the parts of the body, property, parents, brothers, children, country — and in general everybody we live among.

So where should we locate the good? What sort of thing shall we fit it to? To the things in our power?

Is health not a good thing, then, and having sound limbs, and life itself? And are children and parents and country not good? Who will put up with you if you deny it?

Well, let us transfer the notion of good over to those things, then. Is it possible for a man to be happy when he suffers loss and does not get the good things? It is not. And can he keep up proper behaviour toward the people around him? He cannot. For I am naturally formed to look after my own interest. If it is in my interest to have an estate, it is also in my interest to take my neighbour's. If it is in my interest to have a coat, it is also in my interest to steal one at the baths. This is where wars come from, and civil disorder, and tyrannies, and conspiracies.

And how shall I still be able to keep up my duty toward Zeus? If I suffer loss and come off badly, then he is taking no care of me. What is he to me if he cannot help me? And what is he to me, further, if he lets me stay in the state I am in? At that point I begin to hate him. Then why do we build temples and set up statues to Zeus, as men do to evil spirits like Fever? How is he the Saviour, and the giver of rain, and the giver of fruits? And in truth, if we locate the nature of the good in things of that kind, all this follows.

So what are we to do? This is the question the true philosopher is in labour with. As things stand I do not see what the good is or what the bad is. Am I not mad? Yes.

But suppose I put the good somewhere among the things that depend on the will. Everyone will laugh at me. Some grey-haired man will come along with a lot of gold rings on his fingers, and shake his head and say: listen, my boy. It is quite right that you should study philosophy; but you ought to have some brains as well. All this you are doing is silly. You learn the syllogism from philosophers — but you know better than any philosopher how to behave.

Man, if I know it, then why are you blaming me? What am I to say to this slave? If I say nothing he will burst. I have to put it like this: forgive me, as you would forgive a man in love. I am not my own master. I am mad.
