Chapter 23: Against Epicurus

Even Epicurus sees that we are social by nature; but having once located our good in the husk, he cannot go on to say anything else. On the other side he strongly maintains that we ought not to admire or accept anything cut off from the nature of the good — and he is right to maintain it.

How is it, then, that we feel any misgiving, if we have no natural affection for our children? Why do you advise the wise man not to bring up children? Why are you afraid he may fall into trouble that way? Does he fall into trouble on account of the mouse that lives in his house? What does he care if a little mouse in the house squeaks at him?

But Epicurus knows that once a child is born it is no longer in our power not to love it and care about it. That is why he says that a man with any sense does not go into politics either — he knows what a man in politics has to do. If you mean to behave among men as you would among a swarm of flies, what is stopping you?

And knowing this, Epicurus still dares to say that we should not bring up children. But a sheep does not desert its young, nor does a wolf. And is a man to desert his child? What do you mean — that we should be as stupid as sheep? They do not desert their young. As savage as wolves? Not even wolves desert their young.

Come now: who would take your advice if he saw his child fall on the ground and start crying? For my part I think that even if your own mother and father had been told by an oracle that you would say what you have just said, they would not have thrown you away.
