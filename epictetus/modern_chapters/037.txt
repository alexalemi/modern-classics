Chapter 6: On Things That Are Neither Good nor Bad

A hypothetical proposition is indifferent; the judgment about it is not — that is either knowledge or opinion or error. In the same way life is indifferent, and the use of it is not.

So when somebody tells you that these things too are indifferent, do not become slack; and when somebody urges you to be careful about them, do not become abject and struck with admiration for material things.

It is good for you to know your own preparation and your own strength, so that in matters where you have not been prepared you can keep quiet and not be annoyed if others have the advantage of you. In syllogisms you will claim the advantage over them yourself; and if they are annoyed at that you will console them by saying, I have learned this and you have not. In the same way, wherever some practice is needed, do not go looking for what that practice brings; give way there to the men who have had it, and be content yourself with steadiness of mind.

"Go and pay your respects to so-and-so."

And how am I to do it?

Not meanly.

"But I was shut out. I never learned to get in through the window; and when I found the door shut I had either to come away or go in by the window."

Still, speak to him.

"In what way?"

Not meanly.

But suppose you did not get what you wanted. Was that your business, or his? Why do you claim what belongs to somebody else? Always remember what is your own and what belongs to another, and you will not be disturbed.

So Chrysippus put it well: "So long as what is coming is uncertain, I always hold to the things that are better suited to getting what is according to nature; for God himself gave me the power of making that choice. But if I knew it was fated for me to be ill, I would move toward the illness." The foot too, if it had understanding, would move to step into the mud.

Why are the ears of corn produced? Is it not so that they may ripen and dry? And do they not dry so that they may be reaped? They are not cut off from their fellowship with everything else. If they had feeling, then, ought they to wish never to be reaped? But never being reaped is a curse on ears of corn. And so we must understand that with men too it is a curse not to die — just as it is a curse not to ripen and not to be reaped.

But since we have to be reaped, and know that we are being reaped, we are annoyed about it; because we neither know what we are, nor have we studied what belongs to a man, as men who study horses know what belongs to horses.

Chrysantas, when he was on the point of striking his enemy, checked himself on hearing the trumpet sound the retreat: it seemed better to him to obey the general's order than to follow his own impulse. But not one of us is willing to obey readily, even when necessity summons; we suffer what we suffer weeping and groaning, and we call it "circumstances".

What kind of circumstances, man? If you give the name of circumstances to the things around you, then everything is circumstances. But if you mean hardships by the word, what hardship is there in the dying of something that came into being? And the thing that does the destroying is a sword, or a wheel, or the sea, or a roof tile, or a tyrant. Why do you mind which road you take down to Hades? They are all the same length. And if you will listen to the truth, the road the tyrant sends you by is shorter. No tyrant ever took six months to kill a man; a fever often takes a year over it. All this is only sound, and the noise of empty names.

"I am in danger of my life from Caesar."

And am I not in danger, living in Nicopolis where there are so many earthquakes? And when you cross the Adriatic, what do you risk? Is it not your life?

"But I am in danger about opinion too."

Your own? How? Who can force you to hold an opinion you do not choose to hold? Somebody else's, then? And what danger is it to you if other men hold false opinions?

"But I am in danger of being exiled."

What is it to be exiled? To be somewhere other than Rome.

"Yes — and what if they send me to Gyara?"

If it suits you, you will go there. And if it does not, you can go somewhere else instead of Gyara — where the man who sends you to Gyara will also go, whether he likes it or not.

Why do you go up to Rome, then, as though it were something great? It is not worth all this preparation, that a well-born young man should say afterwards, "It was not worth listening to so much, and writing so much, and sitting so long beside an old man who is not worth much."

Only remember the division by which what is yours is separated from what is not. Never claim anything that belongs to others. A tribunal and a prison are each a place, one high and one low; but the will can be kept the same in both, if you choose to keep it the same. And we shall be imitators of Socrates when we are able to write hymns in prison.

But in the state we are in now, consider whether we could bear to have somebody in prison say to us: would you like me to read you some hymns? "Why are you bothering me? Do you not know the troubles I am in? Can I listen to hymns in circumstances like these?" What circumstances? "I am going to die." And are other men going to be immortal?
