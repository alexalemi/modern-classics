Chapter 2: What a Student Making Progress Should Train In, and What We Neglect

There are three areas in which a man who intends to be wise and good has to train himself.

The first concerns desires and avoidances: that he may not fail to get what he desires, and may not fall into what he does not want. The second concerns impulses toward a thing and away from it, and in general doing what a man ought to do — that he may act in order, with reason, and not carelessly. The third concerns freedom from deception and rashness in judgment, and in general it concerns assent.

Of these, the chief and most urgent is the one dealing with the passions; for a passion is produced in no other way than by failing to get what one desires or falling into what one wants to avoid. This is what brings in disturbances, disorders, ill luck, misfortunes, griefs, lamentations and envy; this is what makes men envious and jealous; and these are the causes that make us unable even to listen to what reason tells us.

The second area concerns a man's duties. I ought not to be free of feeling like a statue: I ought to keep up my relations, natural and acquired, as a devout man, as a son, as a father, as a citizen.

The third is the one that concerns men who are already making progress. It concerns the security of the other two — so that not even in sleep shall any impression take us by surprise unexamined, nor in drink, nor in a black mood. This, it may be said, is beyond our power.

But the philosophers of today neglect the first and the second and busy themselves with the third: shifting arguments, conclusions drawn by questioning, hypotheses, liar-puzzles. A man engaged on such matters must take care not to be deceived, as the saying goes. Who must? The wise and good man.

So this is all you are short of? Have you successfully worked out the rest? Are you proof against deception in the matter of money? If you see a beautiful girl, do you stand up to the impression? If your neighbour is left an estate in a will, are you not annoyed? Is there nothing else you are short of now except unshakeable firmness of mind?

Wretch, you are hearing these very words in fear and anxiety that somebody may look down on you, asking what people are saying about you. And if a man comes and tells you that in a certain conversation, when the question was who is the best philosopher, somebody present said that a certain person was the leading one, then your little soul, which was a finger's length, stretches out to a yard. But if somebody else present says, "You are mistaken; that man is not worth listening to; what does he know? he has the first principles and nothing more" — then you are dumbfounded, you turn pale, you cry out at once, "I will show him who I am, that I am a great philosopher."

It is shown by these very things. Why do you want to show it by others?

Do you not know how Diogenes pointed out one of the sophists? By sticking out his middle finger. And then when the man went wild with rage, "That," said Diogenes, "is the man in question. I have pointed him out to you." For a man is not shown by pointing a finger at him, as a stone or a piece of wood is; when somebody shows a man's principles, then he shows him as a man.

Let us look at your principles too. Is it not plain that you set no value at all on your own will, and look outside yourself to things not in your power? What will so-and-so say? What will people think of you? Will you be reckoned a man of learning? Have you read Chrysippus, or Antipater? And if you have read Archedemus as well, then you have everything.

Why are you still uneasy in case you fail to show us who you are? Would you let me tell you what sort of man you have shown us you are? You have exhibited yourself as a mean fellow, a complainer, quick-tempered, cowardly, finding fault with everything, blaming everybody, never quiet, vain. That is what you have exhibited.

Go away now and read Archedemus. And then if a mouse drops down and makes a noise, you are a dead man. Such a death is waiting for you as came to — what was the man's name? — Crinis; and he too was proud, because he understood Archedemus.

Wretch, will you not let go of things that are no concern of yours at all? These things suit men who can learn them without being thrown — men who can say: "I am not subject to anger, or grief, or envy. I am not blocked, I am not restrained. What is left for me? I have leisure, I am at peace. Let us see how we must deal with shifting arguments; let us see how a man who has granted a hypothesis is not to be dragged into something absurd."

Such things belong to them. It is right for men who are doing well to light a fire and have dinner, and, if they like, to sing and dance too. But you come to me and hoist the sails when the ship is going down.
