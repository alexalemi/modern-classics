Chapter 7: How to Use Divination

Out of an unreasonable regard for divination, many of us leave many duties undone. What more can the diviner see than death, or danger, or disease, or things of that general kind? So if I have to expose myself to danger for a friend, and if it is my duty even to die for him, what need have I of divination?

Have I not a diviner inside me, who has told me the nature of good and evil and explained the signs of both? What need have I to consult the entrails of victims or the flight of birds? And why do I submit when he says, "It is in your interest"? Does he know what is in my interest? Does he know what good is? Has he learned the signs of good and evil as he learned the signs in the entrails? If he knows those signs, then he knows the signs of the beautiful and the ugly too, and of the just and the unjust.

Tell me, man, what is being signified for me — life or death, poverty or wealth? But whether these things are in my interest or not, I do not intend to ask you. Why do you not give your opinion on points of grammar? And why do you give it here, on the very things we are all in error about and quarrelling with one another over?

So the woman who meant to send a month's provisions by ship to Gratilla in her exile gave a good answer to the man who told her Domitian would only confiscate them. "I would rather," she said, "that Domitian confiscated it all than that I did not send it."

What is it that makes us resort to divination so often, then? Cowardice — the dread of what is coming. That is why we flatter the diviners. Please, master, shall I inherit my father's property? "Let us see; let us sacrifice about it." Yes, master, as fortune decides. And when he has said, "You will inherit," we thank him as though we had the inheritance from him. And the result is that they play with us.

What should we do, then? We ought to come to divination without desire and without avoidance — as a traveller asks a man he meets which of two roads leads where he is going, with no wish for it to be the right-hand one rather than the left, since he has no wish to take any road but the one that leads to his destination.

That is how we ought to come to God as our guide: as we use our eyes, not asking them to show us the things we would prefer, but taking the appearances of things as the eyes present them.

But as it is, we take the augur's hand trembling, and while we call on God we plead with the augur: "Master, have mercy on me. Let me come safely out of this trouble." Wretch, would you have anything other than what is best? And is there anything better than what pleases God? Why do you do your utmost to corrupt your judge and lead your adviser astray?
