Chapter 5: Against the Academics

If a man sets himself against plain facts, said Epictetus, it is not easy to find an argument that will change his mind. This is not because the man is strong or because the teacher is weak. When a man has been refuted and then sets hard, like stone, what argument is going to reach him?

There are two ways of setting hard: the understanding can do it, and so can the sense of shame — when a man has made up his mind neither to assent to what is obvious nor to give up contradicting it. Most of us dread the deadening of the body, and would do anything to prevent it; the deadening of the soul we do not care about. And with the soul, if a man is in such a state that he cannot take anything in or follow anything at all, we say he is in a bad way; but if his sense of shame and self-respect has gone dead, we actually call that strength.

Do you grasp that you are awake?

"No," the man says, "because I do not grasp it in my sleep either, when I am dreaming that I am awake."

Is there no difference at all, then, between the one impression and the other?

"None."

Am I still to argue with this man? What fire, what iron, am I to put to him to make him feel that he has gone dead? He does feel it, and pretends he does not. He is worse off than a corpse. One man does not see the contradiction: he is in a bad way. Another sees it and is not moved by it and does not improve: he is in a worse way still. His self-respect and his sense of shame have been cut out of him, and his reason has not been cut away but brutalised. And am I to call this strength of mind? Certainly not — unless we are also going to call it strength in men who have lost all shame about sex, and so say and do in public whatever comes into their heads.
