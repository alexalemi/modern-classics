Chapter 16: Why We Should Be Careful About the Company We Keep

If a man mixes often with other people — for conversation, or drinking together, or social life generally — he must either become like them or bring them round to his own way. Put a piece of dead charcoal next to a burning one, and either the dead one puts the other out or the burning one lights it.

Since the danger is so great, we must be careful about entering into these intimacies with ordinary people, and remember that it is impossible to keep company with a man covered in soot and not get some of the soot on yourself.

What will you do if a man talks about gladiators, about horses, about athletes — or, worse, about men? So-and-so is bad, so-and-so is good; this was done well, that was done badly. And what if he sneers, or ridicules, or shows a spiteful temper?

Is any man among us prepared as a lute player is when he picks up a lute, so that the moment he has touched the strings he knows which are out of tune and tunes the instrument? Has any of us the power Socrates had, who in all his social dealings could bring his companions round to his own purpose? How should you have that power? So it necessarily follows that you are carried along by ordinary people.

Why are they stronger than you, then? Because they say their useless things out of real conviction, while you say your elegant things only with your lips. That is why yours are feeble and dead, and why it turns the stomach to listen to your exhortations and your miserable virtue that gets talked about up and down. This is how the crowd has the advantage of you: every conviction is strong and unbeatable.

So until fine sentiments are fixed in you, and you have acquired some power to keep yourself safe, I advise you to be careful about your company. If you are not, then every day, like wax in the sun, whatever you write on your minds in the school will be melted away. Get well away from the sun, then, as long as your sentiments are made of wax.

This is also why philosophers advise men to leave their own country: old habits distract them and do not let a different habit get started, and we cannot put up with the people who meet us and say, "Look, so-and-so is a philosopher now, who used to be such and such." In the same way doctors send patients with lingering diseases to a different country and a different air; and they are right.

You too, then: bring in other habits than the ones you have. Fix your judgments and train yourself in them. But you do not. You go from here to a show, to a gladiator display, to the exercise ground, to the circus; then you come back here, and then off you go again to those places, and always the same people.

And there is no good habit in it, no attention, no care about yourself, no watching of this kind: how shall I use the impressions put before me? In line with nature or against it? How am I answering them? As I ought, or as I ought not? Am I saying to the things outside the will that they are nothing to me?

If you are not yet in this condition, run from your old habits, run from the crowd, if you ever intend to begin to be something.
