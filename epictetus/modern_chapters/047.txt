Chapter 16: That We Do Not Practice Applying Our Judgments About Good and Evil

Where is the good? In the will. Where is the evil? In the will. Where is neither of them? In the things outside the will.

Well then — does anybody among us think about these lessons outside the schoolroom? Does anybody practise on his own, giving answers to things as he would to questions? Is it day? Yes. Is it night? No. Well, is the number of the stars even? I cannot say.

When money is held out to you, have you practised making the proper answer, that money is not a good thing? Have you drilled yourself in those answers, or only against sophisms? Why be surprised, then, if in the cases you have studied you have improved, and in the cases you have not studied you are exactly as you were?

When the public speaker knows that he has written well, that he has learned by heart what he wrote, that he brings a pleasant voice with him — why is he still anxious? Because he is not satisfied with having studied. What does he want? To be praised by the audience. So he has been trained for the purpose of being able to declaim; but as to praise and blame, he has not been trained at all. When did he ever hear from anybody what praise is, and what blame is, and what the nature of each is, and what kind of praise should be sought and what kind of blame avoided? And when did he go through the drill that follows from all that?

Why be surprised, then, if in the matters a man has learned he surpasses everybody, and in the matters he has never been trained in he is just like the crowd? So the lute player knows how to play, sings well, has a fine costume — and still trembles when he comes on stage. He understands his own business, but he does not know what a crowd is, or the shouting of a crowd, or what ridicule is. He does not know what anxiety is either, whether it is his own work or somebody else's, whether it can be stopped or not. And so if he is praised he leaves the theatre puffed up, and if he is laughed at, the swollen bladder is punctured and goes down.

It is the same with us. What do we admire? Externals. What are we busy about? Externals. And then do we still wonder why we are afraid and anxious?

What happens when we take the things coming toward us to be evils? It is not in our power not to be afraid; it is not in our power not to be anxious. And then we say: "Lord God, how am I not to be anxious?" Fool, have you not got hands? Did God not make them for you? Sit down now and pray that your nose may not run. Wipe it instead, and stop blaming him.

Well then — has he given you nothing for the present case? Has he not given you endurance? Has he not given you greatness of spirit? Has he not given you courage? When you have hands like these, are you still looking for somebody to wipe your nose for you?

But we neither study these things nor care about them. Give me a man who cares how he does a thing — not about getting the thing, but about his own activity in doing it. Who cares about his own activity while he is walking about? Who, when he is deliberating, cares about his own deliberating rather than about getting the thing he is deliberating over? If he gets it he is puffed up and says, "How well we deliberated! Did I not tell you, brother, that when we have thought a thing out it cannot turn out otherwise?" And if it turns out otherwise, the poor wretch is humbled and does not even know what to say about what has happened.

Which of us ever consulted a seer about this matter? Which of us ever went sleepless over his own acts? Which? Name me one, so that I may see the man I have been looking for so long, who is truly noble and well-born, young or old. Name him.

Why are we still surprised, then, that we are well practised at thinking about a subject, and in our actions are low, indecent, worthless, cowardly, unwilling to work, altogether bad? We do not care about these things and we do not study them.

If we had feared not death or exile but fear itself, we should have studied not to fall into the things that look like evils to us. As it is, in the schoolroom we are quick-tempered and full of words, and if some small question comes up about any of these matters we are quite able to go through it. But drag us out into practice and you will find us miserably shipwrecked. Let one disturbing impression come at us and you will know what we have been studying and what we have been training at.

And so, for want of discipline, we are always adding something to the impression and making things out to be bigger than they are. When I am at sea and look down into the deep, or look around and see no land, I go out of my mind and imagine I shall have to drink up the whole of it if I am wrecked — and it does not occur to me that three pints are enough. So what is disturbing me? The sea? No: my own judgment. And when there is an earthquake, I imagine the city is going to fall on me; but is one small stone not enough to knock my brains out?

What are the things that lie heavy on us and disturb us, then? What but judgments? What but judgments lies heavy on the man who goes away and leaves his companions and friends and places and habits?

Little children, for instance, cry when the nurse leaves them for a moment, and forget their grief the moment they are given a small cake. Would you like us to compare you with little children? No, by Zeus — I do not want to be quieted with a small cake, but with right judgments.

And what are those? What a man ought to study all day long: not to be affected by anything that is not his own, not by a companion, not by a place, not by the gymnasium, not even by his own body; but to remember the law and keep it in front of his eyes.

And what is the divine law? To keep hold of your own; not to lay claim to what belongs to others; to use what is given, and not to want it when it is not given; and when something is taken away, to give it up readily and at once, and to be grateful for the time you had the use of it — unless you would rather cry for your nurse and your mother.

For what difference does it make what a man is mastered by, and what he depends on? How are you better than the man who cries over a girl, if you grieve over a little gymnasium and some little colonnades and young men and places of amusement? Another man comes and laments that he will no longer drink the water of Dirce. Is the Marcian water worse than Dirce's? "But I was used to the water of Dirce." And you will get used to the other in turn. And then if you become attached to that too, cry over it as well, and try to make a line of verse in Euripides' manner:

	The hot baths of Nero and the Marcian water.

See how tragedy is made, when ordinary things happen to silly men.

"When shall I see Athens again, and the Acropolis?" Wretch, are you not content with what you see every day? Have you anything better or greater to look at than the sun, the moon, the stars, the whole earth, the sea? And if you really understand the one who administers the whole, and carry him about inside you, do you still hanker after little stones and a handsome rock?

What will you do, then, when you are going to leave the sun and the moon themselves? Sit and weep like a child?

Well — what have you been doing in the school? What did you hear? What did you learn? Why did you write yourself down a philosopher when you might have written the truth: "I made some introductory studies and I read Chrysippus, but I never even came to the door of a philosopher"?

How could I have anything of the kind Socrates had, who died as he died and lived as he lived? Or anything of the kind Diogenes had? Do you suppose either of those men ever wept or grieved because he was not going to see a certain man, or a certain woman, or because he would be not in Athens or Corinth but, as it might happen, in Susa or Ecbatana? If a man can leave the banquet whenever he chooses and stop amusing himself, does he stay and complain? Does he not stay, as at any entertainment, only so long as he is enjoying it? A man like that, I take it, would put up with exile for life, or with a death sentence.

Will you not be weaned now, like children, and take more solid food, and stop crying after mothers and nurses — which are old women's laments?

"But if I go away, I shall cause them grief."

You cause them grief? By no means. What causes you grief is what will cause them grief: judgment. So what have you to do? Take away your own judgment; and if these women are wise they will take away theirs, and if they do not, they will lament through their own fault.

Man, as the proverb says, make one desperate effort for peace of mind, for freedom, for greatness of spirit. Lift up your head at last, like a man released from slavery. Dare to look up to God and say: deal with me from now on as you will. I am of one mind with you; I am yours. I refuse nothing that pleases you. Lead me where you will. Dress me in whatever clothes you choose. Is it your will that I should hold office, or live as a private man; stay here, or go into exile; be poor, or be rich? I will defend you before men on account of every one of these conditions. I will show what the nature of each thing is.

You will do no such thing. You will sit in the belly of an ox and wait for your mother to come and feed you.

What would Hercules have been if he had sat at home? He would have been Eurystheus, not Hercules. And in his travels through the world, how many close friends did he have? But nothing was dearer to him than God. That is why he was believed to be the son of God — and he was. In obedience to God he went about clearing away injustice and lawlessness.

But you are not Hercules and cannot clear away other men's wickedness; and you are not Theseus either, able to clear the evils out of Attica. Clear away your own. Out of yourself, out of your own thoughts, cast — instead of Procrustes and Sciron — sadness, fear, desire, envy, spite, greed, softness, lack of self-control.

And it is not possible to throw these things out except by looking to God alone, fixing your affections on him alone, and being consecrated to his commands. If you choose anything else, you will follow with sighs and groans whatever is stronger than yourself, always looking for peace of mind and never able to find it — because you look for it where it is not, and neglect to look where it is.
