Chapter 13: On Anxiety

When I see a man anxious I say: what does this man want? If he did not want something outside his power, how could he be anxious?

This is why a lute player singing on his own has no anxiety, but is anxious when he comes into the theatre, even if he has a good voice and plays well. He wants not only to sing well but to be applauded — and that is not in his power. Where he has skill, he has confidence. Bring him any single person who knows nothing about music and the musician does not care. But where he knows nothing and has had no practice, there he is anxious.

What is he ignorant of? He does not know what a crowd is, or what the praise of a crowd is. He has learned to strike the lowest string and the highest; but what the praise of the many is, and what power it has in life, he neither knows nor has thought about. So he must tremble and turn pale.

Now I cannot say a man is not a lute player when I see him afraid; but I can say something else, and not one thing but several. First of all I call him a stranger, and say: this man does not know what part of the world he is in. He has been here all this time and is ignorant of the laws of the state and its customs, of what is allowed and what is not; and he has never employed a lawyer to tell him and explain the laws to him.

A man does not draw up a will if he does not know how a will ought to be drawn; or else he employs somebody who does. He does not seal a bond carelessly, or write a guarantee. But he uses his desire without legal advice — and his avoidance, and his pursuit, and his intention, and his purpose.

"What do you mean, without legal advice?"

He does not know that he is willing what is not allowed, and not willing what must be; and he does not know what is his own and what is another man's. If he did know, he would never be blocked, never hindered, never anxious.

"How so?"

Is any man afraid of things that are not evils? No. Is he afraid of things that are evils, but so far in his own power that they need not happen? Certainly not. So if the things outside the will are neither good nor bad, and everything that does depend on the will is in our power, and nobody can either take them from us or give them to us unless we choose — where is there room left for anxiety?

But we are anxious about our poor body, our little property, about what Caesar wills; and not anxious about anything inside us. Are we anxious about not forming a false judgment? No — because that is in my power. About not making a movement contrary to nature? No, not about that either.

So when you see a man pale, then — as the doctor says, judging by the complexion, this man's spleen is disordered and that man's liver — you too should say: this man's desire and avoidance are disordered; he is not on the right road; he is in a fever. Nothing else changes a man's colour, or makes him tremble, or sets his teeth chattering, or makes him

	sink at the knees and shift from foot to foot.
	— Iliad, xiii 281.

This is why Zeno was not anxious when he was going to meet Antigonus: Antigonus had no power over anything Zeno valued, and Zeno did not care about the things Antigonus did have power over. But Antigonus was anxious when he was going to meet Zeno, because he wanted to please him — and that was an external. Zeno did not want to please Antigonus, since no man skilled in an art wants to please a man who has no skill in it.

Am I to try to please you? Why? I suppose you know the measure by which one man is judged by another. Have you taken any trouble to learn what a good man is and what a bad man is, and how a man becomes either? Then why are you not good yourself?

"How am I not good?" he replies.

Because no good man laments or groans or weeps; no good man turns pale and trembles, or says, "How will he receive me? How will he listen to me?" Slave, just as it pleases him. Why do you care about what belongs to somebody else? Is it not his fault now if he takes badly what comes from you? "Certainly." And can the fault be one man's and the evil in another? "No." Then why are you anxious about what belongs to another?

"That is a fair question. But I am anxious about how I shall speak to him."

Can you not speak to him as you choose?

"But I am afraid of being put out."

If you were going to write the name Dion, would you be afraid of being put out? "By no means." Why not? Is it not because you have practised writing the name? "Certainly." And if you were going to read the name, would you not feel the same? Why? Because every art has a certain strength and confidence in the things belonging to it.

Have you not practised speaking, then? What else did you learn at school — syllogisms and trick propositions? What for? Was it not for the purpose of arguing skilfully? And is arguing skilfully not the same as arguing at the right time, and carefully, and intelligently, and without making mistakes and without getting blocked, and on top of all that, with confidence? "Yes."

Well then, when you are mounted on a horse and ride out onto the plain, are you anxious at being matched against a man on foot — anxious in a matter you have practised and he has not?

"Yes, but the man I am going to speak to has power to kill me."

Then tell the truth, unhappy man, and stop bragging, and stop claiming to be a philosopher, and stop refusing to admit who your masters are. So long as you offer this handle in your body, follow every man who is stronger than you.

Socrates used to practise speaking — he who talked as he did to the tyrants, to the jurymen, and in his prison. Diogenes had practised speaking — he who spoke as he did to Alexander, to the pirates, to the man who bought him. These men were confident in what they had practised.

But you go off to your own affairs and never leave them. Go and sit in a corner and weave syllogisms and set them to somebody else. There is no man in you who can govern a state.
