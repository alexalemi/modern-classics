Chapter 12: On Being Content

About the gods, there are some who say no divine being exists. Others say one exists, but is idle and indifferent and takes no thought for anything. A third group say such a being exists and does take thought, but only about great things and heavenly things, and about nothing on earth. A fourth group say the divine takes thought about earthly things as well as heavenly ones, but only in a general way, not about particular cases. And there is a fifth group, to which Odysseus and Socrates belong, who say: "I do not move without your knowing it."

So before anything else we have to enquire about each of these opinions, and whether it is stated truly or not. For if there are no gods, how can following them be our proper end? And if they exist but take care of nothing, how in that case can it be right to follow them either? And even if they do exist and do look after things, if nothing is communicated from them to men — and in fact to me — how is it right even then?

The wise and good man, then, having thought all this through, submits his own mind to Him who administers the whole, as good citizens submit to the law of their state. A man coming to be taught ought to come with this intention: how am I to follow the gods in everything? How am I to be content with the divine administration? And how can I become free?

For a man is free when everything happens according to his will and no one can block him.

Is freedom madness, then? Certainly not: madness and freedom have nothing in common.

"But I want everything to turn out just as I like, and in whatever way I like."

You are mad. You are out of your senses. Do you not know that freedom is a fine and valuable thing? For me to wish thoughtlessly that things should happen as I thoughtlessly like — that is not fine at all. It is about as low as anything can be.

How do we go about writing? Do I want to write the name Dion however I choose? No: I am taught to want to write it the way it ought to be written. And with music? The same. And universally, in every art or science? Just the same. If it were otherwise, there would be no value in knowing anything, if knowledge were fitted to each man's whim. And is it in this one thing only — in the greatest and chief thing, freedom — that I am allowed to want thoughtlessly? Not at all. Being taught is precisely this: learning to want each thing to happen just as it does happen.

And how do things happen? As the one who ordered them ordered them. He appointed summer and winter, plenty and scarcity, virtue and vice, and all such opposites, for the harmony of the whole; and to each of us he gave a body, and parts of a body, and property, and companions.

Bearing this arrangement in mind, then, we ought to come to be taught — not so that we may change how things are constituted, since we have no power to do that, and it would not be better if we had, but so that, things around us being what they are and being so by nature, we may keep our minds in tune with what happens.

Can we escape from other men? How could we? And if we live among them, can we change them? Who gives us that power? What is left, then? What method is there for dealing with them? Is there a method by which they do whatever seems right to them, and we are no less in a state that is in line with nature?

But you are unwilling to put up with it, and you are discontented. If you are alone, you call it desolation; if you are with people, you call them crooks and robbers; and you find fault with your own parents and children and brothers and neighbours. When you are alone you ought to call that peace and freedom, and think yourself like the gods; and when you are with a crowd you ought not to call it a mob, or a nuisance, or a burden, but a festival and a gathering — and take it all contentedly.

What is the punishment, then, for those who refuse to accept? To be exactly what they are. Is a man dissatisfied at being alone? Let him be alone. Is a man dissatisfied with his parents? Let him be a bad son, and let him grieve. Is he dissatisfied with his children? Let him be a bad father.

Throw him into prison. What prison? The one he is in already — because he is there against his will, and wherever a man is against his will, that is a prison. So Socrates was not in prison: he was there willingly.

"Must my leg be lamed, then?"

Wretch, are you going to find fault with the universe on account of one poor leg? Will you not gladly surrender it to the whole? Will you not let it go? Will you not hand it over cheerfully to the one who gave it? And will you be annoyed and discontented at the arrangements Zeus made — the ones he settled and put in order, with the Fates standing by and spinning the thread of your birth? Do you not know how small a part of the whole you are? In respect of the body, I mean. As for intelligence, you are not the gods' inferior and not their lesser; the size of an intelligence is not measured by length or height, but by its thoughts.

Will you not choose, then, to put your good in the thing where you are the gods' equal?

"Wretch that I am, to have such a father and mother."

Well — were you allowed to step forward beforehand and make your selection and say, let this man couple with this woman at this moment, so that I may be produced? You were not. Your parents had to exist first, and then you had to be begotten. Of what sort of parents? Of the sort they were. Well then, since they are what they are, is no remedy given to you?

If you did not know what the power of sight was for, you would be unlucky and wretched if you shut your eyes when colours were put in front of them. But you possess greatness of spirit and nobility for every event that can happen, and you do not know you possess them — are you not more unlucky and wretched than that? Things are brought right up to you that match the power you have, and you turn that power away precisely at the moment when you ought to be holding it open and alert.

Why not thank the gods instead, that they have set you above the things they did not put in your power, and made you accountable only for the things that are? For your parents they have left you free of responsibility; the same for your brothers, and your body, and property, and death and life. What have they made you responsible for, then? For the one thing that is in your power: the right use of impressions. Why drag onto yourself the things you are not answerable for? That is simply making trouble for yourself.
