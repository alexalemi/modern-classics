Chapter 22: On Friendship

Whatever a man applies himself to seriously, he naturally loves. Do men apply themselves seriously to things that are bad? Not at all. To things that do not concern them in any way? Not to those either. So it remains that they apply themselves seriously only to things that are good; and if they apply themselves seriously to them, they love them too.

So whoever understands what good is can also know how to love. But the man who cannot tell good from bad, and cannot tell what is neither good nor bad from either — how can he have the power of loving? To love, then, is in the power of the wise alone.

"How can that be?" a man may say. "I am a fool, and I love my child all the same."

I am surprised that you have begun by admitting you are a fool. What are you lacking? Can you not use your senses? Do you not tell one impression from another? Do you not use food that suits your body, and clothing, and a house? Why do you admit you are a fool, then?

In truth it is because you are often thrown by impressions and perplexed by them, and their power of persuasion often beats you; and sometimes you think these same things good, and then bad, and finally neither; and in short you grieve, fear, envy, are disturbed, are changed about. That is why you confess you are a fool.

And are you not changeable in loving too? Wealth and pleasure — in a word, things themselves — do you not think them good sometimes and bad at others? And do you not think the same men good at one moment and bad at another? Do you not feel friendly toward them at one time and hostile at another? Do you not praise them at one time and blame them at another?

"Yes, I have these feelings too."

Well then: do you think a man who has been deceived about somebody is that person's friend? "Certainly not." And a man who chooses somebody for a friend and is of a changeable disposition — has he good will toward him? "He has not." And a man who abuses somebody now and admires him later? "He has no good will either."

Well then, did you never see little dogs fondling each other and playing, so that you would say there is nothing more affectionate? But to learn what their friendship is worth, throw a bit of meat between them, and you will find out.

Throw a small estate between you and your son, and you will discover how soon he will want to bury you, and how soon you want your son dead. Then you will change your tune and say: what a son I have brought up. He has been wanting to bury me for a long time. Throw a pretty girl between you, and let the old man love her and the young one love her too. Let a little reputation come between, or dangers, and it will be just the same. You will say what Admetus' father said:

	Life is sweet to you: and why not to your father?

Do you suppose Admetus did not love his own child when the boy was small? That he was not in agony when the child had a fever? That he did not often say, I wish I had the fever instead of the child? And then when the test came and was close at hand, see what words they use.

Were Eteocles and Polynices not sons of the same mother and the same father? Had they not been brought up together, lived together, drunk together, slept together, kissed each other often? So that if any man had seen them, I think, he would have laughed at the philosophers for the paradoxes they utter about friendship. But when a quarrel arose between them over the throne, like dogs over a bit of meat, see what they say:

	Polynices: Where will you take your station before the towers?
	Eteocles: Why do you ask me that?
	Polynices: I will place myself opposite you and try to kill you.
	Eteocles: I want to do the same.

Those are the wishes they utter.

For do not be deceived: in general every creature is attached to nothing so much as to its own interest. Whatever seems to it to stand in the way of that interest — brother, father, child, the one it loves or the one who loves it — it hates, and shoves aside, and curses. Its nature is to love nothing so much as its own interest; that is father and brother and kinsman and country and God to it. And so when the gods look to us like an obstacle to it, we abuse them and throw down their statues and burn their temples, as Alexander ordered the temples of Asclepius burned when his dear friend died.

So if a man puts his interest and his holiness and his goodness and his country and his parents and his friends all in the same place, then all of them are safe. But if he puts his interest in one place and his friends and his country and his family and justice itself in another, all these give way, borne down by the weight of interest.

For wherever the I and the Mine are put, that is the way the creature must lean. If they are in the flesh, the governing power is there; if in the will, it is there; if in externals, it is there. So if I am where my will is, then and only then shall I be the friend I ought to be, and the son, and the father — because it will then be in my interest to keep the character of a man who is trustworthy, self-respecting, patient, restrained, ready to help, observing my relations toward everyone. But if I put myself in one place and honesty in another, then the doctrine of Epicurus grows strong, which says either that there is no such thing as honesty, or that it is whatever opinion holds it to be.

It was through this ignorance that the Athenians and the Spartans quarrelled, and the Thebans with both; and the great king with Greece, and the Macedonians with both; and the Romans with the Getae. And earlier still the Trojan war happened for the same reasons. Paris was the guest of Menelaus, and if anybody had seen how friendly they were, he would not have believed a man who told him they were not friends. But a bit of meat was thrown between them — a handsome woman — and war came out of it.

So now, when you see brothers who seem to be friends and of one mind, do not conclude anything about their friendship from that, not even if they swear to it and say it is impossible for them ever to be parted. The governing part of a bad man cannot be trusted; it is insecure, it has no fixed rule to steer by, and it is overpowered now by one impression and now by another.

Do not examine what other men examine — whether they were born of the same parents and brought up together and under the same tutor. Examine this only: where they put their interest, in externals or in the will. If in externals, do not call them friends, any more than you would call them trustworthy or steady or brave or free. Do not even call them men, if you have any judgment.

For it is no principle of human nature that makes men bite one another and abuse one another and occupy waste places or public squares as if they were mountains, and in the law courts put on the performance of robbers; nor that makes them intemperate, adulterers, corrupters; nor that makes them do anything else men do against each other — nothing but this one opinion, that they place themselves and their interests among the things outside the will.

But if you hear that these men truly think the good is only where the will is and where impressions are rightly used, then do not trouble yourself about whether they are father and son, or brothers, or have been companions for a long time. Once you have established that one thing, declare confidently that they are friends, as you declare that they are trustworthy and just. For where else is friendship but where there is good faith and self-respect, and a sharing of honest things and of nothing else?

"But so-and-so treated me with regard all these years — did he not love me?" How do you know, slave, whether he regarded you as he regards the sponge he wipes his shoes with, or the way he takes care of his beast? How do you know whether, when you have stopped being useful as a pot, he will not throw you out like a broken dish?

"But this woman is my wife, and we have lived together so long." And how long did Eriphyle live with Amphiaraus, and bear him children, and many of them? But a necklace came between them. And what is a necklace? It is the opinion about such things. That was the brutish principle; that was what broke the friendship of husband and wife apart; that was what would not let the wife be a wife or the mother be a mother.

So let every man among you who has seriously resolved either to be a friend himself or to have another man as a friend cut these opinions out, hate them, and drive them out of his soul. And then, first of all, he will not reproach himself, or be at odds with himself, or change his mind, or torture himself. And in the second place, to another man like himself he will be altogether and completely a friend. And with a man unlike himself he will be patient, kind, gentle, ready to pardon him for his ignorance and for being mistaken in the greatest matters; but he will be harsh to nobody, being thoroughly convinced of Plato's doctrine that every mind is deprived of truth unwillingly.

If you cannot do that, you can still do everything else that friends do — drink together, lodge together, sail together. You may even be born of the same parents; so are snakes. But neither they nor you will be friends, so long as you keep these brutish and accursed opinions.
