Chapter 12: On Attention

When you have let your attention go for a short time, do not imagine you will get it back whenever you choose. Have this thought ready instead: because of the fault you committed today, your affairs must be in a worse state for everything that follows.

In the first place — and this causes the most trouble — a habit of not attending is formed in you; and then a habit of putting your attention off. And by putting it off, you drive away, again and again, the happiness of life, and proper behaviour, and being and living in line with nature.

If putting attention off is profitable, then leaving it out altogether is more profitable still. And if it is not profitable, why do you not keep your attention going all the time?

"Today I want to play."

Well — ought you not to play with attention?

"I want to sing."

And what stops you singing with attention? Is there any part of life left out, that attention does not reach to? Will you do a thing worse by using attention and better by not attending at all?

And what else in life is done better by men who do not attend? Does a man who works in wood work better by not attending? Does the captain of a ship handle her better by not attending? Is any of the smaller acts done better by inattention?

Do you not see that once you have let your mind loose, it is no longer in your power to call it back — either to decency, or to self-respect, or to moderation? You do whatever comes into your head, obeying your inclinations.

What ought I to attend to, then? First to the general principles, and to having them ready — not sleeping, not getting up, not drinking, not eating, not spending time with men, without them. That no man is master of another man's will, and that in the will alone lie the good and the bad. So no man has the power either to procure any good for me or to involve me in any evil; I alone have power over myself in these things.

And when these things are secured to me, why do I need to be disturbed about externals? What tyrant is formidable? What disease? What poverty? What insult from anybody?

Well — I have not pleased a certain person. Is pleasing him my work, my judgment? No. Then why should I trouble myself about him? "But he is supposed to be somebody." He will see to that himself, and so will the men who think it. But I have one whom I ought to please, one I ought to submit to and obey: God, and those next after him.

He has placed me with myself, and put my will under my own control alone, and given me rules for the right use of it. And when I follow those rules in syllogisms, I do not care about any man who says something different; in trick arguments I care about nobody. Then why, in greater matters, am I annoyed by men who blame me?

What is the cause of this disturbance? Nothing but that I am not trained in this matter. For every science despises ignorance and the ignorant — and not only the sciences but the crafts. Produce any shoemaker you like, and he laughs at the crowd where his own trade is concerned. Produce any carpenter.

So first we ought to have these rules ready and do nothing without them, and we ought to keep the soul aimed at this mark: to chase nothing external, nothing that belongs to others, but to do as the one who has the power has appointed. We should chase the things within the power of the will altogether, and everything else as it is permitted.

Next to this, we ought to remember who we are and what our name is, and try to direct our duties toward the character of each of our relations: what is the season for singing, what the season for play, and in whose presence; what the consequence of the act will be; whether the men with us will despise us or we shall despise them; when to make a joke, and whom to laugh at; and on what occasion to go along with people and with whom — and, in going along with them, how to keep our own character.

Wherever you have wandered from any of these rules, the damage is immediate — not from anything external, but from the act itself.

What then? Is it possible to be free of faults, if you do all this? It is not possible; but this is possible: to direct your efforts unceasingly at being faultless. We must be content if, by never letting this attention go, we escape at least a few errors.

But when you say, "Tomorrow I will start paying attention," you must be told that what you are saying is: "Today I will be shameless, careless of time and place, mean. It will be in other men's power to give me pain. Today I will be quick-tempered and envious."

See how many evil things you are allowing yourself. If it is good to use attention tomorrow, how much better to use it today? If it is in your interest to attend tomorrow, much more today — so that you may be able to do it tomorrow too, and not put it off again to the day after.
