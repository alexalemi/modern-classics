Chapter 4: Against a Man Caught in Adultery

Epictetus was saying that a man is made for trustworthiness, and that whoever undermines trustworthiness undermines the very thing that makes a man a man, when in came one of those who pass for men of letters — a man who had once been caught in adultery in the city. Epictetus went on:

If we lay aside the trustworthiness we were made for, and start scheming after our neighbour's wife, what are we doing? What but pulling down and destroying? Destroying whom? The man who can be trusted, the man with self-respect, the man who keeps what is holy. Is that all? Are we not also pulling down neighbourliness, and friendship, and the community? And where does that put us?

How am I to regard you, man? As a neighbour? As a friend? What sort of friend? As a fellow citizen? What am I to trust you in?

If you were a pot so worthless that nobody could use you, you would be thrown out on the dung heap and no one would pick you up. And if, being a man, you cannot fill any place that suits a man, what are we to do with you? Suppose you cannot hold the place of a friend: can you hold the place of a slave? And who will trust you? So should you not be content to be thrown out on some dung heap yourself, as a useless pot, a piece of dung?

And then will you say, "Nobody cares about me, a man of letters"? No, they do not — because you are bad and useless. It is exactly as if the wasps complained that nobody cares about them, that everyone runs from them, and that a man will strike one down if he can. You have a sting of that kind: whoever you wound with it, you throw into trouble and pain. What would you have us do with you? There is nowhere to put you.

"What of it? Are women not common property by nature?"

I say so too. A suckling pig at a dinner is common property to all the guests. But once the portions have been handed out, go and snatch up the portion of the man next to you, if you think that is right — or steal it slyly, or slide your hand over and get hold of it, and if you cannot tear off a piece of the meat, then grease your fingers and lick them. A fine companion over the wine you are, a real Socratic guest.

"Well, is the theatre not common property to the citizens?"

Then when they have taken their seats, go and throw one of them out, if you think that is proper. Women are common by nature in just this way. When the lawgiver, like the host at a feast, has distributed them, will you not look for your own portion instead of pilfering and pawing what belongs to somebody else?

"But I am a man of letters, and I understand Archedemus."

Then understand Archedemus, and be an adulterer, and a man nobody can trust; and instead of a man, be a wolf or an ape. What difference is there?
