Chapter 24: How to Wrestle with Circumstances

It is circumstances that show what men are. So when a difficulty falls on you, remember that God has matched you, like a wrestling coach, against a rough young opponent.

"What for?" you may say.

So that you may become an Olympic champion. And it is not done without sweat. In my opinion no man has ever had a more useful difficulty than the one you have had, if you choose to use it the way an athlete uses a young opponent.

We are sending a scout to Rome at the moment. But nobody sends a cowardly scout — one who hears a noise and sees a shadow somewhere and comes running back in terror, reporting that the enemy is at the gates. So if you come now and tell us, "Things at Rome are frightful. Death is terrible, exile is terrible, slander is terrible, poverty is terrible — run, my friends, the enemy is close," we shall answer: get out. Prophesy for yourself. Our one mistake was sending a scout like you.

Diogenes was sent out as a scout before you, and brought back a different report. He says that death is no evil, since there is nothing shameful in it. He says that reputation is the noise of madmen. And what did this spy have to say about pain, and pleasure, and poverty? He says that being naked is better than any purple robe, and that sleeping on bare ground is the softest bed there is. And as proof of every claim he makes he offers his own courage, his peace of mind, his freedom, and the healthy, solid look of his body. "There is no enemy near," he says. "All is quiet."

"How so, Diogenes?"

"Look," he answers. "Have I been struck? Have I been wounded? Have I run from anybody?"

That is what a scout ought to be. But you come to us with one thing after another. Go back, and take another look with the fear laid aside.

"So what am I to do?"

What do you do when you get off a ship? Do you carry away the tiller, or the oars? What do you take? What is yours — your bottle and your bag. And now, if you keep in mind what is yours, you will never lay claim to what belongs to somebody else.

The emperor says: "Take off your senator's stripe." Very well: I put on the narrower one. "Take that off too." Very well: I have only my toga. "Take off the toga." See — I am naked now. "But you still make me envious." Then take my poor body too. If I can throw away my body at a man's command, am I still afraid of him?

But a certain person will not leave me his estate. Well — had I forgotten that none of it was ever mine? Why do we call such things ours, then? The way we call a bed at an inn ours. If the innkeeper dies and leaves you the beds, well and good. If he leaves them to someone else, that man will have them, and you will look for another bed. And if you cannot find one, you will sleep on the ground. Only sleep willingly, and snore; and remember that tragedies belong among the rich and among kings and tyrants, and that no poor man has a part in a tragedy except in the chorus.

Kings begin in prosperity — "hang the palace with garlands!" — and then about the third or fourth act they cry out, "O Cithaeron, why did you receive me?" Slave, where are your crowns? Where is your diadem? Your bodyguards are no use to you at all.

So when you go up to one of these men, remember that you are going up to a tragic figure — not to the actor, but to Oedipus himself.

"But such a man is happy: he walks about with a crowd around him." And I place myself among the crowd and walk about with them too.

In sum, remember this: the door is open. Do not be more timid than small children. When a thing does not please them they say, "I won't play any more." So you: when things look to you like that, say, I won't play any more, and be off. But if you stay, do not complain.
