Arrian's Letter to Lucius Gellius

I did not write these Discourses of Epictetus the way a man writes a book, and I did not make them public myself — indeed, I say I did not write them at all. Whatever I heard him say, I tried to set down in his own words, as nearly as I could, so that I would have a record afterwards of how he thought and how freely he spoke.

They are, naturally enough, the sort of thing one man says to another on the spur of the moment, not the sort of thing anyone writes for others to read. Being what they are, I have no idea how they came into the public's hands, without either my consent or my knowledge.

It matters little to me if I am thought incompetent as a writer, and it matters not at all to Epictetus if anyone despises what he said. At the time he was speaking, it was plain that he had no other aim than to move the minds of the people listening toward the best things. If these Discourses do that, they will have done what I think the words of philosophers ought to do. If they do not, then let whoever reads them know this much: when Epictetus delivered them, the man who heard him could not help being affected exactly as Epictetus meant him to be. And if the Discourses on the page do not manage it, then perhaps the fault is mine — or perhaps it could not have been otherwise.

Farewell.
