Chapter 13: Against Those Who Too Readily Tell Their Own Business

When a man seems to us to have talked frankly about his own affairs, how is it that we are drawn into revealing our own secrets to him in turn, and think this is being frank ourselves?

In the first place, because it seems unfair to have listened to a neighbour's business and not tell him ours in return. And next, because we think we shall not look like frank men if we say nothing about our own affairs. Indeed men often say, "I have told you all my business, and will you tell me nothing of yours? Where is that done?"

And besides, we have the idea that we can safely trust a man who has already told us his own business, because the notion comes into our minds that this man could never give our affairs away, since he would be careful that we should not give away his.

This is also how the incautious are caught by the soldiers at Rome. A soldier sits down beside you in ordinary clothes and begins to speak ill of Caesar; and then you, as though you had been given a pledge of his good faith by his having started the abuse, say what you think as well — and then you are led away in chains.

Something of the kind generally happens to us too. Now this man has confidently entrusted his affairs to me: am I to do the same with any man I meet? No — because when I have heard, I keep quiet, if that is my disposition; but he goes out and tells everybody what he has heard.

And then if I hear what he has done, if I am a man like him, I decide to get my own back and give away what he told me. I disturb others and am disturbed myself. But if I remember that one man does not injure another, and that every man's own acts injure or profit him, then I secure this much: that I do nothing like him. And still I suffer what I suffer, through my own silly talking.

"True — but it is unfair, when you have heard your neighbour's secrets, to tell him nothing in return."

Did I ask you for your secrets, my man? Did you tell me your affairs on terms, that you should hear mine in return? If you are a babbler and think everybody you meet is a friend, do you want me to be like you too?

And why, if you did well in entrusting your affairs to me, and it is not well for me to entrust mine to you, do you want me to be so rash? It is exactly as if I had a watertight cask and you had one with a hole in it, and you came and deposited your wine with me so that I could put it in my cask, and then complained that I would not entrust my wine to you — because you have a cask with a hole in it.

Where is the equality there? You entrusted your affairs to a man who is faithful and self-respecting, a man who thinks that only his own actions harm or help him and that nothing external does. And would you have me entrust mine to you — a man who has dishonoured his own faculty of will, and who wants to gain some small piece of money, or some office, or promotion at court, even if you were going to murder your own children like Medea?

Where is the fairness in that? Show me instead that you are faithful, self-respecting and steady; show me that your judgments are friendly ones; show me that your cask has no hole in it — and you will see how I shall not wait for you to trust me with your affairs, but shall come to you myself and ask you to listen to mine.

For who does not choose to use a good vessel? Who does not value a kindly and faithful adviser? Who will not gladly welcome a man ready to take a share, as we might say, in the difficulty of his circumstances, and by that very act to lighten the load by taking part of it?

"True — but I trust you, and you do not trust me."

In the first place, you do not even trust me. You are a babbler, and for that reason you cannot hold anything in. Indeed, if it were true that you trust me, you would entrust your affairs to me alone. But as it is, whenever you see a man at leisure you sit down beside him and say: "Brother, I have no friend kinder than you or dearer; I ask you to listen to my affairs." And you do it even to men who are not known to you at all.

And if you really do trust me, plainly you trust me because I am faithful and self-respecting, not because I have told you my affairs. So allow me to hold the same opinion about you.

Show me that when one man tells another his affairs, the man who tells them is faithful and self-respecting. If that were so, I would go about telling my affairs to every man, if that would make me faithful and self-respecting. But it is not so; it takes principles that are not common.

So if you see a man busy about things that do not depend on his will, and putting his will in subjection to them, you must understand that this man has ten thousand people to compel him and block him. He needs no pitch and no wheel to make him declare what he knows: a little girl's nod, if it comes to that, will move him — the coaxing of somebody from Caesar's court, the desire for an office or an inheritance, and endless things of the same kind.

So among your general principles you must remember that talk about secret matters requires good faith, and requires judgments to match it. And where are we to find those easily now? Or if you cannot answer that, let somebody point me out a man who can say: I care only about what is my own, about what cannot be blocked, about what is free by nature. This I hold to be the nature of the good; and let everything else be as it is allowed to be. I do not concern myself.
