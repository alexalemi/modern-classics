Introduction

Very little is known about the life of Epictetus. He is said to have been born at Hierapolis in Phrygia, a town between the Maeander and one of its branches, the Lycus. Hierapolis is mentioned in Paul's letter to the Colossians (Colossians 4:13), from which it has been concluded that there was a Christian church there in the apostle's time. The date of Epictetus' birth is unknown. The only recorded fact about his early life is that he was a slave in Rome, and that his master was Epaphroditus, a dissolute freedman of the emperor Nero. There is a story that this master broke his slave's leg by torturing him; but it is better to trust the evidence of Simplicius, the commentator on the Encheiridion, or Manual, who says that Epictetus was physically weak and lame from an early age. How he came to be a slave is not stated, though it has been asserted in modern times that his parents sold the child. I have found no authority for that.

We may suppose that the young slave showed intelligence, since his master sent him — or allowed him to go — to the lectures of Gaius Musonius Rufus, an eminent Stoic philosopher. It may seem strange that such a master should have wanted his slave turned into a philosopher, but Jean-Jacques Garnier, author of a Mémoire sur les ouvrages d'Épictète, explains the matter very well in a letter to Johann Schweighäuser. Garnier writes: "Epictetus, born at Hierapolis in Phrygia of poor parents, apparently owed the advantages of a good education to a fashion common at the end of the Republic and under the first emperors, by which the great men of Rome liked to count grammarians, poets, rhetoricians and philosophers among their many slaves — much as rich financiers in later ages have been moved to assemble large and costly libraries. This is the only supposition that can explain to us how a wretched child, born as poor as Irus, came to receive a good education, and how a rigid Stoic came to be the slave of Epaphroditus, an officer of the Imperial guard. For we cannot suppose that the confidant and manager of Nero's debaucheries would have wanted such a slave out of any liking for Stoic doctrine, or for his own use."

Some writers assume that Epictetus was freed by his master, but I can find no evidence for it. Epaphroditus went with Nero when he fled from Rome before his enemies, and helped the wretched tyrant to kill himself; Domitian afterwards put Epaphroditus to death for that service (Suetonius, Domitian 14). We may conclude that Epictetus somehow obtained his freedom and began to teach at Rome; but after Domitian expelled the philosophers from the city in 89 AD, he withdrew to Nicopolis in Epirus, a city Augustus had built to commemorate his victory at Actium. There Epictetus opened a school, or lecture room, and taught until he was an old man. The date of his death is unknown. He never married, as we learn from Lucian. When Epictetus was finding fault with Demonax and advising him to take a wife and have children — since this too, Epictetus said, was a philosopher's duty, to leave another in his own place in the universe — Demonax refuted the doctrine by answering: then give me one of your daughters, Epictetus. Simplicius says that Epictetus lived alone for a long time. At last he took a woman into his house as a nurse for a child, whom one of his friends had been going to expose because he could not afford to keep it; Epictetus took the child and brought it up.

Epictetus wrote nothing. Everything we have under his name was written down by an affectionate pupil, Arrian — later the historian of Alexander the Great — who tells us himself that he took the philosopher's discourses down in writing (see Arrian's letter to Lucius Gellius). These discourses made eight books, of which only four survive, under the title Diatribai, the Discourses. Simplicius states in his commentary that the Encheiridion, or Manual, was also put together by Arrian, who picked out from the discourses whatever he thought most useful, most necessary, and most likely to move men's minds. Simplicius adds that the contents of the Encheiridion are found almost entirely, and in the same words, scattered through the Discourses. Arrian also wrote a work on the life and death of Epictetus. The events of a scholar's life were probably neither many nor remarkable, but we should have been glad if that book had survived, since — as Simplicius says — it told what kind of man Epictetus was.

Photius lists among Arrian's works Conversations with Epictetus, in twelve books. Upton thinks this is only another name for the Discourses, and that Photius has mistaken the Conversations for a different work; yet Photius counts eight books of the Discourses and twelve of the Conversations. Schweighäuser observes that Photius cannot have seen these works of Arrian at all, which he infers from how briefly Photius notices them. And in fact Photius does not say he has read them, as he generally does say of the books he lists in his Bibliotheca. The conclusion is that we cannot be certain there ever was a work by Arrian called the Conversations of Epictetus.

The Discourses, together with the Encheiridion and the fragments, were translated into English by the learned Elizabeth Carter, who is said to have lived to the age of eighty-nine. The fourth edition, of 1807, contains her last additions and alterations. That translation has an introduction giving a summary view of Stoic philosophy by way of explaining Epictetus, and notes as well. The editor of the fourth edition says that "the Introduction and notes of the Christian translator of Epictetus are, in the estimation of most readers, not the least valuable parts of the work," adding that "this was also the opinion of the late Archbishop Secker, who though he thought very highly of the philosophy of Epictetus, considered the Introduction and notes as admirably calculated to prevent any mistake concerning it, as well as to amend and instruct the world." The introduction is certainly useful, though not free from errors. The notes I do not think valuable. I have used some of them without comment, and others with remarks of my own where I thought Carter mistaken about the original text, or about other matters.

Carter's translation is a good one, and perhaps no Englishman of that time would have made a better. My first intention was to revise it and correct whatever errors I found. I had revised about half when I found I was not satisfied with the work, and a learned friend advised me to translate the whole myself. This was rather a large undertaking for an old man now past seventy-six. I have done it, however, with great care, and as well as I could. I have always compared my translation against the Latin version and against Carter's, which I think the best way of avoiding the errors any translator may fall into. A man who has never tried to translate a Greek or Latin author does not know how hard it is. What seems plain enough while you are reading it often turns very difficult when you try to say it in another language. Epictetus is generally intelligible, it is true; but the manner of the author — or rather of Arrian, who was trying to reproduce what he heard — is sometimes made obscure by the constant run of questions and answers, and for other reasons besides.

Upton remarks, in a note on Book Three, chapter 23, that "there are many passages in these dissertations which are ambiguous or rather confused on account of the small questions, and because the matter is not expanded by oratorical copiousness, not to mention other causes." The discourses of Epictetus were, it is supposed, spoken without preparation, so that one thing after another would come into the speaker's mind as he went (Wolf). Schweighäuser likewise observes that the thread of the argument is sometimes obscure because words needed to show the connection of thought have been left out. The reader will therefore find that he cannot always understand Epictetus unless he reads him very carefully, and some passages more than once. He must think and reflect as well, or he will miss the meaning. I do not say the book is worth all this trouble. Every man must judge that for himself. But I should not have translated it if I had not thought it worth studying, and I think all books of this kind need careful reading, if they are worth reading at all.

The text of Epictetus is corrupt in places, and that corruption causes a few difficulties. They are not numerous enough, however, to cause or even to allow much variety among translations. This will explain why many parts of my translation are the same, or nearly the same, as Carter's. Where that happened, I did not think it necessary to alter my version merely so that it should differ from hers. I made my translation first, and then compared it with hers and with the Latin. I hope I have not made many blunders. I do not suppose I have made none.

The last and best edition of the Discourses, the Encheiridion and the fragments is Johann Schweighäuser's, in six octavo volumes. It contains Simplicius' commentary on the Encheiridion and two volumes of useful notes on the Discourses. These notes are selected from Wolf, Upton and a few other commentators, but a large part of them are Schweighäuser's own, and he was an excellent scholar and a very sensible man. I have read all of them, and I have used them. Many of the notes to this translation are my own.
