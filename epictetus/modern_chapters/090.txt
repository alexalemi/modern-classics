Chapter 3: What We Should Trade for What

Keep this thought ready when you lose something external: what you have got in its place. And if it is worth more, never say, "I have suffered a loss" — not if you have got a horse instead of a donkey, or an ox instead of a sheep; not if you have got a good action instead of a bit of money; not if instead of idle chatter you have got the peace of mind that suits a man; not if instead of dirty talk you have got self-respect.

If you remember this, you will always keep your character as it ought to be. And if you do not, consider that your opportunities are being wasted, and that whatever trouble you take over yourself you are going to throw away and overturn.

It takes only a few things to lose and overturn everything — a small deviation from reason. For a steersman to capsize a ship he does not need what he needs to save it; if he turns her a little into the wind she is lost. And if he does it not on purpose but merely by neglecting his duty for a moment, the ship is lost all the same.

Something of the kind happens here too: nod off for a moment, and everything you have gathered up to now is gone. So attend to the impressions of things, and keep watch over them; because what you have to preserve is no small matter. It is self-respect, and good faith, and steadiness, freedom from the passions, an undisturbed mind, freedom from fear, peace — in a word, liberty.

What will you sell these for? Look at what the things are worth that you will get in exchange.

"But shall I not get anything like that for it?"

Look — and if you do get something in return, look at what you are getting instead. I have decency; he has a tribuneship. He has a praetorship; I have self-respect. But I do not shout applause where it is not fitting; I will not stand up where I ought not to. Because I am free, and a friend of God, and so I obey him willingly.

But I must claim nothing else — not body, nor property, nor office, nor good report, nor in fact anything. He does not allow me to claim them; if he had chosen to, he would have made them good for me. But he has not, and so I cannot break his commands.

Preserve in everything what is your own good; and as for everything else, take it as it is allowed, and behave reasonably about it, and be content with that alone. If you do not, you will be unlucky; you will fail in everything; you will be blocked and obstructed.

These are the laws that have been sent from there; these are the orders. A man ought to be an expounder of these laws and submit to these, not to those of Masurius and Cassius.
