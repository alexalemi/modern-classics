Chapter 15: Why We Should Think Before We Start Anything

In every act, consider what comes before it and what follows from it, and then go on to the act. If you do not consider, you will start off full of spirit, having given no thought to the consequences; and afterwards, when some of them have shown themselves, you will drop the thing shamefully.

"I want to win at the Olympic games." And so do I, by the gods — it is a fine thing. But consider what comes before and what follows, and then, if it is good for you, take the thing on.

You will have to keep to rules, follow a strict diet, keep off delicacies, exercise under compulsion at fixed hours in heat and in cold, drink no cold water and no wine when you have the chance of it. In a word you must hand yourself over to the trainer as you would to a doctor. Then in the contest itself you must be covered in sand, sometimes dislocate a hand or sprain an ankle, swallow a quantity of dust, be flogged with the whip — and after going through all of it, sometimes lose.

When you have counted all that up, then, if you still have the inclination, go to the training ground. If you do not count it up, look what you will be doing: behaving like children, who play at wrestlers one moment, then at gladiators, then blow a trumpet, then act a tragedy, whenever they have seen and admired such things.

So you too: one moment an athlete, then a gladiator, then a philosopher, then a public speaker — and with your whole soul, nothing. Like an ape you copy everything you see, and one thing after another pleases you while whatever has become familiar bores you. You have never taken anything on after consideration, or after exploring the whole matter and putting it to a strict examination; you have taken it on at random and with a lukewarm desire.

So some men, having seen a philosopher and heard one speak like Euphrates — and who can speak like him? — want to be philosophers themselves.

Man, consider first what the business is, and then your own nature, and what it can bear. If you are going to wrestle, look at your shoulders, your thighs, your loins: different men are naturally made for different things.

Do you suppose you can be a philosopher and go on doing what you do every day? Do you suppose you can eat as you eat now, drink as you drink now, and be angry and out of temper in the same way? You must go without sleep, work hard, master certain desires, leave your family, be looked down on by your slave, laughed at by the men you meet, and come off worse in everything — in office, in honours, in the law courts.

When you have thought all this over thoroughly, then, if you think proper, come to philosophy — if you want to get, in exchange for these things, freedom from being thrown about, and liberty, and peace of mind. And if you have not thought it over, do not come near philosophy. Do not behave like children: a philosopher one moment, then a tax collector, then a public speaker, then an agent of Caesar. These things do not go together. You must be one man, either good or bad. You must work either at your own governing part or at externals — either at what is inside or at what is outside. That is, you must take the place either of a philosopher or of one of the crowd.

Somebody said to Rufus, when Galba had been murdered: "Is the world governed by Providence now?" And Rufus answered: "Did I ever, even in passing, build an argument on Galba that the world is governed by Providence?"
