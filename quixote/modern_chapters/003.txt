Chapter 1: Which Treats of the Character and Daily Habits of the Famous Gentleman Don Quixote of La Mancha

In a village of La Mancha, whose name I have no wish to remember, there lived not long ago one of those gentlemen who keep a lance in the rack, an ancient leather shield, a bony old nag and a greyhound for coursing. A stew with rather more beef than mutton in it, cold meat salad most evenings, "griefs and groans" — bacon and eggs — on Saturdays, lentils on Fridays and the odd pigeon by way of a treat on Sundays: this ate up three quarters of his income. The rest of it went on a coat of fine black cloth, velvet breeches for holidays with the slippers to match, and on weekdays he did himself proud in the best homespun he could get. He kept in the house a housekeeper the wrong side of forty, a niece not yet twenty, and a lad for the field and the market who could saddle the nag and handle the pruning-hook. Our gentleman was getting on for fifty. He was strongly built but lean, thin in the face, a great early riser and a keen huntsman. They say his surname was Quixada, or Quesada — the authorities who write about this differ on the point — though the likeliest guess is that it was Quexana. But that hardly matters to our story; it is enough that the telling of it does not stray a hair's breadth from the truth.

You must know, then, that in his idle hours — which were most of the year — this gentleman gave himself up to reading books of chivalry, with such passion and such pleasure that he all but forgot his hunting and even the running of his property. His curiosity and his folly went so far that he sold acres of good arable land in order to buy books of chivalry to read, and carried home every one of them he could get hold of. Of all of them, none pleased him so well as those written by the famous Feliciano de Silva, because the clarity of his prose and those tangled arguments of his struck him as pearls beyond price — above all when he came to the wooings and the challenges, where he would often find written: The reason of the unreason with which my reason is afflicted so weakens my reason that with reason I complain of your beauty. Or again: The high heavens that with their stars divinely fortify you in your divinity make you deserving of the deserts that your greatness deserves.

Over sentences like these the poor gentleman lost his wits. He would lie awake trying to understand them and dig the sense out of them, which Aristotle himself could not have done if he had come back to life for that single purpose. He was not altogether happy about the wounds Don Belianís gave and took, since he reckoned that however great the surgeons who had treated him, his face and his whole body must be covered in scars and seams. Still, he praised the author for ending his book with the promise of that never-ending adventure, and many a time he felt the itch to take up a pen and finish it off exactly as promised there. He would have done it too, and made a success of it, if greater and more constant preoccupations had not got in his way.

He often argued with the curate of his village — a learned man, a graduate of Sigüenza — about which had been the better knight, Palmerín of England or Amadís of Gaul. But Master Nicholas, the barber of that same town, said that neither of them came near the Knight of Phoebus, and that if anyone could be compared to him it was Don Galaor, brother of Amadís of Gaul, because he had a temper suited to anything; he was not a squeamish knight, nor such a weeper as his brother, and in the matter of courage he was not a step behind him.

In short, he buried himself so deep in his reading that his nights went by from dusk to dawn and his days from dawn to dusk; and so, from too little sleep and too much reading, his brain dried up on him and he went out of his mind. His imagination filled with everything he had read in the books — enchantments and quarrels, battles and challenges, wounds, wooings, love affairs, tempests and impossible nonsense; and it planted itself so firmly in his head that the whole machinery of those dreamed-up, drummed-up inventions was true, that for him there was no more certain history in the world.

He used to say that the Cid Ruy Díaz had been a very good knight, but that he was nothing beside the Knight of the Burning Sword, who with a single backhand had split two huge and monstrous giants down the middle. He thought better of Bernardo del Carpio, because at Roncesvalles he had killed the enchanted Roland by using the trick of Hercules, who throttled Antaeus, the son of Earth, in his arms. He spoke very well of the giant Morgante, because although he came of that giant brood, who are all overbearing and ill-mannered, he alone was civil and well brought up. But above all of them he approved of Reinaldos of Montalbán, and most of all when he saw him ride out of his castle and rob everyone he met, and when overseas he stole that idol of Mahomet which was solid gold, according to his history. As for the traitor Ganelon, he would have given his housekeeper for the chance of a good kicking at him — and thrown in his niece as well.

In the end, his wits now completely gone, he fell into the strangest notion that any madman in the world ever fell into: it seemed to him right and necessary, both for the increase of his own honour and for the service of his country, that he should turn knight errant and go off through the world with his armour and his horse to look for adventures, and to practise everything he had read that knights errant practised — righting every kind of wrong, and putting himself in the way of dangers and difficulties from which, once he had come through them, he would win eternal name and fame. The poor man already saw himself crowned by the strength of his own arm as at the very least Emperor of Trebizond; and so, in the grip of these delightful thoughts and carried away by the extraordinary pleasure he took in them, he set about putting his wishes into practice.

The first thing he did was to clean up a suit of armour that had belonged to his great-grandfathers and had lain rusting and mildewed in a corner, forgotten, for centuries. He cleaned and repaired it as best he could, but then he saw that it had one great defect: it had no closed helmet, only a plain headpiece. Here his ingenuity came to the rescue. He made a kind of half-helmet out of cardboard which, fitted to the headpiece, gave the appearance of a whole one. It is true that in order to test whether it was strong and could stand up to a sword-cut, he drew his sword and gave it two blows; and with the first he undid in an instant what he had taken a week to make. It did not please him at all, the ease with which he had knocked it to pieces, and to guard against that danger he made it over again, putting bars of iron inside it, in such a way that he was satisfied with its strength; and without caring to test it a second time, he pronounced it and held it to be a helmet of the very finest make.

Next he went to look at his nag; and although the beast had more cracked quarters in its hooves than there are quarter-pieces in a coin, and more blemishes than Gonella's horse, which tantum pellis et ossa fuit — was nothing but skin and bone — it seemed to him that neither Alexander's Bucephalus nor the Cid's Babieca was a match for it. Four days went by while he thought what name to give it; because, as he said to himself, it was not right that the horse of so famous a knight, and so good a beast in itself, should go without a name of its own. He tried to fit it with one that would declare what it had been before it belonged to a knight errant, and what it was now; for it stood to reason that when its master changed his condition it should change its name too, and take one that was famous and resounding, as suited the new order and the new profession it had now entered. And so, after a great many names that he made up and rubbed out and struck off, added to, pulled apart and put together again in his memory and his imagination, he ended by calling it Rocinante — a name which struck him as lofty, resounding, and expressive both of what the animal had been when it was a common hack, and of what it was now: the first and foremost hack in the world.

Having found a name for his horse, and one so much to his liking, he wanted to find one for himself, and he spent another eight days on that thought, and at the end of it decided to call himself Don Quixote. Which is why — as has been said — the authors of this most truthful history concluded that his name must certainly have been Quixada, and not Quesada, as others have tried to make out. But remembering that the valiant Amadís had not been content to call himself Amadís and nothing more, but had added the name of his kingdom and country in order to make it famous, and called himself Amadís of Gaul, he too, like a good knight, resolved to add the name of his own country to his own and call himself Don Quixote of La Mancha — by which, to his mind, he declared his lineage and his birthplace to the life, and honoured his country by taking his surname from it.

So, with his armour cleaned, his headpiece turned into a helmet, a name found for his nag and his own name settled, he decided that the only thing still wanting was a lady to fall in love with; for a knight errant without a love is a tree without leaves or fruit, a body without a soul. He said to himself:

"If, for my sins, or by my good luck, I should run into some giant hereabouts, as commonly happens to knights errant, and knock him down at the first charge, or split him through the middle, or in short defeat him and force him to surrender — will it not be as well to have someone to send him to as a present, so that he may go in and fall on his knees before my sweet lady and say, in a humble, submissive voice: 'Madam, I am the giant Caraculiambro, lord of the isle of Malindrania, who was defeated in single combat by the never-sufficiently-to-be-praised knight Don Quixote of La Mancha, who commanded me to present myself before your grace, that your highness might dispose of me as she pleases'?"

Oh, how our good gentleman enjoyed himself when he had made that speech — and still more when he had found someone to call his lady! It happened, so it is believed, that in a village near his own there was a good-looking farm girl he had been in love with for a time, though as far as anyone knows she never learned of it or gave it a thought. Her name was Aldonza Lorenzo, and it seemed to him a good idea to give her the title of mistress of his thoughts. Looking for a name for her that should not clash too much with her own, and that should lean and point towards a princess and a great lady, he ended by calling her Dulcinea of El Toboso, because she came from El Toboso — a name which struck him as musical and out of the ordinary and full of meaning, like all the others he had settled on for himself and for everything belonging to him.
