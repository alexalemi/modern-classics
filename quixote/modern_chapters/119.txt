Chapter 60: Of What Happened to Don Quixote on His Way to Barcelona

The morning was cool, and gave signs that the day would be so too, when Don Quixote left the inn, first finding out which was the straightest road to go to Barcelona without touching Saragossa: such was his eagerness to give the lie to that new historian who they said abused him so much.

It happened, then, that for more than six days nothing occurred to him worth putting in writing; at the end of which, going off the road, night caught him among some thick oaks or cork trees — for in this Cide Hamete does not keep the exactness he generally keeps in other things.

Master and man dismounted from their beasts; and, settling themselves against the trunks of the trees, Sancho, who had had his snack that day, let himself go headlong in at the gates of sleep. But Don Quixote, whom his imaginings kept awake much more than hunger, could not close his eyes; instead his thoughts came and went over a thousand kinds of places. Now it seemed to him that he was in the cave of Montesinos; now that he saw Dulcinea, turned into a peasant girl, jump and spring up onto her ass; now that the words of the wise Merlin were ringing in his ears, telling him the conditions and the steps that were to be taken and kept for Dulcinea's disenchantment.

He was in despair at seeing the slackness and the small charity of Sancho his squire, since, as far as he believed, he had given himself only five lashes — an uneven and small number for the infinite ones he still owed. And at this he took such distress and annoyance that he made this argument:

"If the great Alexander cut the Gordian knot, saying, 'Cutting is as good as untying,' and did not for that reason stop being universal lord of all Asia, the same might happen now in Dulcinea's disenchantment if I whipped Sancho in spite of himself. Because if the condition of this remedy lies in Sancho's receiving the three thousand and more lashes, what does it matter to me whether he gives them to himself or somebody else gives them to him, since the substance of it lies in his receiving them, from wherever they come?"

With this idea he went up to Sancho, having first taken Rocinante's reins and arranged them in such a way that he could whip him with them, and began to undo the laces — for it is held that he had only the front one, which held up his breeches. But he had hardly got there when Sancho woke up in full possession of his senses and said:

"What is this? Who is touching me and unlacing me?"

"It is I," answered Don Quixote, "who come to make up your shortcomings and to relieve my own troubles. I come to whip you, Sancho, and to discharge in part the debt you bound yourself to. Dulcinea is perishing; you live in carelessness; I am dying with longing; and so unlace yourself of your own free will, for mine is to give you, in this solitude, at least two thousand lashes."

"Not that," said Sancho. "Let your worship keep still; if not, by the true God the deaf shall hear us. The lashes I bound myself to must be voluntary and not forced, and just now I have no fancy to whip myself. It is enough that I give your worship my word to flog myself and swat myself whenever I feel like it."

"There is no leaving it to your courtesy, Sancho," said Don Quixote, "because you are hard of heart and, peasant though you are, soft of flesh."

And so he kept trying and struggling to unlace him. Which Sancho Panza seeing, he got to his feet and, rushing at his master, grappled with him at close quarters; and, tripping him up, brought him down on the ground on his back. He set his right knee on his chest and held his hands with his own hands, so that he let him neither turn over nor breathe. Don Quixote said to him:

"How, traitor? Do you rebel against your master and natural lord? Do you set on the man who gives you his bread?"

"I neither unmake a king nor make one," answered Sancho, "but only help myself, who am my own lord. Let your worship promise me that you will keep still and will not try to whip me for now, and I shall leave you free and unencumbered; if not,

	Here shall you die, traitor,
	enemy of Doña Sancha."

Don Quixote promised it him, and swore by the life of his thoughts not to touch him on a hair of his clothes, and to leave the whipping entirely to his own will and choice, whenever he liked.

Sancho got up and moved a good way off from that place; and, going to lean against another tree, he felt something touch him on the head and, putting up his hands, came upon two human feet with shoes and stockings on them. He shook with fear; he made for another tree, and the same thing happened to him. He shouted, calling on Don Quixote to help him. Don Quixote did so; and, asking him what had happened to him and what he was afraid of, Sancho answered that all those trees were full of human feet and legs. Don Quixote felt them and tumbled at once to what it could be, and said to Sancho:

"You have nothing to be afraid of, because these feet and legs you are feeling and cannot see are undoubtedly those of some outlaws and bandits who are hanged on these trees; for the law generally hangs them about here when it catches them, twenty at a time and thirty at a time. From which I give myself to understand that I must be near Barcelona."

And it was the truth, as he had imagined it.

At daybreak they raised their eyes and saw the clusters on those trees, which were the bodies of bandits. Day was breaking by now; and if the dead had frightened them, they were no less troubled by more than forty living bandits who suddenly surrounded them, telling them in the Catalan language to keep still and to stop until their captain came.

Don Quixote found himself on foot, his horse without a bridle, his lance leaning against a tree and, in short, without any defence at all; and so he thought it well to fold his arms and bow his head, keeping himself for a better season and conjunction.

The bandits set about picking the grey clean and leaving him nothing of everything he was carrying in the saddlebags and the valise; and it turned out well for Sancho that the duke's crowns, and the ones they had brought from home, were in a belt he had girt round him — and even so those good people would have weeded him out and looked even at what he had hidden between his skin and his flesh if their captain had not arrived at that moment.

He looked to be about thirty-four years old, robust, more than middling in height, with a grave look and a dark complexion. He came on a mighty horse, wearing a steel coat of mail, and with four pistols — which in that country they call flintlocks — at his sides. He saw that his squires, for so they call those who follow that trade, were about to strip Sancho Panza; he ordered them not to, and was obeyed at once; and so the money belt escaped.

He was astonished to see a lance leaning against the tree, a shield on the ground, and Don Quixote armed and thoughtful, with the saddest and most melancholy figure that sadness itself could have made. He came up to him, saying:

"Do not be so sad, good man, because you have not fallen into the hands of any cruel Busiris but into those of Roque Guinart, which have more of the compassionate about them than of the severe."

"My sadness," answered Don Quixote, "is not at having fallen into your power, O valorous Roque, whose fame there are no limits on earth to enclose; but at having been so careless that your soldiers have caught me without my bridle, when I am obliged, by the order of knight-errantry that I profess, to live constantly on the alert, being at all hours my own sentinel. Because I let you know, O great Roque, that if they had found me on my horse with my lance and with my shield, it would not have been very easy for them to overcome me; for I am Don Quixote of La Mancha, the man who has filled the whole globe with his exploits."

Roque Guinart at once knew that Don Quixote's disease touched more on madness than on valour; and, although he had heard him named a few times, he had never held his deeds for true, and could not persuade himself that such a humour could reign in a man's heart. And he was extremely glad to have met him, so as to touch close up what he had heard of him at a distance; and so he said to him:

"Valorous knight, do not despair or take this state you find yourself in for ill fortune; for it might be that in these stumblings your twisted luck may come straight. For heaven, by strange and never-before-seen roundabouts that men never imagine, generally raises up the fallen and enriches the poor."

Don Quixote was about to thank him when they heard behind them a noise like a troop of horses — and it was only one, on which there came at full fury a young man of, apparently, about twenty, dressed in green damask with gold braid, in wide breeches and an overjacket, with a hat cocked in the Walloon fashion, waxed boots fitting close, spurs, a gilded dagger and sword, a small musket in his hands and two pistols at his sides. At the noise Roque turned his head and saw this handsome figure, who, on coming up to him, said:

"I was coming to look for you, O valorous Roque, to find in you, if not a remedy, at least a relief in my misfortune. And, so as not to keep you in suspense — for I know you have not recognised me — I want to tell you who I am. I am Claudia Jerónima, daughter of Simón Forte, your particular friend and the particular enemy of Clauquel Torrellas, who is likewise yours, being one of the men of the opposite faction. And you know that this Torrellas has a son called Don Vicente Torrellas — or at least he was called that not two hours ago.

"This man, then — to shorten the tale of my misfortune, I shall tell you in a few words what has caused it. He saw me, he courted me, I listened to him, I fell in love with him, without my father's knowledge; for there is no woman, however sheltered and however careful she may be, who has not time enough to put her headlong desires into execution and effect. Finally he promised to be my husband, and I gave him my word to be his, without our going any further in deeds.

"Yesterday I learnt that, forgetting what he owed me, he was marrying another woman, and that this morning he was to be betrothed — news that disturbed my senses and made an end of my patience. And, my father not being in the place, I had to put myself into the dress you see; and, urging on this horse, I caught up with Don Vicente about a league from here. And, without stopping to make complaints or to hear excuses, I fired these muskets at him and, into the bargain, these two pistols; and, as I believe, I must have shut more than two bullets in his body, opening doors for my honour to come out of, wrapped in his blood. I left him there among his servants, who neither dared nor were able to come to his defence.

"I come to look for you so that you may take me over into France, where I have relations to live with, and likewise to beg you to defend my father, so that Don Vicente's many kinsmen may not dare to take an outrageous revenge on him."

Roque, amazed at the gallantry, the dash, the fine figure and the adventure of the beautiful Claudia, said to her:

"Come, lady, and let us go and see whether your enemy is dead; and afterwards we shall see what matters most to you."

Don Quixote, who was listening attentively to what Claudia had said and what Roque Guinart answered, said:

"Nobody need take any trouble to defend this lady, for I take it upon myself. Give me my horse and my arms and wait for me here, and I shall go and look for this knight; and, dead or alive, I shall make him keep the word he promised to so much beauty."

"Let nobody doubt it," said Sancho, "because my master has a very good hand at matchmaking; for it is not many days since he made another man marry who was also denying his word to another maiden. And if it had not been that the enchanters who persecute him changed his true shape into a footman's, by this hour that maiden would be one no longer."

Roque, who was attending more to thinking about the business of the beautiful Claudia than to the words of master and man, did not take them in; and, ordering his squires to give Sancho back everything they had taken off the grey, ordering them likewise to withdraw to the place where they had been lodged that night, he set off at once in great haste with Claudia to look for the wounded — or dead — Don Vicente.

They came to the place where Claudia had met him, and found nothing there but freshly spilt blood. But, casting their eyes in every direction, they made out some people up a slope, and gave themselves to understand — as was the truth — that it must be Don Vicente, whom his servants were carrying, dead or alive, either to treat him or to bury him. They made haste to catch them up; and, as they were going slowly, they easily did.

They found Don Vicente in his servants' arms, and he was begging them in a weary, weakened voice to let him die there, because the pain of his wounds would not let him go any further.

Claudia and Roque threw themselves off their horses and came up to him; the servants were afraid at Roque's presence, and Claudia was thrown into confusion at seeing Don Vicente's. And so, between tenderness and severity, she came up to him and, taking him by the hands, said to him:

"If you had given me these, according to our agreement, you would never have found yourself in this pass."

The wounded knight opened his almost closed eyes, and, recognising Claudia, said to her:

"I see well enough, beautiful and deceived lady, that you have been the one to kill me — a punishment neither deserved nor owing to my desires, with which, or with my deeds, I never wished or knew how to offend you."

"Then is it not true," said Claudia, "that you were going this morning to be married to Leonora, the daughter of the rich Balvastro?"

"Certainly not," answered Don Vicente. "My bad fortune must have brought you this news so that, out of jealousy, you should take my life away — which, since I leave it in your hands and in your arms, I count my lot fortunate. And, to assure you of this truth, press my hand and take me for your husband, if you will; for I have no greater satisfaction to give you for the wrong you think you have received from me."

Claudia pressed his hand, and her heart was pressed so hard that she fell in a faint on Don Vicente's blood and breast; and a mortal spasm took hold of him. Roque was confused and did not know what to do. The servants ran to look for water to throw in their faces, and brought it and bathed them. Claudia came out of her faint, but Don Vicente did not come out of his spasm, because his life ended.

Which Claudia seeing, and having made certain that her sweet husband was no longer alive, she broke the air with sighs, struck the heavens with her cries, mistreated her hair, giving it to the wind, disfigured her face with her own hands, with all the signs of grief and feeling that could be imagined from a wounded breast.

"O cruel and thoughtless woman," she said, "how easily you were moved to put so bad a thought into execution! O raging power of jealousy, to what a desperate end you lead the man who gives you room in his breast! O husband of mine, whose unfortunate lot, for being my possession, has carried you from the marriage bed to the grave!"

Such and so mournful were Claudia's cries that they drew tears out of Roque's eyes, which were not used to shedding them on any occasion. The servants wept; Claudia fainted away at every step; and that whole circuit looked like a field of sadness and a place of misfortune.

Finally Roque Guinart ordered Don Vicente's servants to carry his body to his father's village, which was close by, so that they might give it burial. Claudia told Roque that she would like to go to a convent where an aunt of hers was abbess, in which she meant to end her life, accompanied by another and a better and more everlasting husband. Roque praised her good resolution and offered to go with her wherever she liked and to defend her father from Don Vicente's relations and from all the world, if they should want to harm him. Claudia would not have his company on any account; and, thanking him for his offers in the best words she knew, she took her leave of him in tears.

Don Vicente's servants carried away his body, and Roque went back to his men; and this was the end that the loves of Claudia Jerónima came to. But what wonder, when the invincible and severe forces of jealousy wove the plot of her lamentable history?

Roque Guinart found his squires in the place where he had ordered them, and Don Quixote among them, on Rocinante, making them a speech in which he urged them to leave that way of life, so dangerous both for the soul and for the body; but, as most of them were Gascons, rough and disorderly people, Don Quixote's speech did not go into them very well.

When Roque arrived, he asked Sancho Panza whether they had given him back and restored the goods and valuables that his men had taken off the grey. Sancho answered that they had, except that he was short of three headcloths that were worth three cities.

"What are you saying, man?" said one of those present. "I have them, and they are not worth three reals."

"That is so," said Don Quixote, "but my squire values them at what he has said, on account of the person who gave them to me."

Roque Guinart ordered them given back to him at once; and, ordering his men to draw up in line, he ordered all the clothes, jewels and money to be brought there in front of him, and everything they had robbed since the last division. And, making the reckoning quickly, giving back what could not be divided and turning it into money, he divided it out among the whole company with such fairness and prudence that he did not go a point beyond distributive justice or defraud it in anything.

This done, at which they were all left contented, satisfied and paid, Roque said to Don Quixote:

"If this exactness were not kept with these men, it would be impossible to live with them."

To which Sancho said:

"By what I have seen here, justice is such a good thing that it must be used even among thieves themselves."

One of the squires heard him and raised the butt of a harquebus, with which he would undoubtedly have split Sancho's head open if Roque Guinart had not shouted to him to stop. Sancho was thunderstruck, and resolved not to unstitch his lips as long as he was among those people.

At this there came up one, or some, of those squires who were posted as sentries along the roads to see the people who came along them and give notice to their chief of what was afoot, and this one said:

"Sir, not far from here, along the road that goes to Barcelona, there comes a great troop of people."

To which Roque answered:

"Have you seen whether they are of those who are looking for us, or of those we are looking for?"

"No, but of those we are looking for," answered the squire.

"Then all of you go out," replied Roque, "and bring them here to me at once, and let none of them escape you."

They did so; and, Don Quixote, Sancho and Roque being left alone, they waited to see what the squires would bring. And in the meantime Roque said to Don Quixote:

"Our way of life must seem a new one to Señor Don Quixote — new adventures, new events, and all of them dangerous; and I do not wonder that it should seem so, because I really confess to you that there is no way of living more restless or more full of alarms than ours. What has put me into it is a certain desire for revenge, which has the power to trouble the quietest hearts; I am by nature compassionate and well-meaning, but, as I have said, the wanting to avenge myself for a wrong that was done me throws all my good inclinations so completely to the ground that I persevere in this state in spite and in defiance of what I understand. And, as one abyss calls to another and one sin to another sin, the revenges have linked themselves together in such a way that I take on not only my own but other people's. But God is pleased that, although I see myself in the middle of the labyrinth of my confusions, I do not lose the hope of coming out of it into a safe harbour."

Don Quixote was astonished at hearing Roque speak such good and orderly words, because he had thought that among men of trades like robbing, killing and holding up travellers there could be nobody with good judgement; and he answered him:

"Señor Roque, the beginning of health lies in knowing the disease and in the sick man's being willing to take the medicines the doctor orders him. Your worship is sick; you know your ailment; and heaven — or God, better said, who is our doctor — will apply medicines to you that will heal you, which generally heal little by little and not suddenly and by a miracle. And besides, judicious sinners are nearer to mending than simple ones; and, since your worship has shown your prudence in your words, there is nothing for it but to have good courage and hope for an improvement in the disease of your conscience. And if your worship wants to save the journey and set yourself easily on the road to your salvation, come with me, and I shall teach you to be a knight errant, in which one goes through so many labours and misfortunes that, taking them as penance, they will put you in heaven in two shakes."

Roque laughed at Don Quixote's advice; and, changing the subject, he told him the tragic business of Claudia Jerónima, which grieved Sancho extremely, for the girl's beauty, dash and spirit had not seemed bad to him.

At this the squires of the raid came up, bringing with them two gentlemen on horseback and two pilgrims on foot, and a coach of women with about six servants who were attending them on foot and on horseback, along with two mule-boys the gentlemen had brought. The squires took them into their midst, conquered and conquerors keeping a great silence, waiting for the great Roque Guinart to speak. And he asked the gentlemen who they were and where they were going and how much money they were carrying. One of them answered him:

"Sir, we are two captains of Spanish infantry; we have our companies in Naples and we are going to embark on four galleys which they say are at Barcelona with orders to cross to Sicily. We are carrying about two or three hundred crowns, with which, to our mind, we go rich and contented, since the ordinary tightness of soldiers does not allow greater treasures."

Roque asked the pilgrims the same as the captains; he was answered that they were going to embark to cross to Rome, and that between the two of them they might have about sixty reals. He also wanted to know who was going in the coach and where, and the money they were carrying; and one of the men on horseback said:

"My lady Doña Guiomar de Quiñones, wife of the regent of the Vicarial Court of Naples, with a small daughter, a maid and a duenna, are the ones going in the coach; six servants attend her, and the money is six hundred crowns."

"So that," said Roque Guinart, "we now have here nine hundred crowns and sixty reals; my soldiers must be about sixty; let it be worked out how much falls to each one, because I am a bad accountant."

The highwaymen, hearing this said, raised their voices, saying:

"Long live Roque Guinart for many years, in spite of the lladres — the thieves — who seek his ruin!"

The captains showed themselves distressed, the lady regent's wife was saddened, and the pilgrims were not at all pleased at seeing their goods confiscated. Roque kept them like this in suspense for a while; but he did not want their sadness to go any further, for by now it could be seen from a harquebus-shot away; and, turning to the captains, he said:

"Your worships, gentlemen captains, be so good as to lend me sixty crowns out of courtesy, and the lady regent's wife eighty, to content this squadron that goes with me — for the abbot dines on what he sings — and then you may go your way freely and unhindered with a safe conduct I shall give you, so that if you come across other squadrons of mine that I have divided about these parts, they may do you no harm; for it is not my intention to wrong soldiers or any woman, especially women of quality."

Infinite and well-spoken were the words with which the captains thanked Roque for his courtesy and generosity — for such they held it — in leaving them their own money. The lady Doña Guiomar de Quiñones wanted to throw herself out of the coach to kiss the great Roque's feet and hands, but he would not consent to it on any account; on the contrary, he asked her pardon for the wrong he was doing her, forced to it by the strict obligations of his bad trade. The lady regent's wife ordered a servant of hers to hand over at once the eighty crowns that had been assessed on her, and the captains had already paid out the sixty. The pilgrims were about to hand over all their poor pittance, but Roque told them to keep still; and, turning to his men, he said:

"Of these crowns two fall to each man, and twenty are left over: let ten be given to these pilgrims, and the other ten to this good squire, so that he may speak well of this adventure."

And, being brought writing materials, with which he always went provided, Roque gave them in writing a safe conduct for the chiefs of his squadrons; and, taking his leave of them, he let them go free, astonished at his nobility, his gallant bearing and his strange conduct, and holding him rather for an Alexander the Great than for a known thief. One of the squires said in his mixed Gascon and Catalan:

"This captain of ours is more fit to be a friar than a bandit: if from now on he wants to show himself generous, let him be so with his own property and not with ours."

The unlucky man did not say it so softly that Roque did not hear it; and he, laying hand to his sword, split his head almost in two, saying to him:

"This is how I punish loose-tongued and impudent men."

They were all thunderstruck, and none of them dared to say him a word: so great was the obedience they had for him.

Roque drew aside and wrote a letter to a friend of his in Barcelona, letting him know that the famous Don Quixote of La Mancha was with him — that knight errant of whom so many things were said; and letting him know that he was the most amusing and the most understanding man in the world, and that in four days' time, which would be the day of Saint John the Baptist, he would set him down in the middle of the city beach, armed in all his arms, on Rocinante his horse, and his squire Sancho on an ass; and that he should give notice of this to his friends the Niarros, so that they might amuse themselves with him. He wished that his enemies the Cadells could be deprived of this pleasure, but that this was impossible, because Don Quixote's madnesses and judicious speeches and his squire Sancho Panza's charms could not fail to give general pleasure to all the world.

He sent these letters off by one of his squires who, changing the bandit's dress for a farm labourer's, went into Barcelona and gave it to the man it was going to.
