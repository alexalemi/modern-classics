Chapter 65: Where It Is Made Known Who the Knight of the White Moon Was, Along with Don Gregorio's Release and Other Events

Don Antonio Moreno followed the Knight of the White Moon, and a great many boys followed him too — and even chased him — until they shut him up in an inn inside the city. Don Antonio went in, wanting to know him; a squire came out to receive him and to disarm him; he shut himself up in a room on the ground floor, and Don Antonio with him, whose bread would not bake until he knew who he was. The Knight of the White Moon, seeing, then, that this gentleman would not leave him, said to him:

"I know very well, sir, what you have come for, which is to find out who I am; and, since there is no reason to deny it you, while this servant of mine disarms me I shall tell you, without falling a point short of the truth of the case.

"Know, sir, that they call me the bachelor Sansón Carrasco. I am from the same village as Don Quixote of La Mancha, whose madness and folly move all of us who know him to pity him; and among those who have pitied him most I have been one. And, believing that his health lies in his rest and in his staying in his own country and his own house, I laid a plan to make him stay in it.

"And so, three months ago I came out onto the road as a knight errant, calling myself the Knight of the Mirrors, meaning to fight him and conquer him without doing him any harm, laying it down as a condition of our fight that the beaten man should be at the winner's discretion. And what I meant to ask of him — for I already judged him beaten — was that he should go back to his village and not leave it for a whole year, in which time he might be cured.

"But fortune arranged it otherwise, because he conquered me and knocked me off my horse, and so my plan came to nothing. He went on his way and I came back, beaten, put out and battered by the fall, which was besides a dangerous one. But this did not take away my wish to go back and look for him and conquer him, as has been seen today. And, as he is so exact in keeping the orders of knight-errantry, he will beyond any doubt keep the one I have given him, in fulfilment of his word.

"This, sir, is what is afoot, and I have nothing else at all to tell you. I beg you not to give me away or to tell Don Quixote who I am, so that my good intentions may take effect and a man may recover his judgement who has an excellent one, if only the follies of chivalry would leave him alone."

"Oh, sir," said Don Antonio, "God forgive you the wrong you have done all the world in trying to make the most amusing madman in it sane! Do you not see, sir, that the good that Don Quixote's sanity may do cannot come near the pleasure he gives with his ravings? But I imagine that all the master bachelor's contrivance will not be enough to make a man so utterly mad sane again; and if it were not against charity, I should say: let Don Quixote never get well, because with his health we lose not only his own charms but Sancho Panza his squire's, any one of which could gladden melancholy itself again. For all that, I shall be quiet and shall say nothing to him, to see whether I turn out right in suspecting that the pains taken by Señor Carrasco will have no effect."

Carrasco answered that as things stood the business was at a good point, and that he hoped for a happy outcome from it. And, Don Antonio offering to do whatever else he might order, he took his leave of him; and, having had his arms tied up on a mule, that very instant, on the horse he had gone into the battle with, he left the city that same day and went back to his own country, without anything happening to him that obliges us to tell it in this true history.

Don Antonio told the viceroy everything Carrasco had told him, at which the viceroy took no great pleasure, because in Don Quixote's retirement everybody who had news of his madnesses lost the pleasure they might have had of them.

Six days Don Quixote spent in bed, downcast, sad, thoughtful and in a bad way, his imagination coming and going over the unfortunate business of his defeat. Sancho consoled him, and among other things said to him:

"My dear sir, let your worship lift up your head and be glad if you can, and give thanks to heaven that, since it threw you down on the ground, you did not come out of it with a broken rib. And, since you know that where they give they take, and that there are not always flitches of bacon where there are hooks, snap your fingers at the doctor, for you do not need him to cure you of this illness. Let us go back home and leave off going about looking for adventures in lands and places we do not know.

"And if it is properly considered, I am the greatest loser here, although your worship is the worst used. I, who left with the government the wish to be a governor again, did not leave the wish to be a count — which will never come off if your worship leaves off being a king by leaving the exercise of your chivalry; and so my hopes come to turn into smoke."

"Be quiet, Sancho, since you see that my seclusion and retirement is not to go beyond a year; for then I shall go back to my honourable exercises, and I shall not lack a kingdom to win and an earldom to give you."

"May God hear it," said Sancho, "and sin be deaf; for I have always heard it said that a good hope is worth more than a poor possession."

They were at this when Don Antonio came in, saying with signs of the greatest contentment:

"A reward, Señor Don Quixote! Don Gregorio and the renegade who went for him are on the beach! What am I saying, on the beach? They are already at the viceroy's house, and will be here in a moment."

Don Quixote cheered up somewhat and said:

"Truly I am half inclined to say that I should have been glad if it had all turned out the other way round, because it would have obliged me to cross over into Barbary, where with the strength of my arm I should have given liberty not only to Don Gregorio but to all the Christian captives there are in Barbary. But what am I saying, wretched man? Am I not the one who has been beaten? Am I not the one who has been thrown down? Am I not the one who cannot take up arms for a year? Then what am I promising? What am I boasting of, when the distaff suits me better than the sword?"

"Leave that alone, sir," said Sancho. "Long live the hen, even with her pip; for today it is your turn and tomorrow mine; and in these matters of encounters and knocks there is no taking any measure of them, since the man who falls today can get up tomorrow — unless he wants to stay in bed; I mean unless he lets himself faint away without gathering fresh spirit for fresh quarrels. And let your worship get up now to receive Don Gregorio, for it seems to me that the people are in a stir and he must be in the house by now."

And it was the truth; because, Don Gregorio and the renegade having already given the viceroy an account of their going and coming, Don Gregorio, eager to see Ana Félix, came with the renegade to Don Antonio's house. And although Don Gregorio, when they brought him out of Algiers, was in woman's clothes, in the boat he exchanged them for those of a captive who came out with him; but in whatever he had come, he would have shown himself a person to be coveted and served and valued, because he was extremely beautiful, and his age to look at him was seventeen or eighteen.

Ricote and his daughter came out to receive him: the father in tears and the daughter with modesty. They did not embrace one another, because where there is much love there is generally not much freedom. The two beauties of Don Gregorio and Ana Félix together particularly amazed all those who were present. Silence was what spoke there for the two lovers, and their eyes were the tongues that revealed their glad and modest thoughts.

The renegade told the contrivance and the means he had used to get Don Gregorio out; Don Gregorio told the dangers and the straits he had been in with the women he had been left with — not in a long speech but in a few words, in which he showed that his judgement went ahead of his years. Finally Ricote paid and satisfied both the renegade and those who had pulled at the oar generously. The renegade was reincorporated and reconciled with the Church, and from a rotten limb came back clean and sound with penance and repentance.

Two days later the viceroy discussed with Don Antonio what way they might have of getting Ana Félix and her father allowed to stay in Spain, since it seemed to them no drawback at all that so Christian a daughter and so apparently well-meaning a father should stay in it. Don Antonio offered to come to the court to negotiate it, where he had of necessity to come on other business, giving it to be understood that there, by means of favour and gifts, a great many difficult things are brought off.

"There is no hoping," said Ricote, who was present at this talk, "in favours or in gifts, because with the great Don Bernardino de Velasco, Count of Salazar, to whom His Majesty gave charge of our expulsion, entreaties are worth nothing, nor promises, nor gifts, nor pity. Because although it is true that he mixes mercy with justice, since he sees that the whole body of our nation is contaminated and rotten, he uses on it the cautery that burns rather than the ointment that softens.

"And so, with prudence, with shrewdness, with diligence and with the fears he sets up, he has carried on his strong shoulders the weight of this great machine to its due execution, without our contrivances, stratagems, entreaties and frauds having been able to dazzle his eyes of Argus, which he keeps constantly on the alert so that none of our people may stay behind or be hidden — like a hidden root that with time may afterwards come to sprout and put out poisonous fruits in Spain, now clean and now rid of the fears our numbers held it in. A heroic resolution of the great Philip the Third, and unheard-of prudence in having entrusted it to that same Don Bernardino de Velasco!"

"Come what may, I shall take what steps I can once I am there, and let heaven do what it is best pleased to do," said Don Antonio. "Don Gregorio shall go with me to console the grief his parents must be feeling for his absence; Ana Félix shall stay with my wife in my house, or in a convent; and I know that the lord viceroy will be glad for good Ricote to stay in his, until we see how I get on."

The viceroy consented to everything proposed; but Don Gregorio, learning what was afoot, said that on no account could he or would he leave Doña Ana Félix. But, meaning to see his parents and to lay a plan for coming back for her, he agreed to the settled arrangement. Ana Félix stayed with Don Antonio's wife, and Ricote in the viceroy's house.

The day of Don Antonio's departure came, and that of Don Quixote and Sancho, which was two days later — for the fall did not let him take the road any sooner. There were tears, there were sighs, faintings and sobs when Don Gregorio took his leave of Ana Félix. Ricote offered Don Gregorio a thousand crowns if he wanted them; but he took none, only five that Don Antonio lent him, promising to repay them at court. With this the two of them set off, and Don Quixote and Sancho afterwards, as has been said: Don Quixote unarmed and in travelling dress, Sancho on foot, because the grey was loaded with the arms.
