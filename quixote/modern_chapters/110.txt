Chapter 51: Of the Progress of Sancho Panza's Government, with Other Events as Good as They Are Entertaining

The day dawned that followed the night of the governor's rounds, which the head steward spent without sleeping, his mind taken up with the face, spirit and beauty of the disguised maiden; and the steward spent what was left of the night in writing to his master and mistress about what Sancho Panza was doing and saying, as amazed by his deeds as by his sayings — because his words and his actions went about mixed together, with glimmerings of the judicious and of the foolish.

The lord governor got up at last; and, by Doctor Pedro Recio's orders, they made him breakfast on a little preserve and four swallows of cold water — a thing Sancho would have traded for a piece of bread and a bunch of grapes; but, seeing that this was more force than free will, he put up with it, with a good deal of grief to his soul and distress to his stomach, Pedro Recio making him believe that scanty and delicate food sharpened the wits, which was what best suited persons set up in commands and in grave offices, where they have to make use not so much of their bodily strength as of the strength of their understanding.

With this sophistry Sancho suffered hunger, and to such a degree that in secret he cursed the government and even the man who had given it him. But, with his hunger and with his preserve, he set himself to give judgement that day; and the first thing that offered itself was a question a stranger put to him, with the steward and the rest of his acolytes present at the whole of it, which was:

"Sir, a great river divided two districts of one and the same lordship — and let your worship be attentive, because the case is important and rather difficult. I say, then, that over this river there was a bridge, and at the end of it a gallows and a kind of court house, in which there were ordinarily four judges who judged by the law laid down by the lord of the river, of the bridge and of the lordship, which was in this form: 'If anybody crosses this bridge from one side to the other, he must first swear where he is going and what he is going for; and if he swears the truth, let them let him pass; and if he tells a lie, let him die for it, hanged on the gallows that stands there, without any remission at all.'

"This law being known, and the severity of its condition, a great many people crossed; and then, from what they swore, it could be seen that they were telling the truth, and the judges let them pass freely. It happened, then, that, taking a man's oath, he swore and said that by the oath he was making, he was going to die on that gallows over there, and for nothing else. The judges stuck at the oath and said: 'If we let this man pass freely, he lied in his oath, and according to the law he must die; and if we hang him, he swore that he was going to die on that gallows, and, having sworn the truth, by the same law he must go free.'

"It is asked of your worship, my lord governor, what the judges are to do with this man; for even now they are still in doubt and suspense. And, having had news of your worship's sharp and lofty understanding, they sent me to beg your worship on their behalf to give your opinion in so intricate and doubtful a case."

To which Sancho answered:

"Certainly those gentlemen the judges who send you to me could have spared themselves the trouble, because I am a man who has more of the dull ox in him than of the sharp fellow. But for all that, repeat the business to me once more so that I understand it: perhaps I might hit the mark."

The questioner went over what he had said before once and again, and Sancho said:

"To my mind I can set this business out in two shakes, and it is this: the man swears he is going to die on the gallows; and if he dies on it, he swore the truth, and by the law laid down he deserves to go free and to cross the bridge; and if they do not hang him, he swore a lie, and by the same law he deserves to be hanged."

"It is just as the lord governor says," said the messenger; "and as to the completeness and understanding of the case, there is nothing more to ask or to doubt."

"I say, then, now," replied Sancho, "that of this man they should let the part that swore the truth cross, and hang the part that told a lie; and in this way the condition of the crossing will be carried out to the letter."

"Well, my lord governor," replied the questioner, "it will be necessary for this man to be divided into parts, the lying part and the truthful part; and if he is divided, he must of necessity die, and so nothing at all is achieved of what the law requires, and it is expressly necessary that the law should be carried out."

"Come here, my good man," answered Sancho. "This traveller you speak of — either I am a blockhead, or he has the same reason for dying as for living and crossing the bridge; because if the truth saves him, the lie condemns him equally. And this being so, as it is, I am of the opinion that you should tell those gentlemen who sent you to me that, since the reasons for condemning him and for absolving him are evenly balanced, they should let him cross freely; for doing good is always more praised than doing harm. And I would give you this signed with my name, if I knew how to sign.

"And in this case I have not spoken out of my own head, but there came into my memory one precept, among a great many others that my master Don Quixote gave me the night before I came to be governor of this isle: which was that when justice was in doubt I should lean and take refuge in mercy. And God has willed that I should remember it now, since it comes into this case as though it were cast in the mould."

"That is so," answered the steward, "and I hold for myself that Lycurgus himself, who gave the Spartans their laws, could not have given a better sentence than the one the great Panza has given. And let the morning's audience end with this, and I shall give orders for the lord governor to dine very much to his taste."

"That is what I ask, and fair play," said Sancho. "Give me something to eat, and let cases and doubts rain down on me, and I shall polish them off in the air."

The steward kept his word, thinking it would be a matter of conscience to starve so judicious a governor to death — and the more so as he meant to make an end of him that very night by playing him the last trick he had a commission to play.

It happened, then, that, having dined that day against the rules and aphorisms of the doctor of Get-You-Gone, as the cloth was being taken up a courier came in with a letter from Don Quixote for the governor. Sancho ordered the secretary to read it to himself, and, if there were nothing in it worth keeping secret, to read it out loud. The secretary did so; and, going over it first, he said:

"It may well be read out loud, for what Señor Don Quixote writes to your worship deserves to be stamped and written in letters of gold; and it goes like this:

Letter from Don Quixote of La Mancha to Sancho Panza, Governor of the Isle of Barataria

When I was expecting to hear news of your carelessnesses and impertinences, Sancho my friend, I heard news of your judicious sayings; for which I gave particular thanks to heaven, which knows how to raise the poor out of the dunghill and to make wise men out of fools. They tell me that you govern as if you were a man, and that you are a man as if you were a beast, so humble is the way you conduct yourself.

And I want you to note, Sancho, that many times it is fitting and necessary, for the authority of the office, to go against the humility of the heart; because the good dress of a person set in grave charges must be according to what those charges require and not according to the measure of what his humble condition inclines him to. Dress well, for a stick well got up does not look like a stick. I do not say that you should wear trinkets and finery, or that, being a judge, you should dress like a soldier, but that you should adorn yourself with the habit your office requires, so long as it is clean and well arranged.

To win the good will of the people you govern, among other things you must do two: one, be well-mannered with everybody, although I have already told you this before; and the other, take care that there is plenty of food, for there is nothing that wears at the hearts of the poor more than hunger and scarcity.

Do not make many decrees; and if you make them, try to make them good ones, and above all see that they are kept and carried out — for decrees that are not kept are the same as if they had never been made; on the contrary, they give it to be understood that the prince who had the judgement and the authority to make them had not the courage to make them kept. And laws that frighten and are not enforced come to be like the log, king of the frogs: at first it terrified them, and in time they thought nothing of it and climbed up on top of it.

Be a father to the virtues and a stepfather to the vices. Do not always be severe, nor always mild, and choose the middle way between these two extremes, for in that lies the point of good judgement.

Visit the prisons, the butchers' shops and the markets, for the presence of the governor in such places is of great importance: he comforts the prisoners, who are hoping for a quick despatch of their cases; he is a bogey to the butchers, who for that occasion make their weights even; and he is a scarecrow to the market women, for the same reason.

Do not show yourself — even if by any chance you are so, which I do not believe — greedy, a womaniser or a glutton; because when the people and those who deal with you know your settled inclination, they will lay their batteries against you there until they bring you down into the depths of ruin.

Look and look again, go over and over again the advice and the precepts I gave you in writing before you set out from here to your government, and you will see how you find in them, if you keep them, a help in hand to carry you through the labours and difficulties that come to governors at every step.

Write to your lord and lady and show yourself grateful to them; for ingratitude is the daughter of pride and one of the greatest sins there is, and the person who is grateful to those who have done him good gives an indication that he will also be grateful to God, who has done him so many good things and goes on doing them.

My lady the duchess sent a special messenger with your suit and another present to your wife Teresa Panza; we are expecting an answer at any moment.

I have been a little out of sorts from a certain cat-mauling that happened to me not very much to the good of my nose; but it was nothing, for if there are enchanters who ill-treat me, there are also enchanters who defend me.

Let me know whether the steward who is with you had anything to do with the doings of the Trifaldi, as you suspected; and you shall keep giving me notice of everything that happens to you, since the road is so short. What is more, I mean to leave soon this idle life I am in, since I was not born for it.

A business has come up for me that I believe will put me out of favour with these nobles; but although it matters a great deal to me, it does not matter to me at all, since in the end I must fulfil my profession sooner than their pleasure, according to what they say: amicus Plato, sed magis amica veritas. I say this Latin to you because I give myself to understand that, since you have been a governor, you will have learnt it. And God be with you, and may He keep you from anybody's pitying you.

Your friend,

Don Quixote of La Mancha.

Sancho listened to the letter with great attention, and it was praised and held for judicious by those who heard it. And then Sancho got up from the table and, calling the secretary, shut himself up with him in his chamber; and, without putting it off any longer, he decided to answer his master Don Quixote at once, and told the secretary to write down what he said to him without adding or taking away anything at all. And so he did; and the letter of reply was of the following tenor:

Letter from Sancho Panza to Don Quixote of La Mancha

The business of my affairs is so great that I have no time to scratch my head or even to cut my nails; and so I wear them so long that God help me. I say this, my dear lord of my soul, so that your worship should not be surprised that until now I have given no notice of how I am faring, well or badly, in this government, in which I am hungrier than when the two of us were going about through the woods and the wastelands.

The duke my lord wrote to me the other day, letting me know that certain spies had come into this isle to kill me; and up to now I have not discovered any but a certain doctor who is here on a salary to kill all the governors who come to this place. He is called Doctor Pedro Recio and he is a native of Get-You-Gone: so your worship may see what a name it is, for me not to be afraid of dying at his hands!

This doctor says of himself that he does not cure diseases when they are there but prevents them, so that they may not come; and the medicines he uses are diet and more diet, until he has the person down to bare bones — as though thinness were not a greater evil than fever. In short, he is killing me with hunger and I am dying of vexation; for when I thought I was coming to this government to eat hot and drink cold and rest my body between fine linen sheets on feather mattresses, I have come to do penance like a hermit — and, since I do not do it of my own will, I think that in the end the devil will carry me off.

Up to now I have not touched a fee or taken a bribe, and I cannot think what this means; because here they have told me that the governors who usually come to this isle, before coming into it, have been either given or lent a great deal of money by the townspeople, and that this is the ordinary custom with the rest who go to governments, not only in this one.

Last night, going the rounds, I came across a very beautiful maiden in man's clothes and a brother of hers in woman's; my head steward fell in love with the girl and picked her out in his imagination for his wife, as he has said, and I picked out the young man for my son-in-law. Today the two of us shall put our ideas to their father, who is a certain Diego de la Llana, a gentleman and as much of an old Christian as you please.

I visit the markets, as your worship advises me; and yesterday I found a stallholder selling new hazelnuts, and I established that she had mixed a bushel of new hazelnuts with another of old, empty and rotten ones. I gave the whole lot to the charity-school children, who would know how to tell them apart, and I sentenced her not to come into the market for a fortnight. They have told me that I did it valiantly; what I can tell your worship is that it is common talk in this town that there are no worse people than the market women, because they are all shameless, soulless and brazen — and so I believe, from the ones I have seen in other towns.

That my lady the duchess should have written to my wife Teresa Panza and sent her the present your worship speaks of, I am very much satisfied with; and I shall try to show myself grateful in due time. Let your worship kiss her hands on my behalf and say that I say she has not thrown it into a torn sack, as she shall see by the deed.

I should not like your worship to have any falling-out or unpleasantness with these lord and lady of mine; because if your worship gets angry with them, it is plain that it must redound to my harm — and it will not be right that, when I am given advice to be grateful, your worship should not be so with people who have done him so many kindnesses and by whom he has been treated with such indulgence in their castle.

That business of the cat-mauling I do not understand, but I imagine it must be one of the bad turns the bad enchanters generally play on your worship; I shall know when we see each other.

I should like to send your worship something, but I do not know what to send, unless it is some syringe tubes for use with bladders, which they make very neatly in this isle; although if the office lasts me I shall find something to send by hook or by crook.

If my wife Teresa Panza writes to me, let your worship pay the postage and send me the letter, for I have the greatest longing to know the state of my house, my wife and my children. And with this, God deliver your worship from ill-meaning enchanters, and bring me safely and peacefully out of this government — which I doubt, for I think I shall leave it along with my life, the way Doctor Pedro Recio treats me.

Your worship's servant,

Sancho Panza, the Governor.

The secretary closed the letter and sent the courier off at once. And the people who were playing tricks on Sancho, coming together, arranged among themselves how to get him out of the government. And Sancho spent that afternoon in making some ordinances touching the good government of what he imagined to be an isle; and he ordered that there should be no hoarders of provisions in the commonwealth, and that people might bring wine into it from wherever they liked, on condition that they declared the place it came from, so that a price could be set on it according to its value, its goodness and its reputation; and that anybody who watered it or changed its name should lose his life for it.

He brought down the price of all footwear, chiefly of shoes, since it seemed to him that it was running exorbitantly high. He set a limit to servants' wages, which were travelling at a loose rein along the road of self-interest. He laid the gravest penalties on those who sang lascivious and improper songs, either by night or by day. He ordered that no blind man should sing of a miracle in ballads unless he brought a genuine certificate that it was true, since it seemed to him that most of those the blind men sing are made up, to the damage of the true ones.

He made and created a constable of the poor, not to persecute them but to examine them as to whether they really were poor; because in the shadow of a pretended crippling and a false sore there go about thieving arms and drunken good health. In short, he ordained things so good that to this day they are kept in that place, and are named The Constitutions of the Great Governor Sancho Panza.
