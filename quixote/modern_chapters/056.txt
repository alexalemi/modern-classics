Chapter 51: Which Treats of What the Goatherd Told All Those Who Were Carrying Don Quixote Away

"Three leagues from this valley there is a village which, although small, is one of the richest anywhere in this district; and in it there was a very honourable farmer — so much so that, although being honoured goes with being rich, he was more honoured for the virtue he had than for the wealth he possessed.

"But what made him happiest, as he used to say, was having a daughter of such extraordinary beauty, rare good sense, charm and virtue that anybody who knew her and looked at her was astonished to see the extraordinary qualities heaven and nature had enriched her with. As a little girl she was beautiful, and she went on growing in beauty, and at the age of sixteen she was most beautiful of all.

"The fame of her beauty began to spread through all the villages round about — and why do I say through the villages round about only, when it spread to distant cities and even got into the halls of kings and into the ears of every kind of person, who came from all quarters to see her as a rare thing or as an image that works miracles? Her father guarded her, and she guarded herself; for there are no padlocks, guards or locks that guard a young girl better than those of her own reserve.

"The father's wealth and the daughter's beauty moved a great many people, both from the village and from outside it, to ask for her as a wife. But he, as the man whose business it was to dispose of so rich a jewel, went about bewildered, unable to decide which of the endless number who pestered him he would hand her to. And among the many who had that good wish I was one, and I was given many great hopes of success by knowing that her father knew who I was: that I was a native of the same village, clean in blood, in the flower of my age, very rich in property and no less finished in my wits.

"With all these same qualities another man of the same village asked for her too, which was the reason for suspending and putting into the balance her father's inclination — since it seemed to him that his daughter would be well bestowed on either of us. And to get out of this difficulty, he decided to tell Leandra about it, for that is the name of the rich girl who has put me in this misery, noting that since the two of us were equal, it was right to leave the choosing to his beloved daughter's own liking — a thing worth imitating by all fathers who want to settle their children in life. I do not say they should let them choose in low and wicked matters, but that they should put good ones before them, and of the good ones let them choose as they like.

"I do not know what Leandra's liking was; I only know that her father put us both off with his daughter's youth and with general words, which neither bound him nor released us either. My rival is called Anselmo and I Eugenio, so that you may have the names of the people this tragedy contains — whose ending is still hanging in the balance, though it can easily be understood that it will be a disastrous one.

"At this time there came to our village a certain Vicente de la Rosa, the son of a poor farmer of the same place; which Vicente came from Italy and from various other parts, where he had been a soldier. A captain who happened to pass through with his company had taken him away from our village when he was a boy of about twelve, and the lad came back twelve years later dressed as a soldier, painted with a thousand colours, covered with a thousand glass trinkets and fine steel chains. Today he put on one piece of finery and tomorrow another, but all of them flimsy, painted, of little weight and less substance.

"The country people, who are naturally spiteful, and who when idleness gives them the chance are spite itself, noticed it and counted his finery and ornaments point by point, and found that the outfits were three, of different colours, with their garters and stockings; but he made so many combinations and inventions out of them that if they had not been counted, there would have been people ready to swear he had shown off more than ten changes of clothing and more than twenty sets of plumes. And do not let what I am telling you about the clothes seem impertinent and excessive, because they make a good part of this story.

"He used to sit on a stone bench under a great poplar that stands in our square, and there he kept us all open-mouthed, hanging on the exploits he went on telling us about. There was no country in the whole globe he had not seen, nor any battle he had not been in. He had killed more Moors than Morocco and Tunis hold, and had fought more single combats — according to him — than Gante and Luna, Diego García de Paredes and a thousand others he named; and he had come out of all of them victorious, without their having shed a single drop of his blood.

"On the other hand, he showed the marks of wounds which, although they could not be made out, he gave us to understand were musket shots got in different skirmishes and actions. And finally, with unheard-of arrogance, he addressed his equals — and the very men who knew him — with the familiar 'you', and said that his father was his own arm, his family his own deeds, and that as a soldier he owed nothing even to the king himself.

"Added to these arrogances was his being a bit of a musician and playing a guitar with a strumming style, so that some people said he made it talk. But his gifts did not stop there, for he also had the gift of poetry; and so out of every trifle that happened in the village he composed a ballad a league and a half long.

"This soldier, then, that I have painted here — this Vicente de la Rosa, this bravo, this dandy, this musician, this poet — was seen and looked at many times by Leandra from a window of her house that looked onto the square. The tinsel of his showy clothes made her fall in love; his ballads enchanted her, for of every one he composed he handed round twenty copies; the exploits he had recounted of himself came to her ears; and finally — as the devil must have arranged it so — she came to fall in love with him before any notion of courting her had been born in him.

"And as in matters of love there is none that is more easily brought off than the one that has the lady's own wish on its side, Leandra and Vicente easily came to an understanding; and before any of her many suitors had realised what she wanted, she had already got it — having left the house of her dear and beloved father, for a mother she has none, and gone away from the village with the soldier, who came out of this enterprise with more triumph than out of all the many he laid claim to.

"The event astonished the whole village, and everybody who heard of it. I was left taken aback, Anselmo appalled, her father grief-stricken, her relatives shamed, the law busy, the officers of the Brotherhood alert. The roads were watched, the woods were searched and everything else there was; and at the end of three days they found the capricious Leandra in a cave in a mountain, stripped to her shift, without a great deal of money and the most precious jewels she had taken from her home.

"They brought her back into her injured father's presence; they asked her about her misfortune; she confessed without any pressing that Vicente de la Rosa had deceived her, and under his word to be her husband had persuaded her to leave her father's house; that he would take her to the richest and most delightful city there was in the whole universe, which was Naples; and that she, badly advised and worse deceived, had believed him; and, robbing her father, had given herself up to him the very night she disappeared; and that he had taken her to a rough mountain and shut her up in that cave where they had found her.

"She also told how the soldier, without taking her honour, had robbed her of everything she had, and had left her in that cave and gone away — an event that astonished everybody all over again.

"It was hard for us to believe the young man's continence, but she maintained it so earnestly that it was enough to comfort the disconsolate father, who made no account of the wealth they had taken from him, since they had left him his daughter with the jewel which, once lost, leaves no hope of ever being recovered again.

"The same day Leandra reappeared, her father made her disappear from our eyes and took her away to shut her up in a convent in a town near here, hoping that time would wear away some part of the bad opinion his daughter had put herself in. Leandra's few years served as an excuse for her fault, at least with those who had no interest in her being bad or good; but those who knew her good sense and great understanding did not put her sin down to ignorance, but to her forwardness and to the natural inclination of women, which for the most part is generally headstrong and ill-ordered.

"Leandra being shut up, Anselmo's eyes were left blind — or at least with nothing to look at that gave him any pleasure; and mine in darkness, with no light to guide them to anything enjoyable. With Leandra's absence our sadness grew and our patience shrank; we cursed the soldier's finery and abominated Leandra's father's want of caution.

"Finally Anselmo and I agreed to leave the village and come to this valley, where he, pasturing a great quantity of sheep of his own and I a numerous flock of goats, also mine, pass our lives among the trees, giving vent to our passions, either singing together the praises or the reproaches of the beautiful Leandra, or sighing alone and communicating our complaints to the sky by ourselves.

"In imitation of us, a great many others of Leandra's suitors have come to these rough mountains, following the same occupation as ours; and there are so many of them that it seems this place has been turned into pastoral Arcadia, so full is it of shepherds and folds, and there is no part of it where the name of the beautiful Leandra is not heard. This man curses her and calls her capricious, changeable and immodest; that one condemns her as easy and light; another absolves her and forgives her; another arraigns her and reproaches her; one celebrates her beauty, another disowns her character; and in the end they all dishonour her and they all adore her.

"And the madness of them all goes so far that there are men who complain of her disdain without ever having spoken to her, and even men who lament and feel the raging disease of jealousy, which she never gave anybody cause for — because, as I have said already, her sin was known before her wish was. There is no hollow in a rock, no bank of a stream, no shade of a tree that is not occupied by some shepherd telling his misfortunes to the air. Echo repeats the name of Leandra wherever it can be formed: Leandra the mountains ring with, Leandra the streams murmur, and Leandra keeps us all in suspense and enchanted, hoping without hope and fearing without knowing what we fear.

"Among all these deranged men, the one who shows himself to have the least and the most judgement is my rival Anselmo, who, having so many other things to complain of, complains only of absence; and to the sound of a rebeck, which he plays admirably, he makes his complaint in singing, with verses that show his good understanding. I follow another and easier road, and to my mind the sounder one, which is to speak ill of the lightness of women, of their inconstancy, of their double dealing, of their dead promises, of their broken faith and, in the end, of the little judgement they have in knowing where to place their thoughts and intentions.

"And this, gentlemen, was the reason for the words and arguments I made to this goat when I got here; for because she is female I think little of her, although she is the best in my whole fold. This is the story I promised to tell you; and if I have been long-winded in telling it, I shall not be short in serving you: near here I have my hut, and in it I have fresh milk and very tasty cheese, along with various ripe fruits no less agreeable to the sight than to the taste."
