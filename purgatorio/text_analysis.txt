TEXT ANALYSIS — Dante's Purgatorio
Read this AND inferno/text_analysis.txt before translating. The
consistency ledger is running_notes.txt.


WHAT THIS EDITION IS
---------------------------------------------------------------------
The second cantica, complete: 33 cantos, 4,755 lines, from the Italian
(Gutenberg #998, the Petrocchi text), with Longfellow's 1867 English as
a per-canto crib under reference/ — the ovid/ and de-officiis/ pattern.
USE THE CRIB TO CHECK A LINE, NEVER TO BORROW A PHRASE. Longfellow
keeps Italian word order in English and scores 28.7 archaisms per 1,000
words; the whole point of this edition is not to sound like him, and
check.py's archaism sweep exists to catch the drift.


THE FORM IS THE INFERNO'S AND DOES NOT CHANGE
---------------------------------------------------------------------
  - VERSE STAYS VERSE, every line its own line, tab-indented.
  - TERCETS STAY TERCETS: same number, same order, three lines each and
    one line closing every canto. check.py compares the shape exactly.
  - THE LINE COUNT PER CANTO IS EXACT (136, 133, 145, 139, ...), and
    the total is 4,755 in 33 cantos.
  - NO RHYME and no imposed metre. Terza rima cannot cross into English
    without padding, and padding is what this poem must not suffer.
  - ENJAMBMENT ACROSS A TERCET BOUNDARY IS DANTE'S; keep it where he
    has it and do not tidy the sentence back inside its tercet.
  - ONE LINE OF ENGLISH PER LINE OF ITALIAN in sense, not in syllables.


HOW THIS CANTICA DIFFERS, AND IT IS NOT A SMALL DIFFERENCE
---------------------------------------------------------------------
The Inferno is a place where nothing can change. The Purgatorio is
about people who are changing, and that is a different book to render.

  1 EVERYONE HERE IS SAVED AND KNOWS IT. The suffering is real and
    severe — fire, weights, blindness, starvation — and it is entered
    willingly and left behind. Nobody complains and nobody argues his
    sentence. That is the tone to hold: pain without despair. Do not
    import the Inferno's bitterness, and do not sweeten the pain
    either.

  2 TIME EXISTS. This is the only cantica with a sunrise, a noon, three
    nights and three dawn dreams; the climb stops when the light goes.
    Dante marks the hour constantly, often by where the sun stands.
    KEEP EVERY TIME-MARKER; they are the spine of the narrative, not
    ornament, and they are why the reader feels the mountain being
    climbed rather than described.

  3 IT IS THE MOST HUMAN OF THE THREE. The dead here embrace, apologise,
    tease, remember meals and songs, and ask for prayers. Casella sings
    a love song of Dante's own and everybody stands still to listen;
    Belacqua sits behind a rock making fun of him; Forese jokes about
    his own thinness. That warmth is load-bearing and must survive.

  4 IT IS A POEM ABOUT ART, and about the vanity of being good at it.
    Oderisi on fame in canto XI, Statius on being converted by reading
    Virgil in XXI-XXII, Bonagiunta naming the "sweet new style" in XXIV,
    Guinizelli and Arnaut Daniel in XXVI: Dante keeps putting poets in
    front of the reader and asking what any of it is worth. These
    passages are where the writing is most careful and they repay the
    most work.

  5 THE GREAT LOSS IS VIRGIL'S DEPARTURE. He crowns and mitres Dante
    over himself in XXVII, and then in XXX he is simply gone, named
    three times in three lines. Play it exactly as written: Dante does
    not say goodbye and Virgil never speaks again. Nothing may be added
    to help the reader feel it.


LITURGY, LATIN AND SONG
---------------------------------------------------------------------
The mountain sings almost continuously — psalms at the shore, "Salve
Regina" in the valley, "Te lucis ante" at nightfall, a Beatitude on
every terrace, "Summae Deus clementiae" in the fire, "Benedictus qui
venis" at Beatrice's arrival.

  - LATIN STAYS LATIN, exactly as in the Inferno. It is meant to be
    heard as Latin, by souls who are singing it; translating it would
    turn a liturgy into a sentence.
  - Where Dante himself translates or paraphrases a Latin line into
    Italian in the next tercet, so does this edition.
  - "In exitu Israel de Aegypto", "Miserere", "Asperges me", "Deus,
    venerunt gentes", "Beati pauperes spiritu", "Labia mea, Domine",
    "Virum non cognosco", "Manibus, oh, date lilia plenis": all kept.

CANTO XXVI ENDS IN PROVENÇAL, AND IT IS THE ONE PLACE WHERE THE LATIN
RULE IS NOT FOLLOWED. Arnaut Daniel answers in his own language, eight
lines of it, and they are Dante's lines in Dante's tercets. They are
rendered in ENGLISH all the same, and the reason is the burke rule:
an untranslated block is a hole in the poem rather than a piece of
scholarship, and there is no room to gloss it, because the tercet
count and the line count are fixed. Dante's own readers could read
Provençal — it was the literary language of love poetry in Italy — so
keeping it would reproduce the surface and destroy the effect, which
is Arnaut speaking and being understood. What carries the fact of the
other language instead is the line that introduces him: "liberamente"
becomes "freely, in his own tongue", which tells the reader exactly
what happened and costs no line. Logged in running_notes.txt.


VOICE
---------------------------------------------------------------------
As the Inferno: concrete, fast, physical, not solemn. THE hume RULE
GOVERNS HARD — the famous lines are famous for being plain, and a line
that is already clear is left alone.

What changes is the light. The Purgatorio is full of colour and
weather: the sapphire of the first dawn, the trembling of the sea at
sunrise, shadow on grass, smoke thick as night, snow on the Apennines,
the last sunlight going out on the seventh terrace. Do not generalise
these into "beauty"; Dante names the actual colour and the actual hour,
and so does this edition.

Dante himself is lighter here — curious, sociable, embarrassed when
caught being vain, and repeatedly told to hurry up. Virgil is warmer
and more often uncertain, because he does not know this mountain and
says so. The angels are brisk and kind. Cato is severe. Beatrice, when
she comes, is merciless for a hundred lines and then not.

THE ADDRESSES TO THE READER (there are more here than in the other two
cantiche) are direct second-person and stay that way: "Reader, if God
ever lets you take the fruit of your reading..."


NAMES, REFERENCES, NO NOTES
---------------------------------------------------------------------
  - FAMILIAR ENGLISH FORMS where they exist: Virgil, Cato, Beatrice,
    Statius, Florence, Rome. Italian forms for Italians: Manfredi,
    Belacqua, Sordello, Oderisi, Forese, Bonagiunta, Piccarda.
  - NO FOOTNOTES ON THE PAGE, as in the Inferno. Where a reference is
    genuinely unfollowable, let the line carry the smallest possible
    identification ("Manfred, King of Sicily") rather than a note. Every
    such case is logged in running_notes.txt.
  - THE PROPHECIES STAY DARK. The "cinquecento diece e cinque" of canto
    XXXIII is deliberately a riddle and is rendered as one; so is the
    DXV's counterpart in the Inferno. Do not solve them for the reader.
  - DANTE'S NAME appears exactly once in the whole Comedy, in canto XXX
    line 55, and he apologises for recording it. It stays.


THE VERNE RULE
---------------------------------------------------------------------
Everything stands as Dante wrote it, with no note anywhere in the
volume:
  - MANFRED, excommunicate, dies with two mortal wounds and is saved
    anyway, and the poem says plainly that no curse of the Church can
    put a soul beyond return. That is a claim against the institution
    Dante belonged to and it is the point of canto III.
  - The lust terrace holds heterosexual and homosexual sinners together
    and Guinizelli explains the difference between the two groups
    without a flicker of extra condemnation; canto XXVI is translated
    plainly, in full, and the tenderness in it is not tidied (the
    Brunetto precedent from the Inferno).
  - The invective is savage and stays: "Ah, Italy, slave and hostel of
    grief" in VI, the two suns of Rome in XVI, the Capetian kings in
    XX, the whore and the giant in XXXII.
  - The theology is his: the late-repentant kept waiting, the value of
    prayers offered by the living, the making of the soul in XXV.


CONSISTENT VOCABULARY
---------------------------------------------------------------------
  purgatorio            "Purgatory" in the body; "Purgatorio" is the title.
  cornice               "terrace" (the seven ledges), never "cornice".
  girone                "circle" only when Dante means one of the seven.
  antipurgatorio        not Dante's word; do not use it in the text.
  scoglio, balzo        "ledge", "bank".
  duca, maestro, segnore, dolce padre
                        "guide", "master", "my lord", "sweet father" —
                        Dante varies them deliberately and so do we.
  ombra                 "shade" (not "ghost", not "spirit").
  P                     the seven P's cut in Dante's forehead stay "P",
                        and the angel's wing wipes one away each time.
  contrapasso           not named here; do not import it from the Inferno.
  gentile, cortese      "noble", "courteous" — social vocabulary.
  pietà / pietoso       swings between "pity" and "piety"; Dante uses
                        the ambiguity and so must the English.
  disio, voglia, talento
                        "longing", "will", "desire" — kept distinct;
                        the whole cantica is about wanting the right
                        thing, so these words are technical.
  libero arbitrio       "free will", and it is an argument, not a phrase.
