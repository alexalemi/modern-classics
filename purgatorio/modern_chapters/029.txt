Canto Thirty

	When the seven stars of the first heaven,
	which never knew setting or rising
	nor any veil of mist but that of guilt,

	and which made everyone there aware
	of his duty, as the lower seven do
	for whoever turns the tiller to come to port,

	stood still: the truthful people,
	who had come first between the griffin and them,
	turned to the chariot as to their peace;

	and one of them, as if sent from heaven,
	singing "Veni, sponsa, de Libano"
	cried three times, and all the others after him.

	As the blessed at the last trumpet
	will rise quickly, each from his own hollow,
	singing hallelujah with the voice put on again,

	so on the divine chariot there rose up
	a hundred, ad vocem tanti senis,
	ministers and messengers of eternal life.

	They were all saying: "Benedictus qui venis!",
	and, throwing flowers above and around,
	"Manibus, oh, date lilia plenis!"

	I have seen before now, at the beginning of the day,
	the eastern quarter all rose-coloured
	and the rest of the sky adorned with clear beauty;

	and the face of the sun rise shadowed,
	so that through the tempering of the vapours
	the eye could bear it a long while:

	so, inside a cloud of flowers
	that rose from the angels' hands
	and fell down again inside the chariot and outside it,

	over a white veil, girdled with olive,
	a lady appeared to me, under a green mantle,
	dressed in the colour of living flame.

	And my spirit, which for so long a time
	had not been broken, trembling,
	by wonder in her presence,

	without having any further knowledge through the eyes,
	by a hidden power that went out from her,
	felt the great force of the old love.

	As soon as that high power struck my sight
	which had already pierced me
	before I was out of my boyhood,

	I turned to my left with the trust
	with which a small child runs to his mother
	when he is afraid or when he is hurt,

	to say to Virgil: "There is less than a gram
	of blood left in me that does not tremble:
	I know the signs of the old flame."

	But Virgil had left us without him —
	Virgil, sweetest father,
	Virgil, to whom I gave myself for my salvation;

	and all that our first mother lost
	could not keep my cheeks, though washed with dew,
	from turning dark again with weeping.

	"Dante, because Virgil is going away,
	do not weep yet, do not weep yet;
	for you must weep for another sword."

	Like an admiral who goes to stern and to prow
	to see the men who serve on the other ships
	and heartens them to do well,

	on the left side of the chariot,
	when I turned at the sound of my own name,
	which of necessity is written down here,

	I saw the lady who had appeared to me first,
	veiled under the angels' festival,
	turning her eyes towards me across the stream.

	Although the veil that came down from her head,
	circled with Minerva's leaves,
	would not let her be plainly seen,

	royally, still stern in her bearing,
	she went on like one who speaks
	and keeps the hottest words for later:

	"Look at me well. I am indeed, I am indeed Beatrice.
	How did you think fit to come to the mountain?
	Did you not know that here man is happy?"

	My eyes fell down into the clear water;
	but seeing myself in it, I drew them to the grass,
	so heavily did shame weigh on my forehead.

	So a mother seems severe to her son,
	as she seemed to me; because the taste
	of harsh pity has bitterness in it.

	She was silent; and the angels sang at once
	"In te, Domine, speravi";
	but they did not go past "pedes meos".

	As the snow freezes among the living rafters
	along the back of Italy,
	blown and packed by the Slavonian winds,

	and then, melting, filters down into itself,
	as soon as the land that loses its shadow breathes,
	so that it seems a fire melting a candle;

	so I was without tears and without sighs
	before the singing of those who always sing
	after the notes of the eternal circles;

	but when I understood in their sweet harmonies
	their pity for me, more than if they had said:
	"Lady, why do you break him down so?" —

	the ice that had gathered tight round my heart
	became breath and water, and with anguish
	came out of my breast through my mouth and my eyes.

	She, still standing motionless on the said side
	of the chariot, then turned her words
	to the merciful beings in this way:

	"You keep watch in the eternal day,
	so that neither night nor sleep steals from you
	a step the world may take on its roads;

	so my answer is made with more care
	that the one who is weeping over there should understand me,
	so that guilt and grief may be of one measure.

	Not only by the working of the great wheels,
	which direct every seed to some end
	according to the stars that keep it company,

	but by the generosity of divine graces,
	which have for their rain such high vapours
	that our sight does not come near them,

	this man was such in his new life,
	in what he might become, that every good habit
	would have made a marvellous proof in him.

	But the ground that has more good earthly strength
	becomes more wild and more malignant
	with bad seed and left untilled.

	For a while I held him up with my face:
	showing him my young eyes,
	I took him with me, turned the right way.

	As soon as I was on the threshold
	of my second age and changed my life,
	he took himself from me and gave himself to others.

	When I had risen from flesh to spirit,
	and beauty and virtue had grown in me,
	I was less dear to him and less welcome;

	and he turned his steps by a way that was not true,
	following false images of good
	which keep no promise whole.

	Nor did it help me to win inspirations for him,
	with which, in dreams and otherwise,
	I called him back: so little did he care!

	He fell so low that all arguments
	for his salvation were already too short,
	except to show him the people who are lost.

	For this I visited the doorway of the dead,
	and my prayers were carried, weeping,
	to the one who has brought him up here.

	God's high decree would be broken
	if Lethe were crossed and such food
	were tasted without any payment

	of repentance that sheds tears."
