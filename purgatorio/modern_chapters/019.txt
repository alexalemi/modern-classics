Canto Twenty

	Will fights badly against a better will;
	so, against my own pleasure, to please him,
	I drew the sponge unfilled out of the water.

	I moved off; and my guide moved along
	the clear spaces close by the rock,
	as one walks on a wall close to the battlements;

	for the people who melt out through their eyes,
	drop by drop, the evil that fills the whole world,
	come too near the edge on the other side.

	Curse you, ancient she-wolf,
	who have more prey than all the other beasts
	because of your endlessly deep hunger!

	Oh heaven, in whose turning men seem to believe
	that conditions down here are changed,
	when will he come who will drive her away?

	We went with slow and short steps,
	and I attentive to the shades, whom I heard
	weeping and lamenting pitifully;

	and by chance I heard "Sweet Mary!"
	called out in front of us, in the weeping,
	the way a woman does who is in labour;

	and going on: "You were as poor
	as can be seen by that stable
	where you laid down your holy burden."

	Next I heard: "Oh good Fabricius,
	you chose to have goodness with poverty
	rather than great riches with vice."

	These words had pleased me so much
	that I drew on further to make the acquaintance
	of the spirit they seemed to come from.

	He was still speaking about the generosity
	Nicholas showed to the young girls,
	to bring their youth to honour.

	"Oh soul who speak of so much good,
	tell me who you were," I said, "and why
	you alone renew these worthy praises.

	Your words will not be without reward,
	if I go back to finish the short road
	of that life which flies to its end."

	And he: "I will tell you, not for any comfort
	I expect from over there, but because so much
	grace shines in you before you are dead.

	I was the root of the evil plant
	that overshadows all the Christian land,
	so that good fruit is rarely picked from it.

	But if Douai, Lille, Ghent and Bruges
	had the power, there would soon be vengeance for it;
	and I ask it of him who judges everything.

	Over there I was called Hugh Capet;
	from me are born the Philips and the Louis
	by whom France is ruled these days.

	I was the son of a butcher of Paris.
	When the ancient kings had all come to an end,
	except for one who had turned monk in grey cloth,

	I found the reins of the government of the kingdom
	tight in my hands, and so much power
	newly gained, and so many friends,

	that to the widowed crown my son's head
	was promoted, and from him
	began the consecrated bones of these men.

	As long as the great Provençal dowry
	had not taken the shame out of my blood,
	it was worth little, but at least it did no harm.

	There its plundering began, by force
	and by lying; and afterwards, by way of amends,
	it took Ponthieu and Normandy and Gascony.

	Charles came into Italy and, by way of amends,
	made a victim of Conradin; and then
	pushed Thomas back to heaven, by way of amends.

	I see a time, not long after today,
	that draws another Charles out of France,
	to make himself and his people better known.

	He comes out unarmed, and only with the lance
	that Judas jousted with, and he aims it so
	that he makes the belly of Florence burst.

	From it he will win no land, but sin and shame,
	the heavier for him the more lightly
	he reckons up harm of that kind.

	The other, who once came out of a ship a prisoner,
	I see selling his daughter, and haggling over her
	as the corsairs do over their other slave women.

	Oh avarice, what more can you do to us,
	now that you have drawn my blood so far to you
	that it does not care for its own flesh?

	So that past and future evil may seem less,
	I see the fleur-de-lis come into Anagni,
	and Christ made prisoner in his vicar.

	I see him mocked a second time;
	I see the vinegar and the gall renewed,
	and him killed between living thieves.

	I see the new Pilate so cruel
	that this does not satisfy him, but without any decree
	he carries his greedy sails into the Temple.

	Oh my Lord, when shall I be glad
	to see the vengeance which, being hidden,
	makes your anger sweet in your secret?

	What I was saying about that only bride
	of the Holy Spirit, and which made you
	turn to me for some explanation,

	is the answer to all our prayers
	as long as the day lasts; but when night comes on,
	we take up the opposite sound instead.

	Then we say over Pygmalion,
	whom his throat greedy for gold made
	a traitor and a thief and a parricide;

	and the misery of the miserly Midas,
	which followed on his gluttonous request,
	and which men must always laugh at.

	Then everyone remembers foolish Achan,
	and how he stole the spoils, so that Joshua's anger
	seems still to bite him here.

	Then we accuse Sapphira with her husband;
	we praise the kicks Heliodorus got;
	and Polymestor, who killed Polydorus,

	goes round the whole mountain in infamy.
	Last of all the cry here is: "Crassus,
	tell us, since you know: what does gold taste like?"

	Sometimes one speaks loudly and another low,
	according to the feeling that spurs us
	to go now at a greater step and now a smaller;

	so, over the good that is spoken of here by day,
	I was not alone just now; but nobody else
	near here was raising his voice."

	We had already left him,
	and were struggling to get over the road
	as fast as we were allowed by our strength,

	when I felt the mountain tremble, like something
	falling; at which a chill took me
	such as usually takes a man going to his death.

	Certainly Delos was not shaken so hard
	before Latona made her nest in it
	to give birth to the two eyes of heaven.

	Then a shout began on every side,
	such that the master drew close to me,
	saying: "Do not be afraid, while I am guiding you."

	"Gloria in excelsis" they were all
	saying, "Deo", by what I understood
	from those near, from whom the cry could be made out.

	We stood still and hanging there
	like the shepherds who first heard that singing,
	until the trembling stopped and the song was finished.

	Then we took up our holy road again,
	looking at the shades lying on the ground,
	who had gone back already to their usual weeping.

	No ignorance ever made me want to know
	with so much war in me,
	if my memory does not go wrong about it,

	as I seemed to have then, thinking it over;
	and I did not dare to ask, because of the hurry,
	nor could I see anything there by myself:

	so I went on, timid and full of thought.
