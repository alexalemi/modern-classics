Chapter Six: Waves and Ripples in the Aether

(Part 5 of 6)

The aerial, the antenna, is simply a rod connected to the earth; but it has a certain inductance and also a certain electrical capacity, so any metal rod merely stuck into the ground at one end has a perfectly definite periodic time for the electrical oscillations that can be produced in it. In this respect we may compare the rod with a piece of steel spring held at one end in a vice. If we pull the spring to one side and let it vibrate, it does so according to its own natural time-period for mechanical vibrations, and the sound waves it gives out have a wave-length four times the length of the spring. In the same way the fundamental wave-length of the electric waves given off by an "earthed aerial," a rod stuck in the earth, when an electric impulse is applied at its lower end and oscillations are set up in it, is four times the length of the rod. So to get the best result, the circuit that includes the aerial A must be electrically "tuned" to the circuit that includes the Leyden jar L.

A little thought about these arrangements will show you that if the hand-key in the primary circuit of the induction coil is pressed for a long or a short time, we get long or short torrents of sparks between the secondary balls, and long or short trains of electric waves sent out from the aerial, the earthed vertical wire.

The process of sending a wireless message consists in working the key in the primary circuit of the induction coil so that a rapid stream of sparks passes between the secondary balls for a shorter or a longer time. This gives rise to a corresponding series of electric waves radiated from the aerial. The dash lasts about as long as three dots; a space equal to three dots is left between letters, and one equal to five dots between words. So in the Morse alphabet the sentence "How are you?" is written:

We have next to explain how the signals sent out are recorded.

[Figure 83: The Marconi receiving arrangement. The aerial A is earthed through the jigger P/S; the coherer T sits across the secondary with the condenser C1, and works the relay, which in turn throws in the heavier battery that drives the Morse printer.]

At the receiving station a second insulated aerial, antenna, or long upright rod, A, is put up (see Figure 83), and its lower end is connected to earth through a coil of fine insulated wire, P, forming one circuit of an oscillation transformer. The secondary circuit, S, of this oscillation transformer — which is called a jigger — is cut in the middle and has a small condenser, C1, inserted in it, made of two sheets of tinfoil separated by waxed paper (see Figure 83). To the ends of this circuit is connected the coherer, the metallic filings tube T, which acts as the sensitive receiver.

The Marconi sensitive tube (see Figure 84) is made as follows. A glass tube about a quarter of an inch across and 2 inches long has two silver plugs put into it, soldered to two platinum wires sealed into the closed ends of the tube. The ends of the plugs are cut on a slant and made very smooth, and they very nearly touch one another. A very small quantity of very fine metallic powder, nineteen parts nickel to one part silver, is then placed between the plugs. The quantity of this powder is scarcely more than could be picked up on the head of a large pin. The tube is then exhausted of its air and sealed, and is attached to a bone rod by which it is held in a clip.

[Figure 84: The Marconi coherer. Two slanted silver plugs almost touching inside a sealed and exhausted glass tube, with a pinch of powder — nineteen parts nickel to one of silver, scarcely more than would sit on a pin's head — bridging the gap between them.]

To the two sides of the condenser mentioned above are connected two wires leading to a circuit which includes a single voltaic cell, V, and a relay, E. The relay is connected to another circuit containing a battery, B, and a piece of apparatus called a Morse printer, M, for marking dots and dashes on a strip of paper.

To explain the working details of this rather complicated system devised by Mr Marconi would need a great deal of technical explanation. The general reader may, however, get a clear enough idea of how it performs from the following.

When the electric waves from the distant transmitting station reach the aerial at the receiving station, they set up sympathetic electrical oscillations in it. Conditions are most favourable when the two aerials at the two stations are exactly alike. These oscillations, these rapid electric currents, set up an electromotive force in the secondary circuit of the oscillation transformer, and this acts, as already explained, on the metal filings in the coherer tube and makes the tube a conductor. The cell attached to the relay then sends a current round the circuit so completed and works the relay. The relay is merely a very delicate switch, a circuit-closer, thrown into action by a small current through one of its circuits; it closes a second circuit and so allows another and much larger battery to send a current through the Morse printer. The printer then prints a dot on a moving strip of paper, and a signal is recorded.

One other element in this rather complicated arrangement remains to be noticed: the tapper. Underneath the coherer tube is a little hammer worked by an electromagnet, like the hammer of an electric bell. This tapper is set going by the same current that passes through the Morse printer, so almost as soon as the printer has begun to print, the sensitive tube gets a little tap which turns the filings back into a non-conductor and cuts off the whole flow of current. Were it not for the tapper, the arrival of an electric wave would set the printer drawing a line and it would go on drawing it. The dot is, so to speak, an arrested line. If trains of electric waves keep arriving, then dots keep being printed in close order, and they form a dash on the paper strip.

You will see, then, that the whole arrangement is an exceedingly ingenious device of such a kind that a single touch on the hand-key at one station, causing a spark or two between the spark-balls, makes a dot appear on a band of paper at the distant station; while if the hand-key is held down so that a stream of sparks passes at the transmitting station, a dash is recorded at the receiving station. The means by which this distant effect is produced is the train of electric waves travelling over the earth's surface, setting out from the one aerial and arriving at the other.

Any reader who finds the above explanations hard to follow may perhaps get a clear enough notion of the processes at work by considering a reduced, simplified arrangement. Imagine two long insulated rods, A and A′ (see Figure 85), like lightning conductors set up at distant places. Suppose each rod is cut near the bottom, and let a pair of spark-balls, S, be put into one gap and a coherer, a sensitive tube, C, into the other. At one station let an electrical machine have its positive and negative terminals connected to the two spark-balls; at the other let a battery and an electric bell be connected to the ends of the coherer. Then, as long as the coherer stays non-conducting, the bell does not ring. But if a spark is made between the balls, the reader will understand from everything that has been explained that the coherer tube is at once made conducting by the action of the electric wave sent out from the transmitting rod. The battery at the receiving rod then sends a current through the coherer and rings the bell.

All the other complicated details of the receiver exist to make the process of stopping the bell and starting again self-acting, and to produce two kinds of signal, a long and a short, out of which an alphabet can be made. For telegraphy in any proper sense of the word, we must be able to transmit whatever intelligence we please, not merely one arbitrary signal. Transmitting intelligence means having an alphabet at command, and that in turn requires the power of producing two kinds of signal.

It remains to notice a few of the special features of Mr Marconi's system of wireless telegraphy. In setting up wireless communication between two places, the first thing to do is to equip both with aerials. If a station is on land, a strong mast about 150 feet high is usually put up with a sprit at the top, and from that sprit a stranded copper wire is hung on an ebonite insulator, so that the upper end of the wire is insulated. The lower end is led into a little hut, or into a room near the foot of the mast, where the receiving and transmitting apparatus stands.

If the apparatus is to be installed on board ship, a similar insulated wire is hung from a yardarm, or from a sprit attached to a mast. Each station has both transmitting and receiving apparatus, and the attendant switches the aerial from one to the other so as to send or receive at will.

For long-distance wireless telegraphy the aerial is not a single wire but a collection of wires, hung so as to keep them a little apart from each other. In Mr Marconi's first experiments across the Atlantic, the aerial put up on the coast of Cornwall consisted of fifty stranded copper wires, each 150 feet long, hung fan-fashion from a long transverse stay held up between two masts. The wires were spaced out at the top and gathered in together at the bottom.

The question almost everyone asks at once is how it is possible to stop the waves from one station affecting every receiving instrument alike within a certain radius. The answer is that considerable progress has been made in what is called "tuning" the various stations.

In speaking of acoustic resonance it was pointed out that a train of air waves can set up a vibration in other bodies which have the same natural period. If we open a piano so as to expose the strings, and a singer with a strong voice sings a loud true note and then stops suddenly, it will be found that one particular string of the piano is vibrating — the one that would give out the note that was sung — while all the rest are silent. It has been pointed out that every open electric circuit has a natural electrical time-period in which its charge oscillates if it is disturbed by a sudden electromotive force and then left to itself. If the two aerials at two stations are exactly alike, and if the various circuits making up the oscillation transformers in the transmitting and receiving appliances are all adjusted to the same electrical period, then it is found that stations tuned in this way are sympathetic at vastly greater distances than they would otherwise be. So it is possible to arrange wireless telegraph apparatus so that it is not affected by waves arriving from a distance which have not a particular time-period.

Mr Marconi has also proved that it is possible to receive on the same aerial, at the same time, two different messages, on separate receiving instruments, from two distant but properly tuned transmitting stations.

Since the date of these pioneer inventions many different forms of wave detector have been discovered, and wireless telegraphy has shown itself to be of the greatest utility in communication between ship and ship, and between ship and shore. Its value in letting news be sent from lightships or lighthouses to coast stations cannot be overestimated. One very remarkable feature of the apparatus as Mr Marconi has arranged it is how little space it takes up, which makes it admirably suited to use on board ship. It needs only a long insulated vertical wire, easily hung from a mast, and the whole receiving and transmitting apparatus can be housed in a small cabin. With the sensitive tube and the Marconi receiving arrangements, messages can easily be sent 150 miles over the sea by means of an aerial 150 feet high and a 10-inch induction coil.

It is a curious fact that better results are obtained over water than over land. Two similar stations with the same appliances can communicate at two or three times the distance if the sea lies between them than if they are on land with no water in between. This is connected with the fact that electric waves cannot pass through sea water, though they can diffuse through dry earth. The sea surface acts rather like an optical reflector, a mirror, and the electric waves glide along it.

The roundness of the earth, within certain limits, hardly makes any perceptible difference to the ease of communication. The waves sent out by the transmitter of a long-distance wireless station are from 3000 to 20,000 feet long, so there is a considerable amount of bending, of diffraction. It is a familiar fact, as already explained, that a wave-motion, whether on water or in air, spreads round an obstacle to some extent: an intervening rock or wall does not cast a sharply marked sound-shadow, but the edge of the obstacle deflects the air waves somewhat. How much bending takes place depends on the length of the wave.
