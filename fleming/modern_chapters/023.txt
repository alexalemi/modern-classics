Chapter Five: Electric Oscillations and Electric Waves

(Part 4 of 5)

The electrical actions that take place in an electric discharge between two conductors, one charged positively and the other negatively, are exactly analogous to the experiment just described with the two air vessels, one holding compressed air and the other with the air pumped out of it. You will notice, however, that the oscillations of the air in the pipe depend essentially on the fact that air is a substance with inertia, with mass; and you will naturally ask what it is that has inertia, or its equivalent, in the electrical case.

The answer is this. Every electric circuit has a quality called inductance, because of which a current cannot be started in it instantly, no matter what electromotive force is applied — and, conversely, once the current has been started it cannot be brought to rest instantly either. From the similarity of this quality to the inertia of ordinary material substances, it has sometimes been called the electric inertia of the circuit. The word "inertia" really means inactivity, or laziness, but as it is used in mechanics the term implies something more than mere inactivity: it involves the notion of persistence in motion once the body has been set moving.

When a material substance is in motion it possesses energy, and has the power of overcoming resistance to its motion up to a certain point. This energy-holding power, this capacity for storing up energy of motion, which every material substance has, is a consequence of its inertia. The same fact can be put another way by saying that the mass of a material substance is one of the elements that go to make energy of motion.

An electric current in one sense resembles a moving substance, for it is a display of energy in association with matter. The energy of the current is measured by the product of two factors: one is half the square of the current-strength, the other is the inductance of the circuit. The analogy between the two cases comes out more exactly if we point out that the energy of motion of a moving body is measured by the product of its mass and half the square of its velocity. It follows that the power a heavy moving body has of overcoming resistance — in other words, of doing useful work, or mischief — is proportional not simply to its speed but to the square of its speed. If a bullet moving at a certain speed can just pass through one plank 1 inch thick, then at twice that speed it will pass through four such planks, and at three times the speed through nine planks of the same thickness.

The energy of an electric current is measured in the same way, by the product of the inductance of the circuit and half the square of the current-strength. In the same circuit, or in equal circuits, two currents whose strengths are in the ratio of 1 to 2 have energies in the ratio of 1 to 4. The greater the inductance of a circuit, then, the stronger the tendency of a current set flowing in it to run on after the electromotive force is withdrawn. The inductance of a circuit is increased by winding it into a coil of many turns, and decreased by stretching it out into a straight line.

The important idea to grasp in this part of the subject is that just as there are two forms of mechanical energy — energy of mechanical strain and energy of motion — so there are two forms of electrical energy: energy of electrostatic strain and energy of electric current.

If we bend a bow or stretch a spring, the action involves spending mechanical energy, doing work; and the energy so spent is stored up as energy of strain, or, as it is called, distortional energy, in the bent bow or the stretched spring. But when the bow gives its energy to the arrow, or the spring to a ball, and sets them moving, we have in the flying arrow or ball a store of energy of motion.

If a strip of steel spring is fixed at one end and set vibrating, we have a continual transformation of energy from the motional form to the distortional and back again. At one moment the spring is moving violently, at the next it is bent to its utmost extent, and these two states follow one another over and over. The store of energy in the vibrating spring is nevertheless gradually frittered away — partly because the continual bending of the steel heats it, and that heat dissipates some of the energy, but also because the spring, if it is vibrating fast enough, gives its energy to the surrounding air and creates air waves, which travel away and rapidly rob it of its stock of energy.

In precisely the same way, all electrical oscillation effects depend on the fact that electric energy can show itself in two forms. In one form it is electrostatic energy, energy of electric strain. That is the form we have when we charge a Leyden jar: the glass is then, as explained, in a state of electrical strain, and its condition is analogous to that of a stretched spring. The same holds when we have two conductors insulated from one another in air; we then have an electrical strain in the air.

It is important to notice, however, that since a perfect vacuum can support electric strain, it follows that in the cases where air or glass forms the non-conductor, the dielectric, of a condenser, the whole of the energy cannot be stored in the material substance, in the glass or the air. The real storehouse of the energy is the aether, as modified by the presence of ordinary matter in the same place.

When we discharge the Leyden jar or condenser, the electrostatic energy in the dielectric disappears, and in its place we get an electric current in the connecting conductor — which, as described, is a display of energy in another form. If the resistance of the connecting conductor is small, then we have electrical oscillations set up, consisting of an alternate transformation of the energy from the electrostatic form to the current form and back again.

At each oscillation some energy is frittered away into heat in the conductor; and if the conductor and the condenser have a special form, energy may be carried off rapidly from the system by the electric waves formed in the surrounding aether, the surrounding dielectric. These waves consist of the propagation of lines of electric strain through the medium, just as an air wave consists of the propagation of regions of compression through the air, or a water wave of the propagation of an elevation over the surface.

Returning to the production of electrical oscillations, we must consider in a little more detail how we can create an electrical oscillation in what we have called an open electric circuit. Let me begin with an experiment; it will then be easier for you to understand the particular points that need explaining.

The dotted lines show the electric strain in the air around them — a Leyden jar whose dielectric is the whole of the surrounding space.]

In front of me are two long brass rods, each about 5 feet long, with polished brass balls at their ends (see Figure 71). The rods are set in one line, supported on pieces of ebonite, and fixed so that the two balls are about a quarter of an inch apart. The two rods therefore make up two insulated conductors. They are connected by coils of wire to the terminals of an instrument called an induction coil, which I shall not stop to describe, but which you may regard as a kind of electrical machine for producing electromotive force.

If we set the induction coil going, it creates between its terminals an intermittent but very powerful electromotive force, which increases steadily up to a certain value, at which it breaks down the resistance of the air-gap between the two balls.

Let us think carefully about what happens as the electromotive force of the coil is increasing. One rod is in effect being electrified positively and the other negatively, and these charges are growing. The two rods form, as it were, the two coated surfaces of a kind of Leyden jar, or condenser, of which the surrounding air is the non-conductor. From everything explained so far, you will easily understand that there is an electric strain in the air along certain lines — lines of electrostatic strain — and that the state of the air is exactly like the state the glass of a Leyden jar is in when the jar is charged. If we were to draw the direction of this strain as lines through the space round the rods, we should have to draw them roughly as the dotted lines show in Figure 71.

As the electrical state of the rods grows more and more intense, a point is reached at which the air between the balls can no longer keep up the strain, and it breaks down and passes into a conducting condition. The state of things round the rods is then like that of a Leyden jar being discharged. An electric current is produced across the air-gap, moving from one rod to the other, and the intensely heated air between the balls is visible to us as an electric spark. If that spark were photographed, it would be found to be an oscillatory spark.

The current in the rods cannot go on indefinitely: it gradually falls off in strength. But as it flows, it creates in the space round the rods an electric strain in the opposite direction to the one that produced it, though along the same lines.

So after a very short time the electrical conditions that existed the moment before the air broke down are exactly reproduced, only with the direction of the strain reversed: the rod that was positively electrified is now negatively, and the other the other way about. Then this state of strain begins to disappear in its turn, producing a current in the rods, again in the reverse direction. So the energy which was originally given to the space round the rods in the form of an electric strain continually changes its form — existing one moment as energy of the current passing across the spark gap, and the next as energy of electric strain.

We may ask why this state of things does not go on for ever, and the answer is twofold. First, because the rods possess a property called electrical resistance, which acts on the electric current just as friction acts on the motion of material substances: it fritters the energy away into heat. So at each reversal of the current in the rods, a certain quantity of the original store of energy has disappeared through resistance.

There is, however, a further and more important drain on the energy, and it comes from the fact that an electrical oscillation of this kind, taking place in a finite straight circuit — an open electric circuit, as it is called — creates an electric wave in the space around. The rapid reversal of electric strain in the air results in the production of an electric wave, just as, when an explosion is made in air, the rapid compression of the air results in the production of an air wave.

It is not easy for anyone coming to the subject for the first time to grasp fully what is implied by the term "an electric wave."

In the first lecture, you may remember, I pointed out that a wave can be produced in a medium of any kind if the medium has two properties. First, it must elastically resist some change, some distortion. Second, when that distortion has been made, it must tend to disappear if the medium is left to itself; and in disappearing, the displacement of the medium must overshoot the mark and be reproduced in the opposite direction, because of some inertia-like quality, some power of persistence, in the medium.

It would take us into matters beyond the scope of elementary lectures to try to summarise all the evidence tending to show that the phenomena of electricity and magnetism must depend on actions taking place in some medium — the electro-magnetic medium. All the great investigators at the beginning of the last century, when electrical and magnetic phenomena were first being explored, came to this conclusion; and in the writings of Joseph Henry, of Ampère and of Faraday we find references again and again to their conviction that the phenomena of electricity imply the existence of a medium in exactly the way the phenomena of optics do. It is only in recent years, however, that we have had before us the evidence — some of which will be reviewed in the next lecture — which convincingly proves that the luminiferous aether and the electro-magnetic medium must be the same thing.

Consideration of the simplest electrical effects is enough to show that if this medium exists, it possesses at least two properties, one of which is that it offers an elastic resistance to the production of electric strain in it by electromotive force.

A question sure to arise in the minds of anyone who thinks carefully about this subject is: what is the nature of an electric strain? And the only answer we can give at present is that we must be content to leave the question unanswered. We do not yet know enough about the mechanical structure of the electro-magnetic medium, of the aether, to pronounce in detail on the nature of the change we call an electric strain. It may be a motion of some kind; it may be a compression, or a twist; or it may be something totally different and at present unthinkable by us. Whatever it is, it is some kind of change produced under the action of electromotive force, which disappears when that force is removed.

Clerk-Maxwell, to whom we owe some of the most suggestive conceptions in modern electricity, coined the phrase electric displacement to describe the change we are here calling an electric strain. One essential element of Maxwell's theory of electricity is that an electric strain or displacement, while it is being made and while it is disappearing, is in effect an electric current — which is why it is sometimes spoken of as a displacement current.

We have seen that every electric circuit possesses a quality analogous to inertia: that is, when a current is produced in it, the current tends to persist, and cannot be created at its full value instantly by any electromotive force whatever.

And just as we cannot at present pronounce in detail on the real nature of electric strain, so we cannot say whether the quality we call the inductance of a circuit depends on a true inertia of the electro-magnetic medium, or on some entirely different and more fundamental quality.
