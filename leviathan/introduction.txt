Thomas Hobbes wrote this book in Paris. He had left England in 1640, at fifty-two, because a manuscript of his defending the king's authority was circulating and Parliament had begun arresting men for less. He stayed away eleven years, teaching mathematics for a while to the exiled Prince of Wales, the future Charles II. While he wrote, England fought two civil wars and cut off the head of Charles I in January 1649. Leviathan was published in London in 1651, and Hobbes came home that year and made his peace with the government that had won.

So the argument is not speculation about a hypothetical past. When Hobbes describes what happens to people once there is no common power over them, he is describing something he had just watched from across the Channel. Without such a power there is no industry, no farming, no navigation, no building, no arts, no letters, and continual fear and danger of violent death, and the life of man is solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short. That is five adjectives. It is almost always quoted as three. The claim underneath it is thinner than its reputation: not that people are monsters, but that they are roughly equal in strength and cunning, so that anybody can kill anybody, and that fear alone is then enough to produce a war of every man against every man. The way out is an agreement to hand the right of self-defence to one sovereign power, because covenants, without the sword, are but words.

The book is longer and stranger than the part everybody knows. It runs to forty-seven chapters in four parts, and the state of nature occupies one of them. Slightly over half the whole text is about religion: what Scripture actually means by spirit, prophet, miracle, hell and the kingdom of God, who has the right to say what it means, and why the Roman church is the ghost of the deceased Roman Empire, sitting crowned upon the grave thereof. That was the live quarrel in 1651. Hobbes was arguing that no church can hold authority independent of the state. This edition contains the whole thing, all four parts and the Review and Conclusion, which is the section that told Englishmen when they could lawfully submit to whoever had won.

It satisfied nobody. Royalists read the Conclusion as a licence for Cromwell, churchmen read the rest as atheism, and in 1666 a committee of the Commons named the book in a bill against profaneness. Hobbes burned papers and never again published on religion in England. He is also very funny, in a dry and contemptuous way, particularly about universities. Rousseau's Social Contract and Burke's Reflections are both in this collection, and all three men are arguing with each other.
