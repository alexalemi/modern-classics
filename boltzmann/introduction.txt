Ludwig Boltzmann (1844–1906) was the Austrian physicist who explained heat as the motion of atoms and showed why a hot cup of coffee cools and never warms itself: of all the ways the molecules can be arranged, the disordered ones vastly outnumber the ordered. His formula for entropy is carved on his grave in Vienna. In 1905 he was sixty-one, a professor at the University of Vienna, famous, short-sighted, asthmatic and fond of his food, and still fighting a long argument with colleagues who doubted that atoms existed at all.

That summer the University of California invited him to teach at its summer school in Berkeley. He agreed to give thirty lectures in English, a language he had studied with a tutor in Vienna. He left straight from a Thursday meeting of the Vienna Academy, went by way of a meeting in Leipzig and a steamer to New York, crossed America by train in four days and nights, and came home through Yellowstone and Chicago. The trip took about two months. Back in Vienna he wrote it up as a comic essay and put it at the end of Populäre Schriften (Popular Writings), a collection of his lectures and essays published in Leipzig later that year.

It is a travel essay, not a physics lesson. Boltzmann writes about his stomach, the dry town of Berkeley where he had to buy his wine in Oakland and drink it in secret, the sung menus of his boarding house, the millionaire Phoebe Hearst's hacienda, the Lick Observatory on Mount Hamilton, and the American sleeping car. He quotes Schiller constantly, because 1905 was the centenary of the poet's death, and he expects the reader to catch the lines. The encyclopedia he mentions at the start is Felix Klein's Encyclopedia of the Mathematical Sciences, for which he was writing the article on the theory of gases; Zermelo, the colleague he did not want writing it instead, had attacked his work.

Boltzmann died the following September, by suicide, on holiday at Duino near Trieste. Nothing in the essay foreshadows it.

This edition is translated from the German of the 1905 book. His English words and his phonetic attempts at English are kept as he wrote them, and his Schiller quotations are translated. One sentence, a joke about Black railway workers, and its punchline a page later have been left out.
