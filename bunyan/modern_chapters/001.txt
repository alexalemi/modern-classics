The City of Destruction

As I walked through the wilderness of this world, I came upon a certain place where there was a den, and I lay down in that place to sleep. And as I slept, I dreamed a dream. I dreamed, and look — "I saw a man dressed in rags, standing in a certain place, his face turned away from his own house, a book in his hand, and a great burden on his back." I looked, and I saw him open the book and read in it; and as he read, he wept and shook; and at last, unable to hold it in any longer, he broke out in a miserable cry: "What shall I do?"

[Note: the den is Bedford jail, where Bunyan wrote this book during the twelve years he spent in prison for preaching without a licence.]

In this condition he went home, and held himself in as long as he could, so that his wife and children would not see how distressed he was. But he could not stay silent for long, because his trouble kept growing. So at last he opened his mind to his wife and children, and began to talk to them like this. "My dear wife," he said, "and you, my own children — I am a ruined man, because of a burden that lies heavy on me. And more: I have it on certain word that this city of ours is going to be burned with fire out of heaven. In that terrible overthrow I myself, and you, my wife, and you, my sweet children, will all come to a wretched end — unless some way of escape can be found, which as yet I cannot see."

At this his family were badly shaken — not because they believed a word of what he had told them, but because they thought some kind of madness had got into his head. So, since it was getting on towards night and they hoped a night's sleep might settle his brain, they hurried him off to bed. But the night was as bad for him as the day had been, and instead of sleeping he spent it sighing and weeping. When morning came, they wanted to know how he was. Worse, he told them; and he set about talking to them again — but by now they were starting to harden against him. They decided to drive his madness out of him by being rough and short with him. Sometimes they laughed at him, sometimes they told him off, and sometimes they simply took no notice of him at all. So he began to go off to his room by himself, to pray for them and pity them, and to grieve over his own misery. He also took to walking alone in the fields, sometimes reading and sometimes praying; and he spent his days that way for some time.

Now I saw one day, as he was walking in the fields, that he was reading in his book as he usually did, and in great distress of mind; and as he read he burst out, as he had before, crying: "What shall I do to be saved?"

I saw too that he looked this way and that, as if he meant to run — and yet he stood still, because, as far as I could see, he could not tell which way to go. Then I looked, and I saw a man named Evangelist coming towards him, who asked, "Why are you crying out?"

He answered: Sir, I can see from the book in my hand that I am condemned to die, and after that to come to judgment; and I find that I am not willing to do the first, nor able to face the second.

Then Evangelist said: Why not willing to die, when this life comes with so many troubles in it?

The man answered: Because I am afraid that this burden on my back will sink me lower than the grave, and I shall fall into Tophet, the fire-pit itself. And Sir, if I am not fit to go to prison, then I am certainly not fit to go to judgment, and from there to execution. Thinking about all this is what makes me cry out.

Then Evangelist said: If that is your condition, why are you standing still?

He answered: Because I do not know where to go.

Then Evangelist gave him a roll of parchment, and written inside it was: "Flee from the wrath to come."

So the man read it, and looking very closely at Evangelist, said: Where must I flee to?

Then Evangelist pointed with his finger across a very wide field and said: Do you see that little gate over there?

The man said: No.

Then the other said: Do you see that shining light?

He said: I think I do.

Then Evangelist said: Keep that light in your eye and go straight towards it, and you will see the gate; and when you knock at it, you will be told what you are to do.

So I saw in my dream that the man began to run. He had not got far from his own door when his wife and children saw him going and began calling after him to come back. But the man put his fingers in his ears and ran on, crying, "Life! Life! Eternal life!" He never looked behind him, but fled towards the middle of the plain.

The neighbours came out too, to watch him run; and as he ran, some jeered, some threatened, and some shouted after him to come back. Among these last were two who were determined to fetch him back by force. One of them was called Obstinate, and the other Pliable. By this time the man had got a good distance ahead of them, but they were set on chasing him all the same — and they did, and before long they caught up with him.

Then the man said: Neighbours, why have you come?

They said: To persuade you to come back with us.

But he said: That is out of the question. You live in the City of Destruction — the place where I was born too. I can see that it is so. And dying there, sooner or later, you will sink lower than the grave, into a place that burns with fire and brimstone. Be sensible, good neighbours, and come along with me.

What, said Obstinate, and leave our friends and our comforts behind us?

Yes, said Christian — for that was his name — because everything "that you would leave behind" is not worth comparing with the smallest part of what I am going to find. And if you will come along with me, and hold to it, you will fare as well as I do; because where I am going there is enough and to spare. Come on, and put my words to the test.

Obstinate. What are these things you are after, that you would leave the whole world to find them?

Christian. I am after "an inheritance that cannot spoil or be spoiled or fade away." It is kept safe in heaven, to be handed over at the appointed time to those who look for it in earnest. Read it for yourself, if you like, in my book.

Obstinate. Nonsense, said Obstinate. Away with your book. Are you coming back with us or not?

Christian. Not I, said the other, because I have put my hand to the plough.

Obstinate. Come on, then, neighbour Pliable, let us turn round and go home without him. There is a whole tribe of these crackbrained fools who, once they get a notion by the end of it, think themselves wiser than seven men who can give you a reason.

Pliable. Don't be so hard on him, said Pliable. If what the good man Christian says is true, then the things he is after are better than ours. My heart rather inclines to go with my neighbour.

Obstinate. What, more fools yet? Take my advice and come back. Who knows where a man with a fever in his head will lead you? Go back, go back, and be sensible.

Christian. No — come with your neighbour Pliable. There really are such things to be had as I told you of, and far greater glories besides. If you will not believe me, read here in this book; and as for whether what is written in it is true, look — every word of it is guaranteed by the blood of the one who made it.

Pliable. Well, neighbour Obstinate, said Pliable, I am coming to a decision. I mean to go along with this good man and throw in my lot with him. But, my good companion, do you know the way to this place you want so much?

Christian. I have been directed by a man named Evangelist to hurry to a little gate that lies ahead of us, where we will be given instructions about the road.

Pliable. Come on, then, good neighbour, let us be going.

So the two of them went off together.

Obstinate. And I am going back where I came from, said Obstinate. I will be no companion to such deluded, fanciful fellows.

Now I saw in my dream that when Obstinate had gone back, Christian and Pliable went on across the plain, talking; and this is how their conversation began.

Christian. Come, neighbour Pliable, how are you? I am glad you were persuaded to come with me. If Obstinate himself had felt what I have felt of the power and the terror of what is still unseen, he would not have turned his back on us so lightly.

Pliable. Come, neighbour Christian, since there is nobody here but the two of us, tell me more. What are these things we are going to, and how are they to be enjoyed?

Christian. I can picture them in my mind better than I can speak them with my tongue. But since you want to know, I will read about them out of my book.

Pliable. And do you really think the words of your book are certainly true?

Christian. Yes, truly — because it was made by one who cannot lie.

Pliable. Well said. What things are they?

Christian. There is a kingdom without end to live in, and everlasting life to be given us, so that we may live in that kingdom forever.

Pliable. Well said. And what else?

Christian. There are crowns of glory to be given us, and clothes that will make us shine like the sun in the sky.

Pliable. That sounds very fine. And what else?

Christian. There will be no more crying, and no more sorrow; because the one who owns the place will wipe every tear from our eyes.

Pliable. And what company will we have there?

Christian. There we will be with seraphim and cherubim — creatures so bright they will dazzle your eyes to look at them. There you will meet thousands upon thousands who have gone on ahead of us to that place. Not one of them will do you any harm; they are all loving and holy, every one of them walking in the sight of God and standing accepted in his presence forever. In a word: there we will see the elders with their golden crowns; there we will see the holy virgins with their golden harps; there we will see men who were cut to pieces by this world, burned in the fire, eaten by wild beasts, drowned in the sea, all for the love they had for the Lord of that place — and every one of them well, and dressed in immortality as if it were a coat.

Pliable. Just hearing it is enough to carry a man's heart away. But are these things really to be had? How are we to get a share in them?

Christian. The Lord, the Governor of that country, has set it down in this book; and the sum of it is that if we are truly willing to have it, he will give it to us free.

Pliable. Well, my good companion, I am glad to hear it. Come on, let us pick up the pace.

Christian. I cannot go as fast as I would like, because of this burden on my back.
