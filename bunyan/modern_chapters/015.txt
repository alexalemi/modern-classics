Hopeful, and Mr. By-ends

Now I saw in my dream that Christian did not go out alone. There was one whose name was Hopeful — made so by watching Christian and Faithful, in their words and their behaviour, through their sufferings at the Fair — who joined himself to Christian, and entering into a brotherly agreement with him, told him he would be his companion. So one man died to bear witness to the truth, and another rose out of his ashes to be Christian's companion on his pilgrimage. This Hopeful also told Christian that there were a good many more of the men at the Fair who would take their time and follow on after them.

So I saw that soon after they had got out of the Fair, they caught up with somebody walking ahead of them, whose name was By-ends. So they said to him: What part of the country are you from, sir? And how far are you going this way? He told them he came from the town of Fair-speech and was going to the Celestial City — but he did not tell them his name.

From Fair-speech! said Christian. Is there anything good living there?

By-ends. Yes, said By-ends, I hope so.

Christian. Tell me, sir — what may I call you? said Christian.

By-ends. I am a stranger to you and you to me. If you are going this way, I shall be glad of your company; if not, I shall have to be content.

Christian. This town of Fair-speech, said Christian — I have heard of it; and as I recall, they say it is a wealthy place.

By-ends. Yes, I can promise you it is. And I have a great many rich relations there.

Christian. Tell me — who are your relations there, if a man may be so bold?

By-ends. Nearly the whole town; and in particular my Lord Turnabout, my Lord Timeserver, my Lord Fair-speech — from whose ancestors the town first took its name — and Mr. Smooth-man, Mr. Facing-both-ways and Mr. Anything. And the parson of our parish, Mr. Two-tongues, was my mother's own brother on the father's side. And to tell you the truth, I have become a gentleman of good standing; and yet my great-grandfather was only a waterman, looking one way and rowing another — and I got most of my estate by the same trade.

Christian. Are you a married man?

By-ends. Yes, and my wife is a very virtuous woman, the daughter of a virtuous woman. She was my Lady Feigning's daughter, so she comes of a very honourable family, and has reached such a pitch of breeding that she knows how to conduct herself with everybody, prince and peasant alike. It is true that we differ a little in religion from the stricter sort, but only on two small points. First, we never struggle against wind and tide. Second, we are always at our most enthusiastic when religion goes about in his silver slippers; we love to walk with him in the street if the sun is shining and the people are applauding him.

Then Christian stepped a little aside to his companion Hopeful and said: It runs in my mind that this is one By-ends of Fair-speech; and if it is, we have as complete a scoundrel in our company as lives anywhere in these parts.

Then Hopeful said: Ask him. I should not have thought he would be ashamed of his name.

So Christian came up with him again and said: Sir, you talk as though you knew rather more than the rest of the world does; and if I am not mistaken, I think I can half guess who you are. Is your name not Mr. By-ends of Fair-speech?

By-ends. That is not my name. It is a nickname given me by certain people who cannot stand me; and I must be content to bear it as an insult, as other good men have borne theirs before me.

Christian. But did you never give people any reason to call you by that name?

By-ends. Never, never! The worst I ever did to give them the excuse for it was that I always had the luck to find my own judgment falling in with the way of the times, whatever it happened to be — and my chance was to profit by it. But if things come to me that way, let me count it a blessing; and let not the malicious load me with abuse on account of it.

Christian. I did think you were the man I had heard about; and to tell you what I think, I am afraid this name belongs to you rather more properly than you would like us to believe.

By-ends. Well, if you choose to imagine so, I cannot help it. You will find me a fair-minded companion, if you will still let me be your associate.

Christian. If you are going to come with us, you must go against wind and tide — which I gather is against your opinion. You must also acknowledge religion in his rags as well as in his silver slippers; and stand by him when he is in irons as well as when he walks the streets to applause.

By-ends. You must not impose on me, or lord it over my faith. Leave me my liberty, and let me come with you.

Christian. Not a step further, unless you will do exactly what I have just said, as we do.

Then By-ends said: I shall never desert my old principles, since they are harmless and profitable. If I may not go with you, I must do what I did before you caught up with me — go by myself, until somebody catches up with me who will be glad of my company.

Now I saw in my dream that Christian and Hopeful left him and kept their distance ahead of him. But one of them, looking back, saw three men following Mr. By-ends; and as they came up with him he made them a very low bow, and they paid him a compliment in return. The men's names were Mr. Hold-the-world, Mr. Money-love and Mr. Save-all — men Mr. By-ends had known before, because in their boyhood they had been schoolfellows together, taught by one Mr. Gripeman, a schoolmaster in Love-gain, which is a market town in the county of Coveting, in the north. This schoolmaster taught them the art of getting, whether by force, fraud, flattery, lying, or by putting on a show of religion; and these four gentlemen had picked up a great deal of their master's art, so that any one of them could have kept such a school himself.

Well, when they had greeted each other, as I said, Mr. Money-love said to Mr. By-ends: Who are those on the road ahead of us? — because Christian and Hopeful were still in sight.

By-ends. They are a couple of men from a far country who, in their own fashion, are going on pilgrimage.

Money-love. What a pity! Why did they not wait, so that we might have had the pleasure of their company? Because they, and we, and you too, sir, I hope, are all going on pilgrimage.

By-ends. So we are, indeed. But the men ahead of us are so rigid, and so much in love with their own notions, and think so little of anybody else's opinions, that let a man be as godly as you please, if he does not fall in with them on every point they push him clean out of their company.

Save-all. That is hard. But we do read of some who are righteous overmuch; and men like that are driven by their own rigidity to judge and condemn everybody but themselves. But tell me, please — what were the things you differed about, and how many?

By-ends. Why, in their headstrong way they conclude that it is a duty to rush on with their journey in all weathers, while I am for waiting for wind and tide. They are for risking everything for God at a single stroke; I am for taking every advantage to secure my life and my property. They are for holding on to their notions though everybody else is against them; I am for religion in whatever way, and as far as, the times and my own safety will allow. They are for religion when he is in rags and out of favour; I am for him when he walks in his golden slippers, in the sunshine, and with applause.

Mr. Hold-the-world. Yes — and you stay right there, good Mr. By-ends. Because for my part I can count a man nothing but a fool who, having the freedom to keep what he has, is unwise enough to lose it. Let us be as wise as serpents. It is best to make hay while the sun shines. You see how the bee lies still all winter and stirs herself only when she can have profit along with her pleasure. God sends sometimes rain and sometimes sunshine; if they are fools enough to go through the first, let us at least be content to take the fair weather along with us.

For my part, I like that religion best which will stand together with the security of God's good blessings to us. Because who that is governed by his reason could imagine, since God has given us the good things of this life, that he does not mean us to keep them for his sake? Abraham and Solomon grew rich in religion. And Job says that a good man shall pile up gold like dust. But he must not be the sort of man these two ahead of us are, if they are as you have described them.

Mr. Save-all. I think we are all agreed on this, and so there is no need for any more words about it.

Mr. Money-love. No, there is no need for more words about it indeed; because a man who believes neither Scripture nor reason — and you see we have both on our side — neither knows his own liberty nor looks after his own safety.

Mr. By-ends. My brothers, we are all going on pilgrimage, as you see. And to keep our minds better off bad things, allow me to put this question to you. Suppose a man — a minister, or a tradesman, or whatever he may be — should have an opportunity in front of him to get the good blessings of this life, but in such a way that he can only get at them by becoming, in appearance at least, unusually enthusiastic about some points of religion that he never bothered with before. May he not use this means to reach his end, and still be a thoroughly honest man?

Mr. Money-love. I see the bottom of your question; and with these gentlemen's permission I will try to shape an answer for you.

And first, to speak to your question as it concerns a minister. Suppose a minister, a worthy man, who holds only a very small living and has his eye on a greater one, far richer and fatter. And suppose he now has an opportunity of getting it, but only by being more studious, by preaching more often and more zealously, and — because the temper of the people requires it — by altering some of his principles. For my part, I see no reason why a man may not do this, provided he has a call to it. Yes, and a good deal more besides, and still be an honest man. And here is why.

First, his desire for a greater living is lawful — this cannot be denied — since Providence has set it in front of him. So he may get it if he can, and raise no question of conscience about it.

Second, his desire for that living makes him more studious, a more zealous preacher, and so on, and so makes him a better man; yes, and makes him improve his abilities better, which is according to the mind of God.

Third, as for his falling in with the temper of his people by giving up some of his principles in order to serve them, this shows: one, that he is of a self-denying temper; two, that he has a sweet and winning manner; and so, three, that he is all the fitter for the ministry.

Fourth, I conclude, then, that a minister who exchanges a small living for a large one should not be judged greedy for doing it, but rather — since his abilities and his industry are improved by it — should be counted a man who is following his calling and the opportunity put into his hand to do good.

And now to the second part of the question, which concerns the tradesman you mentioned. Suppose a man like that has only a poor business in the world, but by becoming religious he may improve his trade, perhaps get a rich wife, or better and more numerous customers for his shop. For my part I see no reason why this may not lawfully be done. And here is why.

First, to become religious is a virtue, by whatever means a man becomes so.

Second, it is not unlawful to get a rich wife, or more trade for my shop.

Third, besides, the man who gets these things by becoming religious gets what is good, from people who are good, by becoming good himself. So then here is a good wife, and good customers, and good profit, and all of them got by becoming religious, which is good. Therefore to become religious in order to get all these is a good and profitable plan.

This answer, made by Mr. Money-love to Mr. By-ends' question, was highly applauded by all of them; so they concluded, on the whole, that it was thoroughly wholesome and advantageous. And because they thought nobody was able to contradict it, and because Christian and Hopeful were still within calling distance, they jointly agreed to attack them with the question as soon as they caught up — all the more since the two had opposed Mr. By-ends a little earlier.

So they called after them, and Christian and Hopeful stopped and stood still until they came up. But they had settled as they went that it should not be Mr. By-ends but old Mr. Hold-the-world who put the question to them, because they supposed the answer given to him would be free of the last of the heat that had flared up between Mr. By-ends and the two of them at their parting a little while before.

So they came up to one another; and after a short greeting, Mr. Hold-the-world put the question to Christian and his companion, and told them to answer it if they could.

Christian. Then Christian said: Even a baby in religion could answer ten thousand questions like that. Because if it is unlawful to follow Christ for the sake of bread — as it is in the sixth chapter of John — how much more disgusting is it to make him and religion into a stalking-horse for getting and enjoying the world! Nor do we find anybody but heathens, hypocrites, devils and witches who are of this opinion.

First, heathens. When Hamor and Shechem had a mind to Jacob's daughter and his cattle, and saw that there was no way for them to get at them except by being circumcised, they said to their companions: if every male among us is circumcised, as they are, will not their cattle and their goods and every beast of theirs become ours? Their daughter and their cattle were what they were after, and their religion was the stalking-horse they used to get at them. Read the whole story.

Second, the hypocritical Pharisees were of this religion too. Long prayers were their pretence, but getting widows' houses was their intention; and greater damnation from God was their sentence.

Third, Judas the devil was of this religion. He was religious for the money-bag, so that he could get hold of what was in it; but he was lost, thrown away, the very son of destruction.

Fourth, Simon the sorcerer was of this religion too, because he wanted the Holy Spirit so that he could make money with it; and his sentence out of Peter's mouth was accordingly.

Fifth, I cannot get it out of my mind that the man who takes up religion for the sake of the world will throw religion away for the sake of the world. Because just as surely as Judas had the world in view when he became religious, so surely did he sell religion and his Master for it too.

So to answer this question in the affirmative, as I gather you have done, and to accept such an answer as sound, is heathenish, hypocritical and devilish alike; and your reward will be according to what you have done.

Then they stood staring at one another, but had nothing to answer Christian with. Hopeful also approved the soundness of Christian's answer; so there was a great silence among them. Mr. By-ends and his company faltered too and kept back, so that Christian and Hopeful could get ahead of them.

Then Christian said to his companion: If these men cannot stand before the judgment of men, what will they do with the judgment of God? And if they are struck dumb when they are dealt with by vessels of clay, what will they do when they are rebuked by the flames of a devouring fire?

Then Christian and Hopeful got ahead of them again, and went on until they came to a delightful plain called Ease, where they went along very contentedly. But that plain was narrow, so they were quickly over it.

Now on the far side of that plain there was a little hill called Lucre, and in that hill a silver mine, which some of those who had gone that way before had turned aside to look at, because of how unusual it was. But going too near the edge of the pit, they found the ground treacherous under them; it gave way, and they were killed. Some had also been maimed there, and could never to their dying day be themselves again.
