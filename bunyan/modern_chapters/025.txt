The House of the Interpreter

So now, when they had talked away a little more time, they came near to a house that stood beside the road — a house that had been built for the relief of pilgrims, as you will find more fully told in the First Part of these Records of the Pilgrim's Progress. So they came on towards the house, the House of the Interpreter; and when they got to the door they heard a great deal of talk going on inside. So they listened, and heard, as they thought, Christiana mentioned by name.

Because you have to understand that news of her and her children going on pilgrimage had run on ahead of her. And this was all the more pleasing to the household because they had heard she was Christian's wife — that woman who had been so unwilling, not long since, even to hear of going on pilgrimage. So they stood still and listened to the good people inside praising her, who little thought she was standing at the door.

At last Christiana knocked, as she had done at the gate before. And when she had knocked, a young woman named Innocent came to the door and opened it and looked out — and there were two women standing there.

Innocent. Then the young woman said to them: Who is it you would like to speak to here?

Christiana. Christiana answered: We understand that this is a privileged place for those who have become pilgrims; and we standing at this door are such. So we ask that we may have a share in what we have come here for; because the day, as you see, is nearly over, and we are unwilling to go any further tonight.

Innocent. Tell me, please, what may I call your name, so that I can tell my Lord inside?

Christiana. My name is Christiana. I was the wife of that pilgrim who travelled this way some years ago, and these are his four children. This young woman is my companion, and is going on pilgrimage too.

Innocent. Then Innocent ran in — for that was her name — and said to those inside: Can you imagine who is at the door? Christiana is there, and her children, and her companion, all waiting to be received here.

Then they leapt for joy, and went and told their Master. So he came to the door, and looking at her, he said: Are you that Christiana whom Christian, the good man, left behind him when he took up a pilgrim's life?

Christiana. I am that woman who was so hard-hearted as to think nothing of my husband's troubles, and who left him to go on his journey alone. And these are his four children. But now I have come too, because I am convinced that no road is the right one but this.

Interpreter. Then what is written of the man who said to his son is fulfilled: "Go and work in my vineyard today. He answered and said, I will not — but afterwards he repented, and went."

Christiana. Then Christiana said: So be it, amen. God make it a true saying about me, and grant that I may be found by him at the last in peace, without spot and blameless.

Interpreter. But why are you standing here at the door? Come in, daughter of Abraham. We were talking about you just now, because word came to us before you did that you had become a pilgrim. Come in, children; come in, young woman.

So he took them all into the house.

So when they were inside, they were told to sit down and rest. And when they had done that, those who looked after the Pilgrims in the house came into the room to see them. And one smiled, and another smiled, and they all smiled, for joy that Christiana had become a pilgrim. They looked at the boys too, and stroked them over the faces with their hands, as a sign of how gladly they received them. They behaved lovingly to Mercy as well, and welcomed them all into their Master's house.

After a while, since supper was not ready, the Interpreter took them into his rooms of emblems and showed them what Christian, Christiana's husband, had seen some time before. So here they saw the man in the cage, and the man and his dream, and the man who cut his way through his enemies, and the picture of the greatest of them all, together with the rest of the things that had been so useful to Christian then.

This done, and after Christiana and her company had digested it a little, the Interpreter took them aside again and brought them first into a room where there was a man who could look nowhere but downwards, with a muckrake in his hand. There was somebody standing over his head as well with a heavenly crown in his hand, offering him that crown for his muckrake; but the man neither looked up nor took any notice, but went on raking together the straws and the little sticks and the dust of the floor.

Then Christiana said: I am fairly sure I know something of what this means. This is a picture of a man of this world, is it not, good sir?

Interpreter. You have said the right thing, said he. And his muckrake shows his earthbound mind. And as for seeing him pay more attention to raking up straws and sticks and the dust of the floor than to the one who calls him from above with the heavenly crown in his hand — that is to show that heaven is nothing but a fable to some people, and that the things down here are counted the only solid things there are. Now as for its also being shown you that the man could look nowhere but downwards, that is to let you know that earthly things, when they have real power over people's minds, carry their hearts clean away from God.

Christiana. Then Christiana said: Oh, deliver me from this muckrake!

Interpreter. That prayer, said the Interpreter, has lain by so long it is nearly rusty. "Give me not riches" is hardly the prayer of one man in ten thousand. Straws and sticks and dust are the great things most people are after now.

At that Mercy and Christiana wept, and said: It is only too true.

When the Interpreter had shown them this, he took them into the very best room in the house — a very fine room it was. So he told them to look round and see whether they could find anything useful there. So they looked round and round; and there was nothing at all to be seen except a very large spider on the wall — and they overlooked that.

Mercy. Then Mercy said: Sir, I see nothing. But Christiana held her tongue.

Interpreter. But, said the Interpreter, look again. So she looked again, and said: There is nothing here but an ugly spider, hanging by her hands on the wall.

Then he said: Is there only one spider in all this spacious room?

Then the tears came into Christiana's eyes, because she was a woman quick to understand; and she said: Yes, Lord, there is more than one here. Yes — and spiders whose venom is far more destructive than what is in that one.

The Interpreter then looked at her kindly and said: You have spoken the truth. This made Mercy blush, and made the boys cover their faces, because they were all beginning to understand the riddle now.

Then the Interpreter said again: "The spider takes hold with her hands," as you see, "and is in kings' palaces." And why is that written down, except to show you that however full of the venom of sin you may be, still, by the hand of faith you may lay hold of, and live in, the best room that belongs to the King's house above?

Christiana. I did think, said Christiana, of something along those lines; but I could not work it all out. I thought we were like spiders, and that we looked like ugly creatures whatever fine room we were in. But that from this spider — this venomous, ill-favoured creature — we were to learn how to exercise faith, that never came into my mind. And yet she has taken hold with her hands, as I can see, and lives in the best room in the house. God has made nothing in vain.

Then they all seemed glad, but there were tears in their eyes; and they looked at one another, and bowed to the Interpreter.

He then took them into another room where there was a hen with chickens, and told them to watch for a while. So one of the chicks went to the trough to drink; and every time she drank she lifted up her head and her eyes towards heaven.

See, said he, what this little chick does; and learn from her to acknowledge where your mercies come from, by looking up as you receive them.

Now again, said he, watch and look. So they paid attention, and saw that the hen had four different ways of dealing with her chicks. First, she had an everyday call, which she used all day long. Second, she had a special call, which she used only sometimes. Third, she had a brooding note. And fourth, she had a cry of alarm.

Now, said he, compare this hen to your King, and these chicks to those who obey him. Because, answering to her, he has his own methods that he uses towards his people. By his everyday call he gives nothing; by his special call he always has something to give; he has a brooding voice for those who are under his wing; and he has a cry of alarm to give warning when he sees the enemy coming. I chose, my dear ones, to bring you into the room where such things are, because you are women, and these are things you will follow easily.

Christiana. And sir, said Christiana, please let us see some more.

So he took them into the slaughterhouse, where a butcher was killing a sheep; and the sheep was quiet, and took her death patiently. Then the Interpreter said: You must learn from this sheep to suffer, and to bear wrongs without grumbling and complaining. Look how quietly she takes her death, and how without any protest she lets her skin be pulled over her ears. Your King calls you his sheep.

After this he led them into his garden, where there was a great variety of flowers; and he said: Do you see all these?

So Christiana said, Yes.

Then he said again: See how the flowers differ in height, and in quality, and in colour, and in scent, and in usefulness; and some are better than others. And where the gardener has planted them, there they stand, and do not quarrel with one another.

Then he took them into his field, which he had sown with wheat and corn; but when they looked, the tops had all been cut off and only the straw was left. He said again: This ground was manured and ploughed and sown. But what shall we do with the crop?

Then Christiana said: Burn some, and make muck of the rest.

Then the Interpreter said again: Fruit, you see, is the thing you are looking for; and for want of it you condemn the crop to the fire and to be trodden underfoot. Take care that you do not condemn yourselves in the same way.

Then, as they were coming in from outside, they spotted a little robin with a large spider in his beak. So the Interpreter said: Look at this.

So they looked, and Mercy wondered at it; but Christiana said: What a disgrace this is to such a pretty little bird as the robin — a bird above most others, that likes to keep up a sort of friendship with people. I had always thought they lived on breadcrumbs or on other such harmless things. I think worse of him than I did.

The Interpreter then answered: This robin is an emblem, and a very apt one for a certain kind of believer. To look at, they are, like this robin, pretty in their song, their colour and their bearing. They also seem to have a very great love for believers who are sincere, and above everything to want to keep company with them, as if they could live on the good man's crumbs. They claim as well that this is why they frequent the houses of the godly and the appointed services of the Lord. But when they are on their own, like the robin, they can catch and gobble up spiders; they can change their diet, drink wickedness and swallow sin down like water.

So when they had come back into the house — because supper was still not ready — Christiana asked again whether the Interpreter would either show or tell them some other useful things. So the Interpreter began, and said:

The fatter the sow, the more she wants the mud. The fatter the ox, the more friskily he goes to the slaughter. And the healthier and stronger a man is, the more prone he is to evil.

There is a desire in women to go neat and fine; and it is a fitting thing to be adorned with what is of great value in God's sight.

It is easier to sit up one or two nights than to sit up a whole year together. So it is easier to make a good start at professing faith than to hold out to the end as one ought.

Every ship's master, in a storm, will willingly throw overboard whatever is of the smallest value in the vessel. But who would throw the best out first? Nobody but the man who does not fear God.

One leak will sink a ship, and one sin will destroy a sinner.

The man who forgets his friend is ungrateful to him; but the man who forgets his Saviour is merciless to himself.

The man who lives in sin and expects happiness afterwards is like a man who sows weeds and expects to fill his barn with wheat or barley.

If a man wants to live well, let him fetch his last day up close to him, and make it his constant companion.

Whispering, and the changing of people's minds, prove that sin is in the world.

If the world, which God thinks little of, is reckoned by men to be of such value — what is heaven, which God commends?

If this life, which comes with so many troubles, is one we are so unwilling to let go of, what must the life above be?

Everybody will loudly praise the goodness of men. But who is there who is properly moved by the goodness of God?

We seldom sit down to a meal without eating and leaving something. In the same way there is more merit and righteousness in Jesus Christ than the whole world has any need of.

When the Interpreter had finished, he took them out into his garden again and brought them to a tree whose inside was all rotten and gone, and yet it was still growing and had leaves on it.

Then Mercy said: What does this mean?

This tree, said he, whose outside is fine and whose inside is rotten, is what a great many people in the garden of God may be compared to: people who speak up loudly for God with their mouths, but who in fact will do nothing for him; whose leaves are fine, but whose hearts are good for nothing but to be tinder for the devil's tinderbox.

Now supper was ready, the table laid, and everything set out on the board; so they sat down and ate, when somebody had given thanks. And the Interpreter usually entertained those who lodged with him with music at meals; so the musicians played. There was also somebody who sang, and he had a very fine voice. His song was this:

	The Lord alone is my support,
	the one who gives me feed;
	how can I then want anything
	that I might stand in need?

When the song and the music were finished, the Interpreter asked Christiana what it was that first moved her to take up a pilgrim's life.

Christiana answered: First, the loss of my husband came into my mind, which grieved me deeply — but all that was only natural affection. Then, after that, my husband's troubles and his pilgrimage came into my mind, and also how churlishly I had behaved towards him over it. So guilt took hold of my mind, and would have drawn me into the pit; except that just at the right moment I had a dream about my husband's well-being, and a letter sent me by the King of the country where my husband lives, telling me to come to him. The dream and the letter together worked on my mind so much that they forced me onto this road.

Interpreter. But did you meet with no opposition before you left the house?

Christiana. Yes — a neighbour of mine, one Mrs. Timorous. She was related to the man who tried to persuade my husband to turn back for fear of the lions. She called me every kind of fool over what she called my intended desperate adventure, and urged everything she could to put me off it: the hardship and the troubles my husband met with on the road. But I got over all of that fairly well.

But a dream I had about two evil-looking creatures, who I thought were plotting how to make me come to grief on my journey — that has troubled me a great deal. Yes, it still runs in my mind and makes me afraid of everybody I meet, in case they should meet me to do me some harm and turn me off the road.

Indeed, I may tell my Lord, though I would not have everybody know it, that between here and the gate we came in by, we were both so badly attacked that we were made to cry out, Murder! And the two who made that attack on us were like the two I saw in my dream.

Then the Interpreter said: Your beginning is good; your latter end shall greatly increase.

So he turned to Mercy and said to her: And what moved you to come here, my dear?

Then Mercy blushed and trembled, and for a while stayed silent.

Interpreter. Then he said: Do not be afraid — only believe, and speak your mind.

Mercy. So she began, and said: Truly, sir, it is my lack of experience that makes me want to keep quiet; and that is what fills me with fears of falling short at the end. I cannot tell of visions and dreams as my friend Christiana can; nor do I know what it is to mourn over having refused the advice of good relations.

Interpreter. What was it, then, dear heart, that prevailed with you to do what you have done?

Mercy. Why — when our friend here was packing up to leave our town, another woman and I happened to go and see her. So we knocked at the door and went in. And when we were inside and saw what she was doing, we asked her what she meant by it. She said she had been sent for to go to her husband; and then she went on and told us how she had seen him in a dream, living in a wonderful place among immortals, wearing a crown, playing on a harp, eating and drinking at his Prince's table and singing praises to him for bringing him there.

Now while she was telling us these things, I thought my heart burned inside me. And I said in my heart: if this is true, I will leave my father and my mother and the land where I was born, and I will go along with Christiana if she will have me. So I asked her further whether these things were true, and whether she would let me go with her; because I could see now that there was no living any longer in our town without the danger of ruin.

And yet I came away with a heavy heart — not because I was unwilling to come away, but because so many of my relations were left behind. And I have come with all the longing of my heart, and I will go, if I may, along with Christiana, to her husband and his King.

Interpreter. Your setting out is good, because you have given credit to the truth. You are a Ruth, who out of the love she had for Naomi and for the Lord her God left father and mother and the land of her birth, to come out and go with a people she had never known before. "The Lord repay your work, and may a full reward be given you by the Lord God of Israel, under whose wings you have come to trust."

Now supper was over, and the beds were made ready. The women were put to sleep on their own, and the boys by themselves. And when Mercy was in bed she could not sleep for joy, because now her doubts about falling short at the last were further away from her than they had ever been. So she lay there blessing and praising God, who had shown her such favour.

In the morning they rose with the sun and got themselves ready to leave; but the Interpreter wanted them to wait a while, because, he said, you must go from here in proper order.

Then he said to the young woman who had first opened to them: Take them into the garden to the bath, and wash them there, and make them clean of the dirt they have picked up travelling.

Then Innocent took them and brought them into the garden to the bath. So she told them they must wash and be clean there, because that was what her Master wanted the women who called at his house on their way to do. Then they went in and washed — they and the boys and all — and they came out of that bath not only sweet and clean, but with new life and strength in their limbs. So when they came back in, they looked a great deal better than they had when they went out to wash.

When they had come back from the bath in the garden, the Interpreter took them and looked at them and said to them: Fair as the moon.

Then he called for the seal that is used to seal those who have been washed in his bath. So the seal was brought, and he set his mark on them, so that they might be recognized in the places they still had to go. Now the seal was the substance and sum of the Passover that the children of Israel ate when they came out of the land of Egypt; and the mark was set between their eyes. This seal added greatly to their beauty, because it was an ornament to their faces. It added to their dignity too, and made their faces more like the faces of angels.

Then the Interpreter said again to the young woman who was waiting on these women: Go into the vestry and fetch out clothes for these people.

So she went and fetched out white clothing and laid it down in front of him; and he told them to put it on. It was fine linen, white and clean.

When the women had been dressed like this, they seemed almost frightening to one another; because each could not see on herself the glory that she could see on the other. So now they began to think each other better than themselves. "You are lovelier than I am," said one; and "you are more beautiful than I am," said the other. The children too stood amazed at the state they had all been brought into.
