The Cross and the Sepulchre

Now I saw in my dream that the road Christian had to go up was fenced on both sides with a wall, and that wall was called Salvation. So burdened Christian ran up this road — but not without great difficulty, because of the load on his back.

He ran on like that until he came to a place where the ground rose a little; and on that rise stood a cross, and a little below it, at the bottom, a tomb. So I saw in my dream that just as Christian came level with the cross, his burden came loose from his shoulders and fell off his back, and began to roll, and went on rolling until it came to the mouth of the tomb, where it fell in; and I never saw it again.

Then Christian was glad and light, and said with a happy heart, "He has given me rest by his suffering, and life by his death." Then he stood still a while to look and to wonder, because it was a great surprise to him that the sight of the cross should relieve him of his burden like that. So he looked, and looked again, until the springs in his head sent the water running down his cheeks.

Now as he stood there looking and crying, three Shining Ones came to him and greeted him with "Peace be to you." The first said to him, "Your sins are forgiven you." The second stripped off his rags and dressed him "in a change of clothing." The third set a mark on his forehead and gave him a scroll with a seal on it, which he told him to look at as he ran, and to hand in at the Celestial Gate. Then they went on their way.

And Christian gave three leaps for joy, and went on singing:

	This far I came, and all the way I bore
	my sin, and nothing eased the grief I wore
	till I came here. What kind of place is this!
	Must here be the beginning of my bliss?
	Must here the burden fall from off my back?
	Must here the cords that bound it to me crack?
	Blessed the cross, and blessed the tomb — but he
	who was put to shame there, blessed most, for me.

Then I saw in my dream that he went on like this until he came to a low place, where he saw three men a little off the road, fast asleep, with irons on their heels. One of them was called Simple, another Sloth, and the third Presumption.

Christian, seeing them lying there in that state, went over on the chance that he might wake them, and called out: You are like men asleep at the top of a mast, with the Dead Sea underneath you — a gulf with no bottom to it. So wake up and come away; and if you are willing, I will help you get those irons off. He also told them that if the one who "goes about like a roaring lion" came past, they would certainly end up between his teeth.

At that they looked at him and began to answer him, each in his own way. Simple said, "I don't see any danger." Sloth said, "Just a little more sleep." And Presumption said, "Every tub has to stand on its own bottom — what other answer do you expect me to give you?" And so they lay down to sleep again, and Christian went on his way.

Still, it troubled him to think that men in that much danger should think so little of the kindness of somebody who had freely offered to help them — waking them, warning them, and offering to get their irons off. And while he was still troubled about it, he caught sight of two men come tumbling over the wall on the left-hand side of the narrow road, and they came up to him at a good pace. One of them was called Formalist and the other Hypocrisy. So, as I say, they came up alongside him, and he got into conversation with them.

Christian. Gentlemen, where have you come from, and where are you going?

Formalist and Hypocrisy. We were born in the land of Vainglory, and we are going to Mount Zion for the credit of it.

Christian. Why did you not come in at the gate that stands at the beginning of the road? Do you not know it is written that anyone who does not come in by the door, "but climbs up some other way, is a thief and a robber"?

Formalist and Hypocrisy. They said that going round to the gate to get in was reckoned much too long a way about by everybody in their country; and that their usual practice was therefore to take a short cut and climb over the wall, as they had just done.

Christian. But will it not be counted an offence against the Lord of the city we are all bound for, to break his declared will like that?

Formalist and Hypocrisy. They told him that as far as that went, he need not trouble his head about it; because what they did they did by custom, and they could produce evidence, if it came to that, to prove the custom went back more than a thousand years.

Christian. But, said Christian, would your practice stand up in court?

Formalist and Hypocrisy. They told him that a custom of such long standing — over a thousand years — would surely be allowed as legal now by any impartial judge. And besides, they said, if we get onto the road, what does it matter how we got on? If we are on it, we are on it. You are on the road, and came in at the gate as far as we can see; and we are on the road too, and came tumbling over the wall. So how is your position any better than ours?

Christian. I walk by my Master's rule; you walk by the crude workings of your own imaginations. You are already counted thieves by the Lord of this road, so I doubt you will be found honest men at the end of it. You came in by yourselves, without his direction; and you will go out by yourselves, without his mercy.

To this they gave him very little answer; they only told him to look to himself. Then I saw that they all went on, each man his own way, without much conversation between them — except that these two men told Christian that as for laws and observances, they had no doubt they kept them quite as conscientiously as he did. So, they said, we cannot see any difference between you and us, except for that coat on your back, which we suppose some of your neighbours gave you to cover up the shame of being naked.

Christian. Laws and observances will not save you, since you did not come in by the door. And as for this coat on my back, it was given me by the Lord of the place I am going to — and given me, as you say, to cover my nakedness. I take it as a sign of his kindness to me, because I had nothing but rags before. And besides, I comfort myself with this as I go along: surely, I think, when I come to the gate of the city, the Lord of it will recognize me as one of his own, since I have his coat on my back — a coat he gave me the day he stripped me of my rags. What is more, I have a mark on my forehead, which perhaps you have not noticed, put there by one of my Lord's closest companions on the day my burden fell off my shoulders. And I will tell you something else: I was given a sealed scroll that day, to comfort me by reading it as I go along the road, and I was told to hand it in at the Celestial Gate as proof that I am certainly to be let in.

All of which, I suspect, you are without — and without because you did not come in at the gate.

To all this they gave him no answer at all; they only looked at each other and laughed. Then I saw that they all went on, except that Christian kept ahead, and had nobody to talk to but himself — sometimes with a sigh and sometimes cheerfully. He would also often read in the scroll that one of the Shining Ones had given him, and it refreshed him.
