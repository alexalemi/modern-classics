Vanity Fair

Then I saw in my dream that when they had got out of the wilderness, they soon saw a town ahead of them, and the name of that town is Vanity. And at the town there is a fair kept, called Vanity Fair. It is kept all the year round. It is called Vanity Fair because the town where it is held is emptier than emptiness itself, and also because everything sold there, and everything that comes there, is vanity. As the wise man says, "All that comes is vanity."

This fair is no newly set-up business, but a thing of long standing; and I will show you how it began.

Almost five thousand years ago there were pilgrims walking to the Celestial City, just as these two honest men are. And Beelzebub, Apollyon and Legion, with their companions, seeing from the path the pilgrims made that their road to the city lay through this town of Vanity, arranged to set up a fair here — a fair where every kind of vanity should be sold, and one that should last all the year round.

So at this fair all such goods are sold as houses, land, trades, positions, honours, promotions, titles, countries, kingdoms, appetites, pleasures and delights of every sort: whores, pimps, wives, husbands, children, masters, servants, lives, blood, bodies, souls, silver, gold, pearls, precious stones, and anything else you like. And what is more, at this fair there is at all hours to be seen conjuring, swindles, games, plays, fools, apes, cheats and rogues, and of every kind there is.

Here too are to be seen, and for nothing, thefts, murders, adulteries and perjuries — and those of a blood-red colour.

And just as at other, smaller fairs there are the various rows and streets with their own names, where such and such goods are sold, so here likewise there are the proper places, rows and streets — that is to say, countries and kingdoms — where the goods of this fair are most quickly to be found. Here is the Britain Row, the French Row, the Italian Row, the Spanish Row, the German Row, where several sorts of vanities are for sale. But as at other fairs one particular commodity is the leading article of the whole fair, so here the goods of Rome and her merchandise are greatly promoted — only our English nation, along with some others, has taken a dislike to them.

Now as I said, the road to the Celestial City runs right through this town where this lusty fair is kept; and anyone who means to go to the City and yet not go through this town would have to "go out of the world" altogether. The Prince of princes himself, when he was here, went through this town on his way to his own country, and on a fair day at that. Yes, and I think it was Beelzebub, the chief lord of this fair, who invited him to buy some of his vanities — and would have made him lord of the fair, if only he would have paid him homage as he went through the town. Indeed, because he was such a person of honour, Beelzebub took him from street to street and showed him all the kingdoms of the world in a very short time, so that he might, if it were possible, tempt the Blessed One to haggle for some of his goods and buy them. But he had no interest in the merchandise, and so he left the town without laying out so much as a farthing on these vanities.

So this fair is an ancient thing of long standing, and a very great fair. Now these pilgrims, as I said, had no choice but to go through it. Well, so they did. But as soon as they entered the fair, all the people in it were stirred up, and the whole town was in an uproar about them; and that for several reasons.

First, the pilgrims were dressed in a kind of clothing quite different from the clothing of anybody who traded at that fair. So the people of the fair stood and stared at them: some said they were fools, some that they were madmen, and some that they were foreigners.

Second, as they wondered at their clothes, so they wondered at their speech; because hardly anybody could understand what they said. They naturally spoke the language of Canaan, while the people who kept the fair were the men of this world — so that from one end of the fair to the other, each seemed to the other to be talking gibberish.

Third, what bewildered the traders more than a little was that these pilgrims thought nothing at all of their goods. They did not even care to look at them. And when the traders called on them to buy, they would put their fingers in their ears and cry, "Turn my eyes away from looking at vanity," and look upwards, to show that their trade and their business was in heaven.

One man, watching how they behaved, happened to say to them mockingly: What will you buy? But they looked at him gravely and answered, "We buy the truth."

At that, the chance was taken to despise them still more. Some mocked, some jeered, some spoke abusively, and some called on others to hit them. At last things came to an uproar and a great commotion in the fair, so much so that all order broke down. Word was quickly taken to the great man of the fair, who came down at once and appointed some of his most trusted friends to take these men in for examination, since the fair had nearly been overturned on their account.

So the men were brought in for questioning; and those who sat in judgment on them asked where they came from, where they were going, and what they were doing there in such unusual clothes. The men told them that they were pilgrims and strangers in the world, and that they were going to their own country, which was the heavenly Jerusalem; and that they had given the men of the town and the traders no reason at all to abuse them like this and hinder them on their journey — unless it was that when somebody asked them what they would buy, they had said they would buy the truth.

But the men appointed to examine them did not believe they were anything but madmen and lunatics, or else people who had come to throw the whole fair into confusion. So they took them and beat them, and smeared them with filth, and then put them in the cage, so that they might be made a public spectacle to everybody at the fair.

So there they lay for some time, and were made the target of anybody's amusement or spite or revenge, while the great man of the fair went on laughing at everything that happened to them. But the men were patient, and did not give back abuse for abuse but the very opposite — blessing, and giving good words for bad and kindness for injuries done. And some men in the fair who were more observant and less prejudiced than the rest began to check the baser sort and blame them for the constant abuse they were handing out. So those men rounded on them angrily in their turn, reckoning them as bad as the men in the cage, and telling them that they seemed to be in league with them and should be made to share their misfortunes.

The others replied that as far as they could see the men were quiet and sober and meant nobody any harm, and that there were plenty trading in their own fair who deserved the cage a great deal more — yes, and the pillory too — than the men they had been abusing.

So after various words had passed on both sides, with the two men behaving themselves the whole time very wisely and soberly in front of them, they fell to blows among themselves and did each other real harm. Then these two poor men were brought before their examiners again, and charged with being responsible for the recent uproar in the fair.

So they beat them pitilessly, and hung irons on them, and led them up and down the fair in chains, as an example and a warning to everyone else, so that nobody would speak up for them or join them. But Christian and Faithful behaved more wisely still, and took the disgrace and shame that was thrown at them with so much meekness and patience that it won several of the men in the fair over to their side — though only a few, compared with the rest.

This threw the other party into a still greater rage, so that they settled on putting these two men to death. So they announced that the cage and the irons would not do for them after all, but that they should die for the wrong they had done and for leading the men of the fair astray.

Then they were sent back to the cage again until further orders should be given about them. So they put them in, and fastened their feet in the stocks.

And here they called to mind again what they had heard from their faithful friend Evangelist, and were all the more settled in their road and in their sufferings by what he had told them would happen. They also comforted each other now with the thought that whichever of them it fell to to suffer, that one would have the better of it. So each man privately wished that he might be the one given that privilege — but committing themselves to the all-wise ordering of the one who rules everything, they stayed contentedly in the state they were in, until something else should be settled about them.
