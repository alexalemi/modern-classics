Talkative

Moreover, I saw in my dream that as they went on, Faithful happened to look to one side and saw a man whose name is Talkative walking a little way off beside them; because in this place there was room enough for them all to walk. He was a tall man, and rather better-looking at a distance than close to. Faithful spoke to him like this.

Faithful. Friend, where are you off to? Are you going to the heavenly country?

Talkative. I am going to the same place.

Faithful. That is good; then I hope we may have the pleasure of your company.

Talkative. I shall be very glad indeed to be your companion.

Faithful. Come along, then, and let us go together, and spend our time discussing things that will do us good.

Talkative. Talking about good things is very agreeable to me, whether with you or with anybody else; and I am glad to have met people who are inclined to such a good occupation. Because, to tell you the truth, there are very few who care to spend their time that way as they travel — most would far rather be talking about things that do nobody any good. And that has been a grief to me.

Faithful. That is indeed something to be sorry about. Because what things on earth are so worthy of a man's tongue and mouth as the things of the God of heaven?

Talkative. I like you enormously, because what you say carries real conviction. And I will add: what is so pleasant, and what so profitable, as talking about the things of God? What things are so pleasant — that is, if a man takes any delight in things that are wonderful? For instance, if a man delights in talking about the history of things, or the mystery of them; or if a man loves to talk of miracles, wonders and signs — where will he find things recorded so delightfully, and so sweetly written, as in the Holy Scriptures?

Faithful. That is true. But being made better by such things in our talk should be what we are aiming at.

Talkative. That is exactly what I said. Because talking about such things is most profitable; a man may get knowledge of a great many things that way — of the emptiness of earthly things, for instance, and the value of the things above. That is speaking generally; but more particularly, by this a man may learn the necessity of the new birth, the insufficiency of our own works, the need of Christ's righteousness, and so on. Besides that, by talk a man may learn what it is to repent, to believe, to pray, to suffer, and the like. By this too a man may learn what the great promises and consolations of the Gospel are, to his own comfort. And further, by this a man may learn to refute false opinions, to defend the truth, and to instruct the ignorant.

Faithful. All this is true, and I am glad to hear these things from you.

Talkative. Alas — it is the lack of it that is the reason so few understand the need of faith, and the necessity of a work of grace in their souls if they are to have eternal life, but live on in ignorance under the works of the law, by which no man can possibly obtain the kingdom of heaven.

Faithful. But, if you will allow me — a heavenly knowledge of these things is the gift of God. No man arrives at it by his own effort, or merely by talking about them.

Talkative. All this I know very well. Because a man can receive nothing unless it is given him from heaven; it is all of grace, and not of works. I could give you a hundred scriptures to confirm it.

Faithful. Well then, said Faithful, what is the one subject we shall build our conversation on this time?

Talkative. Whatever you like. I will talk of things heavenly or things earthly; things moral or things evangelical; things sacred or things profane; things past or things to come; things foreign or things at home; things more essential or things merely circumstantial — so long as it is all done to our profit.

Faithful. Now Faithful began to wonder; and stepping over to Christian, who had been walking by himself all this while, he said to him quietly: What a splendid companion we have got! Surely this man will make an excellent pilgrim.

Christian. At this Christian smiled modestly, and said: This man you are so taken with will fool twenty people who do not know him, with that tongue of his.

Faithful. Do you know him, then?

Christian. Know him! Yes — better than he knows himself.

Faithful. Tell me — what is he?

Christian. His name is Talkative. He lives in our town. I am surprised you should be a stranger to him — though I suppose our town is a large one.

Faithful. Whose son is he? And whereabouts does he live?

Christian. He is the son of a man called Say-well, and he lived in Prating Row; and everybody who knows him knows him as Talkative of Prating Row. And for all his fine tongue, he is a poor specimen.

Faithful. Well, he seems a very fine man.

Christian. That is to people who do not know him thoroughly. He is at his best away from home; near home he is ugly enough. Your saying he is a fine man puts me in mind of something I have noticed in painters' work: some pictures look their best at a distance, and are a good deal less pleasant close up.

Faithful. But I am inclined to think you are only joking, because you smiled.

Christian. God forbid that I should joke about this — although I did smile — or that I should accuse anybody falsely. Let me tell you more about him. This man is for any company and any kind of talk. Just as he is talking with you now, so he will talk on the alehouse bench; and the more drink he has in his head, the more of these things he has in his mouth. Religion has no place in his heart, or his house, or his way of life. Everything he has is in his tongue, and his religion consists of making a noise with it.

Faithful. Do you say so? Then I have been badly taken in by this man.

Christian. Taken in! You may be sure of it. Remember the proverb, "They say, and do not." But "the kingdom of God is not in word, but in power." He talks about prayer, about repentance, about faith and about the new birth — but talking about them is all he can do. I have been in his family, and I have watched him both at home and away from it, and I know that what I am telling you about him is the truth. His house is as empty of religion as the white of an egg is of flavour. There is no prayer there, and no sign of any repentance for sin. In fact the animals, according to their kind, serve God far better than he does. He is the stain, the reproach and the shame of religion to everybody who knows him; religion can hardly get a good word said for it in that whole end of town, because of him. This is what the ordinary people who know him say: a saint abroad and a devil at home.

His poor family find it so. He is such a boor, such an abuser of his servants and so unreasonable with them, that they neither know how to do anything for him nor how to speak to him. People who have any business dealings with him say they would rather deal with a Turk than with him, because they would get fairer treatment. This Talkative will go one better than any of them, if he possibly can — cheat them, trick them and get the better of them. Besides that, he brings his sons up to follow in his footsteps; and if he finds in any of them what he calls a foolish timidity — which is his name for the first stirrings of a tender conscience — he calls them fools and blockheads, and will not employ them in anything much, or speak well of them in front of others. For my part, I believe that by his wicked life he has caused many people to stumble and fall, and that unless God prevents it he will be the ruin of many more.

Faithful. Well, my brother, I am bound to believe you — not only because you say you know him, but because you give your account of people like a Christian. I cannot think you say these things out of ill-will, but because it really is as you say.

Christian. If I had known him no better than you do, I might perhaps have thought of him as you did at first. And what is more, if this report had come only from people who are enemies of religion, I should have taken it for slander — the sort of thing that often falls from bad men's mouths onto good men's names and reputations. But all these things, and a great many more just as bad, I can prove him guilty of from my own knowledge. Besides, good men are ashamed of him. They can call him neither brother nor friend; the very mention of his name among them makes them blush, if they know him.

Faithful. Well, I see that saying and doing are two different things, and in future I shall keep a better eye on that distinction.

Christian. They are two different things indeed, and as different as the soul and the body. Because just as the body without the soul is nothing but a dead carcass, so saying, if it stands alone, is a dead carcass too. The soul of religion is the practical part: "Pure and undefiled religion before God the Father is this: to visit the fatherless and the widows in their affliction, and to keep oneself unstained by the world." Talkative has no idea of this. He thinks that hearing and saying will make a good Christian, and so he deceives his own soul.

Hearing is only the sowing of the seed; and talking is not enough to prove that there is any fruit in the heart and the life. And let us be quite sure of it: on the day of judgment men will be judged according to their fruit. It will not be asked then, did you believe? — but, were you doers, or talkers only? And they will be judged accordingly. The end of the world is compared to our harvest; and you know that at harvest men take notice of nothing but the fruit. Not that anything can be accepted that does not come from faith; but I say this to show you how worthless Talkative's profession will be on that day.

Faithful. That brings to my mind what Moses says, when he describes the clean animal. It is one that both divides the hoof and chews the cud — not one that only divides the hoof, or only chews the cud. The hare chews the cud, and yet it is unclean, because it does not divide the hoof. And that really is a picture of Talkative. He chews the cud: he goes looking for knowledge, he chews over the word. But he does not divide the hoof; he does not part company with the way of sinners. Like the hare, he keeps the foot of a dog or a bear, and so he is unclean.

Christian. You have given, as far as I know, the true Gospel sense of those texts. And I will add another thing. Paul calls some men — and great talkers too — "sounding brass and a clanging cymbal"; which is, as he explains it elsewhere, "lifeless things giving sound." Lifeless things, that is, without the true faith and grace of the Gospel; and so, things that will never be given a place in the kingdom of heaven among the children of life, even though the sound they make in their talk might be the tongue or the voice of an angel.

Faithful. Well, I was not so very fond of his company at first, and I am thoroughly sick of it now. What are we to do to be rid of him?

Christian. Take my advice and do as I tell you, and you will find that he will soon be sick of your company too — unless God touches his heart and turns it.

Faithful. What would you have me do?

Christian. Why, go to him, and start a serious conversation about the power of religion. And when he has approved of it — which he will — ask him plainly whether this thing is set up in his heart, his house and his way of life.

Faithful. Then Faithful stepped forward again and said to Talkative: Well, how are things? How goes it now?

Talkative. Thank you, well. I thought we should have had a great deal of talk by this time.

Faithful. Well, if you like, we can set about it now. And since you left it to me to name the question, let it be this: how does the saving grace of God show itself, when it is in a man's heart?

Talkative. I see, then, that our talk is to be about the power of things. Well, it is a very good question, and I shall be glad to answer you. And take my answer briefly, like this. First, where the grace and work of God is in the heart, it produces a great outcry against sin. Secondly —

Faithful. No, wait — let us take one at a time. I think you should rather say that it shows itself by inclining the soul to hate its sin.

Talkative. Why, what difference is there between crying out against sin and hating it?

Faithful. Oh, a great deal. A man may cry out against sin as a matter of policy; but he cannot hate it except by a real godly loathing of it. I have heard many men cry out against sin in the pulpit who can put up with it perfectly well in their hearts, their houses and their way of living. Joseph's mistress cried out at the top of her voice as though she were the holiest of women — and would gladly, for all that, have committed the sin with him. Some cry out against sin the way a mother cries out at the child in her lap, calling it a filthy, naughty girl, and then falls to hugging and kissing it.

Talkative. You are trying to catch me out, I see.

Faithful. No, not I. I only want to get things straight. But what is the second thing by which you would prove there is a work of grace in the heart?

Talkative. Great knowledge of Gospel mysteries.

Faithful. This sign should have come first; but first or last, it is false as well. Because knowledge, and great knowledge, can be got in the mysteries of the Gospel with no work of grace in the soul at all. In fact, a man may have all knowledge and still be nothing, and so be no child of God.

When Christ said, "Do you know all these things?" and the disciples answered yes, he added, "You are blessed if you do them." He does not put the blessing in knowing them, but in doing them. Because there is a kind of knowledge that is not accompanied by doing: "He that knows his master's will, and does not do it." A man may know like an angel and still be no Christian; so your sign is not a true one. Knowing is a thing that pleases talkers and boasters, certainly; but doing is what pleases God. Not that the heart can be good without knowledge — without that the heart is worthless. So there is knowledge and there is knowledge: knowledge that rests in the bare theory of things, and knowledge that comes along with the grace of faith and love, which sets a man to doing the will of God from the heart. The first will do well enough for the talker; but without the second the true Christian is not satisfied. "Give me understanding, and I shall keep your law; yes, I shall observe it with my whole heart."

Talkative. You are trying to catch me out again. This is not building anybody up.

Faithful. Well, if you would rather, put forward another sign of how this work of grace shows itself where it exists.

Talkative. Not I — because I can see we are not going to agree.

Faithful. Well, if you will not, will you allow me to do it?

Talkative. You may do as you please.

Faithful. A work of grace in the soul shows itself either to the person who has it, or to those standing by.

To the person who has it, in this way. It gives him a conviction of sin — especially of the corruption of his own nature and the sin of unbelief, on account of which he is certain to be damned if he does not find mercy at God's hand through faith in Jesus Christ. Seeing and feeling this works sorrow and shame for sin in him. He finds, further, the Saviour of the world revealed in him, and the absolute necessity of closing with him for life; at which he finds himself hungering and thirsting after him — and it is to that hunger and thirst that the promise is made. Now, according to how strong or weak his faith in his Saviour is, so is his joy and his peace, so is his love of holiness, so is his desire to know him better and to serve him in this world.

But although I say it shows itself to him like this, it is still only rarely that he is able to conclude that this is a work of grace; because the corruption still in him, and the way his reason has been damaged, make his mind misjudge in this matter. So in the man who has this work, a very sound judgment is needed before he can steadily conclude that this is a work of grace.

To other people, it shows itself in this way.

First, by a confession of his faith in Christ that comes out of experience.

Second, by a life that matches that confession: a life of holiness — holiness in the heart; holiness in the family, if he has one; and holiness in his way of life out in the world. Which in general teaches him inwardly to hate his sin, and to hate himself for it, in private; to put it down in his family; and to promote holiness out in the world — not by talk only, as a hypocrite or a talkative person may do, but by a practical submission, in faith and love, to the power of the Word.

And now, sir, as to this brief description of the work of grace and of how it shows itself: if you have anything to object, object. If not, then allow me to put a second question to you.

Talkative. No — my part now is not to object but to listen. So let me have your second question.

Faithful. It is this. Do you know this first part of the description in your own experience? And does your life and your conduct bear the same witness? Or does your religion stand in word and tongue, and not in deed and truth? Please, if you are inclined to answer me, say nothing more than you know the God above will say amen to, and nothing but what your own conscience can back you up in. "Because it is not the man who commends himself who is approved, but the man the Lord commends." Besides, to say I am this and that, when my own conduct and all my neighbours tell me I am lying, is great wickedness.

Talkative. Then Talkative began to go red; but recovering himself, he answered like this. You are coming now to experience, and conscience, and God, and appealing to him to justify what is said. This kind of conversation is not what I expected, and I am not disposed to answer questions like these, because I do not consider myself under any obligation to — unless you are setting yourself up as my catechist. And even if you did, I might still refuse to make you my judge. But tell me, please: why do you ask me such questions?

Faithful. Because I saw you were forward to talk, and because I did not know that you had anything but theory. Besides — to tell you the whole truth — I have heard of you, that you are a man whose religion is in his talk, and that your way of living gives the lie to everything your mouth professes. They say you are a stain among Christians; that religion is the worse off for your ungodly conduct; that some have already stumbled over your wicked ways, and that more are in danger of being destroyed by them. Your religion, and an alehouse, and greed, and impurity, and swearing, and lying, and keeping empty company, all stand together quite comfortably. The proverb that is said of a whore is true of you — that she is a shame to all women; so you are a shame to all who profess this faith.

Talkative. Since you are so ready to take up rumours and to judge as rashly as you do, I cannot help concluding that you are some peevish or melancholy man, not fit to be talked with. And so, goodbye.

Christian. Then Christian came up and said to his brother: I told you how it would turn out. Your words and his appetites could not agree. He would rather leave your company than reform his life. But he is gone, as I said. Let him go; the loss is nobody's but his own. He has saved us the trouble of leaving him — because carrying on as he is, which I suppose he will, he would only have been a blot on our company. Besides, the apostle says, "From such people turn away."

Faithful. But I am glad we had this short conversation with him. It may be he will think about it again. However that may be, I have dealt plainly with him, and so I am clear of his blood if he is lost.

Christian. You did well to talk to him as plainly as you did. There is very little of this honest dealing with people nowadays, and that is what makes religion stink in so many people's nostrils. It is these talkative fools, whose religion is all words while their conduct is corrupt and empty, who — being allowed so far into the fellowship of the godly — baffle the world, disgrace Christianity and grieve the sincere. I wish everybody would deal with such people as you have done. Then they would either be brought more into line with religion, or the company of the saints would get too hot for them.

Then Faithful said:

	How Talkative at first lifts up his plumes!
	How splendidly he speaks! How he presumes
	to sweep down all before him! But as soon
	as Faithful talks of heart-work, like the moon
	that's past the full, he dwindles as he goes.
	And so will all — except who heart-work knows.

So they went on talking about what they had seen along the way, and made easy going of a stretch that would otherwise, no doubt, have been tedious to them; because now they were going through a wilderness.

Now when they had got almost right out of this wilderness, Faithful happened to glance behind him and spotted somebody coming after them — and he knew him. Oh, said Faithful to his brother, who is that coming there? Then Christian looked, and said: It is my good friend Evangelist. Yes, and my good friend too, said Faithful, because it was he who set me on the road to the gate.

Now Evangelist came up to them, and greeted them like this.

Evangelist. Peace be with you, dearly beloved; and peace to those who help you.

Christian. Welcome, welcome, my good Evangelist. The sight of your face brings back to me your old kindness and your tireless labour for my eternal good.

Faithful. And a thousand times welcome, said good Faithful. Your company, dear Evangelist — how welcome it is to us poor pilgrims!

Evangelist. Then Evangelist said: How has it gone with you, my friends, since we last parted? What have you met with, and how have you conducted yourselves?

Then Christian and Faithful told him everything that had happened to them on the road, and how, and with what difficulty, they had got as far as that place.

Evangelist. I am very glad, said Evangelist — not that you have met with trials, but that you have come through them as victors, and that in spite of many weaknesses you have kept to the road to this very day.

I say I am very glad of it, both for my own sake and for yours. I have sown and you have reaped; and the day is coming when both the one who sowed and those who reaped shall rejoice together — that is, if you hold out, "for in due season you shall reap, if you do not faint." The crown is ahead of you, and it is one that cannot spoil; "so run, that you may obtain it." There are some who set out for this crown, and after they have gone a long way for it, somebody else comes in and takes it from them. So hold fast to what you have; let nobody take your crown.

You are not out of the devil's gunshot yet. You have not resisted to the point of blood in your struggle against sin. Keep the kingdom always in front of you, and believe steadily in the things that cannot be seen. Let nothing on this side of the other world get inside you. And above everything, keep good watch on your own hearts and on the appetites in them, "because they are deceitful above all things, and desperately wicked." Set your faces like flint; you have all the power in heaven and earth on your side.

Christian. Then Christian thanked him for his encouragement, but told him at the same time that they would like him to say more to them, to help them through the rest of the road — and all the more because they knew perfectly well that he was a prophet and could tell them about things that might happen to them, and how they might resist and overcome them. Faithful agreed with the request. So Evangelist began, as follows.

Evangelist. My sons, you have heard in the words of the truth of the Gospel that you must go through many troubles to enter the kingdom of heaven. And again, that in every city chains and afflictions are waiting for you. So you cannot expect to go long on your pilgrimage without them, in one form or another. You have already found something of the truth of these testimonies for yourselves, and more will follow immediately. Because now, as you see, you are almost out of this wilderness, and so you will soon come to a town that you will shortly see ahead of you; and in that town you will be set upon hard by enemies who will strain every nerve to kill you. And you may be certain that one or both of you must seal the testimony you hold with your blood. But be faithful to the point of death, and the King will give you a crown of life.

The one who dies there — although his death will be a violent one, and his pain perhaps great — will still have the better of his companion. Not only because he will arrive at the Celestial City soonest, but because he will escape a great many miseries that the other will meet with in the rest of his journey.

But when you have come to the town and find fulfilled what I have described to you here, then remember your friend, and quit yourselves like men, and commit the keeping of your souls to your God in well-doing, as to a faithful Creator.
