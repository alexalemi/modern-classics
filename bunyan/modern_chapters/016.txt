Demas, and the Pillar of Salt

Then I saw in my dream that a little off the road, opposite the silver mine, stood Demas, looking every inch a gentleman, calling to passers-by to come and look. He said to Christian and his companion: Hey there! Turn aside this way, and I will show you something.

Christian. What is so worth seeing that it would justify turning us off our road?

Demas. Here is a silver mine, and some men digging in it for treasure. If you will come, then for a little trouble you may set yourselves up very handsomely.

Hopeful. Then Hopeful said: Let us go and look.

Christian. Not I, said Christian. I have heard of this place before now, and how many have been killed at it. And besides that, treasure is a trap to those who go looking for it, because it holds them back on their pilgrimage.

Then Christian called out to Demas, saying: Is the place not dangerous? Has it not held a good many people back on their pilgrimage?

Demas. Not very dangerous — except to the careless. (But he went red as he said it.)

Christian. Then Christian said to Hopeful: Let us not move a single step, but keep straight on our way.

Hopeful. I will guarantee you that when By-ends comes up, if he gets the same invitation we did, he will turn aside to have a look.

Christian. No doubt about it, because his principles lead him that way; and it is a hundred to one that he dies there.

Demas. Then Demas called again, saying: But will you not come over and look?

Christian. Then Christian answered him flatly, saying: Demas, you are an enemy to the right ways of the Lord of this road, and you have already been condemned for turning aside yourself by one of his Majesty's judges. Why are you trying to bring us in for the same condemnation? Besides, if we turn aside at all, our Lord the King will certainly hear about it, and will put us to shame there, where we would rather stand before him with confidence.

Demas called out again that he was one of their brotherhood too, and that if they would wait a little he would come and walk with them himself.

Christian. Then Christian said: What is your name? Is it not the same one I called you by?

Demas. Yes, my name is Demas. I am a son of Abraham.

Christian. I know you. Gehazi was your great-grandfather and Judas your father, and you have walked in their footsteps. This is nothing but a devilish trick you are playing. Your father was hanged as a traitor, and you deserve no better reward. You may be sure that when we come to the King, we shall give him word of this behaviour of yours.

So they went on their way.

By this time By-ends and his companions had come back into sight, and at the first signal they went straight over to Demas. Now whether they fell into the pit by leaning over the edge of it, or whether they went down to dig, or whether they were smothered at the bottom by the foul air that usually rises in such places, I am not certain. But this much I noticed: they were never seen on the road again.

Then Christian sang:

	By-ends and silver Demas both agree;
	one calls, the other runs, so he may be
	a sharer in his profit. Both of these
	settle for this world, and go no further on.

Now I saw that just on the other side of this plain, the Pilgrims came to a place where an old monument stood close by the roadside; and they were both troubled at the sight of it, because the shape of it was so strange. It seemed to them to be a woman transformed into a pillar. So they stood there looking at it, and looking again, and for some time could not tell what to make of it.

At last Hopeful noticed writing above its head, in an unfamiliar hand. But being no scholar, he called Christian over, who was the educated one, to see whether he could pick out the meaning. So Christian came, and after piecing the letters together for a little he found it was this: "Remember Lot's wife."

So he read it out to his companion; after which they both concluded that this was the pillar of salt that Lot's wife had been turned into, for looking back with a greedy heart as she was leaving Sodom for safety. And this sudden and astonishing sight gave them the occasion for this conversation.

Christian. Ah, my brother — this is a well-timed sight! It came just when we needed it, right after the invitation Demas gave us to come over and look at the Hill Lucre. If we had gone over as he wanted us to, and as you were inclining to do, my brother, then for all I know we would have been made like this woman ourselves — a spectacle for everybody who came after us to stare at.

Hopeful. I am sorry I was so foolish, and I am left wondering that I am not standing here now as Lot's wife is. Because what was the difference between her sin and mine? She only looked back — and I wanted to go over and see. Let grace be praised, and let me be ashamed that such a thing was ever in my heart at all.

Christian. Let us take note of what we see here, to help us in time to come. This woman escaped one judgment, since she did not die in the destruction of Sodom; and yet she was destroyed by another, as we can see — she is turned into a pillar of salt.

Hopeful. True. And she can serve us as both a warning and an example: a warning, that we should keep clear of her sin; and a sign of what judgment will overtake those who are not stopped by the warning. In the same way Korah, Dathan and Abiram, with the two hundred and fifty men who died in their sin, also became a sign or an example to others to beware.

But above all, one thing puzzles me: how Demas and his companions can stand over there so confidently, looking for the very treasure that this woman was turned into a pillar of salt merely for looking back after — because we do not read that she took so much as one step off the road. And all the more since the judgment that came on her made her an example within sight of where they are standing; they cannot help seeing her, if they would only lift their eyes.

Christian. It is a thing to wonder at, and it shows that their hearts have grown desperate in the matter. I cannot think who to compare them to so exactly as to people who pick pockets in the presence of the judge, or cut purses under the gallows. It is said of the men of Sodom that they were sinners exceedingly, because they were sinners before the Lord — that is, in his sight, and in spite of all the kindnesses he had shown them; because the land of Sodom was in those days like the garden of Eden. That is what provoked him all the more to jealousy, and made their punishment as hot as the fire of the Lord out of heaven could make it.

And it is entirely reasonable to conclude that people like this — sinning in full view, and in the teeth of the very examples set constantly in front of them to warn them off — must come in for the severest judgments there are.

Hopeful. You have certainly spoken the truth. But what a mercy it is that neither you, and above all not I, has been made into this example! This gives us reason to thank God, to fear him, and always to remember Lot's wife.
