FORWARD REFERENCES TO HONOUR

- The burden. It is on Christian's back from the first paragraph and
  falls off by itself at the cross (file 006). Everything before 006
  should keep the reader feeling its weight; nothing before 006 may
  relieve it.
- The key called Promise (file 017) has been in Christian's pocket the
  whole time. Do not foreshadow it. Do not explain it when it appears.
- The certificate / roll with a seal, given at the cross (006), lost on
  the Hill Difficulty (007), and demanded at the gate (020). Ignorance
  has none, which is why he is turned away. Use ONE word for it
  throughout — "certificate" at the gate, "scroll" in Christian's hand —
  and make the identity unmistakable.
- Part Two retraces Part One's road exactly. Every landmark Christiana
  reaches must be named as Christian's version named it, and Great-heart
  frequently tells the story of what happened there the first time. The
  two accounts must agree.
- Mr. Fearing, Mr. Feeble-mind, Mr. Ready-to-halt, Mr. Despondency and
  Much-afraid all get across the river in Part Two. That is the answer
  to Part One and it should read as one.


OPEN QUESTIONS

- "Celestial City" vs "the City": Bunyan uses both. Keep both; the short
  form only where the referent is unmistakable.
- Bunyan's marginal glosses are not in the Standard Ebooks text and are
  not being restored.


DECIDED IN THE COURSE OF THE WORK

- A FALSE FRIEND INSIDE A MARKED SCRIPTURE QUOTATION IS STILL A FALSE
  FRIEND, AND MEANING WINS. "Strive to enter in at the strait gate"
  (003) becomes "the narrow gate". The rule that a quoted scripture
  keeps its Authorised Version shape yields here: a modern reader reads
  "strait" as "straight" and gets the sense backwards, which is the
  exact failure this whole volume exists to prevent. Apply the same
  test to any other quotation carrying want / prevent / conversation /
  let / suffer.
- Speaker tags for a two-word name are given in full and unhyphenated
  where Bunyan hyphenates the name itself: "Worldly-Wiseman." keeps its
  hyphen, "Great-heart." keeps its lower-case h.
- Bunyan writes "Mr. Worldly-wiseman" with a small w; we capitalise
  throughout as "Mr. Worldly-Wiseman", which is the form the phrase has
  in modern English and the form must_contain.txt checks.
- Christian's cry at the end of 003 is "I am finished — there is no hope
  for me!" for "Woe is me, for I am undone!" Keep this register for his
  moments of collapse: plain and modern, never archaic-poetic.

- SOURCE NAME VARIANTS ARE NORMALISED TO THE FIRST FORM USED.
  Bunyan is loose with two names and we are not:
    "Formalist" (006) vs "Formality" (008)  -> always FORMALIST
    "Slough of Despond" vs Apollyon's "Gulf of Despond" (009)
                                            -> always SLOUGH OF DESPOND
  The reader has only the assembled page and cannot tell a variant from
  a second place.
- THE SCROLL. Locked as "scroll" in Christian's hands throughout
  (006 onward), "my evidence" where he speaks of what it proves (008),
  and it is handed in at the Celestial Gate. Never "roll".
- The shelter halfway up the Hill Difficulty is a "shelter", not an
  "arbour" (007); Part Two returns to it and must match.
- "professor" (008) is a FALSE FRIEND — one who professes faith, not an
  academic. Rendered "believer". Watch for it again in Part Two, where
  Bunyan uses it more often.
- Bunyan's own "conversation" test case lands in 008: "a man by his
  conversation may soon overthrow what by argument he doth labour to
  fasten upon others" -> "a man's own conduct can quickly knock down
  what he is labouring by argument to fix in other people's minds".
- Christian's original name is Graceless (008). Keep.
- VERSE POLICY IN PRACTICE: rhyme is kept where it comes free and
  dropped rather than padded, and the metre is allowed to stay rough.
  Bunyan's Apology jokes about his own verse; do not out-write him.

- THE FLATTERER (018) — A DELIBERATE CALL, MADE ONCE, APPLY IT AGAIN IF
  IT RECURS. Bunyan's Flatterer is "a man, black of flesh, but covered
  with a very light robe", later "a black man, clothed in white", and
  the Shining One immediately identifies him: "It is Flatterer, a false
  apostle, that hath transformed himself into an angel of light."
  The referent is A FIEND. Black here is the standard Puritan emblem
  for a devil, set against the borrowed robe of light — the colour IS
  the allegory, and Bunyan's readers took it that way.
  Rendered "black of body" / "the black figure", NOT "a black man".
  Reason: "a black man" in modern English names a person's race, which
  is not what the sentence says and not who the character is; it would
  tell the reader something false while looking faithful. Keeping the
  emblem (darkness under a robe of light) and dropping "man" renders
  the MEANING, which is the whole job of this volume. This is the same
  reasoning as ball/'s 1833 Leonid passage: translate what the sentence
  actually does, do not transcribe a word whose sense has moved.
  NOT a softening — nothing is cut and the colour stays.

- THE ETHIOPIAN EMBLEM (035) — KEPT, AND DELIBERATELY. On Mount
  Charity the Shepherds show "one Fool, and one Want-wit, washing of an
  Ethiopian, with intention to make him white". This is the classical
  proverb for attempting the impossible (Aesop, and a commonplace by
  Bunyan's day), and the Shepherds gloss it on the spot as being about
  THE VILE PERSON and about hypocrites — a claim about character, not
  about race.
  Rendered faithfully, with no note and no softening. This is NOT the
  same case as the Flatterer in 018: there, "black" named a devil and
  "a black man" would have misidentified the character, so rendering
  the meaning required dropping "man". Here the proverb's whole
  mechanism IS the man's origin, so there is no rendering that keeps
  the emblem and removes it. Cutting it would be bowdlerising, and
  adding an editorial note would be the wink the Verne rule forbids.
  Kept as Bunyan's, and flagged to the reader of these notes.
