Jules Verne published A Journey to the Center of the Earth in 1864, early in the long run of adventure novels his publisher Hetzel issued as the Voyages extraordinaires. It begins in Hamburg in May 1863, in the house of Professor Otto Lidenbrock, a mineralogist with a bad temper and a first-rate mind, who has just bought a secondhand Icelandic manuscript. A scrap of parchment falls out of it covered in runes. The runes turn out to be a coded Latin message left by a sixteenth-century alchemist named Arne Saknussemm, and it gives directions: go down into the crater of a dead volcano in Iceland, and you will reach the center of the Earth.

The narrator is Lidenbrock's nephew Axel, who does not want to go, says so at length, and goes. He is the reason the book works. He is frightened for most of it, he is honest about being frightened, and his running commentary on his uncle's certainties is funny in a way Verne's reputation does not prepare anyone for. The third man is Hans, an Icelandic eider-hunter hired as a guide, who says almost nothing across the whole descent and saves the party repeatedly without appearing to hurry.

What is underneath was an open scientific question in 1864, not a fantasy. Verne has his two geologists argue it out on the page: one holds that the Earth's interior is molten and that the heat rises steadily with depth, which would make the trip impossible, and the other does not. The arguments are real arguments of the period, and none of the science has been quietly corrected here. Neither have the numbers — Verne tracks depth, temperature, barometric pressure and compass bearing the whole way down, and the tracking is what makes a hole in the ground feel like a place.

English readers were badly served by this book for well over a century. The Victorian translations cut and rewrote it freely, and one went so far as to rename the characters, which is why some older editions have a Professor Hardwigg and a nephew called Harry. This edition is made from Verne's French and is complete, as are the two other Verne novels in this collection. Saknussemm's runes and his Latin are kept exactly as printed, along with the puzzle of how to read them, because the decipherment in the opening chapters is one of the best things in Verne and it is meant to be worked through rather than watched.
