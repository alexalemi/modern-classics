ON LIBERTY (1859) — TRANSLATION STRATEGY
========================================

WHY THIS BOOK NEEDS A RETELLING
-------------------------------
It is the hume/ case almost exactly, and it is worth stating precisely,
because the wrong diagnosis produces the wrong translation.

    archaism   0.6 per 1,000 words   (below books struck as too clean)
    calque    34.6                   (screening pass, ROADMAP.md)
    sentences 44% over 35 words

Nothing in On Liberty sounds old. There is no thou, no hath, barely an
archaism at all. What blocks it is two things:

1. SENTENCE ARCHITECTURE. Mill was trained to write Victorian periodic
   prose, and he qualifies as he goes: subordinate clause inside
   subordinate clause, the main verb arriving late, three parenthetical
   concessions before the claim. The first sentence of Chapter One
   spends forty words saying what the book is NOT about. This is the
   bulk of the work: unstack the periods, put the subject and verb
   where a reader can find them, break the longest into two or three.

2. TECHNICAL VOCABULARY USED WITH TOTAL CONSISTENCY — the epictetus
   class 3, the one the archaism measure cannot see at all. "Utility"
   is not usefulness, it is Bentham's greatest-happiness standard, and
   Mill stakes the whole argument on it in Chapter One. "Expediency" is
   not convenience. "Opinion" is belief, held and asserted. "Sentiment"
   is settled feeling or attitude, not sentimentality. "Interest" is a
   stake, not curiosity. "Self-regarding" is Mill's own coinage for the
   part of conduct that concerns only yourself, and it is the hinge of
   the whole book.

AND THE MEASURE OVERSTATES IT — THE hume RULE APPLIES IN FULL
-------------------------------------------------------------
Mill's famous sentences are already perfect and already modern:

    "Over himself, over his own body and mind, the individual is
    sovereign."
    "He who knows only his own side of the case, knows little of that."
    "The only freedom which deserves the name, is that of pursuing our
    own good in our own way."

THE GOVERNING RULE IS THEREFORE THE REVERSE OF THIS PROJECT'S USUAL
ONE: IF A SENTENCE IS ALREADY CLEAR, LEAVE IT ALONE. A file in which
every sentence has been rewritten has been damaged, however defensible
each rewrite is on its own. The obstruction is the apparatus AROUND the
argument — the throat-clearing that opens a section, the periodic
set-up sentence, the triple qualification — and not the argument. Several
must_contain pins are pinned UNCHANGED for exactly this reason.

FALSE FRIENDS — THE bunyan CLASS, AND THERE ARE MORE THAN EXPECTED
------------------------------------------------------------------
A reader who trips on a word looks it up. These do not trip anyone.

    suffer          = allow, permit ("suffering each other to live as
                      seems good to themselves" — the book's warmest
                      sentence turns on it)
    vulgarly        = commonly, popularly (no contempt intended)
    want            = lack
    indifferent     = not caring either way, or morally neutral
    peculiar        = specific to, characteristic of — NOT odd
    prevent         = go before (rare here, but present)
    obtain          = hold good, be in force ("the rules which obtain")
    sensible of     = aware of
    pretension      = claim
    disposition     = tendency
    education       = upbringing in the broadest sense
    improvement     = development; Mill's progressive-being standard
    charity         = tolerance, in the phrase "extend their charity"
    genius          = a person of original power AND the quality itself;
                      Mill uses both senses in Chapter Three, deliberately

MILL'S ITALICS ARE LOAD-BEARING — 52 SPANS, AND DO NOT ADD ANY
--------------------------------------------------------------
As in hume/, this book depends on the emphasis rendering (_x_ / *x*
-> <em>). Mill italicises three things and each means something:
  - the word carrying the logical stress (*That* was a resource against
    rulers; the will of the most active *part* of the people; the people
    *may* desire to oppress);
  - Latin and French he is quoting (*odium theologicum*, *prima facie*);
  - book titles (*Traité de Politique Positive*, *The Sphere and Duties
    of Government*).
Marker parity against the source is checked per file. DO NOT INVENT
EMPHASIS: in the one book where the markers mean something, inflating
them makes them mean less (the hume finding, where three drifts were
caught this way). And a marker that does not render is worse than none —
assemble.EMPH refuses a span whose opening asterisk follows a word
character, so "can*not*" ships as literal asterisks.

THE FOOTNOTES ARE MILL'S AND ARE PART OF THE ARGUMENT
-----------------------------------------------------
Nine of them, inlined by prep.py as "Footnote: ..." paragraphs directly
after the paragraph that cites them. Translate them in Mill's own voice —
they are his, not an editor's, unlike candle/'s Crookes notes. Four are
substantial:
  - the 1858 Press Prosecutions note, which he added rather than change
    a word of his text, and which says so;
  - the note on the persecuting temper surviving in English opinion;
  - the note on the evidence accepted in the Sabbatarian and
    anti-Mormon agitations;
  - the Bombay Parsees.
The five short ones are citations of real 1857 cases and every name,
court and date in them is a fact: Thomas Pooley, Bodmin Assizes, July
31, 1857; George Jacob Holyoake, August 17, 1857; Edward Truelove, July,
1857; Baron de Gleichen, Marlborough-Street Police Court, August 4,
1857. Keep them exactly.

SENSITIVE CONTENT — THE VERNE RULE, AND ONE EXCEPTION
------------------------------------------------------
THE VERNE RULE, IN FULL, WITH NO NOTE ANYWHERE IN THE BOOK:

  - "Despotism is a legitimate mode of government in dealing with
    barbarians, provided the end be their improvement" (Chapter One),
    and with it the exemption of "those backward states of society in
    which the race itself may be considered as in its nonage", and
    "implicit obedience to an Akbar or a Charlemagne". This is the most
    discussed passage in the book's modern reception and it is Mill's
    own limitation on his own principle — it IS his subject. Rendered
    plainly, unhedged, unsoftened, with no editorial wink. Mill was an
    official of the East India Company for thirty-five years when he
    wrote it, and the sentence means what it says.
  - Chapter Four on the Mormons and polygamy, including "a retrograde
    step in civilisation", and his refusal all the same to allow a
    "civilizade" against them.
  - The remarks on China as a warning of what custom does to a people.
  - His contempt for the "Calvinistic theory" and for what he calls the
    pinched and hidebound type of human character.
  - The whole of Chapter Five, including the passages on trade, poisons,
    drunkenness, gambling houses, marriage and — hardest for a modern
    reader — his view that a man who cannot support children should not
    be permitted to have them.
All of it stands as Mill's. The book is an argument about what may be
forbidden and by whom; softening the parts a modern reader dislikes
would replace his argument with one he never made.

THE ONE EXCEPTION, AND THE REASONING IS THE hume PRECEDENT EXACTLY:
"The morality between Spartans and Helots, between planters and negroes,
between princes and subjects, between nobles and roturiers, between men
and women, has been for the most part the creation of these class
interests." The Verne rule protects a dated CLAIM that is part of the
author's subject. Here the subject is the morality produced by one class
holding power over another, and the claim survives entire when the noun
is replaced by the relationship: "between planters and the people they
enslaved". That is MORE explicit, not less — the ball/ Leonid precedent,
where "the negroes" became "the enslaved people" because a modern young
reader would not otherwise know what was being described. The reader
loses no information and gains no false one, which is the test.
("roturiers" is glossed as commoners in the same list.)

REGISTER
--------
Mill is arguing with a particular reader in mind — an educated, decent,
liberal Englishman who agrees with him about religious toleration and
has never once thought about why. He is courteous to the point of
self-effacement, concedes early and often, and then does not budge. He
is also, more than his reputation suggests, ANGRY: the passages on the
1857 prosecutions, on the "moral police", on Sabbatarian legislation and
on the Maine Law are written by a man with his temper on a short leash,
and the anger must survive. So must the two or three moments of open
contempt ("the pinched and hidebound type of human character", the
"deep slumber of a decided opinion"), and the elegiac tone of Chapter
Three, which is the most beautiful writing in the book and is really
about Harriet Taylor.

Keep the first person. Keep "I". Keep the direct address to the reader.
Do not turn a live argument into a textbook exposition of itself.
