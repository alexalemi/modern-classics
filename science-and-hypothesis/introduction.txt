Henri Poincaré (1854–1912) was the leading French mathematician of his generation, and one of the last who worked across the whole of mathematics and mathematical physics at once: celestial mechanics, topology, the theory of functions, electrodynamics. He published La Science et l'hypothèse in Paris in 1902, gathering and reworking essays on the foundations of the sciences. This translation, by W. J. Greenstreet, appeared in London in 1905, with an introduction by Joseph Larmor, the Lucasian Professor of Mathematics at Cambridge.

The book asks how much of what science tells us is found in nature and how much is put there by the scientist. It moves through the sciences in order of their certainty. In arithmetic Poincaré defends mathematical induction as a genuine act of the mind that no logic can replace. In geometry he argues that the axioms are neither truths of reason nor facts of experience but conventions, chosen because they are convenient: "One geometry cannot be more true than another; it can only be more convenient." Mechanics shares that conventional character in part. Physics does not, and there hypotheses of a different kind do real work, which is why a whole Part is given to them, to probability, and to the history of optics and electrodynamics.

A reader should not take Poincaré for a sceptic. He says plainly that "experiment is the sole source of truth", and the conventions he describes are the framework in which experiment is read, not a substitute for it. It also helps to know that the physics is that of 1902, three years before the special theory of relativity: the ether is a working hypothesis throughout, and the book ends on experiments by Crémieu whose results were still in dispute as it went to press.

This is a restored edition: Greenstreet's translation is unchanged, and Larmor's introduction and the translator's note are kept, as they were printed with it. It is built from the typeset transcription made for Project Gutenberg, which was compared word by word with two copies of the 1905 printing. The transcriber corrected eight phrases the translation had got wrong against the French, among them a Lobatschewsky and Bolyai called "a Russian and a Bulgarian", when Bolyai was Hungarian. Those corrections are kept, because the printed words misstate what Poincaré wrote; the transcription's silent modernisations of the translator's spelling are reversed. The formulas are typeset, and references to other pages name the chapter. The edition also adds modern typesetting, a proper ebook, and this introduction.
