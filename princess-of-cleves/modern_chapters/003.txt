"But to come back to Madame de Valentinois: you know that her name is Diane de Poitiers. Her house is very illustrious. It descends from the old Dukes of Aquitaine, and her grandmother was a natural daughter of Louis XI; in short, her birth could hardly be grander. Her father, Saint-Vallier, was caught up in the affair of the Constable de Bourbon, which you have heard of. He was condemned to be beheaded and led out to the scaffold. His daughter, whose beauty was admirable and who had already caught the late King's eye, won her father's life, by what means I do not know. His pardon was brought to him as he stood waiting for the executioner's blow; but fear had taken such hold of him that he had lost his senses, and he died a few days later. His daughter appeared at court as the King's mistress. The Italian campaign and the King's captivity interrupted that passion. When he came back from Spain, and his mother, the Regent, went to meet him at Bayonne, she brought all her maids of honor with her, and among them was Mademoiselle de Pisseleu, who has since become the Duchess of Étampes. The King fell in love with her. She was below Madame de Valentinois in birth, in intelligence, and in beauty, and the only advantage she had over her was that she was much younger. I have heard her say many times that she was born on the day Diane de Poitiers was married. It was hatred that made her say so, not the truth; for I am much mistaken if the Duchess of Valentinois did not marry Monsieur de Brezé, Grand Seneschal of Normandy, at the very time the King fell in love with Madame d'Étampes.

"There has never been so great a hatred as the hatred between those two women. The Duchess of Valentinois could not forgive Madame d'Étampes for taking from her the title of the King's mistress; Madame d'Étampes was violently jealous of Madame de Valentinois, because the King still kept up his relations with her. He was not strictly faithful to his mistresses. There was always one who held the title and the honors, but the ladies they called the little band shared him among them by turns. The loss of his son the Dauphin, who died at Tournon and was believed to have been poisoned, caused him deep grief. He did not feel the same tenderness or the same liking for his second son, who reigns today; he did not find him bold enough or lively enough. One day he complained of it to Madame de Valentinois, and she told him she would make him fall in love with her, to make him livelier and more agreeable. She succeeded, as you can see. That passion has lasted more than twenty years now, and neither time nor obstacles have changed it.

"The late King opposed it at first. Whether he still loved Madame de Valentinois enough to be jealous, or whether he was pushed by the Duchess of Étampes, who was in despair at seeing the Dauphin attached to her enemy, it is certain that he watched this passion with an anger and an irritation that showed every day. His son feared neither his anger nor his hatred, and nothing could make him lessen his attachment or hide it; the King had to get used to putting up with it. This defiance of his wishes estranged him still further from his son and bound him more closely to the Duke of Orléans, his third son. He was a handsome, well-made prince, full of fire and ambition, with a headstrong youthfulness that needed reining in, but he would also have grown into a prince of real stature if age had matured his mind.

"The Dauphin's rank as the elder, and the Duke of Orléans's favor with the King, created a kind of rivalry between them that ran all the way to hatred. It had begun in their childhood and had never ended. When the Emperor Charles V passed through France, he showed an outright preference for the Duke of Orléans over the Dauphin, who felt it so keenly that while the Emperor was at Chantilly he tried to make the Constable arrest him without waiting for the King's orders. The Constable refused. The King later blamed him for not having followed his son's advice, and when he banished him from court, that reason had a great deal to do with it.

"The split between the two brothers gave the Duchess of Étampes the idea of leaning on the Duke of Orléans to support her with the King against Madame de Valentinois. She succeeded. The Duke, without being in love with her, took up her interests almost as fully as the Dauphin had taken up those of Madame de Valentinois. So the court split into two cabals, of the kind you can imagine; but these intrigues did not stop at quarrels between women.

"The Emperor, who had kept a fondness for the Duke of Orléans, had offered several times to hand over the Duchy of Milan to him. In the proposals that were made later for peace, he held out the hope of giving him the seventeen provinces of the Netherlands and of marrying him to his daughter. The Dauphin wanted neither the peace nor the marriage. He used the Constable, whom he has always loved, to make the King see how important it was not to leave his successor a brother as powerful as a Duke of Orléans would be with the Emperor's alliance and the seventeen provinces. The Constable fell in with the Dauphin's views all the more readily because in doing so he was opposing those of Madame d'Étampes, who was his declared enemy and who longed to see the Duke of Orléans raised to greatness.

"The Dauphin was then in command of the King's army in Champagne, and he had reduced the Emperor's army to such straits that it would have perished entirely if the Duchess of Étampes had not acted. She was afraid that too great a success would make us refuse the peace and the Emperor's alliance for the Duke of Orléans, so she secretly warned the enemy to seize Épernay and Château-Thierry, which were full of provisions. They did so, and saved their whole army by it.

"The Duchess did not enjoy the success of her treachery for long. Soon afterward the Duke of Orléans died at Faremoutiers, of a kind of contagious illness. He loved one of the most beautiful women at court, and she loved him. I will not name her to you, because she has lived so virtuously ever since, and has hidden the passion she had for the Duke with such care, that she has earned the right to keep her reputation. As chance would have it, she received the news of her husband's death on the same day she learned of the Duke of Orléans's; so she had that pretext to cover her real grief, and was spared the trouble of pretending.

"The King did not long outlive his son; he died two years later. He advised the Dauphin to make use of the Cardinal de Tournon and the Admiral d'Annebault, and said nothing of the Constable, who was then banished to Chantilly. Nevertheless the first thing the new King did was to recall him and put the government of affairs in his hands.

"Madame d'Étampes was driven away, and was treated as badly as she could expect to be by an enemy who was now all-powerful. The Duchess of Valentinois then took her full revenge, on the Duchess and on everyone who had ever displeased her. Her power over the King's mind proved even more absolute than it had seemed while he was Dauphin. In the twelve years that he has reigned, she has been absolute mistress of everything. Offices and affairs of state are hers to give; she has had the Cardinal de Tournon, the Chancellor Olivier, and Villeroy driven out. Those who have tried to open the King's eyes about her conduct have been ruined in the attempt. The Count of Taix, the Grand Master of the Artillery, who did not like her, could not help talking about her love affairs, and above all about her affair with the Count of Brissac, of which the King had already been very jealous. Nevertheless she managed things so well that the Count of Taix was disgraced and stripped of his office; and, what is almost beyond belief, she had it given to the Count of Brissac, and has since had him made a Marshal of France. Still, the King's jealousy grew to such a pitch that he could not bear to have the Marshal remain at court. But jealousy, which is bitter and violent in everyone else, is mild and moderate in him, because of the extreme respect he has for his mistress; so he dared not send his rival away except on the pretext of giving him the government of Piedmont. He has spent several years there. He came back last winter on the pretext of asking for troops and other things needed for the army he commands. The wish to see Madame de Valentinois again, and the fear of being forgotten by her, perhaps had a great deal to do with that journey. The King received him very coldly. The Guises, who do not like him but dare not show it because of Madame de Valentinois, used the Vidame, who is his declared enemy, to keep him from getting any of the things he had come to ask for. It was not hard to do him harm: the King hated him, and his presence made the King uneasy. So he was forced to go back with nothing to show for his journey, except perhaps that he had rekindled in Madame de Valentinois's heart feelings that absence was beginning to put out. The King has had plenty of other grounds for jealousy; but either he has not known of them, or he has not dared to complain."

"I don't know, my child," Madame de Chartres added, "whether you won't feel I have told you more than you wanted to know."

"I have no such complaint to make, Madame," replied Madame de Cleves. "If I weren't afraid of tiring you, I would ask you about a number of other things I know nothing about."

Monsieur de Nemours's passion for Madame de Cleves was so violent from the first that it robbed him of all taste for, and even all memory of, the women he had loved and had kept up relations with during his absence. He did not even take the trouble to look for pretexts to break with them; he could not find the patience to listen to their complaints or answer their reproaches. The Queen Dauphine, for whom he had had quite passionate feelings, could not hold her place in his heart against Madame de Cleves. Even his eagerness for the English journey began to slacken, and he no longer pushed so hard for the preparations his departure required. He went often to the Queen Dauphine's, because Madame de Cleves went there often, and he was not sorry to let people go on believing what they had imagined about his feelings for that queen. He valued Madame de Cleves so highly that he resolved to give her no sign of his passion rather than risk making it known to the public. He did not even speak of it to the Vidame de Chartres, who was his closest friend and from whom he hid nothing. He behaved so wisely and watched himself with such care that no one suspected him of being in love with Madame de Cleves except the Chevalier de Guise. She would hardly have noticed it herself, if her own feelings for him had not made her pay a particular attention to everything he did, an attention that left her no room for doubt.

She did not feel the same readiness to tell her mother what she thought of Monsieur de Nemours's feelings that she had felt in speaking of her other suitors. She had no settled plan to hide it from her; she simply said nothing about it. But Madame de Chartres saw it only too well, and she saw that her daughter was drawn to him as well. That knowledge caused her real grief. She saw clearly the danger the young woman was in, loved by a man like Monsieur de Nemours, and drawn to him herself. Something that happened a few days later confirmed her suspicions of that attraction entirely.

The Marshal de Saint-André, who looked for every occasion to display his magnificence, begged the King to do him the honor of coming to supper there with the Queen and the Queen Dauphine, on the pretext of showing him his house, which had only just been finished. The Marshal was also glad to put before Madame de Cleves's eyes the dazzling show of an expense that ran to extravagance.

A few days before the day chosen for the supper, the Dauphin, whose health was rather poor, had been taken ill and had seen no one. The Queen Dauphine, his wife, had spent the whole day at his side. Toward evening, as he was feeling better, he had all the people of rank who were waiting in his antechamber shown in. The Queen Dauphine went back to her own rooms, where she found Madame de Cleves and a few other ladies she was closest to.

As it was already rather late and she was not dressed, she did not go to the Queen's. She sent word that she was not receiving, and had her jewels brought so that she could choose some for the Marshal de Saint-André's ball and give some to Madame de Cleves, as she had promised. While they were busy with this, the Prince of Condé arrived; his rank gave him entry everywhere. The Queen Dauphine said that he must have come from the Dauphin's apartments, and asked him what was going on there.

"They are arguing with Monsieur de Nemours, Madame," he answered, "and he defends his side with such heat that it must be his own cause. I think he must have some lady who makes him anxious when she goes to a ball, he finds it so painful for a man in love to see the woman he loves there."

"What," said the Queen Dauphine, "Monsieur de Nemours doesn't want the woman he loves to go to balls! I could well believe that husbands might wish their wives wouldn't go; but as for suitors, I never imagined they could be of that opinion."

"Monsieur de Nemours holds," replied the Prince of Condé, "that a ball is the most unbearable thing there is for men in love, whether they are loved in return or not. He says that if they are loved, they have the vexation of being loved less for several days; that there is no woman whose concern with her dress does not keep her from thinking of the man who loves her; that women are wholly taken up with it; that they adorn themselves for everyone, as much as for the one they love; that when they are at a ball they want to please everyone who looks at them; and that when they are pleased with their own beauty, they feel a joy in which the man who loves them plays only a small part. He says too that when a man is not loved, he suffers even more from seeing the woman he loves at a gathering: the more the public admires her, the unhappier he is not to be loved by her, and he is always afraid her beauty will inspire some love that is luckier than his own. In short, he finds there is no suffering like seeing the woman he loves at a ball, unless it is knowing she is there and not being there himself."

Madame de Cleves pretended not to hear what the Prince of Condé was saying, but she listened closely. She could easily see how large a part she had in the opinion Monsieur de Nemours was defending, and above all in what he said about the vexation of not being at a ball where the woman he loved was; for he was not to be at the Marshal de Saint-André's, since the King was sending him to meet the Duke of Ferrara.

The Queen Dauphine laughed with the Prince of Condé and did not approve of Monsieur de Nemours's opinion.

"There is only one occasion, Madame," the Prince of Condé told her, "when Monsieur de Nemours will consent to the woman he loves going to a ball, and that is when he is the one giving it. He says that last year, when he gave one for Your Majesty, he felt the lady he loved was doing him a favor by coming, even though she only seemed to be following you there; that it is always a kindness to a man in love to go and share in a pleasure he provides; and that it is also a pleasant thing for him to have the woman he loves see him master of a place where the whole court is gathered, and watch him play the host there well."

"Monsieur de Nemours was right," said the Queen Dauphine, smiling, "to approve of the woman he loved going to his ball. In those days he gave that title to so many women that if they hadn't come, there would have been hardly anyone there."

As soon as the Prince of Condé had begun to tell of Monsieur de Nemours's feelings about balls, Madame de Cleves had felt a strong wish not to go to the Marshal de Saint-André's. She readily accepted the idea that a woman should not go to the house of a man who was in love with her, and she was glad that propriety gave her a reason to do something that was a favor to Monsieur de Nemours. All the same she took away the jewels the Queen Dauphine had given her; but that evening, when she showed them to her mother, she told her she did not mean to wear them. The Marshal de Saint-André, she said, took such pains to show that he was attached to her that she had no doubt he would also want people to believe she had some part in the entertainment he was to give the King, and that under the pretext of doing the honors of his house he would pay her attentions that might put her in an awkward position.

Madame de Chartres argued for a while against her daughter's opinion, which she found odd; but seeing that she held to it, she gave way. She told her that in that case she would have to pretend to be ill, so as to have an excuse for not going, because the reasons that kept her away would not be approved of, and they must not even be suspected. Madame de Cleves willingly agreed to spend a few days at home, rather than go to a place where Monsieur de Nemours would not be; and he left without the pleasure of knowing that she would not be there.

He came back the day after the ball and learned that she had not been there. But since he did not know that the conversation at the Dauphin's had been repeated in front of her, it never occurred to him that he might have been lucky enough to be the reason she stayed away.

The next day, while he was at the Queen's, talking with the Queen Dauphine, Madame de Chartres and Madame de Cleves came in and went up to her. Madame de Cleves was a little carelessly dressed, like someone who had been unwell; but her face did not match her clothes.

"You look so beautiful," the Queen Dauphine said to her, "that I cannot believe you have been ill. I think that when the Prince of Condé told you Monsieur de Nemours's opinion about balls, he persuaded you that going to the Marshal de Saint-André's would be doing him a favor, and that is what kept you from coming."

Madame de Cleves blushed, both because the Queen Dauphine had guessed so exactly, and because she was saying what she had guessed in front of Monsieur de Nemours.

Madame de Chartres saw at that moment why her daughter had not wanted to go to the ball; and to keep Monsieur de Nemours from seeing it as clearly as she did, she spoke up with every appearance of telling the truth.

"I assure you, Madame," she said to the Queen Dauphine, "that Your Majesty does my daughter more honor than she deserves. She really was ill; but I believe that if I hadn't prevented her, she would still have followed you and shown herself, changed as she was, for the pleasure of seeing all the extraordinary things at last night's entertainment."

The Queen Dauphine believed what Madame de Chartres said. Monsieur de Nemours was very sorry to find it plausible; and yet Madame de Cleves's blush made him suspect that what the Queen Dauphine had said was not altogether far from the truth. Madame de Cleves had at first been vexed that Monsieur de Nemours had reason to believe it was he who had kept her from the Marshal de Saint-André's; but then she felt a kind of regret that her mother had talked him out of the idea so completely.

Although the assembly at Cercamp had broken up, the peace negotiations had gone on all along, and matters were so arranged that at the end of February the parties met again at Cateau-Cambrésis. The same envoys went back; and the Marshal de Saint-André's absence rid Monsieur de Nemours of a rival who was dangerous to him less for any progress he could make with Madame de Cleves than for the close watch he kept on everyone who came near her.

Madame de Chartres had not wanted to let her daughter see that she knew her feelings for Monsieur de Nemours, for fear of making her distrust what she meant to say. One day she began to talk about him. She spoke well of him, and mixed in a good deal of poisoned praise: of his wisdom in being incapable of falling in love, and of the way he made the company of women a pleasure and not a serious attachment.

"Not that he hasn't been suspected," she added, "of a great passion for the Queen Dauphine. I even see that he goes there very often, and I advise you to avoid speaking to him as much as you can, and above all in private, because, with the Queen Dauphine treating you as she does, people would soon say that you are their confidante, and you know how disagreeable that reputation is. If this talk goes on, I think you should go a little less often to the Queen Dauphine's, so as not to find yourself mixed up in love affairs."

Madame de Cleves had never heard of Monsieur de Nemours and the Queen Dauphine. She was so surprised by what her mother said, and so sure she now saw how mistaken she had been in everything she had thought about his feelings, that her face changed. Madame de Chartres noticed it. People came in at that moment; Madame de Cleves went home and shut herself up in her room.

No words can express the grief she felt at learning, from what her mother had just said, how much she cared for Monsieur de Nemours; she had not yet dared admit it to herself. She saw then that the feelings she had for him were the ones Monsieur de Cleves had so often begged her for, and she saw how shameful it was to have them for someone other than a husband who deserved them. She felt hurt and embarrassed by the fear that Monsieur de Nemours meant to use her as a cover for the Queen Dauphine, and that thought decided her to tell Madame de Chartres what she had not yet told her.

The next morning she went to her mother's room to carry out her resolution; but she found that Madame de Chartres had a slight fever, so she did not want to speak to her. The illness seemed so small a thing, though, that Madame de Cleves still went to the Queen Dauphine's that afternoon. She was in her private room with two or three of the ladies she trusted most.

"We were talking about Monsieur de Nemours," the Queen Dauphine said when she saw her, "and marveling at how changed he is since he came back from Brussels. Before he went he had an endless number of women he was courting, and it was even a fault in him, since he paid court equally to the ones who had merit and the ones who hadn't. Since he came back he pays no attention to either kind. There has never been such a change. I even find there is a change in his temper, and that he is less cheerful than usual."

Madame de Cleves said nothing, and she thought with shame that she would have taken everything that was being said about his change for signs of his passion, if she had not been undeceived. She felt some bitterness toward the Queen Dauphine at seeing her hunt for reasons and marvel at something whose truth she apparently knew better than anyone. She could not help letting something of it show; and as the other ladies moved away, she went up to her and said in a low voice:

"Was that speech meant for me too, Madame? Would you hide from me that you are the one who has made Monsieur de Nemours change his conduct?"

"You are unjust," the Queen Dauphine told her. "You know I keep nothing from you. It is true that before he went to Brussels, Monsieur de Nemours did, I think, mean to let me understand that he did not dislike me; but since he came back, it hasn't even seemed to me that he remembers what he did. And I confess I am curious to know what has changed him. It will be hard for me not to find out," she added. "The Vidame de Chartres, who is his close friend, is in love with a lady over whom I have some power, and through her I will find out what has caused the change."

The Queen Dauphine spoke in a way that convinced Madame de Cleves, and in spite of herself she felt calmer and more at ease than she had been before.

When she went back to her mother's, she learned that her mother was much worse than when she had left her. Her fever had doubled, and in the days that followed it rose so much that it was plainly going to be a serious illness. Madame de Cleves was in extreme distress and never left her mother's room. Monsieur de Cleves spent almost every day there too, both out of concern for Madame de Chartres and to keep his wife from giving way to sadness, but also for the pleasure of seeing her: his passion had not lessened at all.

Monsieur de Nemours, who had always been a great friend of his, had not stopped showing it since his return from Brussels. During Madame de Chartres's illness he found the means to see Madame de Cleves several times, by pretending to look for her husband or to come and fetch him for a walk. He even looked for him at hours when he knew very well he would not be there, and on the pretext of waiting for him he stayed in Madame de Chartres's antechamber, where there were always several people of rank. Madame de Cleves often came through; and grieved as she was, she seemed no less beautiful to Monsieur de Nemours. He let her see how much her sorrow touched him, and he spoke to her of it with such a gentle and submissive air that he easily persuaded her that it was not the Queen Dauphine he was in love with.

The sight of him could not fail to disturb her, and yet she took pleasure in seeing him; but when she no longer saw him, and reflected that the charm she found in seeing him was the beginning of passion, she came close to believing she hated him, because of the grief that thought gave her.

Madame de Chartres grew so much worse that people began to despair of her life. She heard what the doctors told her about her danger with a courage worthy of her virtue and her piety. When they had gone, she sent everyone away and called for Madame de Cleves.

"We must part, my child," she said to her, holding out her hand. "The danger I am leaving you in, and your need of me, make the grief of leaving you worse. You have feelings for Monsieur de Nemours. I am not asking you to confess it to me; I am no longer in a position to use your sincerity to guide you. I saw that attraction long ago; but I did not want to speak to you of it at first, for fear of making you see it yourself. You know it only too well now. You are on the edge of a precipice, and it will take great effort, and great violence to your own feelings, to hold yourself back. Think of what you owe your husband; think of what you owe yourself; and remember that you are about to lose the reputation you have won for yourself, and that I have wanted so much for you. Have strength and courage, my child. Withdraw from the court; make your husband take you away; do not be afraid of choices that are too harsh and too difficult. However dreadful they seem at first, they will be sweeter in the end than the misery of a love affair. If any reasons other than virtue and your duty could move you to do what I wish, I would tell you that if anything could trouble the happiness I hope for in leaving this world, it would be to see you fall like other women. But if that misfortune must come to you, I welcome death gladly, so that I will not have to witness it."

Madame de Cleves wept over her mother's hand, which she held pressed between her own; and Madame de Chartres, feeling moved herself, said:

"Goodbye, my child. Let us end a conversation that moves us both too deeply; and remember, if you can, everything I have just told you."

As she finished these words she turned away and told her daughter to call her women, and she would neither listen nor say anything more. Madame de Cleves left her mother's room in the state one can imagine, and Madame de Chartres thought of nothing but preparing herself for death. She lived two days longer, and in that time she would not see her daughter again, though her daughter was the only thing she still felt bound to.

Madame de Cleves's grief was extreme. Her husband never left her side, and as soon as Madame de Chartres had died he took her to the country, to get her away from a place that only sharpened her sorrow. No one has ever seen such grief. Tenderness and gratitude had the largest part in it, but her need of her mother to defend her against Monsieur de Nemours had a great deal to do with it too. She felt how unhappy it was to be left to herself at a time when she had so little command of her feelings, and when she would so much have wished for someone who could pity her and give her strength. The way Monsieur de Cleves treated her made her more determined than ever never to fail in any of her duties to him. She showed him more friendship and more tenderness than she ever had before; she did not want him to leave her, and it seemed to her that if she held fast to him, he would defend her against Monsieur de Nemours.

Monsieur de Nemours came to see Monsieur de Cleves in the country. He did what he could to pay a visit to Madame de Cleves as well, but she would not receive him. Knowing very well that she could not help finding him attractive, she had made a firm resolution to keep herself from seeing him, and to avoid every chance of it that she could.

Monsieur de Cleves went to Paris to pay his respects at court, and promised his wife to return the next day; but he did not come back until the day after.

"I waited for you all day yesterday," Madame de Cleves said to him when he arrived, "and I must reproach you for not coming as you promised me. You know that if I could feel a new grief in the state I am in, it would be the death of Madame de Tournon, which I learned of this morning. I would have been touched by it even if I had not known her; it is always pitiful when a woman as young and beautiful as she was dies in two days. But besides that, she was one of the people in the world I liked best, and she seemed to have as much virtue as merit."

"I was very sorry not to come back yesterday," replied Monsieur de Cleves, "but an unhappy man needed me so badly to console him that I could not possibly leave him. As for Madame de Tournon, I do not advise you to grieve for her, if you mourn her as a woman full of virtue and worthy of your esteem."

"You astonish me," said Madame de Cleves. "I have heard you say several times that there was no woman at court you esteemed more."

"It is true," he answered, "but women are beyond understanding; and when I look at all of them, I find myself so happy to have you that I cannot admire my good fortune enough."

"You think more of me than I am worth," replied Madame de Cleves with a sigh, "and it is not yet time to find me worthy of you. Tell me, I beg you, what has undeceived you about Madame de Tournon."

"I have been undeceived for a long time," he replied, "and I have long known that she loved the Count of Sancerre, and gave him hopes of marrying him."

"I cannot believe," Madame de Cleves broke in, "that Madame de Tournon, after the extraordinary aversion to marriage she has shown since she was widowed, and after declaring publicly that she would never marry again, gave Sancerre hopes."

"If she had given them only to him," replied Monsieur de Cleves, "there would be nothing to wonder at. But what is surprising is that she was giving them to Estouteville at the same time; and I am going to tell you the whole story.
