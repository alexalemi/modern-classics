Part Two

(Part 2 of 3)

With these words the Queen Dauphine left Madame de Cleves, and the next day the marriage of Madame Elisabeth was known to everyone. In the days that followed, the King and the two queens came to see Madame de Cleves. Monsieur de Nemours had waited for her return with extreme impatience, and he wanted badly to speak to her without witnesses. So he waited to call on her until the hour when everyone else would be leaving and no one was likely to come back. His plan worked, and he arrived just as the last visitors were going out.

She was resting on her bed. The weather was warm, and the sight of Monsieur de Nemours deepened the flush the heat had already given her, and it took nothing from her beauty. He sat down facing her, with the fear and the shyness that only a true passion gives. For some time he could not speak. Madame de Cleves was no less at a loss, so that they kept silent for quite a while. At last Monsieur de Nemours spoke, and offered his sympathy for her loss. Madame de Cleves was glad to keep the conversation on that subject. She talked for some time about her mother's death, and in the end she said that even when time had softened the violence of her grief, it would leave so strong a mark on her that her whole temper would be changed by it.

"Great sorrows and violent passions make great changes in the mind," Monsieur de Nemours replied. "As for me, I have not recognized myself since I came back from Flanders. Many people have noticed the change, and the Queen Dauphine herself was speaking to me of it again only yesterday."

"It is true that she has noticed it," said Madame de Cleves, "and I believe I have heard her say something about it."

"I am not sorry, Madame, that she has seen it," Monsieur de Nemours went on. "But I wish she were not the only one to see it. There are women to whom a man dare not give any sign of his passion except through things that have nothing to do with them. Since he does not dare let them see that he loves them, he would at least like them to see that he wants to be loved by no one else. He would like them to know that there is no beauty, of whatever rank, that he does not look at with indifference, and no crown he would buy at the price of never seeing them again. Women usually judge a man's passion," he continued, "by the trouble he takes to please them and to seek them out. But that is not hard, if they are the least bit attractive. What is hard is not to give in to the pleasure of following them. It is to avoid them, for fear of letting the public, and almost the women themselves, see what he feels for them. And what proves a true attachment even better is to become the complete opposite of what one was, and to have no more ambition and no more pleasure, after having spent one's whole life taken up with both."

Madame de Cleves understood without difficulty how much of this was meant for her. It seemed to her that she ought to answer and not let it pass. It seemed to her too that she ought not to understand it, or show that she took it as meant for her. She felt she had to speak, and she felt she must say nothing. What Monsieur de Nemours said pleased her and offended her almost equally. She saw in it the confirmation of everything the Queen Dauphine had made her think. She found in it something courteous and respectful, but also something bold and far too easy to understand. Her feelings for him threw her into an agitation she could not control. The most veiled words of a man one likes stir more agitation than the open declarations of a man one does not. So she said nothing, and Monsieur de Nemours would have noticed her silence, and perhaps drawn no bad omen from it, if the arrival of Monsieur de Cleves had not ended the conversation and his visit.

Her husband had come to tell her the latest news of Sancerre, but she had no great curiosity about the rest of that story. She was so full of what had just happened that she could barely hide how far away her mind was. When she was free to think, she saw plainly that she had been mistaken when she believed she felt nothing but indifference for Monsieur de Nemours. What he had said had made all the impression he could have wished, and had entirely convinced her of his passion. His actions agreed too well with his words to leave her any doubt. She no longer flattered herself with the hope of not loving him. She thought only of never giving him any sign of it. That was a difficult undertaking, and she already knew what it cost. She knew that the only way to succeed was to avoid his presence, and since her mourning gave her a reason to live more quietly than usual, she used that excuse to stop going anywhere he might see her. She was in a deep sadness. Her mother's death seemed to be the cause of it, and no one looked for any other.

Monsieur de Nemours was in despair at hardly ever seeing her anymore. Since he knew he would not find her at any gathering or at any of the entertainments the whole court attended, he could not bring himself to appear at them. He pretended a great passion for hunting, and arranged hunting parties on the very days when there were receptions at the queens' apartments. A slight illness served him for a long time as an excuse to stay at home and avoid every place where he knew very well that Madame de Cleves would not be.

Monsieur de Cleves fell ill at about the same time. Madame de Cleves never left his room during his illness. But when he was better and began to see company, Monsieur de Nemours among others, who on the excuse of still being weak spent the greater part of every day there, she found that she could no longer stay in the room. All the same, she did not have the strength to leave it the first few times he came. It had been too long since she had seen him for her to resolve not to see him. Monsieur de Nemours found a way to make her understand, through talk that seemed only general, but which she understood because it echoed what he had said to her at her own house, that he went hunting in order to be alone with his thoughts, and that he did not go to the gatherings because she was not there.

At last she carried out the resolution she had made, to leave her husband's room whenever Monsieur de Nemours was there. It cost her an extreme effort. He saw clearly that she was avoiding him, and he was deeply hurt by it.

Monsieur de Cleves did not notice his wife's conduct at first, but in the end he saw that she did not want to be in his room when there was company. He spoke to her about it. She answered that she did not think propriety allowed her to spend every evening with the youngest people at court. She begged him to let her live a more retired life than she had been used to. Her mother's virtue and presence, she said, had made many things acceptable that a woman of her age could not keep up on her own.

Monsieur de Cleves, who was naturally very gentle and indulgent with his wife, was not so on this occasion. He told her that he absolutely did not want her to change her way of life. She was on the point of telling him that it was being said at court that Monsieur de Nemours was in love with her, but she did not have the strength to say his name. She felt ashamed, too, of wanting to use a false reason, and of hiding the truth from a man who thought so well of her.

A few days later, the King was at the Queen's apartments at the hour of the circle, and the talk turned to horoscopes and predictions. Opinions were divided about how much belief they deserved. The Queen put a great deal of faith in them. She maintained that after so many things had been predicted and then seen to happen, no one could doubt that there was some certainty in this science. Others held that among that endless number of predictions, the few that turned out true showed plainly that it was nothing but chance.

"I was once very curious about the future," said the King. "But I was told so many false and unlikely things that I came away convinced that nothing true can be known about it. Some years ago an astrologer of great reputation came here. Everyone went to see him. I went like the others, but without telling him who I was, and I took Monsieur de Guise and Descars with me and made them go in ahead of me. The astrologer spoke to me first all the same, as if he had judged me the master of the others. Perhaps he knew me. Yet he told me something that would not have suited me if he had. He predicted that I would be killed in a duel. Then he told Monsieur de Guise that he would be killed from behind, and Descars that he would have his skull broken by a kick from a horse. Monsieur de Guise was almost offended by his prediction, as if he had been accused of being the kind of man who runs away. Descars was hardly pleased to learn that he was to end by so unlucky an accident. In short, we all came out very displeased with the astrologer. I do not know what will happen to Monsieur de Guise and Descars, but there is very little likelihood that I will be killed in a duel. The King of Spain and I have just made peace, and even if we had not, I doubt we would fight each other, or that I would challenge him, as the King my father challenged the Emperor Charles V."

After the misfortune the King said had been predicted for him, those who had defended astrology abandoned its cause and agreed that it deserved no belief at all.

"For my part," said Monsieur de Nemours aloud, "I am the man in the world who ought to believe in it least." Then, turning to Madame de Cleves, who was beside him, he said quietly: "I was told that I would be made happy by the kindness of the woman for whom I would feel the most violent and the most respectful passion in the world. You may judge, Madame, whether I ought to believe in predictions."

The Queen Dauphine, who gathered from what Monsieur de Nemours had said aloud that what he was saying quietly was some false prediction that had been made to him, asked him what he was telling Madame de Cleves. If he had had less presence of mind, the question would have caught him out. But he answered without hesitating: "I was telling her, Madame, that it was predicted I would rise to a fortune so high that I would not even dare to aspire to it."

"If that is the only prediction they have made you," the Queen Dauphine replied with a smile, thinking of the English affair, "I do not advise you to run down astrology. You might find reasons to defend it."

Madame de Cleves understood very well what the Queen Dauphine meant. But she also understood that the fortune Monsieur de Nemours had in mind was not to be King of England.

Since it was now some time since her mother's death, she had to begin appearing in society again and attending at court as she used to. She saw Monsieur de Nemours at the Queen Dauphine's. She saw him at Monsieur de Cleves's, where he often came with other men of rank his own age, so as not to be noticed. But she never saw him now without an agitation that he easily perceived.

However hard she tried to avoid his eyes and to speak to him less than to anyone else, certain things escaped her on first impulse which made him judge that he was not indifferent to her. A less perceptive man might not have noticed them. But he had been loved so many times already that it was hard for him not to know when he was loved. He saw clearly that the Chevalier de Guise was his rival, and the Chevalier knew that Monsieur de Nemours was his. He was the only man at court who had worked out this truth; his own interest had made him more clear-sighted than the others. Knowing what each other felt gave the two men a bitterness that showed in everything, though it never broke out in an open quarrel. But they were opposed in all things. They were always on different sides in riding at the ring, in fighting at the barrier, and in every entertainment the King took part in, and their rivalry was so great that it could not be hidden.

The English affair often came back into Madame de Cleves's mind. It seemed to her that Monsieur de Nemours would not hold out against the King's advice and the urging of Lignerolles. She saw with pain that Lignerolles had still not returned, and she waited for him impatiently. If she had followed her own impulses, she would have inquired carefully how the matter stood. But the same feeling that made her curious obliged her to hide her curiosity, and she asked only about Queen Elizabeth's beauty, her intelligence, and her character. One of Elizabeth's portraits was brought to the King's apartments, and she found it more beautiful than she wanted to find it. She could not help saying that it flattered her.

"I don't think so," replied the Queen Dauphine, who was present. "That queen has the reputation of being beautiful and of having a mind far above the ordinary, and I know very well that she has been held up to me as an example all my life. She must be attractive, if she is like her mother, Anne Boleyn. No woman ever had so much charm and so much grace, in her person and in her temper. I have heard that there was something lively and unusual about her face, and that she looked nothing like the other English beauties."

"It seems to me," said Madame de Cleves, "that people say she was born in France."

"Those who believed that were mistaken," the Queen Dauphine answered, "and I will tell you her story in a few words.

"She came of a good English family. Henry VIII had been in love with her sister and with her mother, and it has even been suspected that she was his daughter. She came here with the sister of Henry VII, who married King Louis XII. That princess, who was young and fond of love affairs, found it very hard to leave the court of France after her husband's death. But Anne Boleyn, whose tastes were the same as her mistress's, could not bring herself to leave it at all. The late King was in love with her, and she stayed on as a maid of honor to Queen Claude. When that queen died, Madame Marguerite, the late King's sister, Duchess of Alençon and afterward Queen of Navarre, whose stories you have read, took her into her household, and it was from her that Anne Boleyn picked up the first tinge of the new religion. She then went back to England and charmed everyone there. She had the manners of France, which please every nation. She sang well, and she danced admirably. She was made a maid of honor to Queen Catherine of Aragon, and King Henry VIII fell madly in love with her.

"Cardinal Wolsey, his favorite and his first minister, had hoped to be made pope, and he was angry with the Emperor for not supporting him in that ambition. He resolved to take his revenge by binding the King his master to France. He put it into Henry VIII's head that his marriage to the Emperor's aunt was invalid, and proposed that he marry the Duchess of Alençon, whose husband had just died. Anne Boleyn, who had ambition, saw this divorce as a road that might lead her to the throne. She began to give the King of England a taste for the religion of Luther, and she persuaded the late King to support Henry's divorce at Rome, in the hope of a marriage with Madame d'Alençon. Cardinal Wolsey had himself sent to France on other pretexts, to arrange the matter. But his master could not bring himself to let the proposal even be made, and he sent him an order at Calais not to speak of that marriage at all.

"When he came back from France, Cardinal Wolsey was received with honors like those paid to the King himself. No favorite has ever carried pride and vanity to such a height. He arranged a meeting between the two kings, which took place at Boulogne. François I offered his hand to Henry VIII, who did not want to take it. Each in turn entertained the other with extraordinary magnificence, and they gave each other suits of clothes like the ones they had had made for themselves. I remember hearing that the ones the late King sent to the King of England were of crimson satin, trimmed in a pattern of triangles with pearls and diamonds, and the gown of white velvet embroidered with gold. After a few days at Boulogne they went on to Calais. Anne Boleyn was lodged with Henry VIII, with a queen's household, and François I gave her the same presents and paid her the same honors as if she had been queen already. At last, after a passion of nine years, Henry married her without waiting for the dissolution of his first marriage, which he had been asking of Rome for a long time. The Pope hurried to pronounce his sentence against him, and Henry was so enraged by it that he declared himself head of the Church and dragged all of England into the unhappy change in which you see it today.

"Anne Boleyn did not enjoy her greatness for long. Just when she thought it was made secure by the death of Catherine of Aragon, she was watching with the whole court one day as her brother, the Viscount Rochford, rode at the ring, and the King was struck by such a jealousy that he left the spectacle abruptly, went off to London, and left orders to arrest the Queen, the Viscount Rochford, and several others whom he believed to be her lovers or her confidants. Though that jealousy seemed to be born in that moment, it had for some time been fed by the Viscountess Rochford. She could not bear her husband's close friendship with the Queen, and she made the King see it as a criminal attachment. And so the King, who was in love with Jane Seymour besides, thought of nothing but getting rid of Anne Boleyn. In less than three weeks he had the Queen and her brother tried, had their heads cut off, and married Jane Seymour. He had several wives after that, whom he put away or put to death, and among them Catherine Howard, whose confidante the Viscountess Rochford had been, and who was beheaded along with her. So she was punished for the crimes she had invented against Anne Boleyn, and Henry VIII died having grown prodigiously fat."

All the ladies who had been present for the Queen Dauphine's story thanked her for teaching them so much about the court of England. Madame de Cleves was among them, and she could not help asking her several more questions about Queen Elizabeth.

The Queen Dauphine was having miniature portraits painted of all the beautiful women at court, to send to the Queen her mother. On the day the portrait of Madame de Cleves was being finished, she came to spend the afternoon at her house. Monsieur de Nemours did not fail to be there. He let no chance of seeing Madame de Cleves slip by, though he never let it show that he was looking for one. She was so beautiful that day that he would have fallen in love with her if he had not been in love already. Yet he did not dare keep his eyes fixed on her while she was being painted, and he was afraid of showing too plainly the pleasure he took in looking at her.

The Queen Dauphine asked Monsieur de Cleves for a small portrait he had of his wife, so as to see it beside the one that was being finished. Everyone gave an opinion of the two, and Madame de Cleves told the painter to correct something in the hair of the one that had just been brought in. To obey her, the painter took the portrait out of its case, and when he had worked on it he set it back on the table.

Monsieur de Nemours had long wanted to have a portrait of Madame de Cleves. When he saw the one that belonged to Monsieur de Cleves, he could not resist the urge to steal it from a husband he believed she loved tenderly, and he thought that among so many people in the same room, he would be no more suspected than anyone else.

The Queen Dauphine was sitting on the bed and speaking quietly to Madame de Cleves, who stood before her. Through one of the bed curtains, which was only half drawn, Madame de Cleves caught sight of Monsieur de Nemours with his back against the table at the foot of the bed, and she saw that without turning his head he was deftly taking something from that table. She had no trouble guessing that it was her portrait, and she was so agitated by it that the Queen Dauphine noticed she was not listening, and asked her aloud what she was looking at. At those words Monsieur de Nemours turned around. He met the eyes of Madame de Cleves, which were still fixed on him, and he thought it not impossible that she had seen what he had just done.

Madame de Cleves was in no small difficulty. Reason said she should ask for her portrait back. But to ask for it in public was to tell everyone what Monsieur de Nemours felt for her, and to ask for it in private was almost to invite him to speak of his passion. In the end she judged it better to let him keep it, and she was glad to grant him a favor that she could do him without his even knowing she was doing it. Monsieur de Nemours noticed her awkwardness and almost guessed its cause. He came up to her and said quietly: "If you saw what I dared to do, be kind enough, Madame, to let me believe that you know nothing of it. I dare not ask you for more." And with those words he withdrew, without waiting for her answer.

The Queen Dauphine went out for a walk, followed by all her ladies, and Monsieur de Nemours went home and shut himself in, unable to bear in public his joy at having a portrait of Madame de Cleves. He felt all the sweetest things that passion can make a man feel. He loved the most attractive woman at court. He was making her love him in spite of herself, and he saw in everything she did that agitation and awkwardness that love causes in the innocence of first youth.

That evening the portrait was searched for with great care. Since the case it should have been in was found, no one suspected that it had been stolen, and everyone believed it had fallen somewhere by chance. Monsieur de Cleves was distressed at the loss. After another fruitless search, he said to his wife, though in a way that showed he did not mean it, that she must surely have some hidden suitor to whom she had given the portrait, or who had stolen it, since anyone but a man in love would not have been content with the painting without its case.

Those words, though said in jest, made a sharp impression on the mind of Madame de Cleves. They gave her remorse. She reflected on the violence of the feeling that was drawing her toward Monsieur de Nemours. She found that she was no longer in command of her words or her face. She thought that Lignerolles had come back, that she no longer feared the English affair, that she no longer had any suspicions about the Queen Dauphine, and that, in short, there was nothing left to protect her, and no safety for her except in going away. But since going away was not in her power, she found herself in a desperate position, on the point of falling into what seemed to her the greatest of all misfortunes, which was to let Monsieur de Nemours see what she felt for him. She remembered everything Madame de Chartres had said to her on her deathbed, and her advice to take any course whatever, however difficult it might be, rather than get involved in a love affair. What Monsieur de Cleves had said about sincerity, when he was speaking of Madame de Tournon, came back into her mind. It seemed to her that she ought to confess to him what she felt for Monsieur de Nemours. That thought occupied her for a long time. Then she was shocked at having had it. She saw madness in it, and fell back into the awkward position of not knowing what course to take.

The peace was signed. Madame Elisabeth, after great reluctance, had resolved to obey the King her father. The Duke of Alba had been named to come and marry her in the name of the Catholic King, Philip II of Spain, and he was expected soon. The Duke of Savoy was expected as well. He was coming to marry Madame Marguerite, the King's sister, and the two weddings were to be held at the same time. The King thought of nothing but making these weddings famous through entertainments in which he could show off the skill and the magnificence of his court. The grandest ballets and plays that could be staged were proposed, but the King found such entertainments too private, and he wanted something more brilliant. He resolved to hold a tournament, open to foreigners, which the people could watch. All the princes and young lords took up the King's plan with joy, above all the Duke of Ferrara, Monsieur de Guise, and Monsieur de Nemours, who surpassed everyone else in exercises of that kind. The King chose them to be the four champions of the tournament with him.

It was proclaimed throughout the kingdom that in the city of Paris, on the fifteenth of June, the lists would be opened by His Most Christian Majesty the King and by the princes Alfonso d'Este, Duke of Ferrara, François of Lorraine, Duke of Guise, and Jacques of Savoy, Duke of Nemours, to be held against all comers. The first combat would be on horseback in the lists, in double armor, four courses with the lance and one for the ladies. The second would be with swords, one against one or two against two, as the marshals of the field chose. The third would be on foot, three strokes of the pike and six of the sword. The champions would supply lances, swords, and pikes, and the comers would choose among them. Anyone who struck his opponent's horse in a charge would be put out of the ranks. There would be four marshals of the field to give the orders, and those comers who had broken the most lances and performed best would receive a prize, its value left to the judges. Every comer, French or foreign, would be required to come and touch one of the shields hung on the stand at the end of the lists, or several of them, as he chose. There he would find a herald to receive him and enroll him according to his rank and the shields he had touched. The comers would be required to have a gentleman bring their shield with their coat of arms, to hang on the stand three days before the tournament began. Otherwise they would not be admitted without the champions' leave.

A great field for the tournament was laid out near the Bastille. It ran from the palace of the Tournelles across the rue Saint-Antoine to the royal stables. On both sides stood stands and tiers of seats with covered boxes, which formed a kind of gallery, made a very fine sight, and could hold an endless number of people. All the princes and lords now thought of nothing but ordering what they needed to appear with brilliance, and of working into their monograms or their mottoes some touch of love that referred to the women they loved.
