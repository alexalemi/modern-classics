Part Three

(Part 2 of 3)

When Monsieur de Cleves came home, she told him that she wanted to go to the country: she was not feeling well, and she needed fresh air. Her husband looked at her and saw a beauty that did not suggest anything seriously wrong, so at first he laughed at the idea of the trip. He reminded her that she was forgetting the princesses' weddings and the tournament, which were about to take place, and that she had none too much time to get ready to appear there as magnificently as the other women. His reasons did not change her plan. She asked him to let her go to Coulommiers while he went to Compiègne with the King. Coulommiers was a fine house a day's journey from Paris that they were having built with great care. Monsieur de Cleves agreed. She went, meaning not to come back soon, and the King left for Compiègne, where he was to stay only a few days.

Monsieur de Nemours had been deeply unhappy not to have seen Madame de Cleves since that afternoon he had spent with her so pleasantly, the one that had raised his hopes. He was so impatient to see her again that he could not rest, and so, when the King returned to Paris, he decided to visit his sister, the Duchess of Mercoeur, who was in the country quite near Coulommiers. He suggested that the Vidame come with him, and the Vidame readily agreed. Nemours made the plan in the hope of seeing Madame de Cleves, and of calling on her with the Vidame.

Madame de Mercoeur welcomed them with great joy and thought of nothing but entertaining them and giving them every pleasure the country offers. While they were out hunting the stag, Monsieur de Nemours lost his way in the forest. As he asked which road would take him back, he learned that he was close to Coulommiers. At that one word, Coulommiers, without any reflection and without knowing what he meant to do, he rode at full gallop in the direction he was shown. He came into the forest and let himself be led at random along carefully made paths, which he judged must lead toward the house. At the end of these paths he found a pavilion. The lower floor was a large salon with two smaller rooms off it; one opened onto a flower garden, separated from the forest only by a fence, and the other looked out on a wide avenue of the park. He went into the pavilion, and he would have stopped to admire its beauty, except that he saw Monsieur and Madame de Cleves coming down the avenue, followed by a large number of servants. He had not expected to find Monsieur de Cleves, whom he had left with the King, and his first impulse was to hide. He went into the room that opened onto the flower garden, meaning to leave by a door that stood open onto the forest. But then he saw that Madame de Cleves and her husband had sat down in the pavilion, that their servants were staying in the park, and that they could not reach him without passing through the room where Monsieur and Madame de Cleves were sitting. He could not deny himself the pleasure of seeing her, nor resist the curiosity of listening to her conversation with a husband who made him more jealous than any of his rivals.

He heard Monsieur de Cleves saying to his wife: "But why won't you come back to Paris? What can keep you in the country? For some time now you have had a taste for solitude that surprises me and grieves me, because it separates us. I even find you sadder than usual, and I am afraid you have some cause for sorrow."

"There is nothing troubling my mind," she answered, with an awkward air, "but the commotion of the court is so great, and there are always so many people at your house, that body and mind cannot help growing tired and looking for rest."

"Rest," he replied, "is hardly right for a person of your age. At home and at court you are not the kind of person to tire yourself out. I would sooner fear that you are glad to be away from me."

"You would do me a great injustice to think so," she said, more and more awkward, "but I beg you to leave me here. If you could stay here, I would be very glad of it, provided you stayed alone and were willing to do without that endless crowd of people who almost never leave you."

"Ah, Madame!" cried Monsieur de Cleves. "Your look and your words show me that you have reasons for wanting to be alone which I do not know, and I beg you to tell me what they are."

He pressed her for a long time to tell him, without being able to make her. And after she had defended herself in a way that only increased her husband's curiosity, she fell into a deep silence, her eyes lowered. Then all at once she spoke, and looked at him.

"Do not force me," she said, "to confess something to you that I have not the strength to confess, though I have meant to several times. Only consider that prudence does not allow a woman of my age, who is her own mistress in her conduct, to remain exposed in the middle of the court."

"What are you making me imagine, Madame?" cried Monsieur de Cleves. "I would not dare say it, for fear of offending you."

Madame de Cleves did not answer, and her silence confirmed her husband in what he had thought.

"You say nothing," he went on, "and that is telling me that I am not mistaken."

"Well then, Monsieur," she answered, throwing herself at his knees, "I am going to make you a confession that no wife has ever made to her husband. But the innocence of my conduct and of my intentions gives me the strength to make it. It is true that I have reasons to keep away from the court, and that I want to avoid the dangers that people of my age sometimes find themselves in. I have never shown the least sign of weakness, and I would not be afraid of showing any, if you left me free to withdraw from the court, or if I still had Madame de Chartres to help guide me. However dangerous the course I am taking, I take it gladly, to keep myself worthy of being yours. I ask your pardon a thousand times if I have feelings that displease you; at least I will never displease you by my actions. Consider that to do what I am doing, a woman must have more affection and more esteem for her husband than any woman has ever had. Guide me, have pity on me, and love me still, if you can."

Through this whole speech Monsieur de Cleves had sat with his head in his hands, beside himself, and it had not occurred to him to raise his wife from her knees. When she stopped speaking and he looked at her, and saw her at his knees, her face covered with tears and so admirably beautiful, he thought he would die of grief. He raised her up and embraced her.

"Have pity on me yourself, Madame," he said. "I deserve it. And forgive me if, in the first moments of a grief as violent as mine, I do not respond as I ought to conduct like yours. You seem to me more worthy of esteem and admiration than any woman who has ever lived in the world. But I also find myself the unhappiest man who ever lived. I have been in love with you from the first moment I saw you. Neither your coldness nor possessing you has been able to put that love out; it lasts still. I have never been able to make you love me, and now I see that you are afraid of loving someone else. And who is he, Madame, this fortunate man who gives you this fear? Since when has he pleased you? What has he done to please you? What road did he find to your heart? I had consoled myself in some way for never having touched your heart with the thought that it could not be touched. Yet another man does what I could not do. I feel at once the jealousy of a husband and the jealousy of a man in love. But it is impossible to feel a husband's jealousy after conduct like yours. It is too noble not to give me complete security; it even consoles me as a man who loves you. The trust and the sincerity you have shown me are beyond any price. You think well enough of me to believe that I will not abuse this confession. You are right, Madame; I will not abuse it, and I will not love you any the less. You make me unhappy by the greatest proof of faithfulness that a woman has ever given her husband. But, Madame, finish, and tell me who it is you want to avoid."

"I beg you not to ask me," she answered. "I am resolved not to tell you, and I believe prudence does not allow me to name him."

"Do not be afraid, Madame," Monsieur de Cleves went on. "I know the world too well to be unaware that respect for a husband does not stop a man from being in love with his wife. One should hate the men who are, and not complain of them. And once more, Madame, I beg you to tell me what I want to know."

"You would press me in vain," she replied. "I have the strength to keep silent about what I believe I ought not to say. The confession I made you was not made out of weakness, and it takes more courage to admit this truth than to undertake to hide it."

Monsieur de Nemours did not miss a word of this conversation, and what Madame de Cleves had just said made him hardly less jealous than her husband. He was so desperately in love with her that he believed everyone else felt the same. It was true, too, that he had several rivals; but he imagined still more, and his mind wandered in search of the man Madame de Cleves meant. He had believed many times that she did not dislike him, but he had built that judgment on things which seemed so slight to him at this moment that he could not imagine he had inspired a passion that must be very violent indeed to drive her to so extraordinary a remedy. He was so carried away that he hardly knew what he was seeing, and he could not forgive Monsieur de Cleves for not pressing his wife harder to tell him the name she was hiding.

Monsieur de Cleves was nevertheless doing everything he could to learn it, and after he had pressed her in vain, she answered: "It seems to me that you ought to be satisfied with my sincerity. Do not ask me for more, and do not give me reason to regret what I have just done. Content yourself with the assurance I give you once again: that none of my actions has betrayed my feelings, and that no one has ever said anything to me that I could have taken offense at."

"Ah, Madame," Monsieur de Cleves broke in suddenly, "I cannot believe you. I remember how confused you were the day your portrait was lost. You gave it away, Madame; you gave away that portrait which was so dear to me, and which belonged to me so rightfully. You have not been able to hide your feelings. You are in love; it is known; your virtue has protected you, so far, from the rest."

"Is it possible," she cried, "that you can think there is any pretense in a confession like mine, which no reason obliged me to make? Trust my words. I am paying a high enough price for the trust I ask of you. Believe me, I beg you: I did not give away my portrait. It is true that I saw it taken, but I did not want to show that I saw, for fear of exposing myself to being told things that no one had yet dared say to me."

"Then how has he shown you that he loves you," Monsieur de Cleves went on, "and what proofs of passion has he given you?"

"Spare me the pain," she replied, "of repeating details that make me ashamed of myself for having noticed them, and which have only too well convinced me of my weakness."

"You are right, Madame," he answered. "I am unjust. Refuse me every time I ask you such things; but do not be offended, all the same, when I ask."

At that moment several of their people, who had stayed in the avenues, came to tell Monsieur de Cleves that a gentleman had come looking for him on the King's behalf, with orders for him to be in Paris that evening. Monsieur de Cleves was obliged to leave, and he could say nothing to his wife except that he begged her to come the next day, and that he implored her to believe that, grieved as he was, he had a tenderness and an esteem for her that she ought to be satisfied with.

When he had gone and Madame de Cleves was left alone, and she looked at what she had just done, she was so appalled that she could hardly believe it was real. She felt that she had robbed herself of her husband's heart and esteem, and that she had dug herself a pit she would never climb out of. She asked herself why she had done something so dangerous, and she found that she had gone into it almost without meaning to. The strangeness of such a confession, which she could find no example of, showed her all its peril.

But when she came to think that this remedy, violent as it was, was the only one that could defend her against Monsieur de Nemours, she found that she ought not to regret it, and that she had not risked too much. She spent the whole night full of uncertainty, agitation, and fear. But at last calm returned to her mind. She even found a sweetness in having given this proof of faithfulness to a husband who deserved it so well, who had so much esteem and so much affection for her, and who had just given her fresh proofs of it by the way he had received what she confessed.

Meanwhile Monsieur de Nemours had left the place where he had heard a conversation that touched him so deeply, and had gone deep into the forest. What Madame de Cleves had said about her portrait had brought him back to life, because it told him that he was the man she did not hate. He gave himself up to that joy at first. But it did not last long, once he reflected that the very thing which had just taught him he had touched her heart ought also to convince him that he would never receive any sign of it, and that it was impossible to win over a woman who would go to so extraordinary a remedy. And yet he felt a keen pleasure at having driven her to this extremity. He took pride in having made himself loved by a woman so unlike every other woman of her sex. In short, he found himself a hundred times happy and unhappy at once. Night overtook him in the forest, and he had great trouble finding the road back to Madame de Mercoeur's. He arrived at daybreak. He was hard put to explain what had kept him; he got out of it as best he could, and returned to Paris that same day with the Vidame.

Nemours was so full of his passion, and so astonished by what he had heard, that he fell into a fairly common imprudence: talking in general terms about one's own particular feelings, and telling one's own adventures under borrowed names. On the way back he turned the conversation to love. He dwelt on the pleasure of being in love with a woman worthy of being loved; he spoke of the strange effects of that passion; and at last, unable to keep to himself the shock Madame de Cleves's action had given him, he told it to the Vidame, without naming her and without saying that he had any part in it. But he told it with so much warmth and so much admiration that the Vidame easily suspected the story concerned Nemours himself. He pressed him hard to admit it. He said that he had known for a long time that Nemours had some violent passion, and that it was unjust to distrust a man who had trusted him with the secret of his own life. Monsieur de Nemours was too much in love to admit his love. He had always hidden it from the Vidame, though the Vidame was the man at court he liked best. He answered that one of his friends had told him this story and had made him promise not to speak of it, and that he begged the Vidame to keep the secret too. The Vidame assured him that he would say nothing. Nevertheless Monsieur de Nemours regretted having told him so much.

Meanwhile Monsieur de Cleves had gone to the King, his heart pierced with a mortal grief. No husband had ever felt so violent a passion for his wife, or esteemed her so much. What he had just learned did not take away his esteem, but it gave him an esteem of a different kind from the one he had felt until then. What occupied him most was the wish to guess who had managed to please her. Monsieur de Nemours came into his mind first, as the most attractive man at court, and then the Chevalier de Guise and the Marshal de Saint-André, as two men who had once hoped to please her and who still paid her a great deal of attention. So he settled on the belief that it had to be one of the three. He reached the Louvre, and the King took him into his private room to tell him that he had chosen him to escort Madame Elisabeth to Spain: he had thought that no one would carry out this commission better, and that no one would do France so much honor there as Madame de Cleves. Monsieur de Cleves received the honor of this choice as he ought, and he even looked on it as something that would take his wife away from the court without any visible change in her conduct. Yet the time of this departure was still too far off to be a remedy for the awkward position he was in. He wrote at once to Madame de Cleves to tell her what the King had just said to him, and he added that he absolutely wanted her back in Paris. She came back as he ordered, and when they saw each other, they both found themselves in an extraordinary sadness.

Monsieur de Cleves spoke to her like the most honorable man in the world, and the one most worthy of what she had done.

"I have no anxiety about your conduct," he said. "You have more strength and more virtue than you think. It is not fear of the future that grieves me, either. The only thing that grieves me is to see you feel for another man what I could never make you feel for me."

"I do not know what to answer," she said. "I am dying of shame to speak of it with you. Spare me, I beg you, such cruel conversations. Govern my conduct; arrange it so that I see no one. That is all I ask of you. But let me say no more to you about a thing that makes me look so little worthy of you, and that I find so unworthy of myself."

"You are right, Madame," he replied. "I am abusing your gentleness and your trust. But have some compassion, too, for the state you have put me in, and consider that, whatever you have told me, you are hiding from me a name that gives me a curiosity I cannot live with. I do not ask you to satisfy it. But I cannot help telling you that I believe the man I ought to envy is the Marshal de Saint-André, the Duke of Nemours, or the Chevalier de Guise."

"I will answer nothing," she said, blushing, "and I will give you no ground, by my answers, either to weaken or to strengthen your suspicions. But if you try to clear them up by watching me, you will make me so awkward that the whole world will see it. In God's name," she went on, "let me see no one, on the pretext of some illness."

"No, Madame," he replied. "People would soon work out that it was a pretense. Besides, I will trust no one but you. That is the road my heart advises me to take, and my reason advises it too. With your nature, by leaving you your freedom I set you narrower limits than any I could lay down."

Monsieur de Cleves was not mistaken. The trust he showed his wife strengthened her all the more against Monsieur de Nemours, and made her take sterner resolutions than any constraint could have forced on her. So she went to the Louvre and to the Queen Dauphine's as usual; but she avoided Monsieur de Nemours's presence and his eyes with such care that she took away almost all the joy he had in believing she loved him. He saw nothing in her conduct that did not persuade him of the opposite. He hardly knew whether what he had heard had not been a dream, so unlikely did it seem. The one thing that assured him he was not mistaken was the extreme sadness of Madame de Cleves, whatever effort she made to hide it. Perhaps kind looks and kind words would not have increased Monsieur de Nemours's love as much as this stern conduct did.

One evening, when Monsieur and Madame de Cleves were at the Queen's, someone said there was a rumor that the King was going to name another great lord of the court to escort Madame Elisabeth to Spain. Monsieur de Cleves had his eyes on his wife when the speaker added that it might be the Chevalier de Guise or the Marshal de Saint-André. He noticed that she was not moved by either of these two names, nor by the prospect of their making the journey with her. That made him believe that neither of the two was the man whose presence she feared. To settle his suspicions, he went into the Queen's private room, where the King was. After staying there a while he came back to his wife and whispered to her that he had just learned it would be Monsieur de Nemours who was going with them to Spain.

The name of Monsieur de Nemours, and the thought of being exposed to seeing him every day on a long journey, in front of her husband, threw Madame de Cleves into an agitation she could not hide. Trying to give it other reasons, she answered: "That is a very unwelcome choice for you. He will share all the honors, and it seems to me you ought to try to have someone else chosen."

"It is not honor, Madame," Monsieur de Cleves went on, "that makes you dread Monsieur de Nemours coming with me. Your displeasure at it has another cause. Your displeasure tells me what another woman's joy would have told me. But do not be afraid. What I have just told you is not true; I invented it to make sure of something I already believed only too well."

He left after these words, not wanting to add by his presence to the extreme awkwardness he saw his wife in.

Monsieur de Nemours came in at that instant, and noticed at once the state Madame de Cleves was in. He went up to her and said in a low voice that out of respect he did not dare ask her what made her more pensive than usual. The sound of his voice brought her to herself. She looked at him without having heard what he had just said, full of her own thoughts and of the fear that her husband would see him beside her.

"In God's name," she said, "leave me in peace."

"Alas, Madame," he answered, "I leave you in peace only too well. What can you complain of? I do not dare speak to you; I do not even dare look at you; I never come near you without trembling. How have I brought on myself what you have just said to me? And why do you let me see that I have some part in the sorrow I find you in?"

Madame de Cleves was very sorry to have given Monsieur de Nemours an opening to speak more plainly than he had ever done in his life. She left him without answering and went home, her mind more agitated than it had ever been. Her husband easily saw that her awkwardness had grown. He saw that she was afraid he would speak to her about what had happened. He followed her into a small room she had gone into.

"Do not avoid me, Madame," he said. "I will say nothing to you that could displease you. I ask your pardon for the surprise I sprang on you earlier; I am punished enough for it by what I learned. Of all men, Monsieur de Nemours was the one I feared most. I see the danger you are in. Have power over yourself, for your own sake, and, if it is possible, for mine. I do not ask it of you as a husband, but as a man whose whole happiness you are, and who has a tenderer and more violent passion for you than the man your heart prefers to him."

Monsieur de Cleves was overcome as he spoke these last words, and could hardly finish them. His wife was pierced by them, and bursting into tears she embraced him with a tenderness and a grief that left him in a state little different from her own. They stayed some time without saying anything, and parted without the strength to speak.

The preparations for Madame Elisabeth's marriage were complete. The Duke of Alba arrived to marry her by proxy. He was received with all the magnificence and all the ceremony such an occasion could call for. The King sent to meet him the Prince of Condé, the Cardinals of Lorraine and of Guise, and the Dukes of Lorraine, Ferrara, Aumale, Bouillon, Guise, and Nemours. They brought several gentlemen with them and a great many pages dressed in their liveries. The King himself waited for the Duke of Alba at the first gate of the Louvre with the two hundred gentlemen of his household, the Constable at their head. When the Duke came near the King, he tried to embrace his knees; but the King prevented him, and had him walk at his side to the Queen's apartments and to Madame Elisabeth's, where the Duke of Alba presented her with a magnificent gift from his master. He then went to Madame Marguerite, the King's sister, to pay her the compliments of the Duke of Savoy and to assure her that he would arrive within a few days. Great assemblies were held at the Louvre to show the Duke of Alba, and the Prince of Orange who had come with him, the beauties of the court.

Madame de Cleves did not dare stay away from them, however much she wanted to, for fear of displeasing her husband, who commanded her absolutely to go. What decided her still more was the absence of Monsieur de Nemours. He had gone to meet the Duke of Savoy, and after the Duke arrived, he was obliged to stay almost always at his side to help him with everything that concerned the ceremonies of his wedding. So Madame de Cleves did not meet Nemours as often as she had been used to, and she found in that a kind of peace of mind.

The Vidame de Chartres had not forgotten the conversation he had had with Monsieur de Nemours. It had stayed in his mind that the story Nemours told him was his own, and he watched him so closely that he might perhaps have worked out the truth, if the arrival of the Duke of Alba and of the Duke of Savoy had not brought the court a change and a bustle of business that kept him from seeing what might have enlightened him. The wish to find out, or rather the natural tendency we all have to tell everything we know to the person we love, made him repeat to Madame de Martigues the extraordinary action of this woman who had confessed to her husband the passion she felt for another man. He assured her that Monsieur de Nemours was the man who had inspired this violent passion, and he begged her to help him watch him. Madame de Martigues was very glad to hear what the Vidame told her, and the curiosity she had always seen in the Queen Dauphine about everything that concerned Monsieur de Nemours made her all the more eager to get to the bottom of this affair.
