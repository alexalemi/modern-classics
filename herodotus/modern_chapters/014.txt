Chapter 14: The Labyrinth, and the Reigns from Psammetichus to Amasis

148. The twelve kings also decided to join together and leave a memorial of themselves. Having decided this, they had a labyrinth built, a little above Lake Moeris and nearly opposite the place called the City of Crocodiles. I have seen it myself, and I found it greater than words can say. If you were to put together and add up all the buildings and great works the Greeks have produced, they would turn out to have cost less labor and money than this labyrinth, even though the temple at Ephesus and the one at Samos are certainly works worth noting. The pyramids, too, were greater than words can say, and each one of them is equal to many of the Greeks' works, great as those may be; but the labyrinth surpasses even the pyramids. It has twelve roofed courts, with their gates facing one another, six on the north side and six on the south, all joined to one another, and the same wall surrounds them all on the outside. There are two kinds of rooms in it, one kind below ground and the other above, built on top of them, three thousand in number, fifteen hundred of each kind. The upper rooms we saw for ourselves, walking through them, and we describe them having looked at them with our own eyes. The underground rooms we know only by report, because the Egyptians in charge of them would not on any account show them to us. They said that the tombs of the kings who first built this labyrinth were down there, and the tombs of the sacred crocodiles. So we tell about the lower rooms from what we heard, while the upper ones we saw ourselves and found to be works greater than human. The passages through the rooms, and the winding ways this way and that through the courts, which were splendidly decorated, gave us endless cause for wonder as we went from a court into the rooms beyond it, and from the rooms into colonnades, and from the colonnades into other rooms, and then from the rooms again into other courts. Over the whole of it is a roof of stone, like the walls. The walls are covered with carved figures, and each court is surrounded by a colonnade of white stone, fitted together perfectly. At the far end of the labyrinth, by its corner, there is a pyramid 240 feet high, with large figures carved on it, and an underground passage has been made leading to it.

149. That is the labyrinth. But the lake called Lake Moeris, beside which the labyrinth is built, is an even greater wonder. Its shoreline runs about 400 miles all the way around (sixty schoeni), which is the same length as the whole coast of Egypt along the sea. The lake stretches lengthwise from north to south, and where it is deepest it is 300 feet deep. It is obvious that this lake was made by hand and dug out, because near the middle of it stand two pyramids, each rising 300 feet above the water, with an equal height built below the water. On top of each one is a colossal stone statue of a man seated on a throne. So these pyramids are a hundred fathoms high, and a hundred fathoms make up a stade of six hundred feet; the fathom is six feet, or four cubits, the foot is four palms, and the cubit is six palms. The water in the lake does not come from the spot itself, since the land there is very short of water. It has been brought in from the Nile by a canal. For six months the water flows into the lake, and for six months it flows back out into the Nile. During the six months that it flows out, the fish that are caught bring a talent of silver a day into the royal treasury, and twenty minas a day while the water is flowing in.

150. The people who live there also told me that this lake has an underground outlet to the Syrtis in Libya, running west toward the interior of the continent along the mountain range above Memphis. Now, I could not see the earth from this excavation lying anywhere, and that was something that caught my attention. So I asked the people who lived nearest the lake where the earth that had been dug out had gone. They told me where it had been carried, and I easily believed them, because I had heard that something similar was once done at Nineveh, the city of the Assyrians. There, some thieves once made a plan to carry off the treasure of King Sardanapalus son of Ninus. It was a very great treasure, and it was kept in underground treasure-houses. So the thieves started from their own house and, working out their direction, dug underground toward the king's palace. Each night, when it got dark, they carried the earth from their tunnel down to the river Tigris, which flows past the city of Nineveh, until at last they got what they were after. I heard that the lake in Egypt was dug in a similar way, except that the work was done by day and not by night. As the Egyptians dug, they carried the earth they had dug out to the Nile, and the river, once it received the earth, naturally carried it off and scattered it. That, they say, is how this lake was dug.

151. The twelve kings went on ruling justly. But as time went on, this is what happened. They had offered sacrifice in the temple of Hephaestus, and on the last day of the festival they were about to pour libations. The high priest brought out the golden cups they always used for pouring them, but he miscounted and brought only eleven for the twelve kings. So the one standing last in line, Psammetichus, having no cup, took off his helmet, which was made of bronze, held it out to receive the wine, and poured his libation with it. All the other kings wore helmets too, as was their custom, and they happened to have them on then. Psammetichus held out his helmet without any treacherous intent. But the others took note of what he had done, and they remembered the oracle, which had told them that whichever of them poured a libation from a bronze cup would become the sole king of Egypt. Remembering the oracle's words, they did not think it right to kill Psammetichus, since when they questioned him they found that he had not done it on purpose. But they decided to strip him of almost all his power and drive him off into the marshes, and to forbid him from having any dealings with the rest of Egypt from there.

152. This Psammetichus had once before been an exile. He had fled from Sabacus the Ethiopian, who had killed his father Necho, and he had lived as an exile in Syria. When the Ethiopian left Egypt because of the vision he saw in his dream, the Egyptians of the district of Sais brought Psammetichus back home. Later, when he was one of the kings, it was his fate to be an exile a second time, driven out into the marshes by the eleven kings because of the helmet. Believing that they had done him a great wrong, he thought about how he could take revenge on the men who had driven him out. He sent to the oracle of Leto in the city of Buto, which is the most truthful oracle the Egyptians have, and the answer came back that his revenge would come when men of bronze appeared from the sea. He was strongly inclined not to believe that men of bronze would come to help him. But not long afterward some Ionians and Carians who had sailed out on a raid for plunder were forced to put in to the shore of Egypt. They landed, dressed in bronze armor, and one of the Egyptians, who had never before seen men in bronze armor, went to the marshes and reported to Psammetichus that men of bronze had come from the sea and were plundering the plain. Psammetichus saw that the oracle's words were coming true. He treated the Ionians and Carians in a friendly way, and with large promises he persuaded them to join his side. Once he had persuaded them, he overthrew the other kings with the help of these foreign mercenaries and of the Egyptians who supported him.

153. Now that he had power over all of Egypt, Psammetichus built for Hephaestus the gateway of the temple at Memphis that faces south. Opposite this gateway he built a court for Apis, where Apis is kept whenever he appears. It has a colonnade all around it and is covered with carved figures, and instead of columns the roof of the court is held up by colossal statues eighteen feet tall. Apis, in the Greek language, is Epaphus.

154. To the Ionians and Carians who had helped him, Psammetichus gave plots of land to live on, facing one another across the Nile, and these were called the Encampments. He gave them this land, and he also paid them everything he had promised. He also put Egyptian boys in their care to be taught the Greek language, and the present class of interpreters in Egypt is descended from these boys, who learned the language thoroughly. The Ionians and Carians lived on these lands for a long time. They lie toward the sea, a little below the city of Bubastis, on the branch of the Nile called the Pelusian mouth. Later King Amasis moved them out of there and settled them at Memphis, making them his own bodyguard against the Egyptians. Because they have lived in Egypt, we Greeks, through our dealings with them, know accurately everything that has happened in Egypt from the reign of King Psammetichus onward, for they were the first people of a foreign language to settle in Egypt. In the land they were moved from, the sheds where they hauled up their ships and the ruins of their houses were still there in my own time.

So that is how Psammetichus got Egypt.

155. I have mentioned the Egyptian oracle many times before this, and now I will give an account of it, since it deserves to be described. This oracle in Egypt is sacred to Leto, and it is in a great city near the branch of the Nile called the Sebennytic mouth, on your way upriver from the sea. The name of the city where the oracle is, as I have said before when I mentioned it, is Buto. In this Buto there is a temple of Apollo and Artemis, and the shrine of Leto, where the oracle is, is large in itself and has a gateway sixty feet high. But I will now tell what amazed me most of all the things to be seen there. In this sacred enclosure there is a shrine of Leto made from a single stone, both in height and in length, and all its walls are the same in both directions, sixty feet each. Another stone is laid on top of it to make the roof, and its cornice is six feet deep.

156. Of all the things I saw at that temple, then, this shrine is the most amazing. Next after it comes the island called Chemmis. This island lies in a deep, broad lake beside the temple at Buto, and the Egyptians say that it is a floating island. I myself never saw it floating about or moved from its place, and I am astonished to hear it said, and I wonder whether it really is a floating island. On this island there is a great shrine of Apollo, with three altars set up inside it, and many palm trees grow on the island, along with other trees, some bearing fruit and some not. When the Egyptians say that the island floats, they add this story. This island, they say, did not float before. Leto, who was one of the first eight gods to come into being, and who lived in the city of Buto, where she has this oracle, was given Apollo by Isis to look after, and she kept him safe by hiding him on this island, which is now said to float. That was at the time when Typhon came after him, searching everywhere, hoping to find the son of Osiris. They say that Apollo and Artemis are the children of Dionysus and Isis, and that Leto was their nurse and protector. In the Egyptian language, Apollo is Horus, Demeter is Isis, and Artemis is Bubastis. It was from this story, and from nowhere else, that Aeschylus son of Euphorion took the idea I am about to mention, in which he differs from all the poets before him: he made Artemis the daughter of Demeter. And this, they say, is the reason the island came to float.

That is the story they tell.

157. As for Psammetichus, he was king of Egypt for fifty-four years. For twenty-nine of those years he sat outside Azotus, a great city of Syria, besieging it, until at last he took it. Of all the cities we know of, this Azotus held out longest under a siege.

158. Psammetichus' son was Necho, and he became king of Egypt. He was the first to begin the canal leading to the Erythraean Sea, which Darius the Persian finished later. Its length is a voyage of four days, and it was dug wide enough for two triremes, the three-banked warships, to row along it side by side. Its water is brought in from the Nile. The canal leaves the Nile a little above the city of Bubastis, passes by Patumus, the Arabian city, and runs into the Erythraean Sea. It is dug first along the parts of the Egyptian plain that face Arabia. Just above them run the mountains that stretch along opposite Memphis, where the stone quarries are. The canal follows the foot of these mountains from west to east for a long way. After that it turns toward a gap in the hills and runs from these mountains southward, toward midday and the south wind, to the Arabian Gulf, the Red Sea. Now the shortest and most direct crossing from the Northern Sea to the Southern Sea (which is also called the Erythraean), that is, from the Mediterranean to the Indian Ocean, starts from Mount Casius, the boundary between Egypt and Syria; from there to the Arabian Gulf is exactly 111 miles. But the canal is much longer, since it winds a great deal. During Necho's reign, 120,000 Egyptians died digging it. Necho stopped in the middle of the digging because an oracle stood in his way: it said that he was working for the barbarian. The Egyptians call everyone who does not speak their language a barbarian.

159. After giving up work on the canal, Necho turned to making war. He had triremes built, some for the Northern Sea and others in the Arabian Gulf for the Erythraean Sea, and the sheds where they were kept can still be seen. He used these ships when he needed them. On land, too, Necho fought the Syrians at Magdolus and defeated them, and after this he took Cadytis, a great city of Syria. He dedicated the clothes he was wearing when he won these victories to Apollo, sending them to Branchidae in the territory of Miletus. After this, when he had reigned sixteen years in all, he died and handed the kingdom on to his son Psammis.

160. While this Psammis was king of Egypt, envoys came to him from the Eleans. The Eleans boasted that they ran the games at Olympia in the most just and honorable way possible, and they thought that not even the Egyptians, the wisest of men, could find anything to add to their rules. When the Eleans came to Egypt and explained what they had come for, the king called together the Egyptians who were thought to be the wisest. When these Egyptians had gathered, they heard the Eleans describe everything it was their job to do in running the games. When the Eleans had told them everything, they said they had come to learn whether the Egyptians could find anything to add that was fairer still. The Egyptians talked it over together and then asked the Eleans whether their own citizens took part in the games. The Eleans said that anyone who wanted to, whether one of their own people or any other Greek, was equally free to compete. At this the Egyptians said that in setting things up this way they had completely missed the mark of fairness. It was impossible, they said, for the Eleans not to favor a man from their own city if he was competing, and so to treat the outsider unfairly. If they really wanted to run the games fairly, as they said they did, and if this was the reason they had come to Egypt, the Egyptians advised them to set up the games so that only outsiders could compete, and no Elean would be allowed to. That was the advice the Egyptians gave the Eleans.

161. Psammis had been king of Egypt for only six years. He made an expedition into Ethiopia, and soon afterward he died. Apries, the son of Psammis, inherited the kingdom after him. Apries became the most prosperous of all the kings up to that time, except only for his great-grandfather Psammetichus. He reigned twenty-five years, and during that time he led an army against Sidon and fought a sea battle with the king of Tyre. But since it was fated that disaster should come on him, it came about through something I will tell at greater length in the Libyan part of my history, and only briefly here. Apries sent a great expedition against the people of Cyrene, and it met with a correspondingly great disaster. The Egyptians blamed him for this and rebelled against him. They believed that Apries had deliberately sent them out to certain ruin, so that (as they put it) there might be a slaughter of them, and he could rule the rest of the Egyptians more securely. Angry at this, both the men who had come back from the expedition and the friends of those who had died rose in open revolt.

162. When Apries heard this, he sent Amasis to them to talk them out of it. Amasis came and was trying to restrain the Egyptians. But while he was speaking and telling them not to do it, one of the Egyptians stood up behind him and put a helmet on his head, saying as he did it that he was putting it on him to crown him king. This was not altogether unwelcome to Amasis, as his conduct showed, for as soon as the rebel Egyptians had made him king, he got ready to march against Apries. When Apries heard this, he sent to Amasis one of the Egyptians of his own court, a man of high standing named Patarbemis, with orders to bring Amasis back to him alive. When Patarbemis arrived and summoned Amasis, Amasis, who happened to be sitting on horseback, lifted his leg and broke wind, and told him to take that back to Apries. All the same, they say, Patarbemis demanded that he go to the king, since the king had sent for him. Amasis answered that he had been getting ready to do that for some time, and that Apries would have no reason to complain of him. Patarbemis understood what he meant from what he said, and he also saw his preparations, so he left in a hurry, wanting to let the king know as quickly as possible what was going on. But when he came back to Apries without Amasis, the king would not listen to what he had to say. In a violent rage, he ordered his ears and nose to be cut off. When the rest of the Egyptians who were still on Apries' side saw the most respected man among them treated with such shameful outrage, they waited no longer. They went over to the rebels and gave themselves up to Amasis.

163. When Apries heard this too, he armed his foreign mercenaries and marched against the Egyptians. He had Carian and Ionian mercenaries with him, thirty thousand of them. His royal palace was in the city of Sais, and it was large and well worth seeing. So Apries and his army were advancing against the Egyptians, and Amasis and his men were advancing against the mercenaries. Both sides came to the city of Momemphis and were about to try their strength against each other.

164. The Egyptians are divided into seven classes. One class is called the priests, another the warriors, and the rest are the cowherds, the swineherds, the shopkeepers, the interpreters, and the boatmen. That is the number of the Egyptian classes, and they are named after the work they do. The warriors are called Calasirians and Hermotybians, and they come from the following districts, since all of Egypt is divided into districts.

165. The districts of the Hermotybians are Busiris, Sais, Chemmis, Papremis, the island called Prosopitis, and half of Natho. These are the districts the Hermotybians come from, and at their most numerous they reached 160,000. Not one of them has learned any trade; they are devoted entirely to war.

166. The districts of the Calasirians, in turn, are Thebes, Bubastis, Aphthis, Tanis, Mendes, Sebennytus, Athribis, Pharbaethus, Thmuis, Onuphis, Anytis, and Myecphoris, which last is on an island opposite the city of Bubastis. These are the districts of the Calasirians, and at their most numerous they reached 250,000 men. They, like the others, are not allowed to practice any trade. They practice only what has to do with war, and the skill is handed down from father to son.

167. Now whether the Greeks learned this too from the Egyptians I can't say for certain. I see that the Thracians, the Scythians, the Persians, the Lydians, and nearly all non-Greeks hold those of their citizens who learn trades, and their descendants, in less honor than the rest, while those who have nothing to do with working with their hands are counted noble, and above all those who are devoted to war. However that may be, all the Greeks have learned this attitude, the Spartans most of all. The Corinthians look down least on people who work at crafts.

168. This class, alone among the Egyptians apart from the priests, was given a special privilege: each man was granted twelve arourae of land, free of taxes. The aroura measures a hundred Egyptian cubits each way, about two-thirds of an acre, and the Egyptian cubit happens to be the same as the Samian. This privilege was given to all of them. They also had other benefits in turn, never the same men twice: each year a thousand Calasirians and a thousand Hermotybians served as the king's bodyguard. On top of their land, these men were given a daily ration of five pounds of bread each, two pounds of beef, and four half-pints of wine. That was the ration given to the men serving as the king's bodyguard at any one time.

169. So Apries, leading his foreign mercenaries, and Amasis, at the head of the whole body of the Egyptians, advanced on each other until they reached the city of Momemphis, and there they fought. The foreign troops fought well, but they were far fewer in number, and because of that they were beaten. Apries, they say, believed that not even a god could put an end to his rule, so firmly established did he think it was. But in that battle he was beaten. He was taken alive and brought to the city of Sais, to what had once been his own home and from then on was the palace of Amasis. He was kept in the palace there for some time, and Amasis treated him well. But in the end the Egyptians blamed Amasis, saying he was doing wrong to keep alive the man who was the worst enemy both of themselves and of him. So he handed Apries over to the Egyptians, and they strangled him and then buried him in the burial place of his ancestors. This is in the temple of Athena, close to the sanctuary, on the left as you go in. The people of Sais buried inside this temple all the kings who had come from their district. The tomb of Amasis is there too: it is farther from the sanctuary than that of Apries and his ancestors, but it is also inside the temple court. It is a great stone colonnade, with columns carved to look like date palms, and richly decorated in other ways. Inside the colonnade there are double doors, and inside the doors is the burial chamber.

170. At Sais there is also the burial place of one whose name I think it irreverent to mention in connection with such a matter. It is in the temple of Athena, behind the shrine of the goddess, and it runs along the whole length of its wall. In the sacred enclosure stand great stone obelisks, and near them is a lake, edged with stonework and neatly made in a circle. In size, it seemed to me, it is equal to the one at Delos called the Round Pool.

171. On this lake, at night, the Egyptians act out the story of his sufferings, and they call this the Mysteries. I know more fully how each of these things is done, but I will leave it unspoken. And about the secret rites of Demeter, which the Greeks call the Thesmophoria, I will also say nothing, although I know about them, except as much as religion permits me to tell. It was the daughters of Danaus who brought this rite out of Egypt and taught it to the Pelasgian women. Later, when the Dorians drove out all the inhabitants of the Peloponnese, the rite was lost, and only those Peloponnesians who were left behind and not driven out, that is, the Arcadians, kept it.

172. Now that Apries had been overthrown, Amasis became king. He came from the district of Sais, and the city he came from is called Siuph. At first the Egyptians looked down on Amasis and thought little of him, because he had been a commoner and came from no distinguished family. But later Amasis won them over by cleverness rather than by force. Among countless other treasures he owned, there was a golden footbath, in which Amasis himself and all his dinner guests always washed their feet. He had it broken up and made into the statue of a god, and he set it up in the city in the most suitable spot. The Egyptians kept coming to visit the statue and worshiped it devoutly. When Amasis learned what the people of the city were doing, he called the Egyptians together and told them the truth. The statue, he said, had been made from the footbath, which the Egyptians had once used to throw up in and urinate in and wash their feet in, and now they worshiped it devoutly. And just like the footbath, he went on, so it had gone with him: he used to be a commoner, but now he was their king. So he told them to honor him and treat him with respect.

173. That was how he won over the Egyptians, so that they agreed to serve him. And this was how he arranged his days. From early morning until the time the marketplace fills up, he worked willingly at whatever business was brought to him. After that he spent his time drinking and joking with his drinking companions, and he was frivolous and playful. His friends were troubled by this, and they warned him in words something like these:

"My king, you are not governing yourself properly when you let yourself sink into such trivial behavior. You ought to be sitting all day long in state on a stately throne and conducting your business. Then the Egyptians would be sure they were ruled by a great man, and you would have a better name. As it is, you are not acting like a king at all."

He answered them like this: "People who own bows string them when they want to use them, and when they have finished using them they unstring them again. If a bow were kept strung all the time, it would break, and then they couldn't use it when they needed it. It's the same with a man. If he wanted to be serious all the time and never let himself relax for a bit of fun at the proper time, he would either go mad or be struck senseless before he knew it. Since I know this well, I give a share of my time to each of the two ways of living."

That was his reply to his friends.

174. It is said, though, that even when he was a private citizen Amasis loved drinking and joking and was not at all a serious man. Whenever he ran out of money because of his drinking and high living, he would go around stealing. The people he stole from would accuse him of having their property, and when he denied it, they would take him for judgment to an oracle, whenever there was one in their area. Many times he was found guilty by the oracles, and many times he was acquitted. And then, when he finally became king, this is what he did. All the gods who had acquitted him and declared that he was not a thief he ignored: he gave nothing toward the upkeep of their temples, and he did not even visit them to offer sacrifice, because he considered them worthless and their oracles liars. But all the gods who had found him guilty of theft he treated with the greatest respect, because he considered them true gods with oracles that did not lie.

175. First, in Sais, he built and completed for Athena a temple gateway that is a great wonder. In it he far outdid everyone who had done such work before, both in its height and in its size, so large and so fine are its stones. Second, he dedicated great colossal statues and very large sphinxes with men's heads, and for repairs he brought in other stones of enormous size. Some of these he had brought from the stone quarries opposite Memphis, and others, of very great size, from the city of Elephantine, which is a voyage of no less than twenty days from Sais. But what amazes me most of all of them is this: a room carved from a single stone, which he brought from the city of Elephantine. It took three years to bring it, and two thousand men were assigned to haul it, all of them from the class of boatmen. On the outside this room is thirty-one and a half feet long, twenty-one feet wide, and twelve feet high. Those are the outside measurements of the one-stone room. Inside, it is twenty-eight feet three inches long, eighteen feet wide, and seven and a half feet high. It lies beside the entrance to the temple. They did not haul it inside the temple, the story goes, because while the room was being dragged along, its master builder groaned aloud, since so much time had gone into it and he was worn out by the work. Amasis took this to heart as a warning and would not let them drag it any farther. Others say, on the other hand, that one of the men moving it with levers was crushed to death under it, and that this is why it was not hauled in.

176. Amasis also dedicated, in all the other temples of any note, works that are worth seeing for their size. Among them is the colossal statue at Memphis that lies on its back in front of the temple of Hephaestus. It is seventy-five feet long, and on the same base, made of the same stone, stand two colossal statues, each twenty feet tall, one on each side of the large one. There is another stone statue of the same size at Sais, lying the same way as the one at Memphis. It was also Amasis who built and finished the temple of Isis at Memphis, which is very large and well worth seeing.

177. It is said that in the reign of Amasis Egypt was more prosperous than at any time before, both in what the river gives the land and in what the land gives its people, and that the inhabited towns in it at that time numbered twenty thousand in all. It was Amasis, too, who made the law that every year each Egyptian had to declare to the governor of his district how he made his living, and anyone who failed to do this, or who could not show that he made an honest living, was to be punished with death. Solon the Athenian took this law from Egypt and had it passed for the Athenians, and they still keep it, since it is a law no one can find fault with.

178. Amasis also became a friend of the Greeks. Among other signs of friendship that he showed to some of them, he gave the city of Naucratis to those who came to Egypt, as a place to live. To those who did not want to settle there but sailed there to trade, he gave plots of land where they could set up altars and sacred enclosures for their gods. The largest of these enclosures, and the most famous and most visited, is called the Hellenion, and it was founded jointly by the following cities: of the Ionians, Chios, Teos, Phocaea, and Clazomenae; of the Dorians, Rhodes, Cnidus, Halicarnassus, and Phaselis; and of the Aeolians, Mytilene alone. The enclosure belongs to these cities, and they are the ones that appoint the overseers of the port. Any other cities that claim a share in it are claiming something they have no right to. Apart from this, the Aeginetans set up a sacred enclosure of their own, dedicated to Zeus, the Samians one to Hera, and the Milesians one to Apollo.

179. In the old days Naucratis was the only trading port in Egypt; there was no other. If anyone came into any of the other mouths of the Nile, he had to swear that he had not come there of his own free will. When he had sworn this oath of innocence, he had to sail his ship around to the Canopic mouth. Or if he could not sail there because of contrary winds, he had to carry his cargo around the tip of the Delta in barges to Naucratis. That was how highly Naucratis was privileged.

180. Then there is the temple at Delphi. The temple that used to stand there had burned down by accident, and the Amphictyons, the league of neighboring states that looks after Delphi, contracted for the building of the temple that stands there now, at a price of three hundred talents. The people of Delphi were to supply a quarter of that payment, so they went around to various cities collecting contributions. When they did this, they got as much from Egypt as from anywhere. Amasis gave them a thousand talents' weight of alum, some 57,000 pounds, while the Greeks living in Egypt gave them twenty minas of silver.

181. Amasis also made a treaty of friendship and alliance with the people of Cyrene. He decided as well to marry a wife from there, either because he wanted a Greek wife, or else out of friendship for the people of Cyrene. However that may be, he married a woman who was, some say, the daughter of Battus, others say of Arcesilaus, and others of Critobulus, a respected citizen; and her name was Ladice. But whenever Amasis went to bed with her, he found he was unable to have sex with her, though with his other wives he managed as usual. This happened again and again, and Amasis said to his wife, this Ladice: "Woman, you have bewitched me with drugs, and you will die a worse death than any other woman ever has." Ladice denied it, but her denials did nothing to soften Amasis' anger against her. So she made a silent vow to Aphrodite: if Amasis had sex with her that night (since that was the only way out of her danger), she would send a statue of the goddess to be dedicated at Cyrene. And right after the vow Amasis did have sex with her, and from then on, whenever he came to her, he had sex with her, and after this he became very deeply attached to her. Ladice paid the vow she had made to the goddess: she had a statue made and sent it to Cyrene, and it was still there in my own time, standing with its face turned away from the city of Cyrene. When Cambyses conquered Egypt and learned from Ladice who she was, he sent her back to Cyrene unharmed.

182. Amasis also dedicated offerings in Greece. At Cyrene he dedicated a gilded statue of Athena and a painted portrait of himself. In the temple of Athena at Lindus he dedicated two stone statues and a remarkable linen breastplate worth seeing. And at Samos he dedicated to Hera two wooden statues of himself, which were still standing in my own time in the great temple, behind the doors. He made his offerings at Samos because of the bond of guest-friendship between himself and Polycrates son of Aeaces. At Lindus it was not because of any guest-friendship, but because the temple of Athena at Lindus is said to have been founded by the daughters of Danaus, who landed there when they were fleeing from the sons of Aegyptus. Those were the offerings Amasis dedicated. And he was the first man ever to conquer Cyprus and make it pay him tribute.
