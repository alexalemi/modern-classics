Chapter 12: The First Kings of Egypt, Helen in Egypt, and the Thief of Rhampsinitus

99. Up to this point, what I have said has rested on my own observation, my own judgment and my own inquiry. From here on I am going to tell the history of Egypt as I heard it, though I will add to it something of what I have seen for myself.

Min was the first king of Egypt, and the priests told me that he walled off the site of Memphis from the river. The whole stream of the river used to run along the sandy range of hills on the Libyan side. But about eleven miles south of Memphis, Min built embankments that formed the river's bend there, and so he dried up the old channel and led the river to flow down the middle, between the two ranges of hills. Even now the Persians keep a very careful watch on this bend of the Nile, to make sure it stays in the channel it has been confined to, and the embankment is repaired every year. For if the river were to break through and overflow on this side, Memphis would be in danger of being swept away by the flood. When Min, the first king, had turned the land he dammed off into dry ground, he did two things. First, he founded on it the city that is now called Memphis (for Memphis, too, lies in the narrow part of Egypt). Outside the city, on the north and west, he dug a lake around it, fed from the river; on the east side the Nile itself shuts the city in. Second, he built the temple of Hephaestus in the city, a great work and very well worth mentioning.

100. After this man, the priests read out to me from a papyrus roll the names of three hundred and thirty other kings. In all these generations of men, eighteen kings were Ethiopians and one was a woman, a native Egyptian; the rest were men, and Egyptians. The woman who reigned had the same name as the Babylonian queen: Nitocris. They told me that she wanted to take revenge for her brother. He had been king of Egypt, and the Egyptians had murdered him and then given his kingdom to her. So, wanting to avenge him, she used a trick to destroy a great many of the Egyptians. She had a very large room built underground. Then, pretending she meant to hold a feast to open it, while in her mind she was planning something else, she invited the Egyptians she knew had played the biggest part in the murder, and gave a great banquet. While they were feasting, she let the river in on them through a large secret channel. That was all they told me about her, except that when she had done this, she threw herself into a room full of hot embers, to escape the revenge that would follow.

101. As for the other kings, the priests could tell me of no great works they had produced, and they said none of them was famous except the last, Moeris. He, they said, left as a memorial of himself the gateway of the temple of Hephaestus that faces the north wind. He also dug a lake (I will set out later how many miles around it is), and in it he built pyramids, whose size I will give at the same time, when I come to speak of the lake itself. These, they said, were the works Moeris produced; none of the others produced any.

102. So I will pass over these kings and turn to the king who came after them, whose name was Sesostris. The priests said that he first set out with warships from the Red Sea and conquered the peoples living along the shores of the Indian Ocean, until in his voyage he reached a sea that could not be sailed any further because of shoals. Then, the priests said, after he came back to Egypt, he took a great army and marched across the continent, conquering every nation that stood in his way. Where he found a people brave and fighting desperately for their freedom, he set up pillars in their land with inscriptions telling his own name and his country's, and how he had conquered them by his power. But where he took the cities without a fight, or easily, he inscribed the pillars with the same words as he had for the nations that had shown courage, and on top of that he carved a woman's genitals on them, wanting to show by this that the people were cowards, no better than women.

103. In this way he went right across the continent, until at last he crossed over from Asia into Europe and conquered the Scythians and the Thracians as well. These, in my opinion, were the furthest people the Egyptian army reached: the pillars can be found set up in their country, but beyond it they are found no longer. From there he turned and started for home. When he came to the river Phasis, what happened next I can't say for certain. Either King Sesostris himself split off a part of his army and left the men there to settle the land, or else some of his soldiers were worn out by his long marches and stayed behind by the river Phasis.

104. For the people of Colchis are plainly Egyptian. I noticed this for myself before I heard it from anyone else. So when it had occurred to me, I asked both peoples about it. The Colchians remembered the Egyptians better than the Egyptians remembered the Colchians, but the Egyptians said they thought the Colchians were descended from part of Sesostris' army. I guessed as much myself, partly because they are dark-skinned and have tightly curled hair (though that by itself proves nothing, since other peoples are like that too), but much more because the Colchians, the Egyptians and the Ethiopians are the only peoples in the world who have practiced circumcision from the beginning. The Phoenicians and the Syrians of Palestine admit themselves that they learned it from the Egyptians. The Syrians who live around the rivers Thermodon and Parthenius, and their neighbors the Macronians, say they learned it recently from the Colchians. These are the only peoples who practice circumcision, and they plainly do it in the same way as the Egyptians. As for the Egyptians themselves and the Ethiopians, I can't say which learned it from the other, for it is clearly a very ancient custom. But that the other nations learned it through their dealings with the Egyptians, I take this, among other things, as strong proof: those Phoenicians who have dealings with Greece stop following the Egyptians' example in this, and do not circumcise their children.

105. Now let me mention one more thing about the Colchians, to show how much they resemble the Egyptians. They and the Egyptians are the only people who work flax in the same way, and the two nations are alike in their whole way of life and in their language too. The Greeks call the linen of Colchis "Sardonic," while the linen from Egypt they call "Egyptian."

106. Most of the pillars that Sesostris of Egypt set up in the various countries can no longer be seen. But in Palestinian Syria I saw them myself, still standing, with the inscription I have mentioned and the carved emblem. In Ionia, too, there are two figures of this man carved in the rock: one on the road from the territory of Ephesus to Phocaea, and the other on the road from Sardis to Smyrna. In each place the figure of a man is cut in the rock, about six feet nine inches tall, holding a spear in his right hand and a bow and arrows in his left. The rest of his gear matches these, for it is both Egyptian and Ethiopian. From one shoulder to the other, across the chest, runs an inscription carved in the sacred Egyptian characters, which says: "I won this land with my own shoulders." Who he is and where he comes from he does not say in these places, though he has said it elsewhere. Some who have seen these carvings guess that the figure is Memnon, the Ethiopian king who fought at Troy, but in this they are very far from the truth.

107. As this Egyptian Sesostris was on his way back, the priests said, bringing with him many men from the nations whose lands he had conquered, he came to Daphnae in the district of Pelusium on his journey home. There his brother, to whom Sesostris had entrusted the running of Egypt, invited him and his sons to a feast. Then the brother piled brushwood all around the house and set it on fire. When Sesostris found out what was happening, he at once asked his wife for advice, since (they said) he had brought his wife along with him too. She advised him to lay two of his sons, out of the six he had, across the burning pile, making a bridge over the flames, so that the rest of them could walk over their bodies and escape. Sesostris did this, they said, and two of his sons were burned to death in this way, but the others got away safely with their father.

108. Then Sesostris came back to Egypt and took his revenge on his brother. After that he put to work the crowds of people he had brought back from the lands he had conquered, in the following way. These were the men who dragged the huge stones that were brought to the temple of Hephaestus in this king's reign. They were also forced to dig all the channels that are now in Egypt. And so, without meaning to, they made Egypt, which until then had been good country for riding horses and driving carts, no longer fit for it. For ever since that time Egypt, flat as it is, has been no good for horses or carts, and the reason is these channels, which are many and run in every direction. The king's reason for cutting up the land in this way was that those Egyptians whose cities stood not on the river but inland ran short of water whenever the river went down, and had to drink brackish water from wells.

109. That is why Egypt was cut up with channels. And they said that this king divided the land among all the Egyptians, giving each man an equal square plot, and that he got his revenue from this by making them pay a fixed rent every year. If the river carried away part of a man's plot, he would come to the king and report what had happened. The king would then send men to inspect it and measure how much smaller the plot had become, so that from then on the man would pay less, in proportion to the rent that had been set. I think this is how geometry was invented, and that it afterwards came to Greece as well. As for the sundial, the gnomon and the division of the day into twelve parts, the Greeks learned those from the Babylonians.

110. Sesostris was also the only Egyptian king who ruled over Ethiopia. As memorials of himself he left two stone statues in front of the temple of Hephaestus, each forty-five feet high, of himself and his wife, and statues of his four sons, each thirty feet high. Long afterwards, the priest of Hephaestus refused to let Darius the Persian set up a statue of himself in front of them. He said that Darius had not done deeds equal to those of Sesostris the Egyptian. For Sesostris had conquered just as many other nations as Darius had, and the Scythians besides, whereas Darius had not been able to conquer the Scythians. So it was not right, he said, for Darius to set up a statue in front of the ones Sesostris had dedicated, unless he outdid him in his deeds. And Darius, they say, took this speech in good part.

111. When Sesostris' life came to an end, they told me, his son Pheros inherited the kingdom. He made no military expeditions, and it so happened that he went blind, through the following accident. The river had come down in a flood that rose to twenty-seven feet, higher than ever before, and had spread over the fields. Then a wind struck it and the river was whipped up into waves. And this king (they say), in a fit of reckless arrogance, took a spear and hurled it into the middle of the swirling water. Right after this he got a disease of the eyes and went blind. He was blind for ten years. In the eleventh year an oracle came to him from the city of Buto, saying that his time of punishment was over and that he would see again if he washed his eyes with the urine of a woman who had slept with her own husband and no other man. He tried his own wife first, and when he was still blind after that, he went on to try all the women, one after another. When at last he got his sight back, he gathered together all the women he had tried, except the one who had given him back his sight, into one city, which is now called Erythrabolus ("Red Soil"). When he had gathered them there, he burned them all, along with the city itself. But the woman who had given him back his sight he took as his own wife. After he was cured of his eye disease, he dedicated offerings at every one of the well-known temples. In particular (to mention only the one most worth mentioning) he dedicated at the temple of the Sun works that are well worth seeing: two stone obelisks, each made of a single block, each a hundred and fifty feet long and twelve feet wide.

112. After him, they said, the throne passed to a man of Memphis whose name, in Greek, was Proteus. There is a sacred precinct for him at Memphis now, very beautiful and well kept, on the side of the temple of Hephaestus that faces the north wind. Phoenicians from Tyre live all around this precinct, and the whole district is called the Camp of the Tyrians. Inside the precinct of Proteus there is a temple called the temple of the "Foreign Aphrodite." My guess is that this is a temple of Helen, the daughter of Tyndareus. I think so partly because I have heard the story of how Helen stayed with Proteus, but above all because it is called the temple of the "Foreign Aphrodite": none of the other temples of Aphrodite anywhere has the word "foreign" added to its name.

113. When I asked them, the priests told me that what happened with Helen was this. Alexander had carried off Helen and was sailing away from Sparta to his own country. When he reached the Aegean Sea, contrary winds drove him off his course into the Egyptian Sea. From there, since the gales did not let up, he came to Egypt itself, to what is now called the Canopic mouth of the Nile, and to Taricheae. On the shore there was then, as there still is now, a temple of Heracles. If any man's slave takes refuge in it and has the sacred marks put on him, giving himself over to the god, it is forbidden to lay a hand on him. This custom has stayed the same from the beginning right down to my own time. So some of Alexander's servants, having heard about this custom of the temple, ran away from him. They sat down as suppliants of the god and, wanting to do him harm, accused Alexander, telling the whole story of Helen and of the wrong done to Menelaus. They made this accusation not only to the priests but also to the warden of that river-mouth, a man named Thonis.

114. When Thonis heard their story, he at once sent a message to Proteus at Memphis, which said: "A stranger has come here, a Teucrian (that is, a Trojan) by birth, who has done an unholy thing in Greece. He seduced the wife of his own host, and now he has come here with the woman herself and a great deal of treasure, driven to your land by the winds. Should we let him sail away unharmed, or should we first take away from him what he has brought with him?" Proteus sent back a messenger in reply, who said: "Arrest this man, whoever he is, who has committed this crime against his own host, and bring him before me, so that I can hear what he has to say for himself."

115. When he heard this, Thonis arrested Alexander and held his ships, and then brought the man himself up to Memphis, together with Helen and his treasure, and the suppliants as well. When they had all been brought up there, Proteus began asking Alexander who he was and where he was sailing from. Alexander told him his ancestry and the name of his homeland, and explained the route he had sailed. After this Proteus asked him where he had got Helen. When Alexander started to wander in his answer and did not tell the truth, the men who had become suppliants proved him a liar and told the whole story of the wrong he had done. In the end Proteus gave them his judgment: "If I did not think it a very serious thing to kill any stranger who has been driven to my land by the winds, as none ever has been until now, I would have punished you on behalf of that Greek. You are the lowest of men. He took you in as his guest, and you committed a most unholy crime against him. You seduced your own host's wife. And even that was not enough for you: you stirred up her desire and went off with her like a thief. And even that by itself was not enough for you: you have come here with loot taken from your host's house. So now, go, since I have decided that it matters a great deal not to be a killer of strangers. But I will not let you take away this woman or this treasure. I will keep them safe for the Greek who was your host, until he comes himself and wants to take them home. As for you and your shipmates, I order you to leave your anchorage within three days and go from my land to some other. If you do not, you will be treated as enemies."

116. That, the priests said, was how Helen came to Proteus. I believe Homer had heard this story too. But since it did not suit his poem as well as the other one he used, he set it aside, while still making it clear that he knew this version as well. That is plain from the way he describes Alexander's wanderings in the Iliad (and nowhere else does he take back what he said there): when Alexander was bringing Helen home, he was carried off his course and wandered to various lands, and among other places he came to Sidon in Phoenicia. The poet mentions this in the part of the Iliad called "The Valor of Diomedes," and the lines run like this:

	There were her robes of many colors, the work of the women of Sidon,
	women her own son, godlike Alexander, had carried
	away from Sidon, the time he sailed over the wide sea's paths
	bringing Helen home, the daughter of a noble father.

He mentions it in the Odyssey too, in these lines:

	Such were the drugs the daughter of Zeus possessed, drugs of exquisite cunning,
	good ones, given to her by Polydamna, the wife of Thon,
	who lived in Egypt, the land whose generous fields grow
	more drugs than any other, many good when mixed, and many deadly.

And this is what Menelaus says to Telemachus:

	The gods still kept me in Egypt, though I longed to come home,
	kept me from sailing home, since I had not made the sacrifice I owed.

In these lines Homer makes it clear that he knew of Alexander's wandering to Egypt, for Syria borders on Egypt, and the Phoenicians, whose city Sidon is, live in Syria.

117. These lines, and this passage above all, also show very clearly that the "Cyprian Epic" (the poem called the Cypria) was not written by Homer but by some other man. For the Cypria says that Alexander reached Troy with Helen on the third day after leaving Sparta, having had "a gently blowing wind and a smooth sea," whereas the Iliad says that he wandered off his course while he was bringing her.

118. So let us leave Homer and the Cyprian Epic. But I will say this. I asked the priests whether the story the Greeks tell about what happened at Troy is just an idle tale. They answered me as follows, saying that they knew it from what they had learned by questioning Menelaus himself. After Helen was carried off, they said, a large Greek army did indeed come to the land of the Teucrians to help Menelaus. When the army had come ashore from the ships and pitched its camp, they sent messengers to Troy, and Menelaus himself went with them. When they were inside the city wall, they demanded Helen back, along with the treasure Alexander had stolen from Menelaus and carried off, and they also demanded compensation for the wrongs done. The Teucrians gave the same answer then and ever afterwards, under oath and without it: that in actual fact they did not have Helen or the treasure being demanded; both were in Egypt, and it was not fair that they should be made to pay for what Proteus, the king of Egypt, was holding. The Greeks, though, thought the Trojans were mocking them, and they besieged the city until at last they took it. But when they had taken the wall and did not find Helen, and heard the same story as before, then they believed the first story and sent Menelaus himself to Proteus.

119. Menelaus came to Egypt and sailed up to Memphis. He told the truth about what had happened, and not only was he given a lavish welcome, but he also got Helen back unharmed, and all his own treasure too. And yet, after being treated like this, Menelaus repaid the Egyptians with ingratitude. When he set out to sail away, contrary winds held him back, and as this went on for a long time, he came up with an unholy plan: he took two children of local people and sacrificed them. When it became known that he had done this, he was hated for it, and when they chased him, he escaped and got away with his ships to Libya. Where he went after that, the Egyptians could not tell me. They said that they had found out part of all this by inquiry, but that what happened in their own land they reported from sure and certain knowledge.

120. That is what the Egyptian priests told me. And I myself agree with the story they told about Helen, and I add this reasoning of my own. If Helen had been in Troy, she would have been handed over to the Greeks, whether Alexander agreed or not. For surely Priam was not so mad, nor were the others of his family, as to want to risk their own lives and their children's and their city's just so that Alexander could have Helen as his wife. And even supposing they had felt that way at first, still, when many of the other Trojans were losing their lives every time they fought the Greeks, and two or three or even more of Priam's own sons were killed in every battle (if we can trust the epic poets at all), then, as things went this way, I believe that even if Priam himself had been married to Helen, he would have given her back to the Achaeans (as Homer calls the Greeks), if at least that would have freed him from the evils pressing on him. Nor was the kingdom going to pass to Alexander next, so that with Priam old the government would have been in his hands. Hector, who was both older than Alexander and more of a man, would have taken it after Priam's death. And it was not like Hector to let his brother go on with his wrongdoing, when such great evils were coming about because of him, both to Hector himself and to all the other Trojans. In truth, though, they did not have Helen to give back. And the Greeks did not believe them, even though they were telling the truth. This was because, in my opinion, the divine power was planning their total destruction, to make it plain to mankind that for great wrongs the gods send great punishments too. That is my opinion on these matters, and I have given it.

121. After Proteus, they told me, Rhampsinitus inherited the kingdom. As a memorial of himself he left the gateway of the temple of Hephaestus that faces west, and in front of the gateway he set up two statues, thirty-seven and a half feet high. The Egyptians call the one standing on the north side Summer, and the one on the south side Winter. The one they call Summer they worship and make offerings to, and to the other, called Winter, they do just the opposite. This king, they said, came to own a great fortune in silver, so great that none of the kings born after him could beat it, or even come close. Wanting to keep his treasure safe, he had a stone chamber built, with one of its walls forming part of the outside wall of his palace. But the builder had designs on it, and he worked out the following trick: he set one of the stones in such a way that two men, or even one, could easily take it out of the wall. When the chamber was finished, the king stored his money in it. Some time later the builder, coming to the end of his life, called his sons to him (he had two) and told them how he had built the king's treasury, and that he had done it all with an eye to their future, so that they would have plenty to live on. He explained to them clearly everything about taking out the stone and gave them the measurements, and told them that if they kept all this in mind, they would be the keepers of the king's treasury. Then he died. His sons lost no time getting to work. They went to the palace at night, found the stone in the wall of the chamber, removed it easily, and carried off a great deal of the treasure inside.

Then the king happened to open the chamber, and he was astonished to see that the jars were not as full as they should be. He did not know whom to blame, since the seals were unbroken and the chamber had been shut tight. But when he opened the chamber a second and a third time and each time the money was seen to have shrunk (for the thieves did not let up in their raids on it), he did this. He had traps made and set them all around the jars that held the money. When the thieves came as before and one of them went in, the moment he went near one of the jars he was caught in a trap. When he saw what a bad situation he was in, he at once called his brother, showed him what had happened, and told him to come in as quickly as he could and cut off his head, so that if he was seen and recognized he would not bring his brother to ruin as well. The other thought he was right, and was persuaded, and did it. Then he fitted the stone back in its place and went home, carrying his brother's head with him.

When day came, the king went into the chamber and was utterly amazed to see the thief's body caught in the trap without its head, and the chamber intact, with no way in or out. Not knowing what to make of it, he hung the thief's dead body on the wall and posted guards there, with orders to arrest anyone they saw weeping or mourning and bring him to the king. When the body had been hung up, the mother was deeply grieved. She spoke to the son who was still alive and ordered him to find some way, any way he could, to take down his dead brother's body and bring it home. And if he failed to do this, she threatened him in earnest, she would go to the king herself and tell him that her son had the money.

So since his mother was being so hard on him, and nothing he said to her could change her mind, the surviving son came up with the following scheme. He got some donkeys, filled some skins with wine and loaded them onto the donkeys, and then drove them off down the road. When he came to the men guarding the body that was hanging there, he pulled two or three of the skins toward him by the necks and loosened the cords that tied them shut. Then, as the wine was pouring out, he began to beat his head and shout loudly, as if he did not know which of the donkeys to deal with first. When the guards saw the wine flowing out in streams, they ran together into the road with cups in their hands and collected the spilling wine, thinking it was all profit for them. He swore at all of them furiously, pretending to be angry. But when the guards tried to calm him down, after a while he pretended to cool off and let his anger go, and finally he drove his donkeys off the road and began to put their loads right. Then they got talking some more, and one or two of the guards made jokes at his expense and got him laughing along with them, and in the end he gave them one of the skins as a present, on top of what they already had. At that they lay down right there without further ado, intending to drink, and they took him into their company and invited him to stay and drink with them. So he (as you might expect) let himself be persuaded and stayed. Then, as they toasted him in a friendly way over their drinks, he made them a present of another skin as well. And so at last, having drunk heavily, the guards got thoroughly drunk, and overcome by sleep they lay down to sleep on the very spot where they had been drinking. Then, since it was now late at night, he first took down his brother's body, and then, to mock them, shaved the right cheeks of all the guards. After that he put the dead body on the donkeys and drove them home. He had done what his mother ordered.

When the king was told that the thief's dead body had been stolen, he was furious. He was determined, whatever it took, to find out who it was who was coming up with these schemes, and so he did the following (or so they said, but I do not believe the story). He put his own daughter in a brothel and ordered her to take every man alike, but before having sex with any of them, to make him tell her the cleverest thing and the most wicked thing he had ever done in his whole life. And whoever told her the story of the thief, she was to grab him and not let him get away. So she did as her father ordered. The thief heard what all this was for, and since he wanted to outdo the king in cunning, he did this. He cut off the arm of a man who had just died, at the shoulder, and went with it hidden under his cloak. He went in to the king's daughter, and when she asked him what she asked the others, he told her that the most wicked thing he had ever done was cutting off his brother's head, when his brother was caught in a trap in the king's treasure chamber, and the cleverest was getting the guards drunk and taking down his brother's body where it hung. When she heard this, she tried to grab him. But the thief held out the dead man's arm to her in the darkness, and she seized it and held on, thinking she was holding the arm of the man himself. The thief left it in her hands and escaped out through the door.

When this too was reported to the king, he was at first astonished at the man's quick wits and daring. Then he sent messengers around to all the cities and proclaimed a free pardon for the thief, and promised him a great reward as well if he would come before him. The thief trusted the proclamation and came to the king. Rhampsinitus admired him enormously and gave him this daughter of his as his wife, reckoning him the cleverest man in the world: for the Egyptians stood out from all other people, and he stood out from the other Egyptians.

122. After these events, they said, this king went down alive to the place the Greeks call Hades, the land of the dead, and there played dice with Demeter. In some throws he beat her, and in others she beat him, and he came back up again with a gift from her, a golden handkerchief. They told me that because of Rhampsinitus' descent, the Egyptians held a festival after his return. I know for myself that they still keep it in my own time, but whether they keep the festival for this reason or for some other, I can't say. In any case, on the very day of the festival the priests weave a robe from start to finish. Then right away they tie a band over the eyes of one of the priests, lead him in the robe to the road that goes to the temple of Demeter, and go back again themselves. They say that this priest, with his eyes bound, is led by two wolves to the temple of Demeter, which is about two and a quarter miles from the city, and that afterwards the wolves lead him back again from the temple to the same spot.

123. Now, as for these stories the Egyptians tell, anyone who finds such things believable is welcome to accept them. As for me, it should be understood throughout this whole history that I write down, just as I heard it, what the people in each place report. The Egyptians say that Demeter and Dionysus rule the world below. The Egyptians are also the first to have put forward the teaching that the human soul is immortal: that when the body dies, the soul enters another creature that happens to be being born at that moment, and when it has gone the round of all the creatures of the land, the sea and the air, it enters a human body again as it is born; and that it makes this round in three thousand years. Certain Greeks have taken up this teaching, some earlier and some later, as if it were their own invention. I know their names, but I will not write them down.
