Chapter 42: Corcyra, Crete and Thessaly, the Storm off Sepias, and the Fleets at Artemisium

163. After this conference with Gelon, the Greek envoys sailed away. Gelon was afraid for the Greeks, that they would not be able to defeat the invader. On the other hand, he thought it outrageous and not to be endured that he, the tyrant of Sicily, should go to the Peloponnese and take orders from the Spartans. So he gave up on that road and took another. As soon as he heard that the Persian king had crossed the Hellespont, he sent Cadmus son of Scythes, a man of Cos, to Delphi with three fifty-oared galleys, carrying a great deal of money and a friendly message. Cadmus was to wait there and watch how the battle would turn out. If the Persian won, he was to give him the money and offer him earth and water on behalf of the lands Gelon ruled. If the Greeks won, he was to bring the money back.

164. Before all this, Cadmus had inherited from his father the rule of Cos, in a flourishing state. Then, of his own free will, with no danger threatening him, moved only by his own sense of right, he had handed the government over to the people of Cos and gone off to Sicily. There, with the Samians, he took and resettled the city of Zancle, which had changed its name to Messene. This was the Cadmus, who had come to Sicily in the way I have described, that Gelon was now sending, having picked him for the honesty he had seen in him in other dealings. And on top of the other honest things this man had done, he left behind him this one too to be remembered, and it was not the least of them. He had in his hands that great sum of money Gelon had trusted him with, and he could have kept it for himself, but he chose not to. When the Greeks had won the sea battle and Xerxes had marched away and gone, Cadmus too came back to Sicily, bringing the whole sum with him.

165. The people who live in Sicily also tell another story: that Gelon would have come to help the Greeks even though he would have been under Spartan command, except for what happened at that very moment. Terillus son of Crinippus, tyrant of Himera, had been driven out of Himera by Theron son of Aenesidemus, the ruler of Acragas. And just at this time Terillus was bringing in an army of 300,000 Phoenicians, Libyans, Iberians, Ligurians, Elisyci, Sardinians and Corsicans, under the command of Hamilcar son of Hanno, the king of the Carthaginians. Terillus had won Hamilcar over partly through their bond as guest-friends, but above all through the eager help of Anaxilaus son of Cretines, the tyrant of Rhegium. Anaxilaus was married to Terillus' daughter, whose name was Cydippe, and to help his father-in-law he worked to bring Hamilcar into Sicily, and gave him his own sons as hostages. That, they say, is why Gelon could not come to help the Greeks, and why he sent the money to Delphi instead.

166. They also say that, as it happened, Gelon and Theron defeated Hamilcar the Carthaginian on the very same day that the Greeks defeated the Persian at Salamis. This Hamilcar was a Carthaginian on his father's side and a Syracusan on his mother's, and he had become king of the Carthaginians because of his own merit. When the battle was joined and he was losing, he vanished, so I am told. He was never seen again anywhere on earth, alive or dead, though Gelon searched for him with every effort.

167. The Carthaginians themselves tell a story about this too, and what they say is believable enough. The non-Greeks and the Greeks fought in Sicily from early morning until late in the afternoon (that is how long the battle is said to have dragged on). All that time Hamilcar stayed in the camp, offering sacrifices to get good omens, burning the bodies of the victims whole on a great pyre. And when he saw his own army being routed, he happened to be in the act of pouring a libation over the victims, and he threw himself into the fire. So he was burned up and vanished. Whether Hamilcar vanished in this way, as the Phoenicians tell it, or in some other way, the Carthaginians offer sacrifices to him now, and at the time they set up memorials to him in all their colonies, with the greatest one in Carthage itself.

168. So much for the affairs of Sicily. As for the Corcyreans, here is the answer they gave the envoys, and here is what they did afterward, as I will tell. The same men who had gone to Sicily also tried to win their help, saying the same things they had said to Gelon. At the time the Corcyreans promised to send a force and help defend Greece. They declared that they must not let Greece be destroyed without lifting a hand, because if it fell, they themselves would be enslaved from the very first day; no, they must give all the help they could. That was the fine-sounding answer they gave. But when the time came to send help, they had other plans in mind. They manned sixty ships, and after a great deal of delay they put out to sea and reached the Peloponnese. Then they kept their ships at anchor off Pylos and Taenarum in Spartan territory, waiting, as Gelon did, to see how the war would turn out. They did not expect the Greeks to win; they thought the Persian would beat them easily and become master of all Greece. So they were acting on purpose, so that they could say something like this to the Persian: "My king, when the Greeks tried to get our help for this war, we would not oppose you or do anything you would not like, even though our power is not the smallest, and we could have supplied ships, not the fewest in number either, but the most after the Athenians." By saying this they hoped to come off better than the rest, and in my opinion that is just what would have happened. Meanwhile they had an excuse ready for the Greeks, and it is the one they actually used. When the Greeks blamed them for not coming to help, they said they had manned sixty triremes but had not been able to get around Cape Malea because of the Etesian winds. That, they said, was why they had not reached Salamis; it was not from any lack of courage that they had missed the sea battle.

169. That is how the Corcyreans got out of what the Greeks asked of them. The Cretans did this. When the Greeks who had been appointed to deal with them tried to win their help, the Cretans joined together and sent men to ask the god at Delphi whether it would be better for them to help Greece. The Pythia answered: "Fools, do you think the sorrows were too few that Minos sent you in his anger for the help you gave Menelaus? The others did not join you in avenging his death at Camicus, but you joined them in avenging the woman a foreigner carried off from Sparta." When the Cretans heard this answer, they held back from giving any help.

170. The story goes that Minos went to Sicania, which is now called Sicily, in search of Daedalus, the craftsman who had fled from him, and there he died a violent death. Some time later, the Cretans, urged on by a god, all of them except the men of Polichne and Praesus, came to Sicania with a great expedition and besieged the city of Camicus for seven years. In my time Camicus was occupied by the people of Acragas. In the end they could neither take it nor stay on outside it, because they were hard pressed by famine, so they gave up and left. But as they sailed past the coast of Iapygia, a great storm caught them and drove them ashore. Their ships were smashed to pieces, and since they could see no way of getting back to Crete, they founded the city of Hyria there. They stayed there, and they were changed: instead of Cretans they became Messapians of Iapygia, and instead of islanders they became people of the mainland. From Hyria they founded the other settlements that the people of Taras, long afterward, tried to destroy, and suffered a great disaster in the attempt. In fact this turned out to be the greatest slaughter of Greeks that we know of, both of the people of Taras themselves and of the citizens of Rhegium. Micythus son of Choerus had forced these men of Rhegium to go and help the people of Taras, and three thousand of them were killed this way. Of the people of Taras who were killed there, no count was ever made. This Micythus was a servant of Anaxilaus, and Anaxilaus had left him in charge of Rhegium. He is the man who, after he was driven out of Rhegium, settled at Tegea in Arcadia and dedicated all those statues at Olympia.

171. This story of the men of Rhegium and the people of Taras has been a side-story in my account. But in Crete, as the men of Praesus tell it, once the island had been emptied of its people in this way, various other peoples settled there, especially Greeks. In the third generation after the death of Minos came the Trojan War, in which the Cretans proved to be far from the worst of those who came to help Menelaus. Then after this, when they came home from Troy, famine and plague fell on both men and cattle, until Crete was emptied of people a second time. Now a third population of Cretans lives there, together with those who were left of the earlier ones. By reminding them of all this, the Pythia stopped the Cretans from helping the Greeks, though they wanted to.

172. As for the Thessalians, they had at first sided with the Persians against their will, and they showed that they did not like what the Aleuadae were planning. As soon as they heard that the Persian was about to cross into Europe, they sent envoys to the Isthmus. Gathered at the Isthmus were delegates of Greece, chosen by the cities that had the better attitude toward Greece. The Thessalian envoys came before them and said: "Greeks, you must guard the pass by Mount Olympus, so that Thessaly and the whole of Greece may be sheltered from the war. We are ready to guard it with you, but you must send a large force too. If you do not send one, you can be sure we will come to terms with the Persian. It is not right that we, posted so far out in front of the rest of Greece, should die alone in your defense. If you are not willing to come and help us, you have no way to force anything on us, since no force can make the powerless do what they cannot. We will try to find some way of saving ourselves."

173. That is what the Thessalians said. So the Greeks decided to send an army of foot soldiers by sea to Thessaly to guard the pass. When the army had gathered, it sailed through the Euripus and came to Alus in Achaea. There the men landed, left the ships behind at Alus, and marched into Thessaly. They arrived at Tempe, the pass that leads from lower Macedonia into Thessaly along the river Peneus, between Mount Olympus and Mount Ossa. There the Greeks camped, about ten thousand hoplites in all, and the Thessalian cavalry joined them. The commander of the Spartans was Euaenetus son of Carenus, who had been chosen from among the polemarchs, Sparta's senior army officers, and was not of the royal family. The commander of the Athenians was Themistocles son of Neocles. But they stayed there only a few days. Messengers came from Alexander son of Amyntas, the Macedonian, advising them to leave and not stay in the pass to be trampled underfoot by the invading army, and telling them how huge the army was and how many ships there were. When these men gave them this advice, the Greeks took it, because they thought it was good advice and the Macedonian was plainly well disposed toward them. But in my opinion it was fear that persuaded them, once they learned that there was another pass into Thessaly besides this one, through upper Macedonia, through the land of the Perrhaebians and past the town of Gonnus. That is in fact the way Xerxes' army came in. So the Greeks went back down to their ships and made their way back to the Isthmus.

174. That was the expedition to Thessaly, and it took place when the king was about to cross from Asia into Europe and was already at Abydos. So the Thessalians, left without allies, now went over to the Persians wholeheartedly and no longer with any hesitation, so that as events went on they proved very useful to the king.

175. When the Greeks had come back to the Isthmus, they discussed, with what Alexander had told them in mind, where they should make their stand and in what regions they should fight. The view that won was to guard the pass at Thermopylae. They could see it was narrower than the pass into Thessaly, and it was a single pass, and nearer to their own land besides. As for the path by which the Greeks who fell at Thermopylae were caught, they did not even know it existed until they reached Thermopylae and the people of Trachis told them. So they decided to guard this pass and keep the invader from getting through into Greece, and they decided that the fleet should sail to Artemisium in the territory of Histiaea. These places are near each other, so that each part of their forces could find out what was happening to the other. And here is how the places lie.

176. Take Artemisium first. Coming out of the Thracian Sea, the wide water narrows down to the slim channel between the island of Sciathus and the mainland of Magnesia. Right after the strait, on Euboea, comes the beach called Artemisium, where there is a temple of Artemis. Second, the way into Greece through Trachis is, at its narrowest, only fifty feet wide. But that is not where the narrowest part of the whole region lies. It is in front of Thermopylae and also behind it. At Alpeni, which lies behind Thermopylae, there is room for only a single cart track, and again at the river Phoenix, near the town of Anthela, there is room for only a single cart track. To the west of Thermopylae rises a mountain that cannot be climbed, sheer and very high, stretching away toward the range of Oeta. To the east of the road lie the sea and marshes, right up against it. In this pass there are hot springs, which the local people call the "Pots," and an altar of Heracles stands near them. A wall had once been built across this pass, and in the old days there was a gate in it. The Phocians built it, out of fear, when the Thessalians came from the land of the Thesprotians to settle in the Aeolian land, the country they hold now. Since the Thessalians, as the Phocians supposed, were trying to conquer them, the Phocians protected themselves in advance. They let the water of the hot springs run over the pass, to turn the place into a gully, and they tried every means to keep the Thessalians from invading their land. This old wall had been built long before, and by then most of it had fallen into ruin with age. The Greeks decided to build it up again and to hold the invader back from Greece at this spot. Very near the road there is a village called Alpeni, and the Greeks counted on getting their supplies from it.

177. So the Greeks judged that these places suited their purpose. They had thought everything through in advance and worked out that the invaders would not be able to make use of either their greater numbers or their cavalry there, and so they decided to meet the invader of Greece at this place. When they learned that the Persian was in Pieria, they broke camp at the Isthmus and set out on the campaign, some by land to Thermopylae, others by sea to Artemisium.

178. So the Greeks were hurrying to the rescue, each to his assigned post. Meanwhile the people of Delphi, terrified, consulted the oracle of the god on behalf of themselves and of Greece. They were told to pray to the Winds, because the Winds would be powerful allies of Greece in the fighting. When the Delphians received the oracle, they first passed it on to those Greeks who wanted to be free, and by telling them at a time when they were deeply afraid of the invader, they earned their undying gratitude. After this the people of Delphi set up an altar to the Winds at Thyia, where the sacred precinct of Thyia daughter of Cephisus is, the woman this place is named after, and they began offering the Winds sacrifices.

179. To this day the Delphians, following the oracle, offer sacrifices to win the Winds' favor. Meanwhile, Xerxes' fleet had set out from the city of Therma, and ten of its ships, the fastest sailers, crossed straight over toward Sciathus. There three Greek ships, one from Troezen, one from Aegina and one from Athens, were keeping watch as an advance guard. When the crews of these ships caught sight of the enemy ships, they ran for it.

180. The ship from Troezen, commanded by Prexinus, was chased and taken at once by the enemy. The Persians then took the best-looking of the marines on board and cut his throat on the ship's prow, making the first and handsomest Greek they had captured into a good omen for themselves. The name of this man who was sacrificed was Leon, which means "lion," and perhaps his name had something to do with what happened to him.

181. The Aeginetan ship, whose captain was Asonides, gave them some trouble to take, because Pytheas son of Ischenous was serving on board as a marine, and he proved himself a very brave man that day. When the ship was being taken, he kept fighting until he was cut all to pieces. He fell, but he did not die; there was still breath in him. So the Persians serving as marines on the ships did everything they could to save his life, because of his courage. They treated his wounds with ointments of myrrh and bound him up in strips of the finest linen. When they got back to their own main force, they showed him off to the whole army, making a marvel of him and treating him well. But the rest of the men they had taken on this ship they treated as slaves.

182. So two of the three ships were captured in this way. The third, whose captain was Phormus of Athens, ran aground at the mouth of the river Peneus as it fled. The enemy got the ship but not the crew. As soon as the Athenians ran the ship aground they leaped out of her, made their way through Thessaly, and got back to Athens.

183. The Greeks stationed at Artemisium learned of all this from fire signals sent from Sciathus. When they heard, they were frightened, and they moved their anchorage from Artemisium to Chalcis, meaning to guard the Euripus, but they left lookouts on the heights of Euboea by day. Of the ten enemy ships, three sailed up to the reef called Myrmex, which lies between Sciathus and Magnesia, and set up a stone pillar on it, which they had brought along for the purpose. Once the hazards of the passage had been cleared up in this way, the main fleet set out from Therma and sailed there with all its ships, eleven days after the king had set out on his march from Therma. It was Pammon of Scyros who had told them about this reef lying right in the middle of the channel. Sailing all day, the enemy fleet reached Sepias in Magnesia and the beach between the city of Casthanaea and the headland of Sepias.

184. As far as this place, and as far as Thermopylae, the army had suffered no disaster. By my own calculation its numbers at that point were still as follows. There were 1,207 ships from Asia. If you count two hundred men to a ship, I find that the original crews provided by the various nations came to 241,400 men. On board each of these ships, besides the marines from its own nation, there served thirty marines who were Persians, Medes or Sacae, and they come to another 36,210 men. To this and to the first figure I will also add the crews of the fifty-oared galleys, assuming there were about eighty men in each, more or less. There were 3,000 of these vessels gathered together, as I said before, so there would have been 240,000 men in them. That was the naval force that came from Asia, 517,610 men in all. The infantry had been counted at 1,700,000, and the cavalry at 80,000. To these I will add the Arabian camel-drivers and the Libyan chariot-drivers, and I put them at 20,000 men. So the ships' crews and the land army together come to 2,317,610. That is the figure for the army brought out of Asia itself, not counting the servants who came with it, or the grain transports and the men who sailed in them.

185. On top of everything added up so far, there is still the force that was being brought along from Europe to be counted, and of this we must give a likely estimate. The Greeks of Thrace and of the islands off the coast of Thrace supplied one hundred and twenty ships, which makes 24,000 men. As for the land force supplied by the Thracians, Paeonians, Eordians, Bottiaeans, the people who live in Chalcidice, the Brygians, Pierians, Macedonians, Perrhaebians, Enianians, Dolopians, Magnesians, Achaeans, and all who live along the coast of Thrace, I estimate that these various nations came to 300,000 men. Adding these to the men from Asia makes a total of 2,641,610 fighting men.

186. That was the number of fighting men. As for the servants who came with them, and the men in the small grain boats and in the other vessels that sailed along with the army, I suppose that they were not fewer than the fighting men but more. But I will assume that they were exactly equal in number, neither more nor fewer. If they were equal to the fighting force, they make up the same number again. So the number of men Xerxes son of Darius led as far as Sepias and Thermopylae was 5,283,220.

187. That is the number of Xerxes' whole army. But as for the women who baked bread for it, and the concubines and the eunuchs, no one could give an exact number, nor could anyone give the number of the draft animals and other pack animals, or of the Indian hounds that came along, there were so many of them. So it does not surprise me that the water of some rivers ran out. What surprises me more is how there was enough food for so many hundreds of thousands. I find by calculation that if each man got a quart of wheat a day and nothing more, then 110,340 medimni, about 165,000 bushels, were used up every day. And here I am not counting anything for the women, the eunuchs, the pack animals or the dogs. Of all these men, so many hundreds of thousands of them, not one was more worthy than Xerxes himself to hold this power, for his good looks and his height.

188. So the fleet set out and sailed. When it put in on the coast of Magnesia, at the beach between the city of Casthanaea and the headland of Sepias, the first ships to arrive lay moored to the shore and the rest rode at anchor behind them. Since the beach was not long, they lay at anchor eight ships deep, with their prows pointing out to sea. They lay like this that night. But at dawn, after clear skies and a dead calm, the sea began to boil, and a great storm fell on them with a strong east wind, the wind the people who live around there call the Hellespontias. Those who noticed the wind rising, and who were moored in a way that let them do it, hauled their ships up on land before the storm hit, and so they and their ships survived. But of the ships the storm caught out at sea, it threw some onto the place called Ipni on Mount Pelion, and others onto the beach. Some were wrecked on the headland of Sepias itself, others at the city of Meliboea, and others were cast up on shore at Casthanaea. The storm was too violent to withstand.

189. There is a story that the Athenians had called on Boreas, the North Wind, to help them, because of an oracle. Another answer had come to them from the god, telling them to call on their brother-in-law for help. Now according to the Greek story, Boreas has a wife from Attica, Orithyia daughter of Erechtheus. Because of this marriage tie, so the story that has gone around says, the Athenians guessed that their "brother-in-law" was Boreas. So when they saw the wind rising, as they lay with their ships at Chalcis in Euboea, or even before that, they offered sacrifices and called on Boreas and Orithyia to help them and to destroy the invaders' ships, as they had done before around Mount Athos. Whether this was the reason the North Wind fell on the invaders as they lay at anchor, I can't say. But however that may be, the Athenians say that Boreas had helped them before, and that this time too he did what I have described for them. When they got home they built a temple to Boreas by the river Ilissus.

190. In this disaster, according to those who give the lowest figure, no fewer than four hundred ships were lost, with men beyond counting and an enormous amount of treasure. So much so that this shipwreck made a fortune for Ameinocles son of Cretines, a Magnesian who owned land around Sepias. He picked up many gold cups that washed up on the shore afterward, and many silver ones too. He found strongboxes that had belonged to the Persians, and he came by other gold things beyond description. But though he became very rich from what he found, in other ways this man was not lucky. He too had his share of misfortune: he was grieved by the killing of a child.

191. Of the grain transports and other vessels that were lost, no count was made. The loss was so great that the commanders of the fleet were afraid the Thessalians might attack them now that they were in such bad shape, and they threw a high stockade around their camp, built from the wreckage. The storm lasted three days. In the end the Magi, sacrificing victims and chanting spells to calm the wind, and also making offerings to Thetis and the Nereids made it stop on the fourth day; or else it died down on its own for some other reason. They sacrificed to Thetis because the Ionians had told them the story that Peleus carried her off from this place, and that the whole headland of Sepias belonged to her and the other Nereids.

192. So the storm had stopped on the fourth day. Meanwhile, on the day after the storm began, the lookouts had run down from the heights of Euboea and told the Greeks everything about the shipwreck. When the Greeks heard it, they first prayed to Poseidon the Savior and poured libations, and then they hurried back to Artemisium, expecting to find only a very few enemy ships left to face them.

193. So they came back a second time and lay with their ships off Artemisium, and from that day to this they have kept calling Poseidon by the name "Savior." Meanwhile, once the wind had dropped and the swell had calmed, the invaders hauled their ships back into the sea and sailed along the coast of the mainland. They rounded the tip of Magnesia and sailed straight into the gulf that leads to Pagasae. In this Magnesian gulf there is a place where Heracles, they say, was left behind by Jason and his companions. They had sent him from the Argo to fetch water, when they were sailing to Aea in Colchis for the Golden Fleece. They meant to cast off from there into the open sea once they had taken on water, and from this the place has come to be called Aphetae, "the Casting-off." This is where Xerxes' fleet now anchored.

194. It happened that fifteen of these ships put out to sea a good deal later than the rest, and they caught sight of the Greek ships at Artemisium. The crews took them for their own ships, so they sailed toward them and fell right among the enemy. Their commander was Sandoces son of Thamasius, the governor of Cyme in Aeolis. Some time before this, King Darius had arrested this Sandoces, who was one of the Royal Judges, and crucified him, because he had taken money to give an unjust verdict. But after Sandoces had been hung up, Darius worked it out and found that the good services he had done the royal house outweighed his offenses. Having found this, and realizing that he himself had acted more hastily than wisely, Darius let him go. So Sandoces escaped King Darius with his life; he did not die but survived. But this time, when he sailed in toward the Greeks, he was not going to escape a second time. When the Greeks saw these ships sailing toward them, they realized the mistake that was being made, put out against them, and captured them easily.

195. On one of these ships they captured Aridolis, tyrant of Alabanda in Caria. On another they took the commander from Paphos, Penthylus son of Demonous. He had brought twelve ships from Paphos, lost eleven of them in the storm off Sepias, and was now captured sailing in toward Artemisium with the one that had survived. After questioning these men about what they wanted to know of Xerxes' forces, the Greeks sent them off in chains to the Isthmus of Corinth.

196. So the invaders' fleet had arrived at Aphetae, all except the fifteen ships that, as I said, Sandoces commanded. Meanwhile Xerxes and the land army had marched through Thessaly and Achaea, and two days earlier had already entered the land of the Malians. In Thessaly he had held a race for his own horses, also testing the Thessalian cavalry, because he had been told it was the best among the Greeks; and in this contest the Greek horses were left far behind. Of the rivers in Thessaly, only the Onochonus did not have enough water for the army to drink. But of the rivers that flow in Achaea, even the largest of them, the Epidanus, held out only barely.

197. When Xerxes reached Alus in Achaea, his guides, who were showing him the way and wanted to tell him everything, told him a local legend about the temple of Zeus Laphystius. They told how Athamas son of Aeolus plotted the death of Phrixus together with Ino, and how after that, by command of an oracle, the Achaeans impose these trials on his descendants. On whoever is the eldest of this family they lay a ban: he is forbidden to enter the town hall, and they themselves keep watch. The Achaeans call the town hall the "Hall of the People." If he does enter it, he may never come out again except to be sacrificed. They also told him that many of those who were going to be sacrificed had before now run away in fear to another country, and that if later on they came back to their own land and were caught, they were put into the town hall. And they described how the man is sacrificed all covered over with wreaths, and in what kind of procession he is led out to the sacrifice. This is done to the descendants of Cytissorus son of Phrixus. When the Achaeans, on the command of an oracle, were making Athamas son of Aeolus a sacrifice to cleanse the land of its guilt, and were just about to sacrifice him, this Cytissorus came from Aea in Colchis and rescued him, and by doing so he brought the anger of the gods down on his own descendants. When Xerxes heard all this and came to the sacred grove, he kept out of it himself and ordered his whole army to do the same. And he showed reverence both to the house of the descendants of Athamas and to their sacred precinct.

198. That is what happened in Thessaly and in Achaea. From there Xerxes went on into the Malian country, along a gulf of the sea where the tide comes in and goes out every day. Around this gulf there is a stretch of flat ground, broad in some places and very narrow in others. High mountains that cannot be crossed surround this flat ground and shut in the whole Malian land; they are called the Rocks of Trachis. The first city on this gulf as you come from Achaea is Anticyra, where the river Spercheus, flowing from the land of the Enianians, runs out into the sea. About two and a quarter miles from this river there is another, called the Dyras, which is said to have sprung up to help Heracles when he was burning. Then about two and a quarter miles from this there is another river, called the Melas.

199. The city of Trachis is just over half a mile from the river Melas. Here, where Trachis lies, is the widest part of this whole district, from the mountains to the sea, for the plain covers about five thousand acres. In the range of mountains that shuts in the land of Trachis there is a gorge to the south of Trachis itself, and through this gorge the river Asopus flows, running along the foot of the mountain.

200. There is another river, the Phoenix, to the south of the Asopus. It is not a large one; it flows down from these mountains into the Asopus. At the river Phoenix is the narrowest place, for the road built there has room for only a single cart track. From the river Phoenix it is about a mile and two-thirds to Thermopylae. Between the river Phoenix and Thermopylae there is a village called Anthela, past which the Asopus flows on into the sea. Around this village there is a wide open space, where a temple of Demeter of the Amphictyons stands, and there are seats for the members of the Amphictyonic Council and a temple of Amphictyon himself.
