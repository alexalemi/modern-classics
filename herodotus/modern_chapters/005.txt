Chapter 5: The Birth of Cyrus and the Fall of Astyages

107. After this Cyaxares died. He had reigned forty years, counting the years when the Scythians ruled, and his son Astyages took over the kingdom from him. Astyages had a daughter, whom he named Mandane. One night he dreamed that so much water flowed from her that it filled his city and flooded the whole of Asia besides. He handed this dream over to those of the Magi who interpreted dreams, and when they told him exactly what it meant, point by point, he was afraid. So later, when Mandane was old enough to marry, he would not give her to any of the Medes who were his equals in rank, because he feared the vision. Instead he gave her to a Persian named Cambyses. He found Cambyses to be of good family and quiet temperament, and he counted him far below a Mede of middling rank.

108. In the first year of Mandane's marriage to Cambyses, Astyages saw a second vision. He dreamed that a vine grew out of his daughter's womb, and that this vine spread over the whole of Asia. After he had seen this and laid it before the dream interpreters, he sent for his daughter, who was now pregnant, to come home from the land of the Persians. When she arrived he kept her under guard, because he meant to destroy whatever child she bore: the Magi who interpreted his dreams had told him that his daughter's child would become king in his place. To guard against this, when Cyrus was born, Astyages called in Harpagus. Harpagus was a close relative of his, the Mede he trusted above all others, and the man he had put in charge of all his affairs. Astyages said to him: "Harpagus, do not neglect in any way the task I am about to give you. Do not betray me, and do not take up anyone else's interests in place of mine, or you will bring ruin on yourself afterwards. Take the child Mandane has borne, carry it to your house, and kill it. After that, bury it any way you like." Harpagus answered: "My king, never in all the time past have you seen me give you any offense, and I mean to watch myself in the time to come as well, so that I never do you wrong. If it is your pleasure that this be done, then it is my duty to carry out your service properly."

109. That was his answer. The child was handed over to him, already dressed for death, and Harpagus went home weeping and told his wife everything Astyages had said. She asked him: "So what do you mean to do now?" And he answered: "Not what Astyages ordered. Not even if he goes still further out of his mind, and grows madder than he is now, will I go along with his wishes or serve him in a murder like this. There are many reasons why I will not kill the child. First, he is my own kin. Then, Astyages is old and has no son. And if, after he is dead, the power passes on with my hand in this, won't the greatest of dangers be waiting for me then? For my own safety this child has to die, but one of Astyages' people must be the one who kills him, not one of mine."

110. So he spoke, and at once he sent a messenger to one of Astyages' herdsmen, a man he knew grazed his herds on the pastures best suited to his purpose, in mountains thick with wild beasts. The man's name was Mitradates, and his wife was a slave like himself. Her name was Cyno in Greek and Spaco in the Median language, because the Medes call a female dog spaca, which in Greek is kyna. This herdsman kept his pastures on the foothills of the mountains north of Ecbatana, toward the North Wind and the Black Sea. Here, in the direction of the Saspeirians, the land of the Medes is high, mountainous and thickly forested; the rest of Media is all flat plain. So the herdsman came in great haste, since he had been summoned urgently, and Harpagus said to him: "Astyages orders you to take this child and leave it on the most desolate part of the mountains, so that it will die as quickly as possible. And he told me to tell you this: if you do not kill it, but save it from death in any way at all, he will put you to death in the most terrible way there is. I have been appointed to see that the child is exposed."

111. When the herdsman had heard this, he picked up the child, went back the way he had come, and reached his home. Now his wife, as it happened, had been expecting her baby any day, and by some chance of heaven she gave birth just then, while the herdsman was away in the city. Each of them was worried about the other. He was afraid for his wife in childbirth, and she was afraid because Harpagus had sent for her husband, which he had never done before. So as soon as he came back and stood before her, she, seeing him again when she had hardly hoped to, spoke first and asked why Harpagus had sent for him so urgently. He said: "Wife, when I got to the city I saw and heard something I wish I had never seen, and I wish it had never happened to our masters. The whole house of Harpagus was full of weeping. I was astonished and went inside, and as soon as I went in I saw a baby lying there in the open, gasping and screaming, dressed in gold jewelry and embroidered clothes. When Harpagus saw me he told me to pick up the child at once and carry it away and leave it on the part of the mountains most full of wild beasts. He said it was Astyages who had given me this job, and he made a lot of threats against me if I failed to do it. So I picked it up and carried it off. I thought it was the child of one of the household slaves, because I could never have guessed where it really came from. But I wondered to see it dressed in gold and fine clothes, and I wondered too that they were openly weeping for it in Harpagus' house. Then right away, on the road, I learned the whole story from the servant who came with me out of the city and handed me the baby. It is really the son of Mandane, Astyages' daughter, and of Cambyses son of Cyrus, and Astyages has ordered it killed. And here it is."

112. As he said this, the herdsman uncovered the baby and showed it to her. When she saw how big and beautiful the child was, she burst into tears, clung to her husband's knees, and begged him not to expose it, whatever happened. But he said he could not do anything else. Harpagus would be sending men back and forth to check that it was done, and he himself would die a miserable death if he failed to do it. When she could not persuade her husband after all, the wife said: "Since I can't persuade you not to expose it, then do this, if a child really has to be seen lying out there. I have had a baby too, but it was born dead. Take that one and expose it, and let us raise the child of Astyages' daughter as our own. That way you will not be caught wronging our masters, and we will not have done badly for ourselves either: the dead child will get a royal burial, and the living one will not lose his life."

113. The herdsman thought that, things being as they were, his wife was right, and he did as she said at once. The child he had brought home to be put to death he handed to his wife. His own child, the dead one, he took and put in the chest he had carried the other in. He dressed it in all the other child's finery, carried it to the most desolate part of the mountains, and left it there. On the third day after the child had been exposed, the herdsman left one of his under-herdsmen to keep watch and went to the city. At Harpagus' house he said he was ready to show the child's dead body. Harpagus sent the most trusted of his bodyguards, and through them he saw the herdsman's child and had it buried. So that child was buried; and the boy who was later called Cyrus was taken in by the herdsman's wife, who brought him up, giving him some other name, no doubt, not Cyrus.

114. When the boy was ten years old, something happened that revealed who he was. He was playing in the village where the cattle sheds were, playing, as I say, in the road with other boys his age. In their game the boys chose as their king this boy who was known as the herdsman's son. He assigned some of them to build palaces and others to be his bodyguards; one of them, no doubt, he made the King's Eye, and to another he gave the job of carrying messages, handing out a task to each. Now one of the boys playing with them, the son of Artembares, a man of standing among the Medes, would not do what Cyrus told him. So Cyrus ordered the other boys to seize him, and when they obeyed, he gave the boy very rough treatment and had him whipped. As soon as the boy was let go, he was all the angrier because he felt he had been treated so far beneath his dignity. He went down to the city and complained to his father about what Cyrus had done to him. He did not call him Cyrus, of course, since that was not yet his name, but "the son of Astyages' herdsman." In the heat of his anger Artembares went straight to Astyages, taking the boy with him. He said he had been treated in a way no one should be, and told the king: "My king, this is how we have been insulted by your slave, the son of a herdsman." And he showed him his son's shoulders.

115. When Astyages had heard and seen this, he wanted to punish the boy to satisfy Artembares' honor, so he sent for the herdsman and his son. When both were there, Astyages looked at Cyrus and said: "You, the son of a man as lowly as this, dared to treat the son of this man, who is first in my favor, with such disgraceful insult?" Cyrus answered: "Master, I was right to do it to him. The boys of the village, and he was one of them, made me their king in their game, because I seemed to them the best fitted for it. Now the other boys did what I told them, but this one disobeyed and paid no attention, until at last he got the punishment he deserved. So if I deserve to suffer anything for that, here I am."

116. As the boy was speaking, Astyages began to recognize him. The features of his face seemed like his own, and his answer seemed rather too free for a herdsman's son, and the time of the exposure seemed to match the boy's age. Struck with amazement at all this, he was speechless for a while. When at last, with difficulty, he had recovered himself, he wanted to send Artembares away so that he could get the herdsman alone and question him. So he said: "Artembares, I will deal with this so that you and your son will have no cause for complaint." With that he dismissed Artembares, and at Astyages' command the servants took Cyrus inside. When the herdsman was left alone with him, Astyages, now alone with the man, asked him where he had gotten the boy and who had handed him over. The herdsman said the boy was his own son, and that his mother was still living with him as his wife. Astyages told him he was ill-advised to want to be brought to the worst extremity, and as he said this he signaled to his bodyguards to seize him. So as he was being led off to be tortured, the herdsman told the story as it really was. He began at the beginning and went through all of it, telling the truth, and at the end he begged the king to pardon him.

117. Now that the herdsman had told the truth, Astyages cared less about him. But he was extremely angry with Harpagus, and ordered his bodyguards to summon him. When Harpagus came, Astyages asked him: "Harpagus, by what death did you kill the child I handed over to you, the child born of my daughter?" Harpagus saw that the herdsman was in the palace, so he did not turn to any false story, in case he should be refuted and caught out. Instead he said: "My king, as soon as I received the child, I thought hard about how I could do what you wanted, and how, without offending against your command, I might still not be guilty of murder against your daughter and against you yourself. So this is what I did. I called this herdsman and handed the child over to him. I told him first that it was you who ordered him to kill it, and in this at least I did not lie, because you did give that order. I handed it over, as I say, to this man, and told him to leave it on a desolate mountain and to stay by it and watch until it died, and I threatened him with every kind of punishment if he failed to carry this out. When he had done what he was ordered and the child was dead, I sent the most trusted of my eunuchs, and through them I saw it and had it buried. That, my king, is how it happened, and that was the death the child died."

118. So Harpagus told the truth. Astyages hid the anger he felt against him for what had happened. First he told the whole story back to Harpagus just as he had heard it from the herdsman. Then, when he had gone through it all, he ended by saying that the child was alive and that what had happened was all for the best. "For," he went on, "I was very troubled by what had been done to this child, and I did not take it lightly that I had fallen out with my daughter. So, since our luck has changed so happily, first send your son to keep company with the boy who has just arrived, and then come and dine with me yourself. I mean to offer a sacrifice of thanks for the boy's rescue to the gods to whom that honor belongs."

119. When Harpagus heard this, he bowed low before the king. He thought it a great thing that his offense had turned out so well for him, and that he had been invited to dinner on such a lucky occasion besides, and so he went home. As soon as he got there he sent off his son, his only son, a boy of about thirteen, telling him to go to Astyages' palace and do whatever the king commanded. And he himself, overjoyed, told his wife what had happened to him. But when Harpagus' son arrived, Astyages cut his throat and cut him apart limb by limb. He had some of the flesh roasted and some boiled, had it all prepared for eating, and kept it ready. When it was time for dinner and the other guests had arrived, Harpagus among them, tables loaded with mutton were set before the other guests and before Astyages himself. But before Harpagus was set the flesh of his own son, all of it except the head, the hands and the feet. These were put aside in a covered basket. When Harpagus seemed to have eaten his fill, Astyages asked him whether he had enjoyed the meal. Harpagus said he had enjoyed it very much. Then the men who had been given their orders brought him his son's head, hands and feet under a cover, stood beside him, and told him to uncover them and take whatever he liked. Harpagus did as he was told and lifted the cover, and he saw what was left of his son. Seeing it, he did not lose his self-control but kept himself in hand. Astyages asked him whether he knew what animal's flesh he had been eating. He said he knew, and that whatever the king did was pleasing to him. With that answer he picked up what was left of the flesh and went home. After that, I suppose, he meant to gather all the remains together and bury them.

120. That was the penalty Astyages laid on Harpagus. As for Cyrus, he thought it over and summoned the same Magi who had interpreted his dream in the way I have described. When they came, Astyages asked them how they had interpreted his vision, and they gave the same answer as before: the child would have become king if he had lived and not died first. He answered them: "The child is alive, not dead. While he was living in the country, the boys of the village made him their king, and he did everything that real kings do. He ruled, he appointed bodyguards and doorkeepers and message-bearers and all the rest, each to his place. So what do you think all this points to?" The Magi said: "If the child is alive and has been king already, without anyone planning it, then take heart and rest easy about him, because he will not rule a second time. Some even of our oracles have come to very little in the end, and what comes from dreams, at any rate, often turns out to mean something trivial." Astyages answered: "I too, Magi, am strongly inclined to think so: since the boy has been called king, the dream has been fulfilled, and he is no longer any danger to me. All the same, think it over carefully and advise me what will be safest both for my house and for yourselves." To this the Magi replied: "My king, it matters a great deal to us as well that your rule should stand firm. Otherwise it passes to foreigners, coming round to this boy, who is a Persian, and we, who are Medes, will be made slaves and count for nothing in the eyes of the Persians, since we are of a different race. But as long as you are established as our king, being one of our own people, we have our share of power and receive great honors from you. So we have every reason to look after you and your rule. If we saw anything in this to be afraid of, we would tell you everything in advance. But since the dream has come out in something trivial, we ourselves are confident, and we urge you to be confident too. As for this boy, send him out of your sight, away to the Persians and to his parents."

121. When he heard this Astyages was glad. He called Cyrus and said to him: "My boy, because of a dream that has not come true I did you a wrong, but by your own destiny you are still alive. So go now, and go in peace, to the land of the Persians, and I will send men with you to escort you. When you get there you will find a father and a mother of a very different kind from Mitradates the herdsman and his wife."

122. With these words Astyages sent Cyrus away. When he reached the house of Cambyses, his parents took him in. And when they learned who he was, they welcomed him warmly, since they had been quite sure that their son had died right after he was born. They asked him how he had survived. He told them that until now he had not known, but had been completely mistaken; on the way, however, he had learned his whole story. He had been quite sure he was the son of Astyages' herdsman, but since the journey from the city began he had heard everything from the men escorting him. He told them he had been raised by the herdsman's wife, and he kept praising her the whole time, so that Cyno was the main person in everything he said. His parents took up this name from him. And so that the Persians would think their son had been saved in a more miraculous way, they spread the story that Cyrus, when he was exposed, had been nursed by a female dog. That is where that story comes from.

123. As Cyrus grew up into a man, he was the bravest and best loved of all those of his age. Harpagus set about making a friend of him and kept sending him gifts, because he wanted to take revenge on Astyages. He could not see how punishment could come to Astyages from himself, a man with no power of his own; but when he saw Cyrus growing up, he tried to make him his ally, seeing a likeness between what Cyrus had suffered and what he had suffered himself. And even before this he had made some progress. Since Astyages was harsh toward the Medes, Harpagus had gone to the leading Medes one by one and persuaded them that they must make Cyrus their leader and put an end to Astyages' reign. When he had done this and everything was ready, Harpagus wanted to reveal his plan to Cyrus, who was living among the Persians. Since the roads were watched, he had no other way to do it, so he came up with this scheme. He got a hare ready and slit open its belly without pulling out any of the fur. Into it, just as it was, he put a letter in which he had written what he had decided. Then he sewed up the hare's belly again, gave some nets to the most trusted of his servants, as if he were a hunter, and sent him off to the land of the Persians. He told him by word of mouth to give the hare to Cyrus, and to tell him at the same time to cut it open with his own hands and to let no one else be there when he did it.

124. This was carried out, and Cyrus took the hare and cut it open. He found the letter inside, took it out, and read it. This is what it said: "Son of Cambyses, the gods watch over you; otherwise you would never have come to such good fortune. So take revenge on Astyages, your murderer. As far as his will goes, you are dead; it is by the care of the gods, and by mine, that you are alive. I think you learned long ago, from first to last, how things happened to you, and also what I have suffered at Astyages' hands because I did not kill you but gave you to the herdsman. Now, if you will take my advice, you will rule all the land Astyages now rules. Persuade the Persians to revolt and march against the Medes. Whether I am appointed to lead the army against you, or some other of the leading Medes, you have what you want, because these will be the first to turn on Astyages, desert him, come over to your side, and try to bring him down. So know that here, at least, everything is ready. Do this, then, and do it quickly."

125. When Cyrus heard this, he began to think about the cleverest way to persuade the Persians to revolt. After thinking it over, he found that this was the most suitable way, and this is what he actually did. First he wrote what he wanted on a sheet of paper, and he called an assembly of the Persians. Then he unrolled the paper and, reading from it, announced that Astyages had appointed him commander of the Persians. "And now, Persians," he went on, "I order each of you to come to me with a sickle." That was the order Cyrus gave. (The Persians are made up of many tribes. Some of them Cyrus gathered together and persuaded to revolt from the Medes: the tribes on which all the other Persians depend, namely the Pasargadae, the Maraphians and the Maspians. Of these the Pasargadae are the noblest, and the Achaemenids, the clan from which the Perseid kings come, are one of their families. The other Persian tribes are these: the Panthaliaeans, the Derusiaeans and the Germanians, who are all farmers; and the rest are nomads, namely the Daans, the Mardians, the Dropicans and the Sagartians.)

126. There was a certain stretch of Persian land overgrown with thorns, about two miles or a little more on each side. When they had all come with what they had been told to bring, Cyrus ordered them to clear this land for farming in a single day. When the Persians had finished the job set them, he told them to come back the next day, bathed and clean. Meanwhile Cyrus gathered in one place all his father's flocks of goats and sheep and his herds of cattle, slaughtered them, and got them ready to entertain the whole Persian host, along with wine and the most agreeable foods besides. When the Persians came the next day, he had them recline in a meadow and gave them a feast. After they had finished eating, Cyrus asked them which they liked better, what they had had the day before or what they had now. They said there was a great difference between the two: the day before had brought them nothing but hardship, and this day nothing but good things. Cyrus took up what they had said and laid bare his whole plan. "Men of Persia," he said, "this is how things stand with you. If you will do as I say, you will have these good things and ten thousand others, with no slave's labor at all. But if you will not do as I say, you will have countless labors like yesterday's. So do as I say, and make yourselves free. I believe I was born by some providence of heaven to take this work in hand, and I don't think you are worse men than the Medes, either in other things or in war. Since that is how things are, revolt from Astyages at once."

127. So the Persians, having found a leader, were glad to try to free themselves, since they had long resented being ruled by the Medes. When Astyages heard what Cyrus was doing, he sent a messenger to summon him. Cyrus told the messenger to report to Astyages that he would be with him sooner than Astyages himself would like. When he heard this, Astyages armed all the Medes, and, blinded by heaven, he appointed Harpagus to command the army, forgetting what he had done to him. When the Medes marched out and engaged the Persians, some of them, those who had not been let in on the plot, kept on fighting. Others went over to the Persians. But most of them deliberately held back and ran away.

128. So the Median army was shamefully scattered. As soon as Astyages heard of it, he said, threatening Cyrus: "Even so, Cyrus will not get away unpunished." With these words he first impaled the Magi who interpreted dreams, the ones who had persuaded him to let Cyrus go. Then he armed the Medes who had been left behind in the city, the young men and the old. He led these out, gave battle to the Persians, and was beaten. Astyages himself was taken alive, and he lost the Medes he had led out as well.

129. When Astyages was a prisoner, Harpagus came and stood beside him, gloating over him and taunting him. Among the other things he said to wound him, he asked him in particular how he liked being a slave instead of a king, meaning that dinner at which Astyages had served him the flesh of his own son. Astyages looked at him and asked in return whether he claimed what Cyrus had done as his own work. Harpagus said that since he had written the letter, the deed was rightly his. Then Astyages told him he was at once the stupidest and the most unjust of men. The stupidest, because when he could have become king himself, as he could have if what had now been done was really his doing, he had handed the supreme power to someone else. The most unjust, because on account of that dinner he had made the Medes slaves. If he really had to give the kingdom to someone else and not keep it himself, it would have been more just to give this prize to one of the Medes rather than to one of the Persians. As it was, the Medes, who had done nothing wrong, had become slaves instead of masters, and the Persians, who used to be the Medes' slaves, had now become their masters.

130. So Astyages, after reigning thirty-five years, was brought down in this way. Because of his cruelty the Medes bowed under the yoke of the Persians, after they had ruled Asia above the river Halys for one hundred and twenty-eight years, not counting the time when the Scythians ruled. Later on, however, they regretted having done this, and they revolted from Darius; but after revolting they were brought under again, beaten in a battle. At this time, then, in the reign of Astyages, the Persians under Cyrus rose up against the Medes, and from then on they were the rulers of Asia. As for Astyages, Cyrus did him no further harm, but kept him at his own court until he died. That is how Cyrus was born and raised and became king. After this he conquered Croesus, who, as I said earlier, was the one who started the quarrel; and once he had conquered him, he became ruler of all Asia.
