Chapter 25: Thera, Cyrene, and the Kings of the House of Battus

145. That, then, was what Megabazus was doing. At this very same time another great expedition was being made, this one against Libya. I will tell what brought it about, but first I must tell the story that follows. The grandchildren of the men who sailed in the Argo had been driven out of Lemnos by the Pelasgians, the same Pelasgians who carried off the Athenian women from Brauron. Driven out of Lemnos by them, they left and sailed to Sparta, and there they sat down on Mount Taygetus and lit a fire. When the Spartans saw this, they sent a messenger to ask who they were and where they came from. They answered the messenger's question by saying that they were Minyans, children of the heroes who sailed in the Argo; for these heroes, they said, had put in at Lemnos and fathered the people they were descended from. The Spartans heard this story of the Minyans' descent and sent a second time to ask what they had come into the country for, and why they were making a fire blaze. They said that they had been thrown out by the Pelasgians and had now come to the land of their fathers, for that was the most just thing to do. And they said that what they asked was to be allowed to live among the Spartans, with a share of the rights of citizens and a portion of the land allotted to them. The Spartans were willing to take in the Minyans on the very terms the Minyans themselves wanted. What moved them to this most of all was that the sons of Tyndareus had been among the crew of the Argo. So they took the Minyans in, gave them a share of land, and distributed them among the tribes. The Minyans at once married Spartan wives, and gave the women they had brought with them from Lemnos in marriage to other men.

146. But before very long the Minyans grew arrogant. They demanded a share of the royal power and did other unholy things as well. So the Spartans resolved to put them to death; they seized them and threw them into prison. Now the Spartans carry out every execution they carry out at night, never by day. So when they were about to kill them, the wives of the Minyans, who were Spartan-born and daughters of the leading men of Sparta, begged to be allowed into the prison so that each of them could speak with her own husband. The Spartans let them in, never suspecting any trick from them. But once they were inside, the women handed over to their husbands all the clothes they were wearing and put on their husbands' clothes themselves. So the Minyans dressed in the women's clothes and walked out of the prison as women. Having escaped this way, they went back to Taygetus and sat down there again.

147. Now at this very same time Theras son of Autesion, son of Tisamenus, son of Thersander, son of Polynices, was getting ready to set out from Sparta to found a colony. This Theras was of the line of Cadmus, and he was the uncle, on their mother's side, of the sons of Aristodemus, Eurysthenes and Procles. While these sons were still children, Theras held the royal power in Sparta as their guardian. But when his nephews grew up and took power into their own hands, Theras could not bear to be ruled by others after he had had a taste of ruling himself. He said he would not stay in Sparta but would sail away to his kinsmen. Now on the island that is now called Thera, but was formerly called Callista, there lived descendants of Membliarus son of Poeciles, a Phoenician. For Cadmus son of Agenor, while he was searching for Europa, put in at the island now called Thera. Whether it was that the country pleased him when he landed, or whether he chose to do it for some other reason, he left on this island a number of Phoenicians, among them one of his own kinsmen, Membliarus. These people had lived on the island called Callista for eight generations before Theras came from Sparta.

148. It was to these people that Theras was getting ready to set out, taking with him men from the tribes. He meant to settle there alongside them, not to drive them out; on the contrary, he very strongly claimed them as his kin. And when the Minyans, after escaping from the prison, went and sat down on Taygetus, and the Spartans were planning to put them to death, Theras begged the Spartans to let there be no killing, and promised that he himself would take them out of the country. The Spartans agreed to this, and he sailed away with three thirty-oared ships to the descendants of Membliarus. He did not take all the Minyans with him by any means, only a few. Most of them turned instead toward the land of the Paroreatae and the Caucones. They drove these peoples out of their country, divided themselves into six groups, and founded the following towns in their territory: Lepreum, Macistus, Phrixae, Pyrgus, Epium, and Nudium. The Eleans sacked most of these within my own lifetime. The island, meanwhile, took its name of Thera from Theras, who led the colony.

149. Theras' son said he would not sail with him. So Theras said that he would leave him behind like a sheep among wolves. Because of that saying, the young man got the name Oeolycus, "Sheep-Wolf," and as it happened this name won out over the one he had before. Oeolycus fathered Aegeus, after whom the Aegids are named, a powerful clan in Sparta. The men of this clan found that their children were not living to grow up, so on the advice of an oracle they founded a temple to the Furies of Laius and Oedipus, the avenging spirits of that house. After this, the descendants of these men kept up the same custom in Thera.

150. Up to this point in the story the Spartans and the people of Thera tell it the same way. But what comes next, only the people of Thera say happened as follows. Grinnus son of Aesanius, a descendant of the Theras I have mentioned, and king of the island of Thera, came to Delphi bringing a hecatomb, a sacrifice of a hundred beasts, from his city. Among the citizens who came with him was Battus son of Polymnestus, who by descent belonged to the family of Euphemus, of the line of the Minyans. While Grinnus, the king of the Therans, was consulting the oracle about other matters, the Pythia answered by telling him to found a city in Libya. He replied: "Lord, I am already rather old and slow to get moving. Tell one of these younger men to do it." As he said this, he pointed at Battus. That was all for the time being. But afterward, when they had come home, they were at a loss what to do about the oracle's words. They knew nothing about Libya, not even what part of the world it was in, and they did not dare to send a colony off into the unknown.

151. After this, for seven years no rain fell on Thera, and in those years every tree on the island withered up except one. When the Therans consulted the oracle, the Pythia declared that the cause was this business of colonizing Libya. So, since they had no other remedy for their trouble, they sent messengers to Crete to find out whether any of the Cretans, or any foreigners living in Crete, had ever been to Libya. As these messengers wandered around the island, they came to the city of Itanus. There they met a fisherman who caught the shellfish for purple dye, a man named Corobius. He told them that he had been carried off course by the winds to Libya, and in Libya to the island of Platea. They paid this man to come with them and took him to Thera. From Thera a party set sail to explore, only a few men at first. Corobius guided them to this same island of Platea, and they left him there with enough food for a certain number of months. Then they sailed back as fast as they could to report about the island to the people of Thera.

152. But they stayed away longer than the time they had agreed on, and Corobius ran out of everything. Then a Samian ship, whose captain was Colaeus, was blown off course on its way to Egypt and came to this island of Platea. The Samians heard the whole story from Corobius and left him food for a year. Then they put out to sea from the island and sailed on, trying to reach Egypt, but they were driven steadily westward by the east wind. Since the wind never let up, they passed through the Pillars of Heracles and came to Tartessus, guided there by the hand of heaven. Now at that time no one had ever traded at this market, so when the Samians came home again they made a bigger profit from their cargo than any other Greeks we have certain knowledge of, except at least for Sostratus son of Laodamas, of Aegina; no other man can compete with him. The Samians set aside six talents, a tenth of their profits, and had a bronze vessel made in the shape of an Argive mixing bowl, with the heads of griffins projecting in a row all around its rim. They dedicated this as an offering in the temple of Hera, and set it on three colossal bronze statues, each about ten and a half feet tall, kneeling on their knees. It was this deed above all that first made the people of Cyrene and Thera close friends of the Samians.

153. Meanwhile the Therans who had left Corobius on the island arrived back at Thera and reported that they had settled an island off the coast of Libya. The people of Thera resolved to send one of every two brothers, chosen by lot, along with men from all the districts of the island, of which there are seven. They resolved, too, that Battus should be both their leader and their king. So they sent off two fifty-oared ships to Platea.

154. That is the Therans' account. From this point on, for the rest of the story, the Therans agree with the people of Cyrene, but only from this point on, because about Battus the Cyreneans tell a very different tale. Their account goes like this. There is a city in Crete called Oaxus, where a man named Etearchus became king. He had a daughter named Phronime, whose mother had died, and in spite of her he took another wife. This woman came into the house and decided to be a stepmother to Phronime in deed as well as in name. She treated her badly and schemed in every way she could to hurt her, and in the end she accused her of sleeping around and persuaded her husband that it was true. He was convinced by his wife and plotted an unholy deed against his daughter. There was in Oaxus a merchant from Thera named Themison. Etearchus made him his guest-friend and had him swear to do him whatever service he asked. When he had made him swear this, he brought out his daughter, handed her over, and told him to take her away and throw her into the sea. Themison was furious at being tricked into the oath. He broke off his friendship with Etearchus, and this is what he did. He took the girl and sailed away, and when he was out on the open sea, to clear himself of the oath Etearchus had made him swear, he tied ropes around her, lowered her into the sea, pulled her up again, and went on to Thera.

155. After that, Polymnestus, a man of standing among the Therans, took Phronime from Themison and kept her as his concubine. In time she bore him a son who had a speech impediment and a lisp. Both the Therans and the Cyreneans say he was given the name Battus. But I think he was given some other name at first, and only took the name Battus after he came to Libya, adopting it as a title because of the oracle that was given him at Delphi and the rank he had won. For the Libyans' word for king is "battus," and that, I think, is why the Pythia called him that in her prophecy: she used the Libyan word because she knew he would be a king in Libya. For when he had grown up, he went to Delphi to ask about his voice, and when he asked, the priestess answered him:

	Battus, you came for a voice; but Phoebus Apollo, your lord,
	sends you to settle in Libya, the land so rich in sheep.

It was just as if she had said in Greek, "King, you came for a voice." He answered: "Lord, I came to you to ask about my voice, and you answer me with other things, impossible things, telling me to go and found a colony in Libya. With what power, with what army of men, am I supposed to go?" Saying this did nothing to persuade her to give him any other answer, and as she began prophesying the same things to him again, Battus walked out while she was still speaking and went home to Thera.

156. After this, things went badly both for Battus himself and for the rest of the people of Thera. The Therans did not understand what was happening to them, and they sent to Delphi to ask about the troubles they were suffering. The Pythia answered that if they joined Battus in founding Cyrene in Libya, they would do better. So after this the Therans sent Battus out with two fifty-oared ships. These sailed to Libya, but then came back again to Thera, because they did not know what else to do. The Therans pelted them with missiles when they tried to land, would not let them come ashore, and told them to sail back. So, having no choice, they sailed back and founded a settlement on an island off the coast of Libya, which, as I said before, is called Platea. This island is said to be the same size as the city of Cyrene as it is today.

157. They stayed on this island for two years. But since things did not go well for them, they left one of their number behind, and all the rest sailed away to Delphi. When they came to the oracle, they consulted it, saying that they were living in Libya, and that for all their living there they were no better off. The Pythia answered them like this:

	If you know Libya, the land so rich in sheep, better than I do,
	you who have not been there, than I who have, I marvel at your wisdom.

When Battus and his companions heard this, they sailed back again; for the god plainly would not let them off the duty of founding a colony until they had come to Libya itself. They came to the island, picked up the man they had left there, and founded a settlement on the mainland of Libya itself, at a place opposite the island called Aziris. It is shut in on both sides by very beautiful woods, and a river runs past it on one side.

158. They lived in this place for six years. In the seventh year the Libyans persuaded them to leave, asking them to go and saying that they would lead them to a better region. So the Libyans led them away from that place, getting them to set out toward evening. They had worked out the hours of daylight so that they could lead them past the finest place of all by night, and the Greeks would not see it as they went through. This region is called Irasa. Then they brought them to what is called the Spring of Apollo and said: "Greeks, here is a good place for you to live, for here the sky is full of holes." They meant that it rained there.

159. Now in the lifetime of the first settler, Battus, who reigned forty years, and of his son Arcesilaus, who reigned sixteen years, the Cyreneans lived there in the same numbers as when they first set out to found the colony. But in the time of the third king, called Battus the Prosperous, the Pythia gave an oracle urging Greeks everywhere to sail out and join the Cyreneans in settling Libya. For the Cyreneans were inviting them, promising them a share of the land. The oracle she gave was this:

	Whoever comes to lovely Libya too late,
	after the land is shared out, I say he will one day regret it.

Then great numbers of people gathered at Cyrene. The Libyans who lived around them had large pieces of their land cut away, and so they and their king, whose name was Adicran, since they were being robbed of their country and also treated with great arrogance by the Cyreneans, sent to Egypt and put themselves in the hands of Apries, the king of Egypt. He gathered a great army of Egyptians and sent it against Cyrene. The men of Cyrene marched out to the region of Irasa and the spring of Theste, and there they fought the Egyptians and defeated them. The Egyptians had never before tested themselves against Greeks in battle and so looked down on them, and they were slaughtered so badly that only a few of them got back to Egypt. Because of this, and because they blamed Apries for it, the Egyptians revolted against him.

160. This Battus had a son named Arcesilaus. As soon as he became king, he quarreled with his own brothers, until finally they left and went to another part of Libya. There they struck out on their own and founded the city that was called Barca then and is still called Barca now. At the same time as they were founding it, they persuaded the Libyans to revolt from the Cyreneans. After this, Arcesilaus marched against the Libyans who had taken his brothers in and had also revolted from Cyrene. The Libyans were afraid of him and fled toward the Libyan tribes in the east. Arcesilaus followed them as they fled, chasing them all the way to Leucon in Libya, and there the Libyans decided to attack him. They fought a battle and beat the Cyreneans so completely that seven thousand Cyrenean hoplites fell there. After this disaster Arcesilaus fell sick and had taken some medicine when his brother Haliarchus strangled him. Then Haliarchus was murdered by a trick by Arcesilaus' wife, whose name was Eryxo.

161. Then Battus son of Arcesilaus inherited the kingdom. He was lame and could not walk properly. In view of the disaster that had struck them, the Cyreneans sent men to Delphi to ask what kind of government they should set up so as to live as well as possible. The Pythia told them to bring in a reformer from Mantinea in Arcadia to set their state in order. So the people of Cyrene asked for one, and the Mantineans gave them the most respected of their citizens, a man named Demonax. This man came to Cyrene and looked thoroughly into everything. First, he gave them three tribes, divided like this: one tribe he made of the Therans and the people living around them, another of the Peloponnesians and Cretans, and a third of all the islanders. Second, he set aside royal estates and priesthoods for King Battus, but all the other powers the kings had held before, he handed over to the people as public property.

162. In the reign of this Battus things stayed that way. But in the reign of his son Arcesilaus there was great unrest over the offices of state. For Arcesilaus, the son of Battus the Lame and of Pheretime, said he would not put up with the arrangements Demonax of Mantinea had made, and demanded back the royal rights of his ancestors. After this he stirred up civil strife, lost, and went into exile in Samos, while his mother went to Salamis in Cyprus. At that time the ruler of Salamis was Euelthon, the man who dedicated the incense burner at Delphi, a piece well worth seeing, which stands in the treasury of the Corinthians. Pheretime came to him and asked him for an army to restore herself and her son to Cyrene. But Euelthon was ready to give her anything rather than that. Whenever she accepted what he gave her, she would say that this too was a fine gift, but a finer one would be the gift she was asking for, an army. Since she said this about every gift, in the end Euelthon sent her a present of a golden spindle and distaff, with wool on it too. When Pheretime said the same thing again about this present, Euelthon said that these were the sort of gifts given to women, not armies.

163. Meanwhile Arcesilaus, in Samos, was gathering everyone he could by promising a redistribution of land. While a great host was being collected, Arcesilaus set out for Delphi to ask the oracle about his return from exile. The Pythia gave him this answer: "For four named Battus and four named Arcesilaus, eight generations of men, Loxias grants you to be kings of Cyrene; beyond that he advises you not even to try. As for you, keep quiet when you have come back to your country. If you find the kiln full of jars, do not fire the jars hard, but send them off with a fair wind. But if you do fire the kiln hard, do not go into the place that has water all around it; for if you do, you will die, both you and the bull that is the finest of the herd."

164. That was the Pythia's answer to Arcesilaus. He took the men from Samos with him and made his return to Cyrene. But once he had got control of the government, he forgot what the oracle had said and set about punishing the men of the opposing party for driving him out. Some of them escaped from the country altogether, but some Arcesilaus got into his hands, and he sent them off to Cyprus to be put to death. These men were driven off course to Cnidus, and the people of Cnidus rescued them and sent them away to Thera. Some other Cyreneans took refuge in a great tower that belonged to a private citizen named Aglomachus, and Arcesilaus piled brushwood around it and burned them alive. After he had done this, he realized that this was what the oracle meant when the Pythia forbade him, if he found the jars in the kiln, to fire them hard. So he deliberately kept away from the city of Cyrene, afraid of the death the oracle had foretold, and supposing that Cyrene was the place with water all around it. Now he was married to a kinswoman of his, the daughter of the king of Barca, whose name was Alazeir. He went to Alazeir, and some men of Barca, together with some of the exiles from Cyrene, caught sight of him walking in the marketplace and killed him, and his father-in-law Alazeir along with him. So Arcesilaus, whether he missed the meaning of the oracle on purpose or not, fulfilled his own destiny.

165. Meanwhile his mother Pheretime, all the time that Arcesilaus was living at Barca after bringing ruin on himself, held her son's royal power at Cyrene herself. She exercised all his other rights and also sat in the council. But when she heard that her son had been killed in Barca, she left and fled to Egypt. For she had in her favor the services Arcesilaus had done for Cambyses son of Cyrus: this was the Arcesilaus who had handed Cyrene over to Cambyses and had agreed to pay him tribute. So Pheretime came to Egypt and sat down as a suppliant before Aryandes, asking him to help her. Her reason, she said, was that her son had been killed because he took the side of the Persians.

166. Now this Aryandes had been made governor of Egypt by Cambyses. Some time after these events he lost his life for trying to make himself Darius' equal. He had heard, and seen for himself, that Darius wanted to leave behind a monument to himself that no other king had ever made, and he copied him, until in the end he got what he deserved. For Darius refined gold to the greatest possible purity and had coins struck from it; and Aryandes, as governor of Egypt, did the same thing with silver. Even now the purest silver is the kind called Aryandic. When Darius learned that he was doing this, he put him to death, though on a different charge: that he was plotting a revolt.

167. But at the time I am speaking of, this Aryandes took pity on Pheretime and gave her all the forces in Egypt, both the army and the navy. He appointed Amasis, a Persian of the Maraphian tribe, to command the army, and Badres, of the Pasargadae, to command the fleet. But before he sent the force off, Aryandes sent a herald to Barca to ask who it was that had killed Arcesilaus. The people of Barca all took the responsibility on themselves, for they said they had suffered many great wrongs at his hands in the past. When he heard this, Aryandes at last sent off the army, and Pheretime with it. That was the pretext given; but in fact, I believe, the army was being sent to conquer Libya. For the Libyans are many peoples of every kind, and only a few of them were subjects of the king, while most of them paid no attention to Darius at all.
