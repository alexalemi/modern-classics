Chapter 34: The Death of Cleomenes, the Story of Glaucus, and the War with Aegina

73. Those things happened later. But at this time, as soon as Cleomenes had carried his business with Demaratus through to success, he took Leotychides with him and went straight against the Aeginetans, because he was furious with them for the insults they had given him. Now that both kings had come against them, the Aeginetans for their part decided to resist no longer. The Spartans picked out the ten Aeginetans who stood highest in wealth and birth and took them away as prisoners. Among them were Crius son of Polycritus and Casambus son of Aristocrates, who had the most power in Aegina. They carried these men off to Attica and left them in the keeping of the Athenians, who were the Aeginetans' bitterest enemies.

74. After this it became known that Cleomenes had plotted against Demaratus, and he grew afraid of the Spartans and slipped away to Thessaly. From there he went to Arcadia and began to stir up trouble, trying to unite the Arcadians against Sparta. He made them swear various oaths that they would certainly follow him wherever he led them, and besides these he was very eager to bring the chief men of Arcadia to the city of Nonacris and make them swear by the water of the Styx, the river of the underworld by which even the gods swear. For the Arcadians say that the water of the Styx is near this city, and there is in fact something of the kind there: a little stream of water can be seen trickling down from a rock into a hollow ravine, and a wall of rough stones runs around the ravine. Nonacris, where this spring happens to be, is a city of Arcadia near Pheneus.

75. When the Spartans heard what Cleomenes was doing, they were afraid, and they brought him back to Sparta to rule on the same terms as before. But as soon as he came back he was struck by madness (he had been a little out of his mind even before this). Whenever he met any Spartan, he would jab his staff into the man's face. Since he kept doing this and had gone completely out of his senses, his relatives bound him in the stocks. While he was bound like this, he saw that his guard had been left alone by the others, and he asked him for a knife. At first the guard refused, but Cleomenes threatened him with what he would do to him later if he did not, until at last the guard, who was one of the helots, was frightened by the threats and gave him a knife. Once Cleomenes had the blade, he began to mutilate himself, starting from his legs. He went on slicing his flesh lengthwise from his shins to his thighs, and from his thighs to his hips and sides, until he came at last to his belly. He cut this into strips, and that is how he died. Most of the Greeks say this happened to him because he persuaded the Pythia to say what she said about Demaratus. The Athenians alone say it was because when he invaded Eleusis he ravaged the sacred precinct of the goddesses, Demeter and her daughter. And the Argives say it was because he called out of their sanctuary of Argos the Argives who had taken refuge there after the battle and cut them down, and then set fire to the sacred grove itself, with no respect for it at all.

76. This is what happened. When Cleomenes consulted the oracle at Delphi, he was told that he would conquer Argos. So he led out the Spartans and came to the river Erasinus, which is said to flow out of the Stymphalian lake. This lake, they say, drains away into a hidden chasm and comes up again above ground in the land of Argos, and from that point on the Argives call its water the Erasinus. When he reached this river, Cleomenes made sacrifices to it. The omens were not at all favorable for crossing, so he said he admired the Erasinus for not betraying its own countrymen, but that even so the Argives would not get away. After this he drew back from there and led his army down to Thyrea. There he sacrificed a bull to the Sea, and then he carried his men in ships to the land of Tiryns and Nauplia.

77. When the Argives learned of this, they marched down to the sea to defend their country. When they had come near Tiryns, to the place called Hesipeia, they camped facing the Spartans, leaving no very wide space between the two armies. There the Argives were not afraid of fighting in the open. What they feared was being beaten by a trick, because they thought this was what the oracle meant that the Pythia had given to them and to the Milesians together. It went like this:

	But when the female at last conquers the male in battle,
	drives him out, and wins glory among the Argives,
	then many Argive wives will tear both their cheeks in mourning,
	so that someday a man of a later age will say,
	"The terrible three-coiled serpent perished, beaten down by the spear."

All these things coming together made the Argives afraid, and to deal with it they decided to make use of the enemy's herald. Having decided this, here is what they did: whenever the Spartan herald gave an order to the Spartans, the Argives did the same thing too.

78. Cleomenes noticed that the Argives were doing whatever the Spartan herald announced. So he passed the word to the Spartans that when the herald announced that they were to get their breakfast, they should instead pick up their arms and go and attack the Argives. The Spartans did exactly that. While the Argives were getting their breakfast as the herald had ordered, the Spartans attacked them. They killed many of them, but many more fled for safety into the sacred grove of Argos, and the Spartans sat down around the place and kept watch on them. Then Cleomenes did the following.

79. He had deserters with him, and by questioning them he found out the names of the Argives who were shut up in the sanctuary. Then he sent a herald to call them out one by one by name, saying that he had received their ransom. Among the Peloponnesians the ransom is fixed at two minas of silver for each prisoner. So Cleomenes called out about fifty of the Argives one at a time and killed them. As it happened, the rest of the men inside the grove did not notice this was going on. The grove was thick, and those inside could not see what was happening to those outside, at least until one of them climbed a tree and saw from above what was being done. After that they no longer came out when they were called.

80. So then Cleomenes ordered all the helots to pile brushwood around the sacred grove, and when they had done it he set the grove on fire. When it was already burning, he asked one of the deserters which god the grove belonged to. The man answered that it belonged to Argos, the hero the city was named after. When Cleomenes heard that, he groaned aloud and said, "Apollo, god of oracles, you have badly deceived me when you said that I would conquer Argos. I take it the oracle has already been fulfilled for me."

81. After this Cleomenes sent most of his army back to Sparta, but he himself took a thousand of his best men and went to the temple of Hera to sacrifice. When he wanted to sacrifice at the altar, the priest forbade him, saying that religious law did not allow a foreigner to sacrifice there. But Cleomenes told the helots to take the priest away from the altar and whip him, and he made the sacrifice himself. When he had done this, he went back to Sparta.

82. After his return his enemies brought him before the ephors. They accused him of taking bribes, and said that was why he had not conquered Argos when he could easily have taken it. He gave them an answer. Whether he was lying or telling the truth, I can't say for certain; at any rate, this is what he said. When he had captured the sanctuary of Argos, he thought the god's oracle had been fulfilled for him. So he did not think it right to make an attempt on the city, at least not before he had sacrificed and learned whether the goddess would allow it or was standing against him. And while he was sacrificing for omens in the temple of Hera, a flame blazed out from the breasts of the statue. From this he knew the truth of the matter: he would not conquer Argos. If the fire had blazed out from the statue's head, he would have conquered the city from top to bottom. But since it blazed from the breasts, everything the god meant to happen had already been done for him. When he said this, the Spartans thought his account was believable and reasonable, and he got away from his accusers easily.

83. Argos, however, was so emptied of men that the slaves took over the whole state, ruling it and running its affairs until the sons of the men who had been killed grew up. Then these young men, trying to win Argos back for themselves, drove the slaves out, and the slaves, once driven out, took Tiryns by force. For a time the two sides were on friendly terms. But later a prophet named Cleander came to the slaves, a Phigalian from Arcadia by birth. This man persuaded the slaves to attack their masters, and as a result there was war between them for a long time, until at last, with difficulty, the Argives got the better of them.

84. The Argives say, then, that this is why Cleomenes went mad and came to a bad end. But the Spartans themselves say that Cleomenes was not driven mad by any divine power. They say he had picked up the habit of drinking his wine unmixed from spending time with Scythians, and that this was what made him go mad. The nomad Scythians, they say, after Darius had invaded their land, were eager to pay him back. So they sent to Sparta and tried to make an alliance. Their plan was that the Scythians themselves would try to invade Media by way of the river Phasis, while the Spartans would set out from Ephesus and march inland, and then the two armies would meet in one place. And they say that when the Scythians came to Sparta for this purpose, Cleomenes spent a great deal of time with them, and from spending more time with them than was good for him, he learned to drink his wine unmixed with water. That, the Spartans think, is why he went mad. And from that time on, as they say themselves, whenever they want a stronger drink they say, "Pour it Scythian-style." That is what the Spartans say about Cleomenes. But to me it seems that this was the price Cleomenes paid for what he did to Demaratus.

85. When the Aeginetans heard that Cleomenes was dead, they sent messengers to Sparta to accuse Leotychides over the hostages who were being held at Athens. The Spartans convened a court and ruled that Leotychides had treated the Aeginetans outrageously. They sentenced him to be taken to Aegina and handed over in exchange for the men who were being held at Athens. Then, as the Aeginetans were about to take Leotychides away, Theasides son of Leoprepes, a man of high standing in Sparta, said to them: "What are you planning to do, men of Aegina? Do you mean to carry off the king of the Spartans, now that his own fellow citizens have handed him over to you? The Spartans have decided this now because they are angry. But be careful: if you do this, they may someday bring ruin on your land that will destroy it." When they heard this, the Aeginetans held back from taking him. Instead they agreed that Leotychides should go with them to Athens and get the men back for the Aeginetans.

86. But when Leotychides came to Athens and asked for the hostages he had left there, the Athenians did not want to give them up, and they made excuses for refusing. They claimed that two kings had left the men with them, and they did not think it right to give them back to one without the other. So when the Athenians said they would not give them back, Leotychides spoke to them like this:

"Athenians, do whichever you want. You know that if you give them up, you are doing what religion requires, and if you refuse, you are doing the opposite. But I want to tell you what happened once in Sparta over something left in someone's keeping. We Spartans tell how, about two generations before my time, there lived in Sparta a man named Glaucus son of Epicydes. We say this man was first-rate in every way, and above all everyone living in Sparta at that time spoke well of him for his honesty. And we tell how in due course this happened to him. A man from Miletus came to Sparta and asked to speak with him, and he gave these reasons: 'I am a Milesian,' he said, 'and I have come here because I want to profit from your honesty, Glaucus. All over the rest of Greece, and in Ionia too, there is a great deal of talk about your honesty. So I thought it over. Ionia is always in danger, while the Peloponnese is safe and settled, and we never see wealth stay with the same people for long. Thinking about all this and talking it over with myself, I decided to turn half of everything I own into money and leave it with you, since I am sure that if it is with you, it will be safe. So please take the money, and take and keep these tallies too. Whoever comes and asks for the money back with the tokens that match these, give it back to him.'

"That was what the stranger from Miletus said, and Glaucus accepted the money on the terms offered. Then, after a long time had passed, the sons of the man who had left the money with Glaucus came to Sparta. They went to talk with Glaucus, showed him the tokens, and asked for the money back. But he turned them away with this answer: 'I don't remember the matter, and nothing you say brings it back to my mind. But I do want to remember and to do everything that is right. If I took the money, I want to return it honestly. And if I never took it at all, I will deal with you according to the customs of the Greeks. So I am putting off settling the matter with you for three months from today.'

"So the Milesians went away unhappy, because they thought they had been robbed of their money. But Glaucus set off for Delphi to consult the oracle. When he asked the oracle whether he should rob them of the money by swearing an oath, the Pythia rebuked him with these lines:

	Glaucus, son of Epicydes, for the moment it pays you better
	to win the case by an oath and rob them of the money.
	Swear, then: death waits for the man who keeps his oath as well.
	But know that Oath has a son, who has no name, no hands,
	no feet; yet without feet he pursues, without hands he seizes, until
	he has destroyed the whole race and house of the man who breaks his oath.
	But the race of the man who keeps his oath fares better in time to come.

"When Glaucus heard this, he begged the god to forgive him for what he had said. But the priestess told him that to put the god to the test and to do the deed came to the same thing.

"So Glaucus sent for the Milesians and gave them back their money. But now, Athenians, I will tell you why I set out to tell you this story. Today there is no descendant of Glaucus left, and no household that is counted as Glaucus' own. He has been wiped out root and branch from Sparta. So it is a good thing, when people have left something in your keeping, not even to think about it except to give it back when they ask for it."

87. That is what Leotychides said, but even so the Athenians would not listen to him, and he went home. Now the Aeginetans had not yet paid the penalty for the wrongs they had done the Athenians earlier, when they attacked them to please the Thebans. But before they paid it, they did this. They complained about the way the Athenians had treated them and thought they had been wronged, so they got ready to take revenge on the Athenians. The Athenians were holding their festival at Sunium, which comes every four years, so the Aeginetans lay in wait for the sacred ship that was sent to it and captured it. The ship was full of leading Athenians, and once they had taken it, they put the men in chains.

88. After the Aeginetans had done them this wrong, the Athenians no longer held back from plotting every possible harm against them. Now there was a man of some standing in Aegina, Nicodromus son of Cnithus. He had a grievance against the Aeginetans because they had earlier driven him out of the island. When he heard that the Athenians had made up their minds to do the Aeginetans harm, he made an agreement with the Athenians to betray Aegina to them. He told them on what day he would make his attempt, and by what day they would need to come and help him.

89. After this Nicodromus seized what is called the Old City, as he had agreed with the Athenians. But the Athenians did not arrive in time to support him. As it happened, they did not have enough ships to fight the Aeginetans. So while they were asking the Corinthians to lend them ships, their cause fell apart. The Corinthians, who were very friendly with them at this time, gave the Athenians twenty ships when they asked. They handed them over by selling them at five drachmas each, since their law did not allow them to give ships away as a free gift. The Athenians took these ships and their own, manned seventy ships in all, and sailed to Aegina. They arrived one day later than the day agreed.

90. Meanwhile, since the Athenians had not come to support him in time, Nicodromus boarded a ship and escaped from Aegina, and some other Aeginetans went with him. The Athenians gave them Sunium to live in, and from there these men kept raiding the Aeginetans on the island.

91. That happened later. But at the time I am talking about, the wealthy Aeginetans got the upper hand over the common people, who had risen up against them together with Nicodromus. Once they had them in their power, they began leading their prisoners out to be executed. Because of this a curse fell on them, and they could not wash it away with sacrifices, though they tried every means they could think of. They were driven out of the island before the goddess was appeased. Here is what happened. They had taken seven hundred of the common people prisoner and were leading them out to execution, when one of them broke free of his chains and fled for sanctuary to the doorway of the temple of Demeter the Lawgiver. He took hold of the door handle and clung to it. When they found they could not drag him away from it by pulling, they cut off his hands and carried him off that way, and his hands were left clinging to the door handle.

92. That is what the Aeginetans did to one another. When the Athenians arrived, the Aeginetans fought them with seventy ships. They were beaten in the sea battle, and so they called for help from the same people they had called on before, the Argives. But this time the Argives would not come to help them. They held a grudge because Aeginetan ships, pressed into service by Cleomenes, had put in on the coast of Argos, and their crews had gone ashore with the Spartans. Men from ships of Sicyon had also landed with them in that same invasion. As a penalty for this the Argives had laid a fine of a thousand talents on them, five hundred for each state. The Sicyonians admitted they had done wrong and made an agreement to pay a hundred talents and be let off the rest. But the Aeginetans would not admit their wrong and were more stubborn. For this reason, when they asked for help this time, no Argives came at the state's expense. But volunteers came, about a thousand of them, and their leader was a commander named Eurybates, a man who had trained in the pentathlon, the five-event contest. Most of these men never came home but were killed by the Athenians on Aegina. Their commander Eurybates himself fought in single combat, killed three men that way, and was killed by the fourth, Sophanes of Decelea.

93. The Aeginetans, however, attacked the Athenian fleet while it was in disorder and beat it, and they captured four of their ships along with the crews.
