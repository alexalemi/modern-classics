Chapter 26: The Peoples of Libya, and the Vengeance of Pheretime

168. Now this is how the Libyans are settled. Starting from Egypt, the first of the Libyans are the Adyrmachidae. For the most part they follow the same customs as the Egyptians, but they dress like the other Libyans. Their women wear a bronze ring on each leg and let the hair on their heads grow long. When one of them catches her lice, she bites each one back in revenge and then throws it away. They are the only Libyans who do this. They are also the only ones who show their girls to the king when they are about to be married, and whichever of them pleases the king is deflowered by him. These Adyrmachidae stretch along the coast from Egypt as far as the harbor called Plynus.

169. Next after them come the Giligamae, who hold the country toward the west as far as the island of Aphrodisias. Within this stretch, off the coast, lies the island of Platea, where the Cyreneans made their first settlement; and on the mainland coast there is Port Menelaus, and Aziris, where the Cyreneans used to live. From here the silphium begins, and it grows along the coast from the island of Platea as far as the mouth of the Syrtis. This people has customs much like those of the rest.

170. West of the Giligamae are the Asbystae. They live inland above Cyrene and do not reach down to the sea, because the land along the sea is held by the Cyreneans. More than any other Libyans they drive four-horse chariots, and in most of their customs they try to copy the Cyreneans.

171. Next after the Asbystae, to the west, come the Auschisae. They live inland above Barca and reach down to the sea at Euesperides. In the middle of the Auschisae's country live the Bacales, a small tribe, who reach down to the sea at the town of Taucheira in the territory of Barca. They follow the same customs as the people above Cyrene.

172. West of the Auschisae come the Nasamonians, a large people. In summer they leave their flocks behind by the sea and go up to the region of Augila to harvest the dates from the palm trees there, which grow thick and very tall and all of them bear fruit. They hunt the wingless locusts, dry them in the sun, pound them up, and then sprinkle them on milk and drink them. Their custom is for each man to have many wives, and they have sex with them in common, in much the same way as the Massagetae: a man sets up his staff in front of the door and then has sex with the woman inside. When a Nasamonian man marries for the first time, it is the custom for the bride, on the first night, to go around all the guests in turn and have sex with each of them; and each man, after he has slept with her, gives her a gift, whatever he has brought with him from home. Their oaths and their ways of telling the future are these. They swear by the men among them who are said to have been the most just and the bravest, laying their hands on those men's tombs as they swear. And to learn the future they go to the burial mounds of their ancestors, pray, and lie down to sleep on them; whatever the man sees in his dream, he takes as his answer. They pledge their word to each other like this: each one gives the other a drink from his own hand and drinks from the other's hand in turn. If they have nothing to drink, they pick up some dust from the ground and lick it.

173. Next to the Nasamonians is the country of the Psylli. They have been wiped out completely, in this way. The south wind blew on them and dried up all their water tanks, and their land, which lies entirely inside the Syrtis, was left without water. So they held a council, agreed on a plan together, and marched out in arms against the south wind. (I am only reporting what the Libyans report.) When they reached the sandy country, the south wind blew and buried them in the sand. So they were wiped out completely, and from that time on the Nasamonians have held their land.

174. Above these, to the south, in the country of the wild beasts, live the Garamantians. They run from every man and avoid the company of everyone; they own no weapon of war and do not know how to defend themselves against an enemy.

175. These live inland above the Nasamonians. Next to the Nasamonians along the coast, toward the west, come the Macae. They shave their heads so as to leave tufts: they let the hair in the middle grow long and shave it close to the skin all around. For fighting they carry shields made of ostrich skin. Through their land the river Cinyps runs out into the sea. It flows from a hill called the Hill of the Charites. This hill is thickly covered with forest, while the rest of Libya I have described so far is bare of trees. From the sea to this hill is about twenty-two miles.

176. Next to these Macae are the Gindanes. Each of their women wears a number of anklets made of animal skin, and the reason, so they say, is this: for every man who has slept with her, a woman ties on an anklet; and the woman with the most anklets is thought the finest, since she has been loved by the greatest number of men.

177. On a headland that juts out into the sea from the land of these Gindanes live the Lotophagi, the Lotus-Eaters, who live on nothing but the fruit of the lotus. The lotus fruit is about the size of the berry of the mastic tree, and it tastes like a date. The Lotus-Eaters even make wine out of it.

178. After the Lotus-Eaters along the coast come the Machlyans. They also use the lotus, but less than the people I just mentioned. Their land reaches to a great river called the Triton, which runs out into a great lake called Tritonis, and in this lake there is an island named Phla. They say that an oracle once told the Spartans to found a settlement on this island.

179. This story is told as well. When Jason had finished building the Argo at the foot of Mount Pelion, he put on board a hecatomb and a bronze tripod besides, and sailed around the Peloponnese, meaning to go to Delphi. But as he was sailing near Cape Malea, a north wind caught his ship and carried it off to Libya, and before he caught sight of land he found himself in the shallows of Lake Tritonis. He did not know how to get his ship out. Then, the story goes, Triton appeared to him and told Jason to give him the tripod, saying that in return he would show them the channel and send them on their way unharmed. Jason agreed. So Triton showed them the way out between the shallows and set the tripod in his own temple. But first he spoke a prophecy over the tripod and told Jason and his crew the whole matter: whenever one of the descendants of the men who sailed with him on the Argo carried off this tripod, it was fated that a hundred Greek cities would be founded around Lake Tritonis. When the native Libyans heard this, they hid the tripod.

180. Next to these Machlyans are the Auseans. They and the Machlyans live around Lake Tritonis, and the river Triton is the border between them. The Machlyans let their hair grow long at the back of the head, and the Auseans let it grow long in front. At a yearly festival of Athena their girls form two sides and fight each other with stones and sticks. In doing this, they say, they are performing the rites their fathers handed down for the goddess who was born in their land, the one we call Athena. Any girls who die of their wounds they call "false maidens." But before they let the fight begin, they do this: all of them together dress up the girl judged the most beautiful that year in a Corinthian helmet and a full set of Greek armor, put her up in a chariot, and drive her all around the lake. What they dressed the girls in long ago, before the Greeks settled near them, I cannot say. But I suppose they used to dress them in Egyptian armor, since it was from Egypt, I maintain, that both the shield and the helmet came to the Greeks. They also say that Athena is the daughter of Poseidon and of Lake Tritonis, and that she had some quarrel with her father, so she gave herself to Zeus, and Zeus made her his own daughter. That is the story they tell. These people have sex with women in common: they do not marry, but couple like cattle. When a woman's child is well grown, the men hold a meeting within three months, and the child is brought before it; whichever man the child looks like is counted as its father.

181. Those, then, are the nomadic Libyans who live along the coast. Above them, inland, is the part of Libya where the wild beasts live. Above the wild-beast country stretches a ridge of sand that runs from Thebes in Egypt to the Pillars of Heracles. Along this ridge, about every ten days' journey, there are great lumps of salt piled up into hills. At the top of each hill, from the middle of the salt, a spring of cold, sweet water shoots up. People live around these springs, at the farthest edge toward the desert, beyond the wild-beast country. The first, ten days' journey from Thebes, are the Ammonians. Their temple is copied from the temple of Theban Zeus; for at Thebes too, as I have said before, the image of Zeus has a ram's head. The Ammonians, as it happens, also have another spring of water. In the early morning it is warm. At the hour when the market fills up it is cooler, and by noon it is quite cold; that is when they water their gardens. As the day wears on, it loses its chill, until at sunset the water is warm. It keeps getting hotter until midnight, when it boils and bubbles up. After midnight it cools down little by little until dawn. This spring is called the Fountain of the Sun.

182. After the Ammonians, as you go on along the ridge of sand, there is, again ten days' journey on, a hill of salt like the Ammonians' and a spring of water, with people living around it. This place is called Augila. It is here that the Nasamonians come every year to harvest the dates.

183. From Augila, again ten days' journey on, there is another hill of salt and spring of water, and a great many date palms bearing fruit, as there are in the other places too. The people who live here are called the Garamantians, a very large people. They carry earth and spread it over the salt, and then they sow their crops in it. From here is the shortest way to the Lotus-Eaters: it is thirty days' journey from them to the country of the Garamantians. Among the Garamantians, too, are found the cattle that graze walking backward. They graze backward for this reason: their horns curve down and forward, and so they walk backward as they graze; they cannot go forward, because their horns would stick into the ground in front of them. In no other way are they different from other cattle, except in this and in the thickness and toughness of their hide. These Garamantians I am speaking of hunt the Cave-Dwelling Ethiopians from their four-horse chariots. For the Cave-Dwelling Ethiopians are the fastest runners of all the peoples we hear reports about. The Cave-Dwellers live on snakes and lizards and other such creeping things, and they speak a language like no other: they squeak in it exactly like bats.

184. From the Garamantians, again ten days' journey on, there is another hill of salt and spring of water, and the people who live around it are called the Atarantians. They are the only people we know of who have no names. All of them together are called Atarantians, but no one of them has a name of his own. They curse the sun when it is high overhead and heap every kind of foul abuse on it, because its burning heat wears them down, both them and their land. After this, ten days' journey on, there is another hill of salt and spring of water, with people living around it. Near this salt hill is a mountain called Atlas. It is narrow and round on every side, and it is said to be so tall that no one can see its peaks, because the clouds never leave them, summer or winter. The natives say it is the pillar of the sky. These people took their name from this mountain, for they are called Atlantians. They are said to eat nothing that has life in it and never to have dreams.

185. As far as these Atlantians I can give in order the names of the peoples who live along the ridge of sand, but beyond them I can no longer do so. The ridge, however, stretches as far as the Pillars of Heracles and beyond them too. On it, ten days' journey from the Atlantians, there is a salt mine with people living by it. All of these people build their houses of blocks of salt, since these parts of Libya we have now reached get no rain; if it rained, walls made of salt could not last. The salt dug there is both white and purple. Beyond the ridge, to the south and into the interior of Libya, the land is empty of people, without water, without wild beasts, without rain and without trees, and there is not a trace of moisture in it.

186. So from Egypt as far as Lake Tritonis, as I have said, the Libyans are nomads who eat meat and drink milk. They will not taste the meat of cows, for the same reason the Egyptians avoid it, and they do not keep pigs. The women of Cyrene, too, think it wrong to eat beef, because of the Egyptian goddess Isis, and they even keep fasts and hold festivals in her honor. The women of Barca will not taste pork either, as well as beef.

187. So much for that. But west of Lake Tritonis the Libyans are no longer nomads. They do not follow the same customs, and they do nothing like what the nomads do to their children. For the nomadic Libyans (whether all of them, I can't say for certain, but many of them) do this: when their children are four years old, they burn the veins on the crown of their heads with a greasy tuft of sheep's wool, and some of them burn the veins at the temples. This is so that for the rest of their lives the cold phlegm will not run down from their heads and harm them. And this, they say, is why they are so healthy. In fact the Libyans are the healthiest of all the peoples we know of. Whether this is the reason I can't say for certain, but the healthiest they certainly are. If a child has a convulsion while they are burning him, they have found a cure for that too: they sprinkle him with a he-goat's urine and so save him. I am reporting what the Libyans themselves report.

188. The nomads sacrifice like this. They cut off a piece of the animal's ear as a first offering and throw it over the house, and when they have done this they wring the animal's neck. They sacrifice only to the Sun and the Moon. All the Libyans sacrifice to these two, but those who live around Lake Tritonis sacrifice most of all to Athena, and after her to Triton and Poseidon.

189. It seems, too, that the Greeks copied the dress and the aegis of Athena's statues from the Libyan women. Except that the Libyan women's dress is of leather, and the tassels hanging from their aegis are not snakes but leather thongs, in every other way Athena is dressed just like them. The name itself shows that the dress of the statues of Pallas came from Libya. For over their other clothes the Libyan women wear goatskins, aigeas, with the hair taken off, fringed with tassels and dyed red with madder; and from the name of these goatskins the Greeks made the word aegis. I think too that the loud, wailing cry raised during sacred rites first began here, since the Libyan women do it very well. And it was from the Libyans that the Greeks learned to yoke four horses together.

190. The nomads bury their dead exactly the way the Greeks do, except for the Nasamonians. They bury their dead sitting up. When a man is dying they take care to sit him up, and they do not let him die lying on his back. Their houses are made of asphodel stalks woven together with rushes, and they are built so that they can be carried about. Those are the customs of these tribes.

191. West of the river Triton, next after the Auseans, come Libyans who farm the land and are used to living in fixed homes. They are called the Maxyans. They let their hair grow long on the right side of their heads and cut it short on the left, and they paint their bodies with red ochre. They say they are descended from the men who came from Troy.

This country, and the rest of Libya to the west, has far more wild beasts and far more forest than the nomads' country. For the eastern part of Libya, where the nomads live, is low and sandy as far as the river Triton; but the part that comes after it to the west, where the farmers live, is very mountainous, thickly wooded, and full of wild beasts. In their land are found the giant snakes and the lions, the elephants, bears, poisonous snakes and horned asses, and also the dog-headed men and the headless men with their eyes in their chests (at least, so the Libyans say), and the wild men and wild women, and a great many other animals that are not made up like these.

192. In the nomads' country, though, there are none of these, but other animals instead: white-rumped antelopes, gazelles, buffaloes, and asses, not the horned kind but another kind that goes without water (in fact they never drink at all); oryxes, whose horns are used for the arms of the Phoenician lyre (this animal is about the size of an ox); small foxes, hyenas, porcupines, wild rams, wolves, jackals, panthers, boryes, land crocodiles about four and a half feet long that look very much like lizards, ostriches, and small snakes, each with one horn. All these wild animals are found in this country, as well as those found elsewhere, except for the deer and the wild boar; Libya has no deer and no wild boars at all. There are also three kinds of mice in this country: one is called the "two-legged" mouse, another the zegeris (a Libyan name, which means "hill" in Greek), and the third the "prickly" mouse. There are also weasels that live in the silphium, very much like the ones at Tartessus. Those are the wild animals of the Libyans' land, as far as we could find out by inquiring as widely as we could.

193. Next to the Maxyan Libyans are the Zaueces, whose women drive their chariots for them in war.

194. Next to them are the Gyzantes. In their country bees make a great deal of honey, but men, they say, make a great deal more, working at it as a trade. However that may be, all these people paint themselves with red ochre and eat monkeys, which are very plentiful in their mountains.

195. Off their coast, the Carthaginians say, lies an island called Cyrauis. It is about twenty-two miles long but narrow, and you can walk across to it from the mainland. It is full of olive trees and vines. On the island, they say, there is a pool, and the local girls bring up gold dust out of its mud with birds' feathers smeared with pitch. Whether this is true I do not know; I am writing down what is reported. But anything is possible, for on Zacynthus I have seen with my own eyes pitch being drawn up out of a pool of water. There are several pools there, and the largest of them is seventy feet across each way and twelve feet deep. They plunge into it a pole with a myrtle branch tied to the end, and with the myrtle branch they bring up pitch. It smells of asphalt, but in every other way it is better than the pitch of Pieria. They pour it into a pit dug next to the pool, and when they have collected a good deal, they pour it from the pit into jars. Anything that falls into the pool goes underground and comes up again in the sea, which is less than half a mile from the pool. So the report about the island off the coast of Libya may well be true too.

196. The Carthaginians tell this story as well. There is a place in Libya, they say, with people living there, beyond the Pillars of Heracles. When the Carthaginians come there, they unload their goods from their ships and lay them out in a row along the beach. Then they go back on board and send up a smoke signal. When the natives see the smoke, they come down to the sea, put down gold in exchange for the goods, and then move back a good distance from the goods. The Carthaginians then come ashore and look at it. If they think the gold is a fair price for their goods, they take it and go on their way. If not, they go back on board their ships and sit there, and the natives come up and add more gold to what they put down before, until the Carthaginians are satisfied. And they say that neither side cheats the other: the Carthaginians do not touch the gold until it matches the value of their goods, and the natives do not touch the goods until the Carthaginians have taken the gold.

197. These are the Libyan tribes we are able to name. Most of them pay no attention to the king of the Medes now, and they paid none then either. I have this much more to say about this land: as far as we know, four peoples live in it and no more. Two of these peoples are native to the land and two are not. The Libyans and the Ethiopians are native, the one living in the northern parts of Libya and the other in the southern; the Phoenicians and the Greeks are newcomers.

198. Besides that, I think that in the quality of its soil Libya is not especially good compared with Asia or Europe, except for the region of Cinyps, which has the same name as its river. This region is as good as the best land anywhere for growing grain, Demeter's gift, and it is not like the rest of Libya at all. It has black soil and is watered by springs; it has nothing to fear from drought, and it is not harmed by drinking in too much rain (for in this part of Libya it does rain). Its crops yield as much as the land of Babylon does. The land of the Euesperites is good too: in its best years it yields a hundredfold, but the land around Cinyps sometimes yields as much as three hundredfold.

199. The land of Cyrene, too, which is the highest part of the Libya held by the nomads, has three harvest seasons within its borders, which is something to marvel at. First the crops along the coast are ripe for reaping and the grapes for picking. When these have been gathered in, the land above the coast, the middle belt they call the Hills, is ripe for harvest. And as soon as this middle crop has been gathered in, the crop in the highest part of the country ripens and is ready. So by the time the first crop has been eaten and drunk, the last one is just coming in. In this way the Cyreneans' harvest lasts eight months. Let what I have said about these things be enough.

200. Now when Pheretime's Persian allies, sent from Egypt by Aryandes, arrived at Barca, they laid siege to the city. They demanded that the people of Barca hand over the men guilty of the murder of Arcesilaus. But since the whole people had shared in the guilt, they refused. So the Persians besieged Barca for nine months, digging tunnels toward the wall and making fierce attacks on it. The tunnels were discovered by a bronzesmith, using a shield plated with bronze. This was his idea. He carried the shield all around the inside of the wall, laying it on the ground of the city. In most places where he laid it, it made no sound, but wherever there was digging going on, the bronze of the shield rang. So the men of Barca would dig a countertunnel there and kill the Persians who were digging. That is how the tunnels were discovered, as I have said, and the attacks were beaten back by the men of Barca.

201. A long time passed in this hardship, and many were falling on both sides, especially on the Persian side. Then Amasis, the commander of the land army, came up with a plan. Seeing that the people of Barca could not be beaten by force but might be beaten by a trick, he did this. At night he dug a wide trench and laid weak planks across it, and on top of the planks he spread earth, so that it was level with the rest of the ground. At daybreak he invited the men of Barca to a parley. They were glad to come, and in the end they agreed to make a treaty. They swore the treaty with each other standing over the hidden trench. The terms were these: as long as this ground stayed as it was, the oath would stand firm; the people of Barca promised to pay the king a fair tribute, and the Persians would do the people of Barca no further harm. After the oath, the people of Barca trusted the agreement. They came out of the city themselves, opened all the gates, and let any of the enemy who wished come inside the walls. But the Persians broke down the hidden bridge and then rushed into the city. The reason they broke down the bridge they had made was so that they would be keeping their oath. They had sworn to the men of Barca that the oath would stand firm for as long as the ground stayed as it then was; once they had broken the bridge down, the oath no longer stood.

202. When the Persians handed the most guilty of the people of Barca over to Pheretime, she impaled them in a ring all around the city wall. She cut off their wives' breasts and set those, too, in a row around the wall. The rest of the people of Barca she told the Persians to take away as plunder, except for those who belonged to the house of Battus and had no share in the murder. To these Pheretime handed over the city.

203. So the Persians enslaved the rest of the people of Barca and set out for home. When they appeared at the gates of Cyrene, the Cyreneans let them pass through their city, so as not to neglect some oracle. While the army was going through, Badres, the commander of the fleet, urged that they capture the city. But Amasis, the commander of the land army, would not agree, saying that they had been sent against no Greek city but Barca. When they had passed through, however, and were camped on the hill of Zeus Lycaeus, they were sorry they had not taken Cyrene, and they tried to get into it again. But the Cyreneans would not let them in. Then, though no one was fighting them, a sudden panic fell on the Persians, and they ran about seven miles before they made camp. After they had pitched camp there, a messenger came from Aryandes calling them back. So the Persians asked the Cyreneans for food for their march, and they got it. With the food they set off for Egypt. From then on the Libyans picked them off: any who lagged or straggled behind at any point, they killed for their clothes and gear, until at last the Persians reached Egypt.

204. This Persian army got as far as Euesperides, and that was the farthest they went into Libya. The people of Barca whom they had enslaved were taken on from Egypt to the king, and King Darius gave them a village in the land of Bactria to settle in. They named the village Barca, and it was still inhabited by them in my own time, in the land of Bactria.

205. But Pheretime did not end her life happily either, any more than they did. As soon as she had come back from Libya to Egypt, after taking her revenge on the people of Barca, she died a horrible death: while she was still alive she suddenly swarmed with worms. For, as it seems, punishments that are too harsh, when men inflict them, are hateful to the gods. Such, and so great, was the vengeance that Pheretime, the wife of Battus, took on the people of Barca.
