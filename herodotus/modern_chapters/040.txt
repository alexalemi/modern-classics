Chapter 40: The March through Thrace and Macedonia, and the Heralds Sent to Greece

105. That was Demaratus' answer. Xerxes turned the whole thing into a joke, showed no anger at all, and sent him away kindly. After this conversation, Xerxes appointed Mascames son of Megadostes to be governor there at Doriscus, removing the governor Darius had appointed. Then he marched his army on through Thrace to invade Greece.

106. Mascames, the man he left behind there, proved to be of such quality that Xerxes used to send gifts to him alone, because he considered him the best of all the governors that either he or Darius had ever appointed. He sent him these gifts every year, and Artaxerxes son of Xerxes did the same for Mascames' descendants. For even before this march, governors had been appointed in Thrace and all around the Hellespont. After the expedition, all of them, both those in Thrace and those on the Hellespont, were conquered by the Greeks, all except the one at Doriscus. Mascames at Doriscus no one was ever able to conquer, though many tried. That is why the gifts keep coming for him from whoever is king of Persia.

107. Of the governors the Greeks did conquer, Xerxes did not think any was a good man except Boges, who was at Eion. Him he never stopped praising, and he gave the highest honors to the children of Boges who survived him in Persia. And truly Boges proved himself worthy of great praise. When he was besieged by the Athenians under Cimon son of Miltiades, he could have marched out under a truce and gone home to Asia. He chose not to, for fear the king would think he had saved his life through cowardice, and he held out to the very end. Then, when there was no food left inside the walls, he built a great pyre. He cut the throats of his children, his wife, his concubines and his servants, and threw them into the fire. After this he scattered all the gold and silver in the city from the wall into the river Strymon, and when he had done that, he threw himself into the fire. So the Persians rightly praise him to this day.

108. From Doriscus Xerxes went on toward Greece, and as he went he forced every people he came to, one after another, to join his march. For, as I have explained before, the whole country as far as Thessaly had been made subject and was paying tribute to the king, conquered first by Megabazus and later by Mardonius. On his way from Doriscus he passed first the strongholds of the Samothracians, of which the farthest to the west is a city called Mesambria. Next to this comes Stryme, a city belonging to the Thasians, and between the two flows the river Lisus. At this time its water was not enough to supply Xerxes' army, and the stream ran dry. This country used to be called Gallaica but is now called Briantica; strictly speaking, though, it too belongs to the Cicones.

109. After crossing the dried-up bed of the river Lisus, he passed by these Greek cities: Maronea, Dicaea and Abdera. He passed them by, and also the well-known lakes that lie near them: the Ismarian lake, between Maronea and Stryme, and the Bistonian lake near Dicaea, into which two rivers pour their water, the Travus and the Compsantus. At Abdera Xerxes passed no lake worth mentioning, but he did pass the river Nestus, which flows into the sea there. After these places he went by the mainland cities. Near one of them there happens to be a lake about three and a third miles around, full of fish and very salty. The baggage animals alone drank it dry. The name of this city is Pistyrus.

110. These coastal cities, which belonged to Greeks, he passed by, keeping them on his left. The Thracian tribes through whose country he marched were these: the Paetians, the Cicones, the Bistones, the Sapaeans, the Dersaeans, the Edonians and the Satrians. Of these, the ones settled along the coast went with him in their ships, and the ones who lived inland, whom I have just listed, were forced to go with him by land, all except the Satrians.

111. The Satrians have never yet obeyed any man, as far as we know. Down to my own time they remain free, the only Thracians who are. They live in high mountains covered with every kind of forest and with snow, and they are also very skilled at war. These are the people who own the oracle of Dionysus, which is on their highest mountains. Among the Satrians the ones who serve as prophets of the temple are the Bessians. It is a priestess who speaks the oracles, as at Delphi, and beyond this there is nothing remarkable about it.

112. After crossing the land I have described, Xerxes next passed the strongholds of the Pierians, one called Phagres and the other Pergamus. He marched this way, close under their walls, keeping on his right Mount Pangaeum. It is a great, high mountain, and in it there are gold and silver mines owned by the Pierians and the Odomantians, and above all by the Satrians.

113. So, passing the Paeonians, the Doberes and the Paeoplae, who live north of Pangaeum, he went on westward until at last he came to the river Strymon and the city of Eion. Boges, the man I was speaking of a little while ago, was commander there as long as he lived. The country around Mount Pangaeum is called Phyllis. It stretches west to the river Angites, which flows into the Strymon, and south to the Strymon itself. At this river the Magi made a sacrifice for good omens, killing white horses.

114. They did this, and many other things besides, as charms to win over the river. Then, at the Nine Ways in the land of the Edonians, they went on by the bridges, for they found the Strymon already spanned by bridges. When they learned that the place was called the Nine Ways, they buried alive there that many boys and girls, children of the local people. Burying people alive is a Persian custom. I am told that Amestris, Xerxes' wife, when she had grown old, also buried fourteen children of distinguished Persians, as a thank-offering for her own life to the god who is said to live beneath the earth.

115. As the army marched on from the Strymon, it came to a stretch of beach running toward the sunset and passed the Greek city of Argilus, which stands there. This region, and the land above it, is called Bisaltia. From there, keeping on its left the gulf that lies off Posideium, it went through what is called the plain of Syleus, passing Stagirus, a Greek city, and so came to Acanthus. As it went it took along every one of these tribes, and also the peoples who live around Mount Pangaeum, just as it had the ones I listed before: the men who lived on the coast served in the ships, and those who lived inland went along on foot. The road along which King Xerxes marched his army the Thracians do not disturb and do not plant crops on; they treat it with the greatest reverence down to my own time.

116. When Xerxes reached Acanthus, he declared the people of Acanthus his guest-friends, presented them with Median dress, and praised them, because he saw how eager they were to help him in the war and because he had heard about the digging of the canal.

117. While Xerxes was at Acanthus, it happened that the man who had been put in charge of making the canal, Artachaees by name, died of an illness. He was a man Xerxes valued highly, and he belonged to the Achaemenid family. He was also the tallest man in Persia, just four finger-widths short of five royal cubits, a little over eight feet, and he had the loudest voice of any man alive. So Xerxes was deeply grieved at his loss. He gave him a magnificent funeral and burial, and the whole army helped to raise a mound over him. The people of Acanthus, at the bidding of an oracle, sacrifice to this Artachaees as a hero, calling on him by name in their worship.

118. So King Xerxes was deeply grieved at the loss of Artachaees. Meanwhile the Greeks who were hosting his army and giving Xerxes dinner had been brought to utter ruin, to the point of being driven from house and home. Take the Thasians, for example. They hosted Xerxes' army and gave him dinner on behalf of their towns on the mainland, and Antipater son of Orgeus, a citizen as respected as any, who had been appointed to handle it, reported that four hundred talents of silver had been spent on that dinner.

119. In the other cities, too, the men in charge reported the bill to be about the same, or nearly so. For the dinner was ordered a long time in advance, and the cities treated it as a matter of great importance. It was done like this. First of all, as soon as they heard the news from the heralds who went around announcing it, the citizens of each city divided grain among their households, and everyone spent many months grinding wheat and barley flour. Then they fattened cattle, searching out the finest animals and paying high prices for them. They kept birds, both land birds and water birds, in cages and ponds, all to entertain the army. Then they had cups and mixing bowls made of gold and silver, along with everything else that goes on a table. These were made for the king himself and for those who ate with him; for the rest of the army only food was provided. Whenever the army arrived somewhere, a tent stood ready and pitched, and Xerxes himself stayed in it, while the rest of the army camped in the open. When dinnertime came, the hosts had all the work. But the guests, once they had eaten their fill and spent the night there, tore down the tent the next day, took all the furnishings with them, and marched on, leaving nothing behind and carrying everything away.

120. It was then that Megacreon, a man of Abdera, made a well-turned remark. He advised the people of Abdera to go all together, men and women alike, to their temples and sit there as suppliants of the gods, begging them to keep off half of the troubles still to come in the future as well. And he told them to be very thankful to the gods for what had already happened, because King Xerxes had not seen fit to eat twice a day. If they had been ordered to prepare breakfast in the same way as dinner, the people of Abdera would have had two choices: either not wait for Xerxes to come at all, or stay and be ruined worse than anyone else on earth.

121. So, hard pressed as they were, the cities carried out what they had been ordered to do. From Acanthus Xerxes sent the fleet off on its own course, after ordering the commanders to wait for him at Therma. This is the Therma that lies on the Thermaic Gulf, which takes its name from it. He did this because he was told that this was the shortest way. From Doriscus as far as Acanthus the army had marched in this order. Xerxes had divided the whole land army into three divisions. One he had sent along the coast to keep pace with the fleet; its commanders were Mardonius and Masistes. Another third of the army had been assigned the inland route, and its generals were Tritantaechmes and Gergis. Meanwhile the third division, with which Xerxes himself traveled, marched in the middle between the other two, and its commanders were Smerdomenes and Megabyzus.

122. When Xerxes let the fleet go, it sailed straight through the canal that had been cut through Athos. The canal ran across to the gulf on which stand the cities of Assa, Pilorus, Singus and Sarte. After picking up a contingent from these cities as well, the fleet sailed on unhindered for the Thermaic Gulf. Rounding Ampelus, the headland of Torone, it passed by these Greek cities, taking ships and men from them: Torone, Galepsus, Sermyle, Mecyberna and Olynthus. This region is called Sithonia.

123. Then Xerxes' fleet cut straight across from the headland of Ampelus to the headland of Canastraeum, which runs farthest out to sea of all Pallene. There it picked up ships and men from Potidaea, Aphytis, Neapolis, Aege, Therambo, Scione, Mende and Sane, since these are the cities of the region now called Pallene, which used to be called Phlegra. Sailing along this coast too, the fleet held its course for the place I mentioned before, picking up contingents also from the cities that come after Pallene and border on the Thermaic Gulf. Their names are Lipaxus, Combrea, Lisae, Gigonus, Campsa, Smila and Aenea, and the region these cities are in is called Crossaea to this day. Sailing on from Aenea, the name I ended my list of cities with, the fleet went straight into the Thermaic Gulf and to the region of Mygdonia, and so it reached Therma, which I mentioned, and the cities of Sindus and Chalestra on the river Axius. This river is the border between Mygdonia and Bottiaea, a district whose narrow strip of coast is held by the cities of Ichnae and Pella.

124. While the fleet was camped near the river Axius, the city of Therma and the cities in between, waiting for the king to arrive, Xerxes and the land army were on their way from Acanthus, cutting straight across the middle by the shortest route to reach Therma. He went through Paeonia and Crestonia to the river Cheidorus. This river rises in the land of the Crestonians, runs through the region of Mygdonia, and comes out beside the marsh at the river Axius.

125. As he was marching this way, lions attacked the camels carrying his supplies. The lions used to come down regularly every night, leaving their own haunts, but they touched nothing else, neither pack animal nor man. They killed only the camels. I wonder what the cause was, and what it was that drove the lions to leave everything else alone and attack only the camels, animals they had never seen before and knew nothing about.

126. In these parts there are many lions, and also wild oxen, the ones with the enormous horns that are often brought into Greece. The lions are found only within certain limits: the river Nestus, which flows through Abdera, on one side, and on the other the Achelous, which flows through Acarnania. East of the Nestus you would not see a lion anywhere in the whole of Europe in front of it, and none in the rest of the continent west of the Achelous; they live only in the land between these two rivers.

127. When Xerxes reached Therma, he settled his army there. His camp took up no less than this stretch of the coast: beginning at the city of Therma and at Mygdonia, it reached as far as the river Lydias and the Haliacmon, which form the border between Bottiaea and Macedonia, joining their waters in one and the same stream. So the king's army was camped in these parts. Of the rivers I have listed, only the Cheidorus, which flows from the land of the Crestonians, was not enough for the army to drink, and its stream ran dry.

128. From Therma Xerxes could see the mountains of Thessaly, Olympus and Ossa, and how very high they were. He learned that between them there was a narrow gorge through which the Peneus flows, and he heard that this way there was a good road into Thessaly. So he wanted to sail there and look at the mouth of the Peneus. He was planning to march by the upper road, through the land of the Macedonians who live inland, until he reached the Perrhaebians, passing by the city of Gonnus, because he had been told that this was the safest way to go. And as he wished, so he did. He went aboard a Sidonian ship, the one he always used whenever he wanted to do anything of this kind, and he signaled for the others to put out to sea as well, leaving the land army where it was. When Xerxes saw the mouth of the Peneus, he was filled with great wonder. He called his guides and asked them whether it would be possible to turn the river aside and lead it out to the sea some other way.

129. Thessaly, they say, was a lake in ancient times, since it is shut in on every side by very high mountains. On the east it is closed off by the ranges of Pelion and Ossa, whose foothills join one another; on the north by Olympus; on the west by Pindus; and on the south by Othrys. The region in the middle, between these mountains, is Thessaly, a kind of bowl. Many rivers flow into it, five of them especially well known: the Peneus, the Apidanus, the Onochonus, the Enipeus and the Pamisus. These gather their waters from the mountains that ring Thessaly and flow down into this plain, each under its own name. But they flow out to the sea by one channel, and a narrow one, after first all joining together in one and the same stream. As soon as they have joined, from that point on the Peneus' name wins out over the rest and the others lose their own names. In ancient times, they say, before there was this channel and outlet between the mountains, these rivers, and besides them Lake Boebeis, did not have names as they do now, but their waters made all of Thessaly one sea. The Thessalians themselves say that Poseidon made the gorge the Peneus flows through, and that is a reasonable thing for them to say. Anyone who believes that Poseidon is the one who shakes the earth, and that cracks made by earthquakes are this god's work, would say on seeing this gorge that Poseidon had made it. For the splitting of the mountains is the work of an earthquake, as is clear to me.

130. So when Xerxes asked whether the Peneus had any other possible outlet to the sea, the guides, who knew the facts exactly, said: "My king, this river has no other way out to the sea but this one, because all of Thessaly is ringed with mountains like a crown." To this Xerxes is said to have replied: "So the Thessalians are sensible men. This, it seems, is what they were guarding against in good time when they changed their minds. They thought about this above everything else: that they had a country that is easy to conquer and can be taken quickly. All anyone would have to do is let the river flood their land, by building a dam to keep it out of the gorge and so turning it from the channel it flows through now, and all of Thessaly except the mountains would be under water." He said this with the sons of Aleuas in mind, since they were Thessalians and the first of the Greeks to give themselves up to the king; Xerxes thought they were offering him friendship on behalf of their whole nation. After saying this and looking over the place, he sailed back to Therma.

131. He stayed in the region of Pieria for many days, because a third of his army was meanwhile cutting the road over the Macedonian mountains, so that the whole host could cross by that way into the land of the Perrhaebians. And now the heralds who had been sent to Greece to demand earth came back, some empty-handed and some bringing earth and water.

132. Among those who gave what was demanded were these: the Thessalians, the Dolopians, the Enianians, the Perrhaebians, the Locrians, the Magnesians, the Malians, the Achaeans of Phthiotis, and the Thebans, together with the rest of the Boeotians except the Thespians and the Plataeans. Against these the Greeks who took up the war against the invader swore an oath. The oath was this: any Greeks who had surrendered to the Persians without being forced to, they would, once their own affairs turned out well, dedicate as an offering to the god at Delphi.

133. That was the oath the Greeks swore. But Xerxes had not sent heralds to Athens or to Sparta to demand earth, and this was the reason. Once before, when Darius had sent heralds for this very purpose, the Athenians had thrown the men who made the demand into the pit where they cast condemned criminals, and the Spartans had thrown theirs into a well, and told them to get earth and water from there and take it to the king. That is why Xerxes did not send anyone to make this demand. What disaster came on the Athenians for doing this to the heralds, I cannot say, except that their land and their city were laid waste; but I do not think it happened for that reason.

134. On the Spartans, though, fell the wrath of Talthybius, Agamemnon's herald in the war against Troy. There is a temple of Talthybius in Sparta, and there are also descendants of Talthybius called the Talthybiads, who have been given the right to carry out every herald's mission that goes out from Sparta. After this event, whenever the Spartans sacrificed, they could not get favorable omens. This went on with them for a long time. The Spartans were distressed and saw it as a great disaster. They held assembly after assembly and made a proclamation asking whether any Spartan was willing to die for Sparta. At last Sperthias son of Aneristus and Bulis son of Nicolaus, Spartans of noble birth and among the very wealthiest, volunteered to pay the penalty to Xerxes for Darius' heralds who had been killed at Sparta. So the Spartans sent these two to the Persians to be put to death.

135. The courage these men showed then deserves admiration, and so do the things they said as well. On their way to Susa they came to Hydarnes, a Persian by birth and commander of the peoples on the coast of Asia, who offered them hospitality and entertained them. While they were his guests he asked them: "Spartans, why do you run away from becoming the king's friends? You can see that the king knows how to honor good men, when you look at me and my position. So you too, if you gave yourselves to the king, since he already thinks of you as good men, could each of you rule over Greek land as a gift from the king." They answered: "Hydarnes, the advice you give us is lopsided. You advise us having tried one thing but with no experience of the other. You know very well what it is to be a slave, but you have never tried freedom, to find out whether it tastes sweet or not. If you ever did try it, you would advise us to fight for it not just with spears, but with axes too."

136. That was their answer to Hydarnes. Then they went up to Susa and came before the king. First the spearmen of the guard ordered them to bow down to the ground before the king, and tried to force them to do it. They said they would never do such a thing, not even if the guards shoved them down headfirst, because it was not their custom to bow down to a man, and that was not what they had come for. When they had fought this off, they next said these words, or words to this effect: "King of the Medes, the Spartans have sent us in place of the heralds who were killed in Sparta, to pay the penalty for their lives." When they said this, Xerxes, moved by a generous spirit, replied that he would not act like the Spartans. They had broken the law that all men keep by killing heralds, but he would not do himself the very thing he blamed them for, nor would he free the Spartans from their guilt by killing these men in return.

137. So the wrath of Talthybius stopped for the time being, even though the Spartans had done no more than this, and even though Sperthias and Bulis came home to Sparta. But a long time afterward it was stirred up again, during the war between the Peloponnesians and the Athenians, as the Spartans tell it. This, I think, was most plainly the work of heaven. That the wrath of Talthybius fell on messengers and did not stop until it had been fully satisfied was only justice. But that it happened to fall on the sons of the very men who had gone up to the king because of it, on Nicolaus son of Bulis and Aneristus son of Sperthias (the Aneristus who defeated the men of Halieis, settlers from Tiryns, by sailing into its harbor in a merchant ship full of soldiers), makes it clear to me that this came about through the work of heaven, because of that wrath. These two men had been sent by the Spartans as envoys to Asia. They were betrayed by Sitalces son of Teres, king of the Thracians, and by Nymphodorus son of Pythes, a man of Abdera, and were captured at Bisanthe on the Hellespont. They were taken off to Attica and put to death by the Athenians, and with them Aristeas son of Adeimantus, the Corinthian. These things happened many years after the king's expedition. Now I return to my earlier story.
