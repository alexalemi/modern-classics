Chapter 13: The Pyramid Builders, the Priests' Reckoning of Time, and the Twelve Kings

124. Down to the time when Rhampsinitus was king, they told me, there was nothing in Egypt but good order and the rule of law, and Egypt prospered greatly. But after him Cheops became king over them, and he brought them to every kind of misery. First he shut up all the temples and kept the people from sacrificing there; then he ordered all the Egyptians to work for him. Some were assigned to drag stones from the quarries in the Arabian mountains down to the Nile. Others he ordered to receive the stones after they had been ferried across the river in boats, and to drag them on to what are called the Libyan mountains. They worked in gangs of a hundred thousand men at a time, each gang for three months on end. Ten years of this oppression went by just in building the causeway along which they dragged the stones. That causeway, in my view, is a work not much smaller than the pyramid itself. It is about 3,000 feet long and sixty feet wide, and at its highest point it is forty-eight feet high, and it is built of polished stone with figures carved on it. The ten years, they said, went on this, and on the underground chambers in the hill where the pyramids stand. He had these made as burial chambers for himself, on an island, by bringing in a channel from the Nile. The building of the pyramid itself took twenty years. The pyramid is square, each side measuring eight hundred feet, and its height is the same. It is built of polished stone, fitted together perfectly, and not one of the stones is less than thirty feet long.

125. This pyramid was built in steps, which some call "rows" and others "bases." When they had first built it in this way, they lifted the remaining stones with machines made of short pieces of timber. They raised a stone from the ground to the first tier of steps, and when it got there it was set on another machine standing on the first tier, and from there it was hauled up to the second tier on yet another machine. There were as many machines as there were tiers of steps; or perhaps they moved one and the same machine, built to be easily carried, from tier to tier, so as to lift the stones. I give it both ways, since both are reported. However that may be, the top of the pyramid was finished first, then the parts just below it, and last of all the parts nearest the ground, the lowest tiers. On the pyramid there is a record in Egyptian writing of how much was spent on radishes, onions, and leeks for the workmen. If I remember rightly what the interpreter said when he read the inscription to me, the sum was 1,600 talents of silver. If that is so, how much more is likely to have been spent on the iron tools they worked with, and on bread and clothing for the workmen? After all, they spent the time I have mentioned on the building itself, and, I suppose, no small time besides on cutting the stones, hauling them, and digging out the chambers underground.

126. Cheops sank to such wickedness, they said, that when he was short of money he put his own daughter in a brothel and ordered her to earn a certain sum from her customers (how much, they did not tell me). She earned the sum her father set. But she also made a private plan to leave a monument of her own, and she asked each man who came to her to give her one stone for her building. From these stones, they told me, was built the pyramid that stands in the middle of the three in front of the great pyramid, each of its sides a hundred and fifty feet long.

127. This Cheops, the Egyptians said, reigned fifty years, and when he died his brother Chephren took over the kingdom. This king behaved in the same way as the other in everything, and in particular he too built a pyramid. It does not reach the size of his brother's (I know this because I measured it myself). Besides, there are no underground chambers beneath it, and no channel comes to it from the Nile as it does to the other. At the other one the water comes in through a conduit built for the purpose and flows around an island inside, where they say Cheops himself is buried. For the base of his pyramid Chephren built the first course of many-colored Ethiopian stone, and he made it forty feet lower than the other, building it close to the great pyramid. The two stand on the same hill, which is about a hundred feet high. And Chephren, they said, reigned fifty-six years.

128. So here they count a hundred and six years, during which, they say, the Egyptians had nothing but misery, and the temples were kept shut and never opened in all that time. The Egyptians hate these kings so much that they are not very willing to name them. In fact they even call the pyramids after Philitis the shepherd, who grazed his flocks in that area at the time.

129. After him, they said, Mycerinus became king of Egypt. He was the son of Cheops, and his father's actions did not please him. He opened the temples, and he let the people, who had been ground down to the depths of misery, go back to their own work and their sacrifices. He also judged their lawsuits more justly than any other king. For this they praise him above all the kings who had ruled Egypt before him. Not only did he give fair judgments, but when a man complained about a decision, he would compensate him out of his own property and so satisfy him. But while Mycerinus was treating his subjects kindly and behaving as I have described, disasters began to fall on him. The first was this: his daughter died, the only child he had in his house. He was grieved beyond measure at what had happened to him, and wanting to bury his daughter in a more remarkable way than anyone else, he had a cow made of wood and covered with gold, and inside it he buried this daughter who, as I said, had died.

130. This cow was not buried in the ground. It could still be seen in my own time in the city of Sais, set inside the royal palace in a richly decorated room. Every day they burn incense of every kind before it, and every night a lamp burns beside it all night long. Near this cow, in another room, stand statues of Mycerinus' concubines, as the priests at Sais told me. There are in fact huge wooden statues there, about twenty of them, carved as naked figures. But who they are I can't say, beyond what is reported.

131. Some people, however, tell the following story about this cow and the huge statues. Mycerinus, they say, fell in love with his own daughter and then raped her. After this, they say, the girl hanged herself in her grief, and he buried her in this cow. Her mother cut off the hands of the maids who had handed her daughter over to her father, and that is why the statues of them now have suffered what the maids suffered in life. In my opinion those who say this are talking nonsense, especially about the statues' hands. For we saw for ourselves that their hands had simply dropped off with age, and they were still lying at the statues' feet in my own time.

132. The cow is covered with a crimson robe, all except the head and neck, which show, overlaid very thickly with gold. Between the horns there is the disk of the sun, worked in gold. The cow is not standing up but kneeling, and it is the size of a large living cow. Every year it is carried out of its room, at the time when the Egyptians beat their breasts in mourning for that god whom I will not name in a matter like this. At that time they also carry the cow out into the light of day. For they say that when the daughter was dying she asked her father Mycerinus to let her see the sun once a year.

133. After his daughter's death, they said, a second thing happened to this king. An oracle came to him from the city of Buto, saying that he had only six more years to live and would die in the seventh. He was indignant at this, and he sent a message to the oracle reproaching the god. His father and his uncle, he complained, had shut up the temples, had forgotten the gods, and had even been destroyers of men, and yet they had lived a long time; while he, who was devout, was to die so soon. A second message came back from the oracle. It said that this was exactly why his life was being cut short: he had not done what he was supposed to do. Egypt was fated to suffer misery for a hundred and fifty years, and the two kings before him had understood this, but he had not. When Mycerinus heard this, he took it that the sentence on him could not be undone. So he had a great many lamps made, and every night when darkness fell he lit them and drank and enjoyed himself, never stopping, day or night. He roamed the marshes and the woods and wherever he heard there were the best places for pleasure. He came up with this plan because he meant to prove the oracle a liar: by turning his nights into days, he would get twelve years of life instead of six.

134. This king too left a pyramid behind him, much smaller than his father's. It is square, each side 280 feet long, and it is built of Ethiopian stone up to half its height. Some of the Greeks say this pyramid was built by Rhodopis the courtesan, but they are wrong. In fact it is clear to me that the people who say this don't even know who Rhodopis was. Otherwise they would never have credited her with building a pyramid like this one, which cost what you might call countless thousands of talents. Besides, they don't know that Rhodopis was in her prime in the reign of Amasis, not in this king's reign; Rhodopis lived a great many years after the kings who left these pyramids. She was Thracian by birth, and she was the slave of Iadmon son of Hephaestopolis, a Samian, and a fellow slave of Aesop the writer of fables. For Aesop too was once Iadmon's slave, and the best proof of it is this. When the people of Delphi, obeying an oracle, proclaimed again and again that they were looking for someone to claim the compensation owed for Aesop's death, no one came forward except, in the end, Iadmon's grandson, who was also called Iadmon, and he took it. That shows that Aesop too was Iadmon's slave.

135. As for Rhodopis, she came to Egypt brought by Xanthes the Samian. She came there to work at her trade, and she was bought out of slavery for a large sum by a man of Mytilene, Charaxus son of Scamandronymus, the brother of Sappho the poet. So Rhodopis was set free. She stayed in Egypt, and her beauty made her so popular that she earned a great deal of money for a woman like Rhodopis, though not nearly enough to pay for a pyramid like this one. In fact there is no need to credit her with great riches, since a tenth of her wealth can still be seen today by anyone who wants to look. Rhodopis wanted to leave a memorial of herself in Greece: to have something made that no one else had happened to think of and dedicate in a temple, and to dedicate it at Delphi in her own memory. So with a tenth of her wealth she had iron spits made, big enough to roast a whole ox on, as many of them as her tenth would pay for, and she sent them to Delphi. They are still lying there today, heaped together behind the altar the Chians dedicated, right opposite the temple's inner shrine. The courtesans at Naucratis, it happens, are apt to become famous. This woman first, the one the story is about, became so famous that every Greek came to know the name of Rhodopis. And after her another, named Archidice, became the subject of songs all over Greece, though she was less talked about than the first. As for Charaxus, when he went home to Mytilene after buying Rhodopis' freedom, Sappho attacked him fiercely in one of her poems. But I will say no more about Rhodopis.

136. After Mycerinus, the priests said, Asychis became king of Egypt. He built the gateway of Hephaestus' temple that faces the sunrise, by far the most beautiful and the largest of the gateways. They all have figures carved on them and countless architectural ornaments besides, but this one has far more than the rest. In this king's reign, they told me, money changed hands very slowly, so a law was made for the Egyptians that a man could borrow the money he needed by putting up his father's dead body as security. A second law was added to this one: the lender would also have a claim on the borrower's whole family tomb. And the man who gave this security faced the following penalty if he refused to repay the debt: he himself would not be allowed burial when he died, either in that family tomb or in any other, and he would not be allowed to bury any of his relatives who died. This king, wanting to outdo the kings who had ruled Egypt before him, left as his monument a pyramid made of bricks. On it there is an inscription carved in stone, which says: "Do not think less of me beside the pyramids of stone, for I surpass them as far as Zeus surpasses the other gods. They thrust a pole into the lake, and whatever mud stuck to the pole they gathered up and made into bricks, and that is how they finished me."

Those were the deeds of this king.

137. After him reigned a blind man from the city of Anysis, whose name was Anysis. In his reign the Ethiopians and Sabacus, the king of the Ethiopians, marched on Egypt with a great army. So this blind man ran away and fled into the marshes, and the Ethiopian ruled Egypt for fifty years. This is what he did in that time. Whenever an Egyptian committed a crime, he never put him to death. Instead he sentenced each man according to how serious his crime was, setting him to work heaping up an embankment in front of the city he came from. In this way the cities were raised even higher than before. They had been built up first by the men who dug the canals in the reign of Sesostris, and then a second time in the reign of the Ethiopian, and so they became very high. Other cities in Egypt also stand high, but I think the earth was piled up most of all at the town of Bubastis. In this city there is a temple well worth mentioning. There are other temples that are larger and more costly, but none is more pleasing to the eye. Bubastis, in the Greek language, is Artemis.

138. Her temple is laid out like this. Except for the entrance it is completely surrounded by water. Two channels come in from the Nile, not joining each other, but each running as far as the temple's entrance, one flowing around one side and the other around the other side. Each is a hundred feet wide and shaded with trees. The gateway is sixty feet high, and it is decorated with figures nine feet tall, very remarkable ones. The temple stands in the middle of the city, and you can look down into it from all sides as you walk around. That is because the city has been built up high, while the temple has stayed where it was first built, so it is possible to see down into it. A stone wall with figures carved on it runs around it. Inside there is a grove of very tall trees, planted around a large shrine, and in the shrine is the statue of the goddess. The temple measures six hundred feet each way. Opposite the entrance there is a road paved with stone, about a third of a mile long, which runs east through the marketplace. It is about four hundred feet wide, and trees that reach to the sky grow on both sides of it. The road leads to the temple of Hermes. That is how this temple is laid out.

139. Egypt was finally rid of the Ethiopian, they said, in this way. He fled because of a dream. In it a man seemed to come and stand over him and advise him to gather together all the priests of Egypt and cut them in half. After he had this dream, he said that it seemed to him the gods were showing him this to give him a pretext for committing a sacrilege, so that he would suffer some harm either from the gods or from men. He would not do it, he said. Besides, in truth, the time was up during which it had been foretold that he would rule Egypt before he left. For when he was in Ethiopia, the oracles the Ethiopians consult had told him that he was fated to rule Egypt for fifty years. Since that time was now running out, and the dream also troubled him, Sabacus left Egypt of his own accord.

140. When the Ethiopian had gone away out of Egypt, the blind man came back from the marshes and began to rule again. He had lived there for fifty years on an island he had made by heaping up ashes and earth. Whenever any of the Egyptians visited him with food, as each of them in turn had been assigned to do without the Ethiopian's knowledge, he told them to bring some ashes too as part of their gift. No one could find this island before Amyrtaeus; that is, for more than seven hundred years the kings before Amyrtaeus were unable to find it. The island's name is Elbo, and it is a little over a mile across each way.

141. After him the throne went to the priest of Hephaestus, whose name was Sethos. This man, they said, treated the warrior class of the Egyptians with contempt, thinking he would never need them. Among other insults, he took away the land they had been given as a special grant under the earlier kings, twelve arourae of grain land to each man (an aroura is about two-thirds of an acre). After this, Sanacharib, king of the Arabians and Assyrians, marched a great army against Egypt. Then the Egyptian warriors refused to come to the rescue. The priest, in desperate straits, went into the inner shrine of the temple and wept before the god's statue over the danger that was hanging over him. As he lamented, sleep came over him, and in his dream the god seemed to come and stand beside him and encourage him. Nothing bad would happen to him, the god said, if he went out to meet the Arabian army, because the god himself would send him helpers. Trusting in this dream, he took with him, they said, those Egyptians who were willing to follow him, and camped at Pelusium, since that was the way the invasion was coming. Not one of the warrior class went with him: his men were shopkeepers, craftsmen, and market traders. When they arrived, field mice swarmed over their enemies in the night and gnawed through their quivers and their bows, and the handles of their shields as well. So the next day the enemy fled, with no weapons to defend themselves, and a great many of them fell. And to this day this king stands in stone in the temple of Hephaestus, holding a mouse in his hand, with an inscription in which he says: "Let whoever looks at me learn to fear the gods."

142. Up to this point in the story it was the Egyptians and their priests who gave the account. They declared that from the first king down to this priest of Hephaestus, who reigned last, there had been 341 generations of men, and that in that time there had been the same number of high priests and of kings. Now 300 generations of men come to 10,000 years, since three generations make a hundred years. And the forty-one generations left over, beyond the 300, come to 1,340 years. So over a period of 11,340 years, they said, no god had appeared in human form; and they did not report anything of the kind happening either before or afterward among the remaining kings of Egypt. In this time, they said, the sun had moved from its usual place of rising four times: twice it had risen where it now sets, and twice it had set where it now rises. And all that time nothing in Egypt had changed from its normal state, not the crops that come from the earth, not what comes to them from the river, not their diseases or their deaths.

143. Some time before me, when Hecataeus the historian was in Thebes, he traced his family tree back and linked it to a god sixteen generations earlier. The priests of Zeus did for him much what they did for me (though I did not trace my ancestry). They took me into the inner hall of the temple, which is very large, and counted up the number, showing me huge wooden statues, as many as they had said. For every high priest there sets up a statue of himself during his lifetime. So the priests counted these and showed them to me, and declared that each one was a son succeeding his own father. They went through the line of statues, starting from the statue of the one who had died most recently, until they had declared this of every one of them. And when Hecataeus traced his descent and linked his family to a god in the sixteenth generation, they set a family tree of their own against his, along with their count. They would not accept from him that a man had been born from a god. They traced their own line this way: each of the statues, they said, had been a Piromis son of a Piromis, until they had declared this of all 345 statues, each one called Piromis; and they linked their line neither to a god nor to a hero. Piromis means, in Greek, "a gentleman," a good and honorable man.

144. So what they showed was that the men the statues stood for had all been like this, and very far from being gods. But before these men, they said, gods had ruled in Egypt. They did not live among men, and there was always one of them in power at a time. The last of them to be king of Egypt was Horus son of Osiris, whom the Greeks call Apollo. He was the last king of Egypt, having overthrown Typhon. Osiris, in Greek, is Dionysus.

145. Among the Greeks, Heracles, Dionysus, and Pan are thought to be the youngest of the gods. But with the Egyptians, Pan is a very ancient god, one of the gods called the Eight; Heracles belongs to the second rank, the gods called the Twelve; and Dionysus belongs to the third rank, the gods who were born from the Twelve. I have already shown how old Heracles is by the Egyptians' own reckoning, counting down to the reign of Amasis. Pan is said to have existed even longer than that, and Dionysus the shortest time of the three. Yet even for him they count 15,000 years down to the reign of Amasis. The Egyptians say they know this for certain, because they have always kept count and written down the years as they passed. But the Dionysus who is said to have been born of Semele, the daughter of Cadmus, was born about 1,600 years before my time; Heracles, the son of Alcmene, about nine hundred years; and Pan, the son of Penelope (for the Greeks say Pan was born of her and Hermes), came into being after the Trojan War, about eight hundred years before my time.

146. Of these two accounts, anyone may adopt whichever he finds more believable when he hears it. I, for my part, have already given my opinion about them. If these two, Dionysus son of Semele and Pan son of Penelope, had also appeared openly and lived out their lives to old age in Greece, as Heracles son of Amphitryon did, then one might have said that they too had been born ordinary men, and named after the gods who had come into being long before. But as it is, the Greeks say of Dionysus that as soon as he was born Zeus sewed him up in his thigh and carried him to Nysa, which is beyond Egypt in Ethiopia; and as for Pan, they cannot say where he went after he was born. So it has become clear to me that the Greeks learned the names of these gods later than those of the others, and date their birth to the time when they first learned their names.

Up to this point, then, the history is told by the Egyptians themselves.

147. But now I will tell what other peoples also tell, with the Egyptians agreeing with them, about what happened in this country. And to this I will also add something of what I have seen myself.

After the reign of the priest of Hephaestus, the Egyptians were free; but they could never live for any length of time without a king, so they set up twelve kings over themselves, dividing all of Egypt into twelve parts. These kings intermarried and ruled together. They made an agreement not to overthrow one another, and not to try to get the better of one another, but to live in perfect friendship. The reason they made these agreements, and guarded them so strictly against being broken, was this. At the very start, when they first took power, they had been given an oracle that whichever of them poured a libation from a bronze cup in the temple of Hephaestus would become king of all Egypt (for they used to meet together in all the temples).
