Chapter 36: The Alcmaeonids, the Wooing of Agariste, and the Fall of Miltiades

121. Now it amazes me, and I do not accept the report, that the Alcmaeonids could ever have held up a shield as a signal to the Persians by a prior arrangement, wanting the Athenians to be under the non-Greeks and under Hippias. For they plainly hated tyrants as much as, or more than, Callias did, the son of Phaenippus and father of Hipponicus. Callias, for his part, was the only man in all Athens who dared to buy the property of Pisistratus when it was put up for sale by the state after Pisistratus was driven out of Athens, and in other ways too he plotted every kind of hostility against him.

122. This Callias deserves to be remembered by everyone, for many reasons. First, for what I have just said: he was a man of real distinction in freeing his country. Then also for what he did at the Olympic games, where he won the horse race and came second in the chariot race; and before that he had won at the Pythian games, so that he was famous among all the Greeks for the money he spent. And finally, because he showed himself so generous toward his daughters, who were three in number. When they were old enough to marry, he gave them the most magnificent dowry and also let them have their own way: whichever man in all Athens each of them wanted to choose as her husband, to that man he gave her.

123. And just like him, the Alcmaeonids hated tyrants as much as he did, or more. So this amazes me, and I do not accept the charge that they were the ones who held up the shield. They were in exile, away from the tyrants, the whole time the tyrants ruled, and it was by their scheming that the sons of Pisistratus gave up their power. It follows that they were the ones who set Athens free, much more than Harmodius and Aristogeiton were, in my judgment. Those two, by killing Hipparchus, only enraged the rest of Pisistratus' family; they did nothing at all to end the others' tyranny. But the Alcmaeonids plainly did set Athens free, at least if they really were the men who persuaded the Pythia to tell the Spartans to set Athens free, as I have explained before.

124. It might be said, however, that they had some grievance against the Athenian people, and so tried to betray their own city. But on the contrary, no men had a higher reputation than they did, at least among the Athenians, and none had been more highly honored. So it is not reasonable to suppose that they would have held up a shield for any such purpose. A shield was held up, though; that cannot be denied, because it happened. But as to who it was who held it up, I cannot say any more than this.

125. Now the family of the Alcmaeonids was distinguished in Athens even in the earliest times, and from the time of Alcmaeon, and of Megacles after him, they became very distinguished indeed. First, Alcmaeon son of Megacles made himself a helper to the Lydians who came from Croesus at Sardis to the oracle at Delphi, and he assisted them eagerly. When Croesus heard from the Lydians who had gone to the oracle that this man had done him good service, he sent for him to come to Sardis. When he came, Croesus offered him a gift of as much gold as he could carry away on his own body at one time. With that gift in mind, and since it was a gift of that kind, Alcmaeon got himself ready and made his preparations like this. He put on a large tunic, leaving a deep fold in it hanging down in front, and he pulled onto his feet the widest boots he could find, and in these he went into the treasury they led him to. There he fell on a heap of gold dust. First he packed as much gold down beside his legs as his boots would hold. Then he filled the whole fold of his tunic with gold, sprinkled some of the gold dust in his hair, and took some into his mouth. After that he came out of the treasury, barely able to drag his boots along and looking like anything in the world rather than a man, since his mouth was stuffed full and every part of him was swollen out. Croesus burst out laughing when he saw him, and not only gave him all of that but gave him as much again on top of it. That is how this house became enormously rich, and that is how this Alcmaeon came to keep chariot horses and won a victory at Olympia.

126. Then, in the next generation, Cleisthenes the tyrant of Sicyon raised the family up, so that it became far better known among the Greeks than it had been before. Cleisthenes son of Arisonymus, son of Myron, son of Andreas, had a daughter named Agariste, and he wanted to find the best man in all Greece and give her to him in marriage. So when the Olympic games were being held and Cleisthenes won at them with a four-horse chariot, he had a proclamation made: any Greek who thought himself worthy to be Cleisthenes' son-in-law should come to Sicyon by the sixtieth day, or earlier if he liked, because Cleisthenes meant to settle the marriage within a year, counting from the sixtieth day. Then all the Greeks who were proud either of themselves or of their families came as suitors, and Cleisthenes had a running track and a wrestling ground built for them and kept them for their use alone.

127. From Italy came Smindyrides son of Hippocrates, of Sybaris, the man who went further in luxury than anyone else on earth (Sybaris was then at the height of its prosperity), and Damasus of Siris, the son of the Amyris who was called the Wise. Those came from Italy. From the Ionian Gulf came Amphimnestus son of Epistrophus, of Epidamnus; he came from the Ionian Gulf. From Aetolia came Males, the brother of the Titormus who was stronger than any other Greek and who fled from human company to the farthest corners of the Aetolian land. From the Peloponnese came Leocedes son of Pheidon, the tyrant of Argos. This was the Pheidon who set up the measures the Peloponnesians use, and who outdid all other Greeks in arrogance, since he threw out the officials the Eleans had appointed to run the games and ran the games at Olympia himself. His son came, and so did Amiantus son of Lycurgus, an Arcadian from Trapezus; and Laphanes, an Azanian from the city of Paeus, son of the Euphorion who (so the story goes in Arcadia) received the Dioscuri as guests in his house, and from then on made a practice of entertaining everyone who came; and Onomastus son of Agaeus, of Elis. Those came from the Peloponnese itself. From Athens came Megacles, the son of the Alcmaeon who went to Croesus, and besides him Hippocleides son of Tisander, who outdid the other Athenians in wealth and good looks. From Eretria, which was flourishing at that time, came Lysanias, the only one from Euboea. From Thessaly came Diactorides of Crannon, one of the Scopad family. And from the Molossians came Alcon.

128. That is how many suitors there were. When they had arrived by the appointed day, Cleisthenes first asked each of them about his country and his family. Then he kept them for a year, testing them continually for their courage, their temper, their upbringing and their manners. He spent time with each of them alone and with all of them together. He tested them by taking the younger ones out to athletic exercises, and above all at the shared table: for the whole time he kept them he did everything that could be done, and at the same time he entertained them magnificently. As it happened, the suitors who pleased him most were the ones from Athens, and of these he rather preferred Hippocleides son of Tisander, both for his manly qualities and because he was related by descent to the Cypselids at Corinth.

129. Then the appointed day came for the wedding feast and for Cleisthenes himself to announce which of them all he had chosen. Cleisthenes sacrificed a hundred oxen and feasted both the suitors themselves and all the people of Sicyon. When dinner was over, the suitors began to compete with each other in music and in speeches before the whole company. As the drinking went on, Hippocleides, who was very much holding everyone's attention, told the flute player to play him a dance tune. When the flute player did, he danced. And it so happened that he was very pleased with his own dancing, but Cleisthenes watched the whole performance with suspicion. After a while Hippocleides told someone to bring in a table. When the table came in, he first danced Laconian figures on it, then Attic ones as well, and third, he planted his head on the table and waved his legs in the air. While he was dancing the first and second rounds, Cleisthenes was already disgusted at the thought that Hippocleides might still become his son-in-law, because of his dancing and his shamelessness, but he held himself back, not wanting to explode at him. But when he saw him waving his legs in the air, he could not hold back any longer, and said:

"You have danced away your marriage, son of Tisander!"

And Hippocleides answered:

"Hippocleides doesn't care!"

130. And that is where the saying comes from. Then Cleisthenes called for silence and spoke to the company: "Suitors of my daughter, I think well of you all, and if it were possible I would satisfy you all, not picking one of you out above the others or rejecting the rest. But since I am deciding about only one girl, it is not possible to please everyone. So to those of you who are turned down for this marriage I give a talent of silver each, for the honor you did me in wanting to marry into my house, and for your time away from home. And to Megacles son of Alcmaeon I engage my daughter Agariste, according to the laws of the Athenians." Megacles said that he accepted the engagement, and so Cleisthenes settled the marriage.

131. That is how the judging of the suitors turned out, and that is how the Alcmaeonids became famous all over Greece. From this marriage was born the Cleisthenes who set up the tribes and the democracy for the Athenians. He was named after his mother's father, Cleisthenes of Sicyon. He was one son born to Megacles; Hippocrates was another. Hippocrates had a son, another Megacles, and a daughter, another Agariste, named after Agariste the daughter of Cleisthenes. This Agariste married Xanthippus son of Ariphron, and when she was pregnant she had a dream in which she seemed to give birth to a lion. A few days later she bore Xanthippus a son, Pericles.

132. After the defeat of the Persians at Marathon, Miltiades, who had already been well thought of in Athens, rose much higher still in the Athenians' esteem. He asked them for seventy ships, an army and money. He did not tell them which country he meant to attack, but said he would make them rich if they followed him, because he would lead them to a land where they could easily get gold in abundance. That was what he said as he asked for the ships, and the Athenians, carried away by his words, handed them over.

133. Once he had the army, Miltiades sailed for Paros. His excuse was that the Parians had started it, by sending triremes with the Persians to Marathon. That was the pretext he gave, but he also had a grudge against the Parians because of Lysagoras son of Tisias, a Parian by birth, who had slandered him to Hydarnes the Persian. When Miltiades arrived where he was sailing, he shut the Parians up inside their wall and laid siege to them with his army. He sent a herald in to demand a hundred talents, saying that if they refused, his army would not go home until it had captured them. But the Parians had no intention of giving Miltiades any money. All their thought was on how to defend their city. They came up with various plans, and this one besides: wherever the wall was open to attack at any point, they built it up at night to twice its former height.

134. Up to this point the story is told by all the Greeks, but what happened next only the Parians tell, and they say it went like this. When Miltiades was at a loss, the story goes, a woman came to talk with him. She was a prisoner of war, a Parian by birth named Timo, an under-priestess of the Earth goddesses. She came before Miltiades, they say, and advised him that if he thought it very important to take Paros, he should do what she would suggest; and then she told him what she meant. So he went to the hill in front of the city and, since he could not open the gate, jumped over the fence of the temple of Demeter the Lawgiver. Once over, he went on toward the shrine to do something inside, whether to lay hands on some of the things that must not be touched or whatever else he meant to do. But when he reached the door, a shuddering terror came over him at once, and he started back the way he had come. As he jumped down from the rough stone wall, he dislocated his thigh, or, as others say, he hit his knee against the wall.

135. So Miltiades, in a bad way, sailed for home. He brought the Athenians no wealth and had not won Paros for them; he had besieged the city for twenty-six days and laid waste the island. When the Parians learned that Timo, the under-priestess of the goddesses, had guided Miltiades, they wanted to punish her for it. As soon as they had a breathing space after the siege, they sent messengers to Delphi to consult the god. The messengers were to ask whether they should put to death the under-priestess of the goddesses, who had guided their enemies to the capture of her own city and had revealed to Miltiades the mysteries that no man may be told. But the Pythia forbade it. Timo, she said, was not the real cause of these things; since it was fated that Miltiades would come to a bad end, she had appeared to him to lead him on to his ruin.

136. That was the Pythia's answer to the Parians. Meanwhile, when Miltiades got back from Paros, the Athenians had a great deal to say about him, and Xanthippus son of Ariphron more than anyone. He brought Miltiades to trial before the people on a capital charge and prosecuted him for deceiving the Athenians. Miltiades did not speak in his own defense, though he was there; he could not, because his thigh was rotting. Instead he lay on a couch in full view, while his friends spoke for him. They talked a great deal about the battle at Marathon and about the taking of Lemnos: how he had conquered Lemnos, punished the Pelasgians, and handed the island over to the Athenians. The people came over to his side so far as to acquit him of the death penalty, but they fined him fifty talents for his wrongdoing. After this Miltiades died, when his thigh turned gangrenous and rotted, and his son Cimon paid the fifty talents.

137. Now this is how Miltiades son of Cimon had taken Lemnos. The Pelasgians had been driven out of Attica by the Athenians, whether justly or unjustly. On that point I can only tell what is reported, which is this. Hecataeus son of Hegesander said in his history that it was done unjustly. According to him, the Athenians had given the Pelasgians the land below Mount Hymettus to live on, as payment for the wall they had once built around the Acropolis. When the Athenians saw that this land, which had been poor and worthless before, was now well farmed, they were seized with envy and a longing to have it, and so they drove the Pelasgians out, without giving any other excuse. But according to the Athenians' own account, they drove them out justly. For the Pelasgians, settled below Hymettus, used it as a base to wrong them, like this. The Athenians' daughters and sons used to go regularly for water to the spring called Enneacrunus, the Nine Fountains, because at that time neither the Athenians nor any other Greeks had household slaves yet. And whenever the girls came, the Pelasgians, out of arrogance and contempt for the Athenians, would assault them. Not content with that, they were finally caught in the act of plotting to attack the city. And the tellers say that in this the Athenians proved themselves better men than the Pelasgians: they could have killed the Pelasgians, who had been caught plotting against them, but they chose not to, and only ordered them to leave the country. Having left the country in this way, the Pelasgians took over various places, and Lemnos in particular. The first story is the one Hecataeus told; the second is the one the Athenians tell.

138. These Pelasgians, then, living on Lemnos after this, wanted revenge on the Athenians. Since they knew the Athenian festivals well, they got some fifty-oared galleys and lay in wait for the Athenian women when they were celebrating the festival of Artemis at Brauron. They carried off a number of them from there, sailed away home, took the women to Lemnos, and kept them as concubines. As these women had more and more children, they made a point of teaching their sons the Attic language and the Athenian ways. These boys would not mix with the sons of the Pelasgian women, and if any one of them was hit by one of those boys, they all came running in a body to help each other. On top of that, the boys claimed the right to rule over the other boys, and easily got the better of them. When the Pelasgians saw this, they talked it over among themselves. As they discussed it, fear came over them. If these boys were already determined to stand by each other against the sons of the lawful wives, and were already trying from the start to rule over them, what would they do once they were grown men? So they decided to kill the sons of the Athenian women, and they actually did it; and along with the sons they killed their mothers too. Because of this deed, and the one done before it, when the women of Lemnos killed Thoas and the rest of their own husbands, it has become the custom all over Greece to call any deed of great cruelty a "Lemnian deed."

139. After the Pelasgians had killed their own sons and wives, the earth bore them no crops, and their women and their flocks and herds did not bear young as they had before. Hard pressed by famine and childlessness, they sent to Delphi to ask how they might be released from the evils that were on them. The Pythia told them to pay the Athenians whatever penalty the Athenians themselves should name. So the Pelasgians went to Athens and announced that they were willing to pay the penalty for all the wrong they had done. The Athenians spread a couch in the town hall as beautifully as they could, set beside it a table loaded with every good thing, and told the Pelasgians to hand over their land to them in that same condition. The Pelasgians answered: "When a ship sails from your land to ours with a north wind in a single day, then we will hand it over." They said this because they were sure it could never happen, since Attica lies far to the south of Lemnos.

140. That was how things stood at that time. Very many years later, after the Chersonese on the Hellespont had come under Athenian control, Miltiades son of Cimon, while the Etesian winds were blowing steadily, made the voyage in a single ship from Elaeus in the Chersonese to Lemnos. He told the Pelasgians to leave the island, reminding them of the oracle, which the Pelasgians had never expected to see fulfilled. The people of Hephaestia obeyed. But the people of Myrina refused to admit that the Chersonese was Attica, and were besieged until at last they too gave in. That is how the Athenians and Miltiades took Lemnos.
