Chapter 19: The Edges of the World, the Death of Polycrates, and Democedes the Physician

114. As you go on past the south and toward the setting sun, the land of Ethiopia is the farthest of all inhabited lands in that direction. It produces gold in abundance, huge elephants, trees of every kind growing wild, and ebony; and its men are the tallest of all men, the most beautiful, and the longest-lived.

115. These, then, are the extremities of the world in Asia and in Libya. But about the extremities of Europe toward the west I cannot speak with certainty. For one thing, I do not accept the story that there is a river which the non-Greeks call the Eridanus, flowing into the sea that lies toward the north wind, and that amber comes from there. Nor do I know for a fact that there are "Tin Islands," from which our tin comes. In the first place, the name Eridanus itself shows that it is Greek, not a word from any foreign language, and that some poet made it up. In the second place, though I have taken pains to find out, I have not been able to hear from anyone who has seen it with his own eyes that there is a sea on the far side of Europe. However that may be, tin and amber certainly do come to us from the far edge of Europe.

116. Then again, in the north of Europe there is clearly far more gold than in any other land. How it is got, here again I cannot say for certain; but it is said that the Arimaspians, a race of one-eyed men, steal it from the griffins. I do not believe this story either, that nature produces one-eyed men who are in every other way like other men. Still, it does seem that the extremities, which surround the rest of the world on every side and enclose it in the middle, have the things we think the most beautiful and the most rare.

117. Now there is a plain in Asia shut in by mountains on every side, and through the mountains there are five gorges. This plain once belonged to the Chorasmians, and it lies on the borders of the Chorasmians themselves, the Hyrcanians, the Parthians, the Sarangians, and the Thamanaeans; but ever since the Persians came to power it has belonged to the king. Out of this ring of mountains flows a great river, and its name is the Aces. In the old days it watered the lands of the peoples I have named: it was divided into five streams, and each stream was led through its own gorge in the mountains to its own people. But since they came under the Persians, this is what has happened to them. The king walled up the gorges in the mountains and set gates in each one. So the water was shut off from its way out, and the plain inside the mountains has become a lake, because the river runs into it and has no way out anywhere.

So the people who used to depend on the water can no longer use it, and they are in great trouble. In winter, it is true, the sky rains on them as it does on other people, but in summer, when they sow their millet and sesame, they need the river. So when no water is given to them, they go to Persia, men and wives together, and they stand at the gates of the king's palace crying and wailing. Then the king orders that the gates leading to the land of those who need it most be opened. When their land has drunk its fill, those gates are shut, and he orders the gates opened for others, the ones of the rest who need it most. And, as I have heard, he charges them large sums of money for opening the gates, on top of their regular tribute.

118. So much for these matters. Now of the seven men who had risen against the Magus, one, Intaphrenes, was put to death soon after their uprising, for an outrage which I will now relate. He wanted to go into the palace and speak with the king. The rule was, in fact, that those who had risen against the Magus could go in to the king without being announced, unless the king happened to be in bed with a woman. So Intaphrenes did not think anyone needed to announce him; as one of the seven, he meant to walk in. But the gatekeeper and the royal messenger tried to stop him, saying that the king was in bed with a woman. Intaphrenes thought they were lying. He drew his sword and cut off their ears and noses, threaded them on his horse's bridle, tied the bridle around their necks, and let them go.

119. They then showed themselves to the king and told him why this had been done to them. Darius was afraid that the six had done it by common agreement, so he sent for each of them separately and sounded him out, to see whether he approved of what had been done. When he was fully satisfied that Intaphrenes had not acted together with them, he arrested Intaphrenes himself, with his sons and all his relatives, since he strongly suspected that Intaphrenes and his family were plotting a revolt against him. Having seized them, he put them in chains, ready for execution.

Then Intaphrenes' wife began to come to the doors of the palace, weeping and lamenting. She kept on doing this, day after day, until she made Darius pity her. So he sent a messenger to say to her: "Woman, King Darius grants you the life of one of your relatives who are in chains, whichever one of them all you choose." She thought it over and answered: "If the king really grants me the life of one, then out of them all I choose my brother." When Darius heard this he was amazed at her answer, and he sent to ask her: "Woman, the king asks you what you were thinking of, to leave your husband and your children to die and choose your brother to live, when he is surely less close to you in blood than your children, and less dear to you than your husband." She answered: "My king, if heaven willed it, I could have another husband and other children, if I lost these. But I could never have another brother, since my father and my mother are no longer alive. That is what I was thinking when I said it." Darius thought the woman had spoken well. He set free the man whose life she had asked for, and the eldest of her sons as well, because he was pleased with her; but all the others he put to death. So one of the seven had died almost at once, in the way I have told.

120. Now at about the time of Cambyses' illness, this is what happened. There was a Persian named Oroetes whom Cyrus had made governor of the province of Sardis. This man set his heart on an unholy deed. He had never suffered any harm from Polycrates of Samos, never heard an offensive word from him, and had never even seen him before; yet he wanted to seize him and put him to death. Most of those who tell the story give this reason. Oroetes and another Persian named Mitrobates, the governor of the province of Dascylium, were sitting at the king's door, and they went from talking to quarreling. As they argued over which of them was the better man, Mitrobates taunted Oroetes: "Do you call yourself a man? You never won Samos for the king, though it lies right next to your province, and it is so easy to conquer that one of the islanders rose up against the government with fifteen hoplites, heavy-armed foot soldiers, took the island, and is its tyrant now." Some say that when Oroetes heard this, the taunt stung him, and he conceived a desire, not so much to take revenge on the man who said it, as to destroy Polycrates at any cost, since Polycrates was the reason he had been insulted.

121. A smaller number of those who tell the story say this instead. Oroetes sent a herald to Samos to ask for something or other (what it was is not recorded). Polycrates happened to be lying on a couch in the men's hall of his palace, and Anacreon of Teos, the poet, was there with him. And somehow, whether on purpose, because he thought little of Oroetes' affairs, or whether it just happened by chance, the envoy from Oroetes came in and spoke to him, and Polycrates, who happened to be turned toward the wall, did not turn around or give him any answer at all.

122. Those are the two different accounts of the cause of Polycrates' death, and you may believe whichever you like. Oroetes, at any rate, who lived at the Magnesia that stands on the river Maeander, sent Myrsus son of Gyges, a Lydian, to Samos with a message, because he had seen what Polycrates was aiming at. For Polycrates was the first of the Greeks we know of who set his mind on ruling the sea, apart from Minos of Cnossus and anyone else who may have ruled the sea before him. Of what we call the human race, Polycrates was the first; and he had high hopes of becoming ruler of Ionia and the islands.

Oroetes, then, seeing that this was his plan, sent him this message: "Oroetes says this to Polycrates. I hear that you are making plans to gain great power, and that you do not have the money to match your ambitions. Now if you do as I say, you will do well for yourself, and you will save me from ruin as well. For King Cambyses is planning to have me killed; this has been reported to me so plainly that I cannot doubt it. So take me out of danger, and my money with me. Keep part of the money yourself and let me keep the rest, and then, as far as money can do it, you will be ruler of all Greece. And if you do not believe what I say about the money, send whoever you trust most, and I will show it to him."

123. When Polycrates heard this he was delighted and inclined to agree. Since he had, it seems, a great desire for money, he first sent Maeandrius son of Maeandrius, a Samian who was his secretary, to look at it. This was the same man who not long afterward dedicated to the temple of Hera all the furnishings of the men's hall in Polycrates' palace, furnishings well worth seeing. When Oroetes heard that the inspector was expected soon, this is what he did. He filled eight chests with stones, all but a shallow layer at the very top of each, and laid gold on top of the stones. Then he tied up the chests and kept them ready. So Maeandrius came and looked at them and brought back his report to Polycrates.

124. Polycrates then got ready to go there himself. His soothsayers and his friends tried hard to talk him out of it, and besides, his daughter had seen a vision in her sleep. It seemed to her that her father was lifted high up in the air, and that Zeus was washing him and the Sun was anointing him. After she saw this vision, she tried every way she could to keep Polycrates from leaving home to go to Oroetes. And as he was walking to his fifty-oared galley, she went along beside him calling out words of ill omen. He threatened her that if he came back safe she would stay unmarried for a long time. But she prayed that it would turn out so, for she said she would rather stay unmarried a long time than lose her father.

125. But Polycrates ignored every warning and sailed off to Oroetes. He took many of his friends with him, and among them Democedes son of Calliphon, of Croton, a physician who practiced his art better than any other man of his time. When he reached Magnesia, Polycrates was put to a miserable death, unworthy of himself and of his great ambitions. For apart from the men who became tyrants of Syracuse, not one of the other Greek tyrants deserves to be compared with Polycrates for magnificence. Oroetes killed him in a way not fit to be told, and then he impaled his body. Of the men who had come with him, he let go all who were Samians, telling them they should be grateful to him for their freedom; but all the others in Polycrates' company, the foreign allies and the servants, he counted as slaves and kept them. And so Polycrates, hanging there, fulfilled his daughter's vision completely: he was washed by Zeus whenever it rained, and he was anointed by the Sun as the moisture ran out of his own body.

126. That was the end of Polycrates' great good fortune, just as Amasis, king of Egypt, had foretold to him. But not long afterward vengeance for Polycrates' murder caught up with Oroetes in his turn. After Cambyses' death, during the reign of the Magi, Oroetes stayed at Sardis and did nothing to help the Persians, when the Medes had taken their empire from them. Instead, in this time of upheaval, he killed Mitrobates, the governor of Dascylium, who had thrown Polycrates in his face as a reproach, and he also killed Cranaspes son of Mitrobates, both of them men of standing among the Persians. He committed many other outrages too. Once, when a royal messenger had come to him from Darius with a message he did not like, he had men lie in wait for the messenger on his way back and kill him; and after he was dead he made away with the bodies of both the man and his horse.

127. So when Darius had become king, he wanted to punish Oroetes for all his crimes, and above all for the murder of Mitrobates and his son. But he did not think it wise to act openly and send an army against him, since his own affairs were still unsettled and he had only just come to the throne, and he heard that Oroetes was very strong. Oroetes had a bodyguard of a thousand Persian spearmen, and he held the provinces of Phrygia, Lydia and Ionia. So Darius worked out a plan. He called together the most distinguished of the Persians and said to them: "Persians, which of you will do this job for me by cleverness, not by force or by making a commotion? Where cleverness is needed, force is no use. Which of you, I say, will either bring Oroetes to me alive or kill him? He has never done the Persians any good, and he has done them great harm. First he destroyed two of us, Mitrobates and his son. Then he kills the men I send to summon him, with an arrogance no one can tolerate. So before he does the Persians any more harm, we must stop him with his death."

128. That was Darius' question, and thirty men offered to do it, each of them wanting the job for himself. Darius stopped their arguing and told them to draw lots. When they drew, Bagaeus son of Artontes won the lot out of them all. Having won it, this is what Bagaeus did. He wrote many letters on all sorts of business, sealed them with Darius' seal, and took them with him to Sardis. When he got there and came before Oroetes, he took the covers off the letters one by one and handed them to the royal secretary to read aloud (every provincial governor has a royal secretary). Bagaeus handed over the letters to test the spearmen of the guard, to see whether they would accept a call to turn against Oroetes. He saw that they showed great respect for the letters, and even more for what was read out of them. So he handed over another letter, which said: "Persians, King Darius forbids you to serve as Oroetes' bodyguard." When they heard this they lowered their spears to him. Seeing that they obeyed the letter this far, Bagaeus took courage and gave the last of the letters to the secretary, and in it was written: "King Darius commands the Persians at Sardis to kill Oroetes." When the spearmen of the guard heard this, they drew their swords and killed him on the spot. So vengeance for the murder of Polycrates of Samos caught up with Oroetes.

129. When Oroetes' wealth had arrived, or been carried, up to Susa, it happened not long afterward that King Darius, out hunting wild animals, twisted his foot as he jumped down from his horse. It was twisted, it seems, quite badly, for the ball of his ankle joint was dislocated. Now he had long kept around him the Egyptians who were considered the best in the art of medicine, and he used their help now. But they twisted and forced the foot and only made the injury worse and worse. For seven days and seven nights Darius could not sleep for the pain. At last, on the eighth day, when he was in a bad way, someone who had heard talk at Sardis of the skill of Democedes of Croton told Darius about him, and Darius ordered them to bring him in at once. They found him somewhere among Oroetes' slaves, overlooked, and they brought him out before everyone, dragging his chains and dressed in rags.

130. When he was standing in front of them all, Darius asked him whether he knew the art of medicine. But he would not admit it, because he was afraid that if he showed what he really was, he would lose all hope of ever returning to Greece. Darius could see clearly that he did know the art and was trying to trick him, and he ordered the men who had brought him to fetch whips and goads. At that he spoke up, saying that he did not know it well, but that he had spent time with a physician and had some poor knowledge of the art. After this, when Darius had put himself in his hands, Democedes used Greek remedies and applied gentle treatment after the earlier rough methods. He got the king to sleep, and in a short time he made him completely well, though Darius had never expected to have a sound foot again.

After this Darius gave him a present of two pairs of golden chains. Democedes asked him whether he was deliberately doubling his misery as a reward for making him well. Darius was pleased with this remark and sent him to visit his wives. The eunuchs who took him in told the women that this was the man who had given the king back his life. Then each of them dipped a bowl into a chest of gold and gave it to Democedes, a gift so lavish that his servant, whose name was Sciton, followed along picking up the coins that fell from the bowls, and collected a very large sum of gold for himself.

131. This Democedes came from Croton, and this is how he had come to be with Polycrates. At Croton he was always quarreling with his father, who had a harsh temper, and when he could not stand him any longer, he left and went to Aegina. He settled there, and in his first year he outdid all the other physicians, though he had no equipment and none of the instruments used in the art. In his second year the city of Aegina hired him for a fee of one talent; in the third the Athenians hired him for a hundred pounds of silver; and in the fourth Polycrates hired him for two talents. That is how he came to Samos. And it was because of this man, more than anything else, that the physicians of Croton became famous. For this was the time when the physicians of Croton began to be spoken of as the best in Greece, with those of Cyrene in second place. At about this same time the Argives too had the reputation of being the best musicians in Greece.

132. So now, having cured King Darius, Democedes had a very large house in Susa and had been made one of the king's table companions. He had everything except the one thing he wanted: to go home to Greece. To begin with, when the Egyptian physicians who had tried to cure the king before him were about to be impaled for being outdone by a Greek physician, he asked the king to spare their lives and saved them from death. Second, he rescued a soothsayer from Elis who had come with Polycrates and had been overlooked among the slaves. In short, Democedes stood very high in the king's favor.

133. Not long after this, something else happened. Atossa, the daughter of Cyrus and wife of Darius, had a growth on her breast, which later broke open and kept spreading. As long as it was small, she hid it and told no one, because she was ashamed. But later, when it had become serious, she sent for Democedes and showed it to him. He said that he would cure her, and he made her swear that in return she would do for him whatever he asked her; and he said he would ask nothing shameful.

134. So after he had treated her and made her well, Atossa, coached by Democedes, said something like this to Darius in their bedroom: "My king, you have such great power, and yet you sit still and win no new nation or power for the Persians. But it is only right for a man who is young and master of great wealth to be seen doing some great deed, so that the Persians may know for sure that it is a real man who rules them. Doing this will help you in two ways: the Persians will know that their ruler is a man, and they will be worn down by war and have no time to plot against you. Now is the time when you could do some great deed, while you are still young. For as the body grows older the spirit grows old along with it, and goes dull for any kind of action."

That was what she said, as she had been told, and he answered her: "Woman, you have said all the things I already have in mind to do. I have decided to build a bridge joining this continent to the other and to march against the Scythians, and these plans will be carried out before long." Then Atossa said: "Listen, don't go against the Scythians first. They will be yours whenever you want them. Instead, please, march against Greece. I have heard about the women there, and I want Spartan women for my maids, and Argive, Athenian and Corinthian ones. And you have the man best fitted of all men to show you everything about Greece and be your guide: the man who cured your foot." Darius answered: "Woman, since you think we should try Greece first, I think it is better to send some Persians there first, together with the man you speak of, to scout it out. When they have learned and seen everything, they can report it all to us in detail, and then I will go and attack them knowing everything."

135. So he said, and he made his words into deeds at once. As soon as day dawned, he summoned fifteen Persians of high standing and ordered them to travel along the coasts of Greece with Democedes, and to make sure that Democedes did not escape from them, but to bring him back at all costs. Having given them these orders, he next summoned Democedes himself and asked him to guide the Persians through all of Greece and show it to them, and then come back. He told him to take all his household goods and carry them as presents to his father and brothers, and said he would give him many times as much in their place. On top of that, he said, he would add to the presents a merchant ship loaded with all kinds of goods, which would sail with him. In my opinion Darius made him these promises with no trick in mind. But Democedes was afraid that Darius was testing him, and he was in no hurry to accept everything offered. He said he would leave his own things where they were, so that he would have them when he came back; but he did accept the merchant ship Darius promised him for the presents to his brothers. Having given these orders to him as well, Darius sent them off to the coast.

136. So they went down to Phoenicia, to the city of Sidon, and at once manned two triremes and also loaded a large cargo ship with all kinds of goods. When they had everything ready they sailed for Greece. They put in at various places along its coasts, looked them over, and wrote down descriptions, until, when they had seen most of the famous places, they came to Taras in Italy. There Aristophilides, the king of Taras, as a favor to Democedes, unfastened and removed the steering oars of the Persian ships, and also locked the Persians up in prison, on the grounds, he said, that they had come as spies. While this was being done to them, Democedes got away and reached Croton. And when he had reached his own home city, Aristophilides set the Persians free and gave them back the parts of their ships he had taken.

137. Then the Persians sailed from there in pursuit of Democedes and reached Croton. They found him in the marketplace and seized him. Some of the men of Croton, afraid of Persian power, were ready to hand him over; but others grabbed hold of him and beat the Persians with their staffs. The Persians pleaded with them like this: "Men of Croton, be careful what you are doing. You are rescuing a runaway slave of King Darius. How do you think King Darius will take an insult like this? And how will it turn out well for you if you take him away from us? What city do you think we will march against before this one, and what city will we try to enslave first?" But for all they said, they did not persuade the men of Croton. Democedes was taken away from them, and so was the cargo ship they had brought with them, and they set sail back to Asia. Without their guide, they made no further attempt to visit or learn about any more of Greece. But as they were putting out to sea, Democedes gave them this message, telling them to say to Darius that Democedes was engaged to the daughter of Milo. For the wrestler Milo had a great name at the king's court. And I suppose that Democedes pushed hard for this marriage, spending a great deal of money on it, so that Darius would see that he was a man of honor in his own country too.

138. After the Persians put out from Croton, they were shipwrecked in Iapygia, the heel of Italy. While they were held there as slaves, Gillus, an exile from Taras, rescued them and brought them back to King Darius. In return Darius offered to give him whatever he wanted. Gillus chose to be allowed to return to Taras, and first he told the story of his misfortune. But so as not to throw all of Greece into turmoil, as would happen if a great armada sailed to invade Italy on his account, he said it would be enough for him if the men of Cnidus alone brought him back, and no one else. He thought that since they were friends of the people of Taras, they could most easily arrange his return from exile. Darius promised and kept his promise: he sent a message to Cnidus ordering them to bring Gillus back to Taras. The men of Cnidus obeyed Darius, but they could not persuade the people of Taras, and they were not strong enough to use force. That is how these things turned out. These were the first Persians who came from Asia to Greece, and they were sent as spies for the reason I have told.
