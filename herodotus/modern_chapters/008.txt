Chapter 8: The Land and Customs of Babylon, and the Death of Cyrus

193. That was the wealth that belonged to the ruler of Babylon. Now the land of the Assyrians gets very little rain. That little is enough to nourish the roots of the grain, but the crop is ripened and the ear grows full by watering from the river. This is not done, as in Egypt, by the river itself rising over the fields; here the crop is watered by hand or with swing-buckets. For the whole Babylonian country, like Egypt, is cut up by channels. The largest of these channels is navigable for ships, and it runs toward the point where the sun rises in winter, from the Euphrates to another river, the Tigris, on whose bank the city of Nineveh stood. Of all the lands we know, this is by far the best for growing grain. As for trees, it does not even try to grow them, neither fig nor vine nor olive. But for grain it is so good that it returns as much as two hundred times what was sown on average, and when it does its very best it yields three hundred times. The leaves of the wheat and barley there grow a full three inches wide. As for millet and sesame, I know for myself how tall a plant they grow into, but I will not record it, because I am well aware that even what I have already said about the crops has been enough to make people who have never been to Babylonia disbelieve me. They use no olive oil, only oil they make from sesame seed. Date palms grow all over the plain, most of them bearing fruit, and from the dates they make food, wine and honey. They tend these palms the same way people tend fig trees. In particular they take the fruit of the palms the Greeks call "male palms" and tie it onto the date-bearing palms, so that the gall wasp can get into the date and ripen it, and so that the fruit of the palm does not drop off. For the male palm carries gall wasps in its fruit, just as the wild fig does.

194. But the greatest wonder of all in that country, after the city itself, to my mind, is the one I am about to describe. Their boats, I mean the ones that go down the river to Babylon, are round and made entirely of leather. They cut willow in the land of the Armenians, who live upstream of the Assyrians, and make ribs out of it. Around the ribs they stretch hides, which serve as an outer covering, a hull. They do not broaden out a stern or draw the bow together to a point; they make the boats round, like a shield. Then they pack the whole boat with straw, load it with cargo, and let it float down the river. Mostly these boats carry down casks of palm wood full of wine. The boat is steered by two oars and two men standing upright: the man inside pulls his oar while the man outside pushes his. Some of these boats are built very large and some smaller; the largest of them carry as much as five thousand talents' weight, something like 140 tons. In each boat there is a live donkey, and in the larger ones several. So when they have finished the voyage to Babylon and disposed of their cargo, they auction off the ribs of the boat and all the straw, but they load the hides onto their donkeys and drive them off to Armenia. For it is simply impossible to sail up the river, because the current is so swift, and that is why they make their boats of hides and not of timber. Then, when they have come back to the land of the Armenians, driving their donkeys with them, they build more boats in the same way.

195. So much for their boats. Here is how they dress. They wear a linen tunic that reaches to the feet, over it another tunic of wool, and over that a white cloak wrapped around them. Their shoes are of a local style, rather like the slippers worn in Boeotia. They wear their hair long and tie bands around their heads, and they rub perfume all over their bodies. Each man has a seal and a hand-carved staff, and on every staff a design is carved: an apple, a rose, a lily, an eagle or something else. For it is not their custom to carry a staff without a design on it.

196. That is how they equip themselves. Their established customs are as follows. The wisest of them, in my opinion, is this one, which I am told the Eneti in Illyria also have. Once a year, in every village, it used to be done like this. When the girls reached the age for marriage, they gathered them all together and brought them as a group to one place, and a crowd of men stood around them. The auctioneer had each girl stand up in turn and put her up for sale. He started with the best-looking of all. Then, when she had been sold for a large sum of money, he would put up the next best-looking after her. They were sold to be married. All the rich Babylonians who were ready to marry would bid against one another for the most beautiful girls. But the ordinary men who were ready to marry had no need of good looks; they would take money along with a plainer girl. For when the auctioneer had finished selling the best-looking girls, he would stand up the least attractive one, or any who was crippled in some way, and announce her for sale, asking who would take her in marriage for the least amount of gold. She went to the man who was willing to accept the smallest sum. The gold came from the sale of the good-looking girls, so the beautiful ones provided dowries for the plain and the crippled. No man was allowed to marry off his own daughter to whomever he pleased, and no buyer was allowed to take a girl away without a guarantee. He had to put up guarantors that he would marry her before he could take her away, and if the couple did not get along, the law said he had to pay the money back. Anyone who wanted to could also come from another village and buy. This, then, was their finest custom. But it no longer exists in our time. Lately they have hit upon another way of doing things, so that the men will not mistreat the girls or take them off to another city. Ever since they were conquered and were oppressed and ruined, every man of the common people who has nothing to live on puts his daughters to work as prostitutes.

197. Next in wisdom after that is this other custom of theirs. They carry their sick out into the marketplace, since they make no use of doctors. People come up to the sick man and give him advice about his illness, if they have ever had anything like what he has themselves, or seen someone else who had it. They come up and advise him and recommend whatever treatment cured them of a similar illness, or cured someone else they saw. And no one is allowed to walk past the sick man in silence without asking what illness he has.

198. They bury their dead in honey, and their ways of mourning are similar to those in Egypt. Whenever a Babylonian man has had sex with his wife, he sits beside burning incense, and his wife does the same on the other side. In the morning they both wash, for they will not touch any vessel until they have washed. The Arabians do the same in this matter.

199. The most shameful of the Babylonians' customs is this. Every woman of the country must, once in her life, sit in the sacred precinct of Aphrodite and have sex with a stranger. Many women who are too proud to mix with the rest, because their wealth makes them arrogant, drive to the temple in covered carriages drawn by pairs of horses and take their places there, with a large crowd of servants following them. But most do it this way. In the sacred enclosure of Aphrodite great numbers of women sit with a wreath of cord around their heads; some are arriving and others leaving. Straight passageways run between the women in every direction, and the strangers walk along them and make their choice. Once a woman has taken her seat there, she does not go home again until one of the strangers has thrown a silver coin into her lap and had sex with her outside the temple. When he throws the coin he must say only these words: "I call on you in the name of the goddess Mylitta." Mylitta is the Assyrians' name for Aphrodite. The coin may be of any value. Whatever it is, she will not refuse it, for that is not lawful for her, since the act makes the coin sacred. She goes with the first man who throws a coin, and rejects no one. After that she goes home, having done her duty to the goddess, and from then on you could not offer her a gift big enough to win her. So the women who are tall and beautiful are soon released, but the plain ones stay there a long time, unable to fulfill the law; some of them stay as long as three or four years. In some parts of Cyprus, too, there is a custom like this one.

200. Those, then, are the established customs of the Babylonians. There are also three tribes among them that eat nothing but fish. When they have caught the fish and dried them in the sun, this is what they do: they throw them into brine, then pound them with pestles and strain them through fine cloth. They eat them either kneaded into a soft cake or baked like bread, as each one likes.

201. When Cyrus had conquered this nation too, he wanted to bring the Massagetae under his rule. This nation is said to be both large and warlike. They live toward the east, where the sun rises, beyond the river Araxes and opposite the Issedonians. Some also say that this nation is Scythian.

202. Some say the Araxes is larger than the Danube (which the Greeks call the Ister) and others that it is smaller. They say there are many islands in it about the size of Lesbos, and that people live on them who in summer eat roots of every kind, which they dig up. They have also found certain fruits on trees good to eat, and they store these up, it is said, in the season when they are ripe and live on them in the winter. It is also said that they have found other trees with a fruit of a special kind. They gather in groups in one place, light a fire, sit around it in a circle, and throw some of this fruit onto the fire. They breathe in the smell of the fruit as it burns and get drunk on the scent, as the Greeks get drunk on wine. The more fruit is thrown on, the drunker they get, until at last they get up to dance and start to sing. That, it is said, is how they live. As for the river Araxes, it flows from the land of the Matieni, the same place the Gyndes flows from, the river Cyrus divided into three hundred and sixty channels. It empties through forty mouths, and all of these but one end in swamps and shallow pools. They say that men live among these marshes who eat raw fish and usually dress in sealskins. But the one remaining mouth of the Araxes flows in a clear channel into the Caspian Sea.

203. The Caspian Sea is a sea on its own, not connected with the other sea. For the whole sea that the Greeks sail, and the sea beyond the Pillars of Heracles (the strait at the western end of the Mediterranean) that is called the Atlantic, and the Erythraean Sea, the Indian Ocean, are in fact all one. But the Caspian is separate and lies apart by itself. Its length is a voyage of fifteen days for a rowed ship, and its breadth, at its broadest, a voyage of eight days. Along its western side runs the Caucasus, the largest of all mountain ranges in extent and the highest. Many different peoples live in the Caucasus, mostly on the wild food of the forests. Among them, it is said, there are trees whose leaves have a special property: they pound them and mix them with water, and with this they paint figures on their clothes. The figures do not wash out, but wear out along with the wool, as if they had been woven into it from the start. And people say these tribes have sex in the open, like cattle.

204. So on the west of the sea called the Caspian, the Caucasus is the boundary, while to the east, toward the rising sun, comes a plain that stretches as far as the eye can see. The Massagetae hold a large part of this great plain, and it was against them that Cyrus was now eager to march. There were many strong reasons urging him on. The first was his birth, that is, the belief that he was something more than an ordinary mortal. The next was the good fortune he had had in his wars, for wherever Cyrus marched, no nation could escape him.

205. The ruler of the Massagetae was a woman, who had become queen after her husband's death, and her name was Tomyris. Cyrus sent to her and courted her, pretending he wanted to marry her. But Tomyris understood that it was not her he was courting but the kingdom of the Massagetae, and she turned down his advances. After this, since trickery got him nowhere, Cyrus marched to the Araxes and began an open campaign against the Massagetae. He built bridges of boats across the river for his army to cross, and put up towers on the boats that were to carry them over.

206. While he was busy with this work, Tomyris sent a herald with this message: "King of the Medes, stop pushing ahead with the work you are pushing ahead with now. You cannot know whether it will turn out to your advantage in the end. Stop, I tell you, and be king over your own people, and put up with seeing us rule the people we rule. But I know you will not want to take this advice, since you would choose anything rather than stay at peace. So if you are so very eager to try your strength against the Massagetae, come on: give up the labor of yoking the river's banks together. We will first withdraw a three days' journey from the river, and then you cross over into our land. Or if you would rather receive us in your land, do the same thing yourself." When Cyrus heard this, he called together the leading men of the Persians and, once they were gathered, put the matter before them for discussion, asking their advice on which of the two he should do. Their opinions all agreed: they told him to let Tomyris and her army come into his own country.

207. But Croesus the Lydian, who was there, found fault with this opinion and declared the opposite view. He said: "My king, I told you before that since Zeus had handed me over to you, I would do all I could to turn aside any fall I saw coming toward your house. And my sufferings, bitter as they have been, have taught me wisdom. If you think you are immortal, and that the army you command is immortal too, there is no point in my telling you what I think. But if you have realized that you are a mortal man yourself, and that the men you command are mortal too, then learn this first: human affairs turn on a wheel, and as it turns it does not let the same people be fortunate forever. So I hold an opinion on the matter before us that is the opposite of theirs. If we agree to let the enemy into our land, this is the danger for you. If you are beaten, you will lose your whole empire besides, because it is clear that if the Massagetae win, they will not turn back and run home; they will march on the provinces of your empire. On the other hand, if you win, your victory will not be as complete as if you had beaten the Massagetae after crossing into their land and then chased them as they fled. For I will set the same argument against the other side and say that once you have won, you will march straight on the kingdom of Tomyris. And besides all that, it would be a disgrace, not to be borne, for Cyrus son of Cambyses to give way to a woman and retreat from her land. So my advice is that we cross over, go forward from the crossing as far as they fall back, and then try to get the better of them like this. The Massagetae, I am told, have no experience of the good things Persians enjoy, and they have never had any great luxuries. So slaughter cattle without stinting, cook the meat, and lay out a feast for these men in our camp. Provide bowls of unmixed wine without stinting too, and food of every kind. When you have done that, leave behind the weakest part of your army and let the rest start to fall back from the camp toward the river. For unless I am mistaken, when they see all those good things they will fall on the feast, and after that it will be up to us to show what we can do."

208. Those were the two opposing opinions. Cyrus dropped the first one and chose Croesus' plan. He told Tomyris to withdraw, since he meant to cross over to her. She withdrew, as she had promised at first. But Cyrus put Croesus in the hands of his son Cambyses, to whom he meant to leave the kingdom, and strictly charged him to honor Croesus and treat him well if the crossing against the Massagetae should go badly. After giving these orders and sending the two of them away to Persia, he crossed the river himself with his army.

209. When he had crossed the Araxes and night had fallen, he had a dream as he slept in the land of the Massagetae. In his sleep Cyrus seemed to see the eldest of the sons of Hystaspes with wings on his shoulders, and with one of them he overshadowed Asia and with the other Europe. Now Hystaspes son of Arsames was a man of the Achaemenid clan, and his eldest son was Darius, who was then, I suppose, a young man of about twenty. He had been left behind in Persia, since he was not yet old enough to go to war. So when Cyrus woke up, he thought the dream over, and since it seemed to him to mean something important, he called Hystaspes, took him aside alone, and said: "Hystaspes, your son has been caught plotting against me and my throne. I will tell you how I know this for certain. The gods watch over me and show me in advance every danger that threatens me. Last night as I slept I saw the eldest of your sons with wings on his shoulders, and with one of them he overshadowed Asia and with the other Europe. Going by this dream, there is no way he is not plotting against me. So go back to Persia by the quickest road, and make sure that when I return there after conquering these lands, you bring your son before me to be questioned."

210. Cyrus said this because he thought Darius was plotting against him. But in fact the divine powers were showing him in advance that he was fated to die there and that his kingdom was going to pass to Darius. Hystaspes answered him like this: "My king, heaven forbid there should be any Persian born who would plot against you, and if there is one, I pray he dies as quickly as possible. For you made the Persians free men instead of slaves, and rulers of all nations instead of subjects of others. And if a dream tells you that my son is planning a rebellion against you, I hand him over to you to do with as you please."

211. With that answer Hystaspes crossed back over the Araxes and set off for Persia to keep watch over his son Darius for Cyrus. Cyrus, meanwhile, went forward a day's march from the Araxes, as Croesus had suggested. After this, Cyrus and the best part of the Persian army marched back to the Araxes, leaving behind the men unfit to fight. Then a third of the army of the Massagetae attacked and began killing the men Cyrus had left behind, who put up some resistance. When they had overcome their enemies and saw the feast laid out, they lay down and ate, and when they were stuffed with food and wine they fell asleep. Then the Persians came down on them. They killed many, and took even more prisoner than they killed. Among the prisoners was the son of Queen Tomyris, who was leading the army of the Massagetae; his name was Spargapises.

212. When she heard what had happened to her army and to her son, she sent a herald to Cyrus with this message: "Cyrus, you who can never get enough of blood, do not be puffed up by what has happened. It was the fruit of the vine that did it, the wine you fill yourselves with until you go mad, so that as the wine goes down into your bodies, ugly words come floating up. It was by setting a trap, I tell you, with a drug like that, that you beat my son, and not by courage in battle. So now take the advice I give you, and it is good advice. Give me back my son and leave this land unpunished, having triumphed over a third of the army of the Massagetae. If you will not, I swear to you by the Sun, the lord of the Massagetae, that I will give you your fill of blood, greedy for it as you are."

213. When this message was brought to Cyrus, he paid no attention to it. As for Spargapises, the son of Queen Tomyris, when the wine wore off and he understood how bad his situation was, he begged Cyrus to have his chains taken off, and Cyrus granted it. But as soon as he was free and had the use of his hands, he killed himself.

214. That was how he died. Tomyris, since Cyrus would not listen to her, gathered all her forces and fought a battle with him. Of all the battles ever fought between non-Greeks, I judge this one to have been the fiercest, and I am told it went like this. First, it is said, they stood at a distance and shot arrows at each other. Then, when their arrows were all used up, they charged and fought hand to hand with spears and daggers. They went on fighting like this for a long time, and neither side would run. But in the end the Massagetae won. Most of the Persian army was destroyed on the spot, and Cyrus himself died there, after reigning twenty-nine years in all. Then Tomyris filled a skin bag with human blood and had the Persian dead searched for Cyrus' body. When she found it, she pushed his head down into the skin, and as she defiled the corpse she said: "I am alive and I have beaten you in battle, and yet you destroyed me by taking my son through trickery. But I will give you your fill of blood, as I threatened." Many stories are told about the end of Cyrus' life, but this one I have told seems to me the most believable.

215. As for the Massagetae, they dress like the Scythians and live like them too. Some of them are horsemen and some do not ride (they follow both ways), and there are archers among them as well as spearmen. It is their custom to carry battle-axes. For everything they use either gold or bronze. For spear points, arrowheads and battle-axes they use bronze, but they decorate their headdresses, belts and the straps they wear around their armpits with gold. In the same way, they put bronze breastplates on their horses' chests, but use gold on their bridles, bits and cheek-pieces. They use no iron or silver at all, because there is none in their country, but they have gold and bronze in plenty.

216. These are their customs. Each man marries a wife, but they share their wives in common. The Greeks say the Scythians do this, but in fact it is not the Scythians who do it but the Massagetae: whenever a man of the Massagetae wants a woman, he hangs his quiver up in front of her wagon and has sex with her freely. They set no fixed limit to a man's life. But when a man grows very old, his closest relatives all come together and kill him as a sacrifice, along with some cattle, and then they boil the meat and feast on it. They consider this the happiest end. A man who has died of disease they do not eat; they bury him in the ground and count it a misfortune that he did not live to be sacrificed. They sow no crops but live on cattle and fish, which they get in plenty from the river Araxes, and they are milk drinkers. The only god they worship is the Sun, and they sacrifice horses to him. The idea behind the sacrifice is this: to the swiftest of the gods they give the swiftest of all mortal things.
