Chapter 23: Anacharsis and Scyles, Darius Marches on Scythia, and the Amazons

76. This nation, too, is strongly against adopting foreign customs. They reject even those of other tribes among themselves, but above all those of the Greeks, as the story of Anacharsis, and later of Scyles, proved. First, Anacharsis. He had traveled to many lands and shown great wisdom in them, and he was on his way back to the Scythians' country. As he sailed through the Hellespont he put in at Cyzicus, and there he found the people of Cyzicus celebrating a festival in honor of the Mother of the gods with great splendor. So Anacharsis made a vow to the Mother: if he got home to his own land safe and sound, he would sacrifice to her with the same rites he saw the people of Cyzicus using, and he would also hold an all-night festival for her. When he reached Scythia, he went down into the region called Hylaea. (It lies alongside the Racecourse of Achilles, and it happens to be full of trees of every kind.) Into this woodland Anacharsis went down, and there he performed the whole ceremony of the goddess' festival, with a drum, and with little images hung all over himself. But one of the Scythians saw him doing it and reported it to Saulius the king. The king came himself, and when he saw Anacharsis doing this, he shot him with an arrow and killed him. And so today, if you ask about Anacharsis, the Scythians say they do not know him, and the reason is that he went abroad to Greece and took up foreign customs. As I heard from Tymnes, the steward of Ariapeithes, Anacharsis was the uncle, on the father's side, of Idanthyrsus king of the Scythians, and he was the son of Gnurus, the son of Lycus, the son of Spargapeithes. If Anacharsis really was of this family, then let it be known that he died at the hand of his own brother. For Idanthyrsus was the son of Saulius, and Saulius was the man who killed Anacharsis.

77. I have heard another story too, told by the Peloponnesians. They say that Anacharsis was sent out by the king of the Scythians and became a student of Greece. When he came back, they say, he told the king who had sent him that all the Greeks were busy with every kind of cleverness except the Spartans, but that the Spartans alone knew how to talk sensibly with one another. But this story is a groundless invention of the Greeks themselves. Whatever the truth of it, the man was killed in the way I described above.

78. That is how badly Anacharsis fared because of foreign customs and his dealings with Greeks. And many years later Scyles the son of Ariapeithes met almost the same fate. Ariapeithes king of the Scythians had other sons, and Scyles was one of them. He was born of a woman from Istria, a Greek city on the coast, and certainly not a native of Scythia, and his mother taught him the Greek language and Greek letters. Some time later Ariapeithes was killed by treachery at the hands of Spargapeithes, king of the Agathyrsi, and Scyles succeeded to the throne. He took over not only the kingdom but also his father's wife, whose name was Opoea. This Opoea was a native Scythian, and she had borne Ariapeithes a son named Oricus. Now when Scyles was king of the Scythians, he was not at all satisfied with the Scythian way of life. Because of the way he had been brought up, he leaned much more toward Greek ways, and this is what he used to do. Whenever he came with his Scythian troops to the city of the Borysthenites (these Borysthenites say they are colonists from Miletus), he would leave his band in the outskirts, go inside the walls himself, and have the gates shut. Then he would take off his Scythian gear and put on Greek clothes. Dressed like that, he would walk about the marketplace with no bodyguard and nobody else with him. Meanwhile people kept watch on the gates, so that no Scythian would see him in this dress. He lived like a Greek in other ways too, and he worshiped the gods according to Greek custom. After staying a month or more, he would put on his Scythian clothes again and leave. He did this many times. He even built himself a house in Borysthenes and took a local woman there as his wife.

79. But since it was fated that things would go badly for him, it came about in this way. He conceived a desire to be initiated into the rites of Dionysus as Bacchus, and just as he was about to be initiated, a very great portent happened. He had a house in the city of the Borysthenites, the one I mentioned a little earlier, very large and built at great expense, with sphinxes and griffins of white stone set all around it. Zeus struck this house with a thunderbolt, and it burned to the ground. All the same, Scyles went through with his initiation. Now the Scythians hold these Bacchic rites against the Greeks. They say it is not right to make up a god like this, who drives people mad. So when Scyles had been initiated into the rites of Bacchus, one of the Borysthenites went off to the Scythians and said: "You laugh at us, Scythians, because we perform the Bacchic rites and the god takes hold of us. Well, now this god has taken hold of your own king too. He is joining in the Bacchic rites, and the god is driving him mad. If you don't believe me, follow me and I will show you." The leading men of the Scythians followed him, and the Borysthenite brought them secretly into the town and placed them on a tower. So when Scyles went by with his band of revelers and the Scythians saw him taking part in the Bacchic rites, they were deeply upset. They went out and told the whole army what they had seen.

80. After this, when Scyles rode back home, the Scythians rose up against him and set up his brother Octamasades as their leader. Octamasades was the son of the daughter of Teres. When Scyles saw what was being done against him, and why, he fled to Thrace for refuge. Octamasades heard of it and marched against Thrace. When he reached the Danube, the Thracians met him. They were about to join battle when Sitalces sent a messenger to Octamasades with this message: "Why should we test each other in a fight? You are my sister's son, and you have my brother in your hands. Give him back to me, and I will hand over your brother Scyles to you. Let neither of us put our armies at risk, neither you nor I." That was the offer Sitalces sent him by a herald, because a brother of Sitalces was with Octamasades, having fled into exile out of fear of him. Octamasades agreed. He handed over his own uncle, his mother's brother, to Sitalces, and received his brother Scyles in exchange. Sitalces took his brother and led him away a prisoner, but Octamasades cut off Scyles' head right there on the spot. That is how carefully the Scythians guard their own customs, and those are the penalties they inflict on anyone who takes up foreign ones besides their own.

81. How many Scythians there are I could not find out exactly. I heard different reports of their number: some say they are very many, others that they are few, at least as far as the true Scythians go. But this much they showed me with my own eyes. Between the rivers Dnieper and Bug there is a place called Exampaeus, which I mentioned a little earlier when I said there was a spring of bitter water in it, whose water flows out and makes the Bug undrinkable. In this place stands a bronze bowl at least six times the size of the mixing bowl at the mouth of the Black Sea, the one dedicated by Pausanias the son of Cleombrotus. For anyone who has never seen that one, I will make it clear this way: the bowl in Scythia easily holds six hundred amphoras, about 6,000 gallons, and this Scythian bowl is six finger-widths thick, about four and a half inches. The people of the place told me it had been made from arrowheads. Their king, they said, whose name was Ariantas, wanted to know how many Scythians there were. So he ordered every Scythian to bring him one arrowhead, each from his own arrow, and he threatened death to anyone who did not bring one. A huge pile of arrowheads was brought in, and he decided to make a memorial out of them and leave it behind him. So, they said, he made this bronze bowl out of them and dedicated it here at Exampaeus.

82. That is what I heard about the number of the Scythians. This land has no marvels, except that its rivers are by far the largest and the most numerous of any land. But there is one thing worth mentioning that it has to show, which is worth wondering at besides the rivers and the vastness of the plain. They point out a footprint of Heracles in the rock by the bank of the river Dniester. It is shaped like the print of a man's foot, but it is three feet long. So much for that. Now I will go back to the story I set out to tell at the beginning.

83. Darius was preparing to march against the Scythians. He was sending messengers out with orders: some peoples were to supply a land army, others ships, and others were to bridge the Thracian Bosporus. Meanwhile Artabanus son of Hystaspes, Darius' brother, begged him not to make the expedition against the Scythians at all, telling him how hard the Scythians were to deal with. But though his advice was good, he did not persuade him, and he stopped urging it. When all his preparations were made, Darius began to march his army out of Susa.

84. Then one of the Persians, Oeobazus, who had three sons all serving in the expedition, asked Darius to leave one of them behind for him. Darius answered that since he was a friend and his request was a reasonable one, he would leave all of them behind. Oeobazus was overjoyed, thinking his sons had been released from service. But Darius ordered the officers in charge of such things to put all the sons of Oeobazus to death.

85. So they were left behind, killed on the spot where they stood. Meanwhile Darius set out from Susa and reached the place on the Bosporus, in the territory of Chalcedon, where the bridge of boats had been built. There he went aboard a ship and sailed to the so-called Cyanean Rocks, which the Greeks say once moved back and forth. He sat down at the temple there and gazed out at the Black Sea, which is well worth seeing. Of all seas it is the most marvelous by nature. It is about 1,230 miles long, and about 370 miles wide at its widest point. The mouth of this great sea is only about half a mile across. The length of the mouth, that is, of the neck of water called the Bosporus, where, as I said, the bridge of boats had been built, is no less than about thirteen miles. The Bosporus runs into the Propontis, the Sea of Marmara. The Propontis is about fifty-five miles wide and about 155 miles long, and it empties into the Hellespont, which is less than a mile wide at its narrowest point, though it is about forty-four miles long. The Hellespont runs out into the open stretch of sea called the Aegean.

86. I worked out these measurements as follows. On average a ship covers about 78 miles in a long day and about 67 miles in a night. Now we know that from the mouth of the sea to the river Phasis (which is where the Black Sea is longest) is a voyage of nine days and eight nights. That comes to about 1,230 miles. Then from the land of the Sindians to Themiscyra on the river Thermodon (which is where the Black Sea is widest) is a voyage of three days and two nights, which comes to about 370 miles. That is how I have measured this sea, the Bosporus and the Hellespont, and they are as I have described them. The Black Sea also has a lake that empties into it, not much smaller than the sea itself. It is called Maeotis, and also the "Mother of the Black Sea."

87. After gazing at the Black Sea, Darius sailed back to the bridge, whose chief builder was Mandrocles of Samos. He gazed at the Bosporus too, and then he set up two pillars of white stone beside it, carved with writing, one in Assyrian letters and the other in Greek. They listed all the nations he was leading with him, and he was leading every nation he ruled. Not counting the fleet, the whole army was reckoned at 700,000 men, cavalry included, and six hundred ships had been gathered. Some time after the events I am describing, the people of Byzantium carried these pillars off to their city and used them for the altar of Artemis Orthosia, except for one stone covered with Assyrian writing, which was left standing beside the temple of Dionysus in Byzantium. The place on the Bosporus where Darius built his bridge is, by my reckoning, halfway between Byzantium and the temple at the mouth of the Black Sea.

88. After this Darius, pleased with his floating bridge, rewarded its chief builder, Mandrocles of Samos, with gifts tenfold. From these gifts Mandrocles paid for a painting as an offering, showing the whole scene: the bridge over the Bosporus, King Darius sitting in a seat of honor, and his army crossing. He had this painted and dedicated it in the temple of Hera, with this inscription:

	He bridged the Bosporus and its waters thick with fish, and to Hera
	Mandrocles gives this as a record of his work.
	He set a crown on his own head and won the Samians glory,
	and he did everything for Darius just as he wished.

89. That was the memorial left by the man who built the bridge. And Darius, after rewarding Mandrocles with gifts, crossed over into Europe. Before crossing, he ordered the Ionians to sail into the Black Sea as far as the Danube, and when they reached the Danube to wait for him there, building a bridge over the river in the meantime. For the leading part of his fleet was made up of Ionians, Aeolians and men of the Hellespont. So the fleet sailed through between the Cyanean Rocks and headed straight for the Danube. They sailed up the river two days' voyage from the sea and began to build a bridge across its neck, so to speak, where the mouths of the Danube branch off. Darius, meanwhile, crossed the Bosporus on the floating bridge and marched through Thrace. When he came to the sources of the river Tearus, he camped there for three days.

90. The people who live near the Tearus say it is the best of all rivers for healing in general, and especially for curing skin diseases in both men and horses. It has thirty-eight springs, all flowing from the same rock, some of them cold and some warm. The way to them is the same length from the city of Heraeum near Perinthus and from Apollonia on the Black Sea: two days' journey by either road. This Tearus runs into the river Contadesdus, the Contadesdus into the Agrianes, and the Agrianes into the Hebrus, which flows into the sea by the city of Aenus.

91. So Darius came to this river and camped there. He was so pleased with it that he set up a pillar there too, with this inscription: "The headwaters of the river Tearus give the best and finest water of all rivers. To them came, leading an army against the Scythians, the best and finest of all men, Darius son of Hystaspes, king of the Persians and of the whole continent." Those were the words written there.

92. Darius set out from there and came to another river, called the Artescus, which flows through the land of the Odrysians. When he reached this river he did this: he marked out a spot for his army and ordered every man to put one stone on that spot as he went past it. When the army had done this, he marched it on, leaving great piles of stones behind.

93. But before he reached the Danube, the first people he conquered were the Getae, who believe they are immortal. The Thracians who hold Salmydessus and live inland from the cities of Apollonia and Mesambria, the ones called the Cyrmianae and the Nipsaei, surrendered to Darius without a fight. But the Getae, who are the bravest and most law-abiding of all the Thracians, stubbornly resisted, and they were conquered at once.

94. This is their belief about immortality. They hold that they do not die, but that anyone who dies goes to Salmoxis, a divine being, whom some of them call Gebeleizis. Every four years they choose one of their own by lot and send him as a messenger to Salmoxis, with instructions about whatever they want to ask for at the time. This is how they send him. Some of them, appointed for the job, stand holding three javelins. Others take the man who is being sent to Salmoxis by his hands and feet, swing him up, and throw him into the air so that he falls on the spear points. If he is pierced through and dies, they think the god is favorable to them. If he does not die, they blame the messenger himself, saying he is a worthless man, and after blaming him they send another. They give him his instructions beforehand, while he is still alive. These same Thracians also shoot arrows up at the sky when there is thunder and lightning, threatening the god, since they do not believe there is any god but their own.

95. I hear from the Greeks who live around the Hellespont and the Black Sea that this Salmoxis was a man, and that he was a slave on Samos; in fact he was the slave of Pythagoras the son of Mnesarchus. Then, they say, he was freed and made a great deal of money, and afterward he went back to his own country. The Thracians lived a hard life and were rather simple-minded. But this Salmoxis knew the Ionian way of life and manners more refined than the Thracians were used to, since he had spent time among Greeks, and not just any Greek but Pythagoras, by no means the least of the Greek philosophers. So he built a banquet hall, where he entertained and feasted the leading men of his people, and taught them as he did so that neither he nor his guests nor their descendants after them would ever die. Instead, he said, they would go to a place where they would live forever and have every good thing. While he was doing what I have described and saying these things, he was also building himself a chamber underground. When the chamber was finished, he vanished from among the Thracians, went down into it, and lived there for three years. The Thracians missed him and mourned for him as if he were dead. Then in the fourth year he appeared to them again, and that is how they came to believe what Salmoxis had told them.

96. That is what they say he did. As for this business of the underground chamber, I neither disbelieve it nor believe it very strongly. But I think this Salmoxis lived many years before Pythagoras. In any case, whether there was ever a man called Salmoxis or whether he is simply a native god of the Getae, let us say goodbye to him now.

97. These Getae, with the customs I have described, were conquered by the Persians and marched on with the rest of the army. When Darius and his land army reached the Danube and everyone had crossed, Darius ordered the Ionians to break up the floating bridge and to follow him overland, together with the rest of the troops from the ships. The Ionians were about to break it up and do as he ordered when Coes son of Erxander, the commander of the men of Mytilene, spoke to Darius, after first asking whether he would be willing to hear an opinion from someone who wanted to give one. "My king," he said, "you are about to march into a land where you will see no farmland and no inhabited town. So let this bridge stand where it is, and leave the men who built it to guard it. Then if we find the Scythians and things go the way we want, we have a way back. And even if we cannot find them, at least our way back is safe. I have never yet been afraid that the Scythians would beat us in battle. What I fear is that we may not be able to find them, and may come to some disaster wandering around. Perhaps someone will say I am saying this for my own good, so that I can stay behind. But in truth, my king, I am offering you the opinion I think best for you, and I myself will go with you and will not be left behind." Darius was very pleased with this advice and answered him: "My friend from Lesbos, when I have come home safely to my palace, be sure to come and see me, so that I can repay your good advice with good deeds."

98. When he had said this, he tied sixty knots in a leather strap, called the Ionian tyrants to a meeting and said: "Men of Ionia, I have given up the opinion I gave you earlier about the bridge. Take this strap and do as I tell you. From the moment you see me set out against the Scythians, begin untying one knot each day. If I am not back within that time, and you find that the days counted by the knots have run out, sail home to your own lands. Until then, since we have changed our plan, guard the floating bridge, and do everything you can to keep it safe and protect it. If you do this, you will be doing me a very great service." That is what Darius said, and he hurried on his march.

99. Now Thrace lies out in front of Scythia toward the sea. Where a bay is formed in this land, Scythia begins, and the Danube flows out into the sea there, its mouth facing southeast. Starting from the Danube, I will now describe the coastline of Scythia proper and give its measurements. Right at the Danube begins the old land of Scythia, which faces south toward midday and the south wind and stretches as far as the city called Carcinitis. Beyond this, the land along the coast of the same sea, a mountainous country that juts out into the Black Sea, is held by the Tauric people as far as the peninsula called the Rugged Chersonese, and this reaches to the sea that lies to the east. For two sides of Scythia's borders run along the sea, one along the sea to the south and the other along the sea to the east, just as with Attica. In fact the Tauri hold a part of Scythia much like a part of Attica. It is as if, in Attica, some other people and not the Athenians held the hill country of Sunium, if you imagine that it juts farther out into the sea at the point: the region, that is, cut off by a line from Thoricus to Anaphlystus. That, if I may compare small things with great, is the shape of the Tauric land. But for anyone who has not sailed along that part of the coast of Attica, I will make it clear with another comparison. It is as if, in Iapygia, some other people and not the Iapygians had cut off and held the tip of the land bounded by a line from the harbor of Brentesium, Brindisi, to Taras. In naming these two similar cases, I am suggesting many others that the Tauric land resembles too.

100. Right after the Tauric land come Scythians again. They hold the country above the Tauri and the coast of the eastern sea, that is, the land west of the Cimmerian Bosporus and of Lake Maeotis, as far as the river Don, which runs into the corner of this lake. On its inland side, as we know, Scythia is bounded first by the Agathyrsi, starting from the Danube, then by the Neuri, then by the Androphagi, and last by the Melanchlaeni.

101. So Scythia can be thought of as a square, with two of its sides along the sea, and its boundaries are the same length in each direction, the one running inland and the one running along the sea. From the Danube to the Dnieper is ten days' journey, and from the Dnieper to Lake Maeotis is ten days more. And the distance inland to the Melanchlaeni, who live beyond the Scythians, is a journey of twenty days. I have reckoned a day's journey at about twenty-two miles. By this reckoning the sides of Scythia that run across would be about 440 miles long, and the sides that run inland would be the same. That is the size of this land.

102. The Scythians, meanwhile, reasoned that they could not drive off Darius' army on their own in a pitched battle, so they sent messengers to their neighbors. The kings of these nations had already met and were holding a council together, since so great an army was marching against them. The kings who had come together were those of the Tauri, the Agathyrsi, the Neuri, the Androphagi, the Melanchlaeni, the Gelonians, the Budini and the Sauromatae.

103. Of these, the Tauri have the following customs. They sacrifice to the Maiden both shipwrecked sailors and any Greeks they can capture by putting out to sea against them. This is how they sacrifice them. After the opening rites over the victim, they strike him on the head with a club. Some say they then push the body off the top of the cliff (for the temple stands on a cliff) and set the head up on a stake. Others agree about the heads but say that the body is not pushed off the cliff but buried in the ground. The Tauri themselves say that this goddess they sacrifice to is Iphigenia the daughter of Agamemnon. With enemies they have defeated, they do this: each man cuts off a head and carries it home, then sticks it on a long pole and sets it up high above his house, usually above the chimney. These heads hung up there, they say, are guards that protect the whole household. This people lives by plunder and war.

104. The Agathyrsi live in great luxury and wear gold ornaments most of the time. They also have their women in common, so that they may all be brothers, and since they are all close kin, they feel no envy or ill will toward one another. In their other customs they have come to resemble the Thracians.

105. The Neuri follow Scythian customs. One generation before Darius' expedition, it happened that they were forced to leave their land entirely because of snakes. Their own land produced huge numbers of snakes, and even more came down on them from the desert country beyond their borders, until at last, hard pressed, they left their land and settled among the Budini. These people may be sorcerers. For the Scythians, and the Greeks who live in Scythia, say that once every year each of the Neuri turns into a wolf for a few days and then turns back into his own shape. For my part, I do not believe them when they say this, but they say it all the same, and they swear to it too.

106. The Androphagi have the most savage ways of any people on earth. They recognize no justice and keep no law. They are nomads and dress like the Scythians, but they have a language of their own, and alone of all these nations they eat human flesh.

107. The Melanchlaeni all wear black clothes, which is where their name, the Black Cloaks, comes from. They follow Scythian customs.

108. The Budini are a great and numerous people, and they all have very blue eyes and fair skin. In their land there is a city built of wood, called Gelonus. Each side of its wall is about three and a third miles long, and the wall is high and all of wood. Their houses are wooden too, and so are their temples. For there are temples of Greek gods there, furnished in the Greek way with sacred images, altars and shrines, all of wood. Every other year they hold a festival for Dionysus and celebrate his Bacchic rites. That is because the Gelonians were originally Greeks. They moved away from the trading posts on the coast and settled among the Budini, and their language is partly Scythian and partly Greek. But the Budini do not speak the same language as the Gelonians, and their way of life is different.

109. The Budini are native to the land and nomads, and alone of the peoples in this region they eat pine cones. The Gelonians farm the land, eat grain and have gardens, and are nothing like the Budini in looks or in coloring. The Greeks, however, call the Budini Gelonians too, which is not right. Their whole land is thickly covered with forests of every kind of tree. In the thickest forest there is a large, deep lake, surrounded by marshland and reeds. Otters are caught in it, and beavers, and other wild animals with square faces. Their fur is sewn as a trim around the edges of the people's leather coats, and their testicles are used as a remedy for diseases of the womb.

110. About the Sauromatae, this story is told. When the Greeks fought against the Amazons, the warrior women of legend (the Scythians call the Amazons Oeorpata, which in Greek means "man-killers," for "oior" is their word for "man" and "pata" means "to kill"), then, as the story goes, the Greeks won the battle at the river Thermodon and sailed away, carrying off in three ships as many Amazons as they had been able to take prisoner. Out at sea the Amazons attacked the men and threw them overboard. But they knew nothing about ships, or how to use a rudder or sails or oars. So after throwing the men overboard they were driven along by wind and wave, and they came to the place on Lake Maeotis where Cremni stands. Cremni is in the land of the free Scythians. There the Amazons got off their ships and made their way inland. The first thing they came across was a herd of horses grazing. They seized them, and mounted on these horses they raided the Scythians' property.

111. The Scythians could not make sense of it. They did not know the raiders' language or their dress or what people they belonged to, and they wondered where they had come from. They took them for men, all of about the same young age they appeared to be, and in the end they fought a battle with them. After the battle the Scythians took possession of the bodies of the dead, and that is how they found out they were women. So they held a council and decided not to go on trying to kill them at all, but to send out their youngest men against them, guessing at their number so as to send just as many men as there were women. These young men were told to camp near the women and to do whatever they did. If the women came after them, they were not to fight but to fall back, and when the women stopped, they were to come close and camp near them again. The Scythians adopted this plan because they wanted to have children by them.

112. So the young men were sent out and did as they had been told. When the Amazons saw that they had not come to do them any harm, they left them alone. Every day the two camps moved closer together. The young men, like the Amazons, had nothing but their weapons and their horses, and they made their living, just as the Amazons did, by hunting and raiding.

113. At midday the Amazons used to scatter, one at a time or two together, going off some distance from each other to relieve themselves. The Scythians noticed this and did the same. One of the Scythians came up to one of these Amazons who was off on her own, and she did not push him away but let him have sex with her. She could not speak to him, since they did not understand each other's language. But she made signs with her hand for him to come the next day to the same place and bring another man with him, signaling that there should be two of them and that she would bring another woman with her. When the young man got back, he told the others. The next day he came to the place himself and brought another man with him, and he found the Amazon waiting with another woman. When the rest of the young men heard about this, they won over the rest of the Amazons for themselves in the same way.

114. After this they joined their camps and lived together, each man keeping as his wife the woman he had first been with. The men could not learn the women's language, but the women picked up the men's. When they understood each other, the men said this to the Amazons: "We have parents and we have property. So let us not live this kind of life any longer. Let us go back to the main body of our people and live with them. We will have you as our wives, and no others." But the women answered: "We could not live with your women, because our customs and theirs are not the same. We shoot with bows, throw javelins and ride horses, but we never learned women's work. Your women do none of the things we have mentioned. They stay in the wagons and do women's work, and never go out hunting or anywhere else. So we could not get along with them. But if you want to keep us as your wives and be thought honorable men, go to your parents and get your share of their property, and then let us go off and live by ourselves."

115. The young men agreed and did this. When they had got the share of property that was theirs and come back to the Amazons, the women said to them: "We are full of fear and dread at the thought of living in this place. We have separated you from your fathers, and we have done great damage to your land. Since you think it right to have us as your wives, do this with us: come, let us leave this land, cross the river Don and live there."

116. The young men agreed to this too. They crossed the Don and traveled east, toward the rising sun, for three days' journey from the Don, and north, toward the north wind, for three days' journey from Lake Maeotis. When they reached the place where they live now, they settled there. And ever since, the women of the Sauromatae have kept their old way of life. They go out hunting on horseback, both with the men and without them; they go to war; and they wear the same clothes as the men.

117. The Sauromatae speak the Scythian language, but they have spoken it badly from the start, because the Amazons never learned it properly. Their rule about marriage is this: no girl may marry until she has killed a man of the enemy. Some of them grow old and die unmarried, because they cannot fulfill the law.
