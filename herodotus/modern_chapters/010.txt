Chapter 10: The Upside-Down Customs of Egypt, and Its Gods

35. I am going to talk about Egypt at length, though, because it has more wonders than any other country, and more works to show than any land, works too great for words. That is why I will say more about it.

The Egyptians have a climate unlike any other, and a river that behaves differently from every other river. To match them, they have set up manners and customs that are the opposite of other people's in almost everything. Among them the women go to market and do the trading, while the men stay at home and weave. Other people weave by pushing the weft upward; the Egyptians push it downward. The men carry loads on their heads, the women on their shoulders. The women urinate standing up, the men squatting down. They relieve themselves indoors and eat outside in the streets, and they give this reason for it: things that are shameful but necessary should be done in private, and things that are not shameful should be done in public. No woman serves as priest of any god or goddess; men are the priests of all of them, male and female alike. Sons are not obliged in any way to support their parents if they don't want to, but daughters are forced to, however unwilling they may be.

36. In other lands the priests of the gods wear their hair long; in Egypt they shave their heads. Among other people the custom in mourning is for those closest to the dead to cut their hair short; but the Egyptians, when someone dies, let the hair grow long, both on the head and on the chin, after being clean-shaven before. Other people keep their daily living separate from their animals; the Egyptians live together with theirs. Other people live on wheat and barley, but for any Egyptian to live on these is a great disgrace; they make their bread from a grain called olyra, which some call spelt. They knead dough with their feet and clay with their hands, and they pick up dung with their hands too. Other men leave their genitals as nature made them, except those who have learned otherwise from the Egyptians; the Egyptians practice circumcision. As for clothing, each man wears two garments, each woman only one. Other people fasten the rings and ropes of the sails on the outside of the ship; the Egyptians fasten them on the inside. Finally, in writing letters and in counting with pebbles, the Greeks move the hand from left to right, but the Egyptians move it from right to left; and while they do this, they say that it is they who go the right way and the Greeks who go the wrong way. They use two kinds of writing, one called sacred and the other common.

37. They are religious to excess, more than any other people, and here are some of their customs. They drink from bronze cups and scour them out every day; not just some of them do this, but all. They wear linen clothes, always freshly washed, and they make a special point of this. They circumcise themselves for the sake of cleanliness, preferring to be clean rather than good-looking. The priests shave their whole bodies every other day, so that no louse or any other unclean thing may get on them while they serve the gods. The priests wear only linen clothes and sandals made of papyrus; they are not allowed to wear any other clothing or any other sandals. They wash in cold water twice a day and twice again every night, and they perform other religious rites that are practically countless. They also enjoy quite a few good things. They do not use up or spend anything of their own: sacred bread is baked for them, each of them gets a large supply of beef and goose meat every day, and they are given wine made from grapes as well. But they are not allowed to taste fish. The Egyptians do not sow beans in their land at all, and any beans that do grow there they will neither eat raw nor cook to eat. In fact the priests cannot even stand to look at them, believing beans to be an unclean kind of vegetable. Each god has not just one priest but many, and one of them is the high priest; and whenever a priest dies, his son takes his place.

38. They believe that bulls belong to Epaphus, and because of him they test them in this way. If the priest sees even a single black hair on the animal, he judges it not clean for sacrifice. One priest, appointed for the purpose, examines all this, both with the animal standing up and with it lying on its back. He pulls out its tongue as well, to see whether it is clean as far as the prescribed marks go, which I will describe in another part of my history. He also looks at the hairs of the tail to see whether they grow in the natural way. If the animal is clean in all these respects, he marks it with a strip of papyrus wound around the horns. Then he smears sealing clay on it and presses his signet ring into it, and after that they lead the animal away. Anyone who sacrifices an animal that has not been sealed is punished by death.

39. That is how the animal is tested, and this is how they sacrifice it. They lead the sealed animal to the altar where they are going to sacrifice, and light a fire. Then they pour wine over the altar so that it runs down onto the victim, call on the god, and cut its throat; and after cutting its throat they cut off its head. They skin the body. Over the head they first pronounce many curses, and then, if they have a market with Greek traders living among them, they carry it to the marketplace and sell it; if they have no Greeks among them, they throw it into the river. The curse they pronounce over the heads is a prayer that if any evil is about to fall on the people offering the sacrifice, or on Egypt as a whole, it may fall on this head instead. Now, as regards the heads of sacrificed animals and the pouring of wine over them, all Egyptians follow the same customs for all their sacrifices alike, and because of this custom no Egyptian will eat the head of this or of any other kind of animal.

40. But the way of taking out the victims' entrails and burning them differs from one sacrifice to another. I will describe the sacrifice to the goddess they consider the greatest of all, in whose honor they hold their greatest festival. When they have skinned the bull and pronounced the curse, they take out the whole of its lower entrails but leave the upper entrails and the fat inside the body. They cut off the legs, the end of the loin, the shoulders and the neck. When this is done, they stuff the rest of the carcass with consecrated loaves, honey, raisins, figs, frankincense, myrrh and every other kind of spice. Once it is filled, they burn it as an offering, pouring a great quantity of oil over it. They fast before they sacrifice, and while the offerings are burning they all beat their breasts in mourning. When they have finished beating themselves, they set out what is left of the sacrifice as a feast.

41. So all the Egyptians sacrifice clean bulls, both full-grown animals and calves. They are not allowed to sacrifice cows, which are sacred to Isis. The image of Isis is of a woman with cow's horns, just as the Greeks picture Io, and all Egyptians alike revere cows far more than any other kind of livestock. For this reason no Egyptian, man or woman, would kiss a Greek man on the mouth, or use a Greek's knife, or his roasting spits, or his cooking pot, or taste the meat even of a clean animal if it has been cut with a Greek knife. Cattle that die are buried this way. The cows they throw into the river. The bulls each town buries in its own outskirts, with one horn, or sometimes both, sticking up out of the ground to mark the spot. When the bodies have rotted and the appointed time comes round, a boat arrives at each city from the island called Prosopitis. This island is in the Delta, and it is about sixty miles around. On the island of Prosopitis there are many cities, and one of them is the city the boats come from to collect the bones of the cattle. Its name is Atarbechis, and it has a holy temple of Aphrodite. Many people go out from this city in all directions, some to one city and some to another. They dig up the bones of the cattle, carry them off, and all bury them together in a single place. They bury their other livestock, when it dies, in the same way as the cattle, because the same law covers those animals too; and they do not kill them either.

42. Now everyone who has a temple of Theban Zeus or who belongs to the district of Thebes sacrifices goats and refuses to sacrifice sheep. This is because not all Egyptians worship the same gods equally. The only exceptions are Isis and Osiris, who they say is Dionysus: these two they all worship alike. But those who have a temple of Mendes, or who belong to the Mendesian district, refuse to sacrifice goats and sacrifice sheep. The Thebans, and those who follow their example in keeping away from sheep, say the custom was established among them for this reason. Heracles, they say, very much wanted to see Zeus, and Zeus did not want to be seen by him. In the end, when Heracles kept begging, Zeus thought of a trick. He skinned a ram, cut off its head and held it in front of his face, put on the fleece, and showed himself to Heracles that way. That is why the Egyptians make their image of Zeus with a ram's face. The Ammonians do the same, following their example; they are settlers from both Egypt and Ethiopia, and they speak a language that is a mixture of the two. In my opinion the Ammonians also took their name from this god, because the Egyptians call Zeus Amun. So the Thebans do not sacrifice rams but hold them sacred, for this reason. On one day a year, however, at the festival of Zeus, they cut up and skin a single ram in the same way, and dress the image of Zeus in its fleece, and then they bring another image, of Heracles, up to it. When this is done, everyone in the temple beats his breast in mourning for the ram, and then they bury it in a sacred tomb.

43. About Heracles, I heard it said that he was one of the twelve gods. But of the other Heracles, the one the Greeks know, I could not hear anything anywhere in Egypt. There is plenty of evidence that the Egyptians did not take the name of Heracles from the Greeks, but that the Greeks took it from the Egyptians (I mean those Greeks who gave the name Heracles to the son of Amphitryon). The chief proof is this: the parents of this Heracles, Amphitryon and Alcmene, were both of Egyptian descent. Then too, the Egyptians say they do not know the names of Poseidon or of the Dioscuri, Castor and Polydeuces, the twin sons of Tyndareus, and these have not been accepted among their gods. Yet if they had taken the name of any god from the Greeks, these are the ones they would most naturally have remembered, assuming that in those days, as now, some Greeks made voyages and were seafaring people. I believe they were, and my judgment forces me to think so. So the Egyptians would have learned the names of these gods even sooner than the name of Heracles. In fact, though, Heracles is a very ancient Egyptian god. As they say themselves, it is 17,000 years from the time when the twelve gods, of whom they count Heracles as one, were born of the eight gods, down to the beginning of the reign of Amasis.

44. I wanted to learn something certain about these matters if I could, so I also made a voyage to Tyre in Phoenicia, since I heard there was a holy temple of Heracles there. I saw that it was richly furnished with many offerings, and in particular there were two pillars in it, one of pure gold and the other of emerald, so big that it shone at night. I got into conversation with the priests of the god and asked them how long it had been since their temple was built. I found that they too disagreed with the Greeks. They said that the temple of the god was built at the same time Tyre was founded, and that it had been 2,300 years since their people first settled at Tyre. I also saw at Tyre another temple of Heracles, the one called the Thasian. I went to Thasos as well, and there I found a temple of Heracles built by the Phoenicians, who had sailed out in search of Europa and had settled Thasos. And all this happened a good five generations before Heracles the son of Amphitryon was born in Greece. So my inquiries clearly show that Heracles is an ancient god. I think the Greeks who have built two temples of Heracles act most correctly: to one Heracles they sacrifice as an immortal god, under the title Olympian, and to the other they make offerings for the dead, as to a hero.

45. The Greeks tell many stories without thinking them through, and one especially silly one is the tale they tell about Heracles. They say that when he came to Egypt, the Egyptians put garlands on him and led him out in procession to sacrifice him to Zeus. For a while he kept quiet; but when they were starting to sacrifice him at the altar, he turned to his strength and killed them all. For my part I think that when the Greeks tell this story they know nothing at all about the nature and customs of the Egyptians. How could people who are not allowed to sacrifice even animals, except pigs, bulls and bull calves (the clean ones) and geese, how could they sacrifice human beings? Besides, how could Heracles, being only one person, and a man at that (as they claim), possibly kill tens of thousands? Having said this much on the subject, I pray that both gods and heroes may look kindly on what I have said.

46. Now the reason why the Egyptians I mentioned do not sacrifice goats, female or male, is this. The Mendesians count Pan as one of the eight gods, and they say these eight gods came into being before the twelve. Their painters and sculptors paint and carve the figure of Pan just as the Greeks do, with a goat's face and legs. They do not think he really looks like that; they think he is like the other gods. But why they represent him in this form I would rather not say. The Mendesians revere all goats, the males more than the females, and the goatherds too are held in greater honor than other herdsmen. One goat in particular is revered, and when he dies there is great mourning throughout the whole Mendesian district. In the Egyptian language both the goat and Pan are called Mendes. And in my own lifetime a wonder happened in that district: a he-goat had sex with a woman in public, and this was done so that everyone could see it.

47. The Egyptians regard the pig as an unclean animal. To begin with, if one of them brushes against a pig in passing, he goes to the river and plunges in at once, clothes and all. Then too, swineherds, even though they are native Egyptians, are the only people who may not enter any temple in Egypt. Nobody is willing to give his daughter in marriage to one of them or to take a wife from among them, so the swineherds marry among themselves, giving their daughters to each other and taking each other's. Now the Egyptians do not think it right to sacrifice pigs to any of the other gods; but to the Moon and to Dionysus alone they sacrifice pigs, at the same time, on the same full moon, and then they eat the meat. The Egyptians tell a story about why they hate pigs at all their other festivals but sacrifice them at this one. I know the story, but it is not a proper one for me to tell. The sacrifice of pigs to the Moon is done like this. When the priest has killed the victim, he puts together the tip of the tail, the spleen and the caul, the fatty membrane around the gut, and covers them with all the fat from around the animal's belly, and then he burns them as an offering. The rest of the meat they eat on the day of the full moon on which they made the sacrifice; on any later day they will not taste it. The poor among them, because they have so little, shape pigs out of dough, bake them, and offer these as their sacrifice.

48. For Dionysus, on the eve of the festival, each man kills a pig by cutting its throat in front of his own door, and then gives the pig back to the swineherd who sold it to him, for him to take away again. The rest of the festival of Dionysus the Egyptians celebrate in almost every way as the Greeks do, except for the choral dances. But instead of the phallus, the carved penis the Greeks carry in procession, they have invented something else: figures about eighteen inches high, worked by strings, which women carry around the villages. The figure's penis is made to move, and it is not much smaller than the rest of the body. A flute player goes in front, and the women follow, singing the praises of Dionysus. As for why the figure has a penis larger than nature and why it moves that one part of the body and no other, there is a sacred story told about it.

49. Now I think that Melampus son of Amytheon was not ignorant of this sacrifice but knew it well. For Melampus is the one who first introduced to the Greeks the name of Dionysus, the way of sacrificing to him and the procession of the phallus. Strictly speaking, he did not reveal the whole of it when he made it known; the wise men who came after him explained it more fully. But it was Melampus who taught about the phallus that is carried in procession for Dionysus, and it is from him that the Greeks learned to do what they do. So I say that Melampus, a clever man, worked out an art of prophecy for himself, and having learned many things from Egypt, he taught them to the Greeks, among them the rites of Dionysus, changing a few details. I will not say that what is done in the god's worship in Egypt and what is done among the Greeks just happened to turn out the same by accident: if so, the rites would be in keeping with Greek worship and not a recent import. Nor will I say that the Egyptians took this or any other custom from the Greeks. But I think it most likely that Melampus learned about Dionysus from Cadmus of Tyre and from those who came with him from Phoenicia to the land we now call Boeotia.

50. In fact, the names of almost all the gods came to Greece from Egypt. My inquiries tell me it is true that they came from non-Greeks, and I think it most likely they came from Egypt. For apart from Poseidon and the Dioscuri, as I said before, and Hera, Hestia (goddess of the hearth), Themis, the Charites (the Graces) and the Nereids (the sea nymphs), the Egyptians have always had the names of all the other gods in their country. What I say here is what the Egyptians themselves believe. As for the gods whose names they claim not to know, I think these were named by the Pelasgians, except for Poseidon. The Greeks learned about this god from the Libyans, because no people except the Libyans have had the name of Poseidon from the beginning and have always honored him. The Egyptians, I may add, have no custom of worshiping heroes at all.

51. So the Greeks adopted these customs, and others I will mention later, from the Egyptians. But making the statues of Hermes with the phallus, as they do, is something they learned not from the Egyptians but from the Pelasgians. The Athenians were the first Greeks to take up the custom, and the rest learned it from them. For it was just when the Athenians were starting to be counted as Greeks that the Pelasgians came to live with them in their land, and that is exactly why they began to be counted as Greeks. Anyone who has been initiated into the mysteries of the Cabeiri, which the Samothracians perform and which they received from the Pelasgians, knows what I mean. For these same Pelasgians who came to live with the Athenians had lived in Samothrace before that, and it was from them that the Samothracians received their mysteries. So the Athenians were the first Greeks to make statues of Hermes with the phallus, having learned it from the Pelasgians. The Pelasgians told a sacred story about it, which is revealed in the mysteries at Samothrace.

52. In the old days the Pelasgians used to make all their sacrifices with prayers to the gods, as I know from what I heard at Dodona, but they gave none of them a title or a name, because they had not yet heard any. They called them "theoi," gods, from the idea that they had "set" (thentes) all things in order and so held the sharing out of everything. Then, after a long time had passed, they learned the names of the gods from Egypt, all except Dionysus, whose name they learned much later. Some time after that, the Pelasgians consulted the oracle at Dodona about the names. This oracle is considered the most ancient of all the oracles among the Greeks, and at that time it was the only one. So when the Pelasgians asked the oracle at Dodona whether they should adopt the names that had come from foreigners, the oracle told them to use them. From then on they sacrificed using the names of the gods, and later the Greeks received them from the Pelasgians.

53. But where each of the gods came from, or whether they had all always existed, and what they looked like, the Greeks did not know until the other day, so to speak, or the day before. For I believe Hesiod and Homer lived four hundred years before my time and no more, and they are the ones who composed the Greeks' account of the gods' births, gave the gods their titles, divided up their honors and their skills, and described their forms. The poets who are said to have lived before these two were really, in my opinion, later than they. The first part of what I have said comes from the priestesses at Dodona; the last part, about Hesiod and Homer, is my own.

54. About the oracles, both the one in Greece and the one in Libya, the Egyptians tell this story. The priests of Theban Zeus told me that two women who served in the temple had been carried off from Thebes by Phoenicians. They had heard, they said, that one of the women was sold into Libya and the other to the Greeks, and that these were the women who first founded oracles among those peoples. I asked them how they knew this story so exactly, and they answered that the priests had made a great search for these women and had not been able to find them, but that afterward they had heard this story about them, the one they were telling me.

55. That is what I heard from the priests at Thebes. What follows is what the prophetesses of Dodona say. They say that two black doves flew from Thebes in Egypt, and one of them came to Libya and the other to their own land. This one settled on an oak tree and spoke in a human voice, saying that an oracle of Zeus must be founded there. The people believed that the message came from the gods, and so they founded one. The dove that went to the Libyans, they say, told the Libyans to found an oracle of Ammon, and this too is an oracle of Zeus. The priestesses of Dodona told me these things. The eldest of them was named Promeneia, the next Timarete, and the youngest Nicandra; and the other people of Dodona who served at the temple told the same story.

56. But my own opinion about it is this. If the Phoenicians really did carry off the consecrated women and sell one into Libya and the other into Greece, then I suppose that this second woman was sold, in the country now called Greece but formerly called Pelasgia, into the land of the Thesprotians. Then, while she was a slave there, she set up a shrine of Zeus under a real oak tree. That would be natural: having served in the sanctuary of Zeus at Thebes, she would remember him in the place she had come to. After this, once she understood the Greek language, she founded an oracle. And she reported, I suppose, that her sister had been sold into Libya by the same Phoenicians who had sold her.

57. I also think the people of Dodona called the women doves because they were foreigners, and because they seemed to them to make sounds like birds. After a while, they say, the dove spoke in a human voice: that is, when the woman began to speak so that they could understand her. As long as she spoke a foreign language, she seemed to them to be making sounds like a bird. For if it really had been a dove, how could it speak with a human voice? And in saying that the dove was black, they show that the woman was Egyptian. As it happens, the ways of giving oracles at Thebes in Egypt and at Dodona are very much alike. The art of prophecy from sacrificed victims came from Egypt too.

58. It is also true that the Egyptians were the first people to hold religious festivals, processions and solemn approaches to the temples, and that the Greeks learned these from them. My evidence for this is that the Egyptian festivals have been held since very ancient times, while the Greek ones were introduced only recently.

59. The Egyptians hold their festivals not once a year but many times. The chief one, held with the greatest zeal and devotion, is at the city of Bubastis, for Artemis. The second is at Busiris, for Isis. In this city there is a very great temple of Isis, and the city stands in the middle of the Delta of Egypt; Isis, in the Greek language, is Demeter. Third, they hold a festival at the city of Sais for Athena; fourth, at Heliopolis for the Sun, Helios; fifth, at the city of Buto in honor of Leto; and sixth, at the city of Papremis for Ares.

60. Now, when they are going to the city of Bubastis, this is what they do. Men and women sail together, a great crowd of both in every boat. Some of the women have rattles and shake them, some of the men play the flute the whole way, and the rest, women and men, sing and clap their hands. As they sail, whenever they come opposite some city along the way, they bring the boat close to shore. Some of the women keep on doing what I have described; others shout and jeer at the women of that city; some dance; and some stand up and pull up their skirts. They do this at every city along the riverbank. When they reach Bubastis, they hold a festival with great sacrifices, and more grape wine is drunk at this festival than in all the rest of the year. The people who gather here every year, so the locals say, number as many as 700,000 men and women, not counting children.

61. That is how it is done there. How they celebrate the festival of Isis at the city of Busiris I have already told. As I said, after the sacrifice they all beat their breasts in mourning, men and women together, many tens of thousands of people. But for whom they beat themselves, religion does not allow me to say. All the Carians who live in Egypt do this even more than the Egyptians themselves, since they also cut their foreheads with knives; and this shows that they are foreigners and not Egyptians.

62. When they gather at the city of Sais for their sacrifices, on a certain night they all light a great many lamps in the open air around their houses. The lamps are saucers filled with salt and oil mixed together, with the wick floating on top, and they burn all night long. The festival is called Lychnocaia, the Lighting of the Lamps. And those Egyptians who have not come to this gathering keep the night of the festival too, and they all light lamps as well, so that the lamps are lit not only in Sais but all over Egypt. As for the reason why this night is given light and honor, there is a sacred story told about that.

63. At Heliopolis and Buto they gather every year and only sacrifice. But at Papremis they sacrifice and worship as they do elsewhere, and besides that, when the sun is starting to go down, a few of the priests busy themselves with the image of the god, while most of them stand at the entrance of the temple holding wooden clubs. Opposite them stand more than a thousand other men, who are there to fulfill vows, and these all carry wooden staves too, and stand in a body. The image is in a small wooden shrine covered with gold, and on the day before, they carry it out to another sacred building. The few priests who were left with the image pull a four-wheeled cart carrying the shrine and the image inside it. The other priests, standing in the gateway, try to stop it from coming in, and the men under vows come to the god's help and strike them, while the priests defend themselves. Then there is a hard fight with staves, and they break each other's heads. I think many of them actually die of their wounds, though the Egyptians told me that nobody died. The local people say that they established this festival for the following reason. The mother of Ares, they say, used to live in this temple. Ares had been raised away from her, and when he grew up he came there wanting to visit his mother. But the attendants of his mother's temple, who had never seen him before, would not let him in and kept him out. So he brought men from another city to help him, roughed up the temple attendants, and went in to see his mother. That, they say, is how this exchange of blows became the custom at Ares' festival.

64. The Egyptians were the first to make it a religious rule not to have sex with women in temples, and not to enter a temple after being with a woman without bathing first. Almost all other people except the Egyptians and the Greeks have sex in temples and go into a temple after being with a woman without bathing, because they think there is no difference in this between people and animals. They say they see animals and all kinds of birds mating in the temples and in the sacred precincts of the gods; and if this did not please the god, the animals would not do it.
