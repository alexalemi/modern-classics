Chapter 4: Croesus on the Pyre, the Customs of Lydia, and the Rise of the Medes

86. So the Persians took Sardis and captured Croesus himself. He had reigned fourteen years and had been under siege fourteen days, and he had fulfilled the oracle: he had brought his own great empire to an end. The Persians who had taken him brought him before Cyrus. Cyrus had a great pyre built and made Croesus climb up onto it, bound in chains, and with him twice seven sons of Lydians. Perhaps he meant to offer them to some god as the first fruits of his victory, or perhaps he wanted to fulfill a vow. Or perhaps he had heard that Croesus was a man who feared the gods, and made him climb onto the pyre because he wanted to find out whether any of the divine powers would save him from being burned alive. That, they say, is what Cyrus did. But as Croesus stood on the pyre, in all his misery, he remembered the saying of Solon: how Solon had said, as if inspired by heaven, that no one who is still alive may be called happy. When this thought came to him, they say, he sighed deeply and groaned aloud after a long silence, and three times he spoke the name "Solon." Cyrus heard this and told the interpreters to ask Croesus who this person was that he was calling on, and they came near and asked him. For a while, it is said, Croesus kept silent when they asked. But at last, when they pressed him, he said: "A man I would have given a great fortune to have every ruler talk with." Since this answer was unclear, they asked again what he meant. They kept pressing and gave him no peace, so he told them how Solon, an Athenian, had once come to him, looked over all his wealth, and made light of it, saying such and such. He told them how everything had turned out for him just as Solon had said, though Solon had not been speaking about Croesus in particular so much as about the whole human race, and especially about those who think themselves happy. While Croesus was telling them this, the pyre had already been lit, and its outer edges were burning. Then, they say, Cyrus heard from the interpreters what Croesus had said and changed his mind. He reflected that he too was only a man, and that he was handing another man over to the fire alive, a man who had once been no less fortunate than himself. Besides, he feared retribution, and he reflected that nothing human beings possess is secure. So, they say, he ordered his men to put out the fire as quickly as they could, and to bring Croesus and the others down from the pyre. But for all their efforts, they could no longer get the flames under control.

87. Then, as the Lydians tell it, when Croesus learned that Cyrus had changed his mind, and saw that everyone was trying to put out the fire but could no longer hold it back, he cried out to Apollo. If any gift of his had ever pleased the god, he begged, let the god come now and rescue him from the evil that was upon him. So he called on the god in tears, and suddenly, they say, out of a clear, calm sky clouds gathered and a storm broke. Rain came down in a violent torrent, and the pyre was put out. Then Cyrus, seeing that Croesus was a good man and dear to the gods, had him brought down from the pyre and asked him: "Croesus, who on earth persuaded you to march against my land and become my enemy instead of my friend?" Croesus said: "My king, I did it, to your good fortune and to my own misfortune. And the one to blame for it was the god of the Greeks, who urged me to march with my army. No one is so foolish as to choose war over peace: in peace sons bury their fathers, but in war fathers bury their sons. But I suppose it pleased the gods that things should turn out this way."

88. That is what he said. Cyrus had his chains taken off, sat him down beside himself, and treated him with great respect. Cyrus himself and everyone around him looked at Croesus with wonder. Croesus sat lost in thought and said nothing. But after a while he turned and saw the Persians plundering the Lydians' city, and he said: "My king, should I tell you what is on my mind, or should I keep quiet, in my present condition?" Cyrus told him to speak freely and say whatever he liked. So Croesus asked him: "What is this great crowd of men doing so eagerly?" Cyrus said: "They are plundering your city and carrying off your wealth." Croesus answered: "It is not my city they are plundering, and not my wealth they are carrying off. None of these things belongs to me anymore. It is your wealth they are carrying and driving away."

89. What Croesus said troubled Cyrus. He sent everyone else away and asked Croesus what he saw in all this that Cyrus ought to act on. Croesus said: "Since the gods have given me to you as your slave, I think it right, if I see anything that others do not, to point it out to you. The Persians are proud and unruly by nature, and they are poor. So if you let them carry off great wealth as plunder and keep it, here is what you can expect: whoever gets hold of the biggest share will rise up in rebellion against you. So, if what I say pleases you, do this. Post spearmen of your guard at every gate. Have them take the goods away from the men carrying them out of the city, and tell them that a tenth of it has to be set aside for Zeus first. That way they will not hate you for taking the things from them by force, and they will let them go willingly, admitting to themselves that you are doing what is just."

90. Cyrus was delighted beyond measure when he heard this, because he thought Croesus' advice was good. He praised him warmly and ordered the spearmen of his guard to do what Croesus had advised. Then he said to Croesus: "Croesus, since you are ready, like the king you are, to do good deeds and speak good words, ask me for a gift, anything you want, and you shall have it at once." Croesus said: "Master, you will please me most if you let me send these chains to the god of the Greeks, whom I honored more than any other god, and ask him whether it is his custom to deceive those who treat him well." Cyrus asked him what charge he had against the god, to make such a request. So Croesus went back over the whole story for him: what he had intended, the answers of the oracles, and above all his offerings, and how the prophecy had urged him to march against the Persians. And as he told it, he came back again to his request that he be allowed to put this reproach to the god. Cyrus laughed and said: "You shall have this from me, Croesus, and anything else you ever ask me for." When he heard this, Croesus sent some Lydians to Delphi. He told them to lay the chains on the threshold of the temple and ask the god whether he was not ashamed of having urged Croesus with his prophecies to march against the Persians, persuading him that he would put an end to Cyrus' empire. And they were to show the chains and say that these were the first fruits of the spoils Croesus had won from it. This was what they were to ask, and also whether it was the custom of the gods of the Greeks to be ungrateful.

91. When the Lydians arrived and said what they had been told to say, the Pythia, it is said, answered them like this: "Not even a god can escape what is fated. Croesus has paid for the crime of his ancestor five generations back. That man was one of the spearmen of the Heraclids; he followed a woman's treacherous plan, killed his master, and took his royal office, which was not his by right. Loxias (that is, Apollo) was eager for the disaster of Sardis to fall on Croesus' sons rather than on Croesus himself, but he could not turn the Fates aside from their course. Whatever they allowed, he accomplished, and gave to Croesus as a gift. He put off the taking of Sardis for three years, and Croesus should know that he was captured three years later than the time fated for him. Second, he came to his rescue when he was about to be burned. As for the oracle, Croesus has no good grounds for complaint. Loxias told him beforehand that if he marched against the Persians he would destroy a great empire. When he heard that, if he had wanted to plan wisely, he should have sent and asked whether the god meant his own empire or Cyrus'. But since he did not understand what was said, and did not ask again, let him admit that he himself is to blame for what followed. And when he consulted the oracle for the last time, Loxias told him what he told him about a mule, and he did not understand that either. Cyrus was that mule. He was born of parents of two different peoples, his mother of nobler birth than his father. She was a Mede, the daughter of Astyages king of the Medes; but he was a Persian, one of a people subject to the Medes, and though he was beneath her in every way, he was the husband of his own royal mistress." That was the Pythia's answer to the Lydians. They brought it back to Sardis and repeated it to Croesus, and when he heard it he admitted that the fault was his own and not the god's. So that is how it went with the empire of Croesus and the first conquest of Ionia.

92. There are many other offerings of Croesus in Greece besides the ones I have mentioned. First, at Thebes in Boeotia there is a golden tripod, which he dedicated to Ismenian Apollo. Then at Ephesus there are the golden cows and most of the columns of the temple. And in the temple of Athena Pronaea, Athena of the Forecourt, at Delphi there is a large golden shield. These were still there in my own time, but others of his offerings have been lost. And his offerings at Branchidae, the shrine of the Milesians, were, as I am told, equal in weight and similar to those at Delphi. Now the offerings he sent to Delphi and to the shrine of Amphiaraus he dedicated from his own property, as the first fruits of the wealth he inherited from his father. But the other offerings came from the fortune of a man who was his enemy. Before Croesus became king, this man had stirred up a faction against him and had joined in trying to make Pantaleon ruler of the Lydians. Pantaleon was a son of Alyattes and Croesus' brother, but not by the same mother: Croesus was born to Alyattes by a Carian woman, and Pantaleon by an Ionian. When Croesus had taken possession of the kingdom, which his father gave him, he put to death the man who had opposed him by dragging him over a carding comb, a board set with iron spikes for combing wool. And the man's property, which he had vowed even before then to dedicate, he offered in the way I have described to the shrines I have named. So much for his offerings.

93. As for wonders worth writing about, Lydia has few compared with other lands, apart from the gold dust that washes down from Mount Tmolus. But it has one work to show that is far larger than any other, except only those in Egypt and Babylon. This is the tomb of Alyattes, Croesus' father. Its base is made of great stones, and the rest of it is a mound of heaped-up earth. It was built with contributions from the tradesmen, the craftsmen, and the girls who worked there as prostitutes. Down to my own time there were still five boundary stones standing on top of the tomb, with inscriptions carved on them recording how much of the work each group had done. When it was measured, the girls' share turned out to be the largest. For in Lydia the daughters of the common people all work as prostitutes, to earn their own dowries, and they keep at it until they marry; and the girls choose their own husbands. The circuit of the tomb is about 3,800 feet, nearly three-quarters of a mile, and its width is 1,300 feet. Next to the tomb is a large lake, which the Lydians say never runs dry, and it is called the Lake of Gyges. That is what this tomb is like.

94. The Lydians have very nearly the same customs as the Greeks, except that they make their daughters prostitutes. They were the first people we know of to strike and use coins of gold and silver, and they were also the first shopkeepers. The Lydians themselves say that the games now played among them and among the Greeks were their invention too. They say these were invented at the same time as they sent out colonists to Tyrrhenia, the land of the Etruscans in Italy, and this is the story they tell about them. In the reign of their king Atys son of Manes there was a severe famine all over Lydia. For a while the Lydians went on putting up with it. But when it did not stop, they looked for remedies, and different people came up with different ideas. That was when, they say, they discovered how to play dice and knucklebones and ball, and all the other games except draughts (the Lydians do not claim to have discovered that one). They invented these games as a way of dealing with the famine, and this is how they used them. On one day they would play games the whole time, so as not to feel the lack of food, and on the next they stopped playing and ate. They went on like this for eighteen years. But the hardship did not let up; it grew worse and worse. So their king divided the whole Lydian people into two halves and drew lots to decide which half would stay and which would leave the country. He put himself in charge of the half that drew the lot to stay, and his son in charge of the half that was leaving. His son's name was Tyrrhenus. So the half that drew the lot to leave went down to the sea at Smyrna. There they built ships, loaded into them all the goods they could carry, and sailed away to look for a living and a land to live in. They passed many peoples, until at last they came to the land of the Ombricans, in Italy. There they founded cities, and they live there to this day. They changed their name and stopped calling themselves Lydians; they took their name from the king's son who had led them out, and called themselves Tyrrhenians after him.

So the Lydians had been made subject to the Persians, as I have said.

95. After this, my history goes on to ask who this Cyrus was who destroyed the empire of Croesus, and how the Persians came to be the leaders of Asia. I shall follow the account of certain Persians, those who do not want to glorify the story of Cyrus but to tell what actually happened; it is their account I shall write. But I could also give the story in three other versions. The Assyrians ruled Upper Asia for five hundred and twenty years, and the first to revolt from them were the Medes. They fought the Assyrians for their freedom and proved themselves brave men; so they threw off the yoke of slavery and were set free. After them the other nations did the same as the Medes. But when all the peoples on the mainland were independent in this way, they came back under the rule of tyrants, and this is how it happened.

96. There was a clever man among the Medes named Deioces, the son of Phraortes. Deioces set his heart on becoming sole ruler, and this is what he did. The Medes lived in scattered villages. Deioces was already well thought of in his own village, and now he set himself to deal justly, much more than before and with greater zeal. He did this even though there was great lawlessness all over Media, and even though he knew that injustice is always at war with justice. The Medes of his village saw how he behaved and chose him as their judge. And since he was aiming at power, he was honest and fair, and by acting this way he won no small praise from his fellow villagers. So much so that people in the other villages heard that Deioces was the one man who judged cases rightly. Before this they had always suffered from unjust verdicts, so when they heard about him, they too gladly came to Deioces to have their disputes settled. In the end they would trust their cases to no one else.

97. More and more people kept coming to him, because they learned that his verdicts matched the truth. When Deioces saw that everything was being brought to him, he refused to sit any longer in the public place where he used to sit and hear cases, and said that he would judge no more. It did him no good, he said, to neglect his own affairs and spend all day judging his neighbors' disputes. So robbery and lawlessness spread through the villages, much worse than before, and the Medes gathered in one place and talked over among themselves the state they were in. And I suppose it was Deioces' friends who did most of the talking, saying something like this: "We can't go on living in this country the way things are. So let us set up a king from among ourselves. Then the country will be well governed, and we ourselves can get on with our work and not be ruined by lawlessness." With words like these they persuaded themselves to have a king.

98. When they then put the question of whom they should make king, Deioces was put forward and praised by everyone, until at last they agreed that he should be their king. He told them to build him a palace worthy of a king and to give him a bodyguard of spearmen for his protection. The Medes did so. They built him a large, strong palace in the part of the country he named, and they let him pick spearmen from among all the Medes. Once he had power over them, he made the Medes build a single fortified city and devote their attention to it, caring less about the other towns. The Medes obeyed him in this too, and he built large, strong walls, the ones now called Ecbatana, set in rings one inside another. The walls are designed so that each ring rises above the one outside it by the height of its battlements and no more. The lay of the land, I suppose, helps this to some extent, since the city stands on a hill; but it was done much more by design, since there are seven rings in all. Inside the innermost ring are the royal palace and the treasuries. The largest of these walls is about as long as the wall around Athens. The battlements of the first ring are white, of the second black, of the third crimson, of the fourth blue, and of the fifth red. So the battlements of all the rings are painted in different colors, and of the last two, the battlements of one are plated with silver and of the other with gold.

99. So Deioces built these walls for himself and around his own palace, and he ordered the people to live outside the wall. When everything was built, Deioces was the first to lay down these rules of royal conduct: no one was to come into the king's presence, but everyone was to deal with him through messengers; no one was to see the king; and besides, it was improper to laugh or spit in his presence, and this last applied to everyone without exception. He surrounded himself with all this ceremony so that the men of his own age, who had grown up with him and were of no lesser family and no less courage than he, would not see him and resent him and plot against him. If they never saw him, he thought, they would believe he was a different kind of being from themselves.

100. Once he had set these things in order and made himself secure in his rule as tyrant, he was strict in upholding justice. People would write down their cases and send them in to him, and he would decide the cases sent in and send them back out. That was how he handled the judging of cases. He also arranged things so that if he heard that anyone was behaving outrageously, he would send for him and punish him as each offense deserved. And he had watchers and listeners all over the land he ruled.

101. So Deioces united the Median people alone, and ruled over them. The tribes of the Medes are these: the Busae, the Paretacenians, the Struchates, the Arizantians, the Budians, and the Magi. That is how many tribes the Medes have.

102. Deioces' son was Phraortes. When Deioces died, after a reign of fifty-three years, Phraortes took the power in his turn. Once he had it, he was not content to rule the Medes alone. He marched against the Persians, attacked them before anyone else, and made them the first to be subject to the Medes. After this, ruling two nations, both of them strong, he set about conquering Asia, going from one people to the next. At last he marched against the Assyrians, the Assyrians who lived at Nineveh. They had once ruled the whole of Asia, but at that time they had been left without support, since their allies had revolted from them, though at home they were still doing well enough. Phraortes marched against these Assyrians, and he himself was killed, after reigning twenty-two years, and most of his army was destroyed.

103. When Phraortes' life had come to its end, the power passed to Cyaxares, son of Phraortes, son of Deioces. This king, they say, was much more warlike than his forefathers. He was the first to organize the men of Asia into separate units; that is, he was the first to separate the spearmen, the archers, and the horsemen from one another, since before that they had all been mixed together without any order. It was he who fought the Lydians on the day the daylight turned to night in the middle of the battle, and who brought under his rule the whole of Asia beyond the river Halys. He gathered all his subjects and marched against Nineveh, to avenge his father and also because he wanted to conquer that city. He fought a battle with the Assyrians and defeated them. But while he was besieging Nineveh, a great army of Scythians came down on him, led by Madyas son of Protohyas, king of the Scythians. They had invaded Asia after driving the Cimmerians out of Europe, and they had come into the land of Media chasing the Cimmerians as they fled.

104. From Lake Maeotis, the Sea of Azov, to the river Phasis and the land of the Colchians is a thirty-day journey for a traveler with no baggage. From Colchis it is not far to cross over into Media. There is only one people in between, the Saspeirians, and once you are past them you are in Media. But the Scythians did not make their invasion by this route. They turned off it and took the upper road, which is much longer, keeping the Caucasus Mountains on their right. Then the Medes fought the Scythians and were beaten in the battle. They lost their power, and the Scythians took control of all Asia.

105. From there the Scythians went on to invade Egypt. When they were in the part of Syria called Palestine, Psammetichus king of Egypt came to meet them, and with gifts and pleading he turned them from their purpose, so that they went no farther. As they were on their way back, they came to the city of Ascalon in Syria. Most of the Scythians passed through without doing any harm, but a few who had stayed behind plundered the temple of Aphrodite Urania, Heavenly Aphrodite. This temple, as I find by inquiry, is the oldest of all the temples of this goddess. The temple in Cyprus was founded from it, as the people of Cyprus themselves say, and the one on Cythera was founded by Phoenicians who came from this part of Syria. The Scythians who had plundered the temple at Ascalon, and their descendants forever after, were struck by the goddess with a disease that made them women instead of men. The Scythians say that this is the reason they suffer from the disease, and that this is why visitors to Scythia can see there, to this day, the condition of the men the Scythians call Enarees.

106. So for twenty-eight years the Scythians ruled Asia, and through their violence and recklessness everything was ruined. Besides demanding from each people the tribute they had laid on it, they rode around apart from the tribute and seized by force whatever each tribe had. Then Cyaxares and the Medes invited most of them to a banquet, got them drunk, and killed them. So the Medes won back their power and ruled the same peoples as before. They also took Nineveh (how it was taken I shall explain in another history), and they made the Assyrians subject to them, all except the land of Babylon.
