Chapter 50: The Battle of Plataea and Its Aftermath

58. When Mardonius learned that the Greeks had pulled out during the night, and saw their position empty, he called in Thorax of Larissa and his brothers Eurypylus and Thrasydeius. "Sons of Aleuas," he said, "what will you say now, when you see this place deserted? You live near these people, and you kept telling me that the Spartans never run from a battle, that they are the best fighters in the world. But first you saw these same men shifting from their post, and now we can all see that they ran away last night. They have shown plainly that when the time came to fight men who really are the best in the world, they were worth nothing after all. They had only been showing off their courage among Greeks, who are worthless themselves. As for you, you had no experience of the Persians, and since you praised these men because you knew some good of them, I for one was quite ready to excuse you. What amazed me far more was Artabazus. He was afraid of the Spartans, and in his fear he gave us that most cowardly advice: that we should break camp and go to the city of the Thebans, to be besieged there. The king will hear about that advice from me. But we will talk of these things another time. Right now we must not let them get away with this. We must chase them until we catch them, and make them pay us for everything they have done to the Persians in the past."

59. With these words he crossed the Asopus and led the Persians at a run on the track of the Greeks, believing that they were running away from him. He aimed his attack at the Spartans and the Tegeans alone, because the Athenians had marched off toward the plain, and the hills hid them from his sight. When the commanders of the other divisions of the king's army saw that the Persians had set off after the Greeks, they all at once raised the signals for battle and joined the chase, each as fast as he could, with no order and no ranks kept.

60. So they came on with shouting, in a disorderly crowd, expecting to make short work of the Greeks. And Pausanias, when the cavalry began its attack, sent a horseman to the Athenians with this message: "Athenians, the greatest contest of all lies before us. Its prize is the freedom or the slavery of Greece. And we have been deserted by our allies, we Spartans and you Athenians, because they ran away last night. So our course is decided: we must defend ourselves and protect one another as best we can. If the cavalry had attacked you first, we and the Tegeans, who stand with us and refuse to betray Greece, would have been bound to come to your help. But as it is, their whole force has come against us, so it is right for you to come and help the part of the army that is hardest pressed. If something has happened to you that makes it impossible to come to our help, then do us the favor of sending us your archers. We know that in this war you have been by far the most eager of all, so we know you will listen to us in this too."

61. When the Athenians heard this, they were eager to come to the rescue and give all the help they could. But they were already on their way when they were attacked by the Greeks on the king's side, who had been posted opposite them, and so they could no longer go to help the Spartans: the force attacking them gave them too much trouble. So the Spartans and the Tegeans were left alone. Counting their light-armed men, the Spartans numbered fifty thousand and the Tegeans three thousand, for the Tegeans never once separated from the Spartans. They began to sacrifice, meaning to give battle to Mardonius and the force that had come against them. But the sacrifices would not turn out favorable, and in the meantime many of them were being killed and many more wounded. For the Persians had made a barricade of their wicker shields and were shooting arrows in great numbers, without stinting. Pausanias saw that the Spartans were hard pressed and that the sacrifices still would not come out favorable. So he turned his eyes toward the temple of Hera at Plataea and called on the goddess for help, praying that they might not be cheated of their hope.

62. While he was still calling on her, the Tegeans rose up before the others and advanced against the enemy. And right after Pausanias' prayer, the sacrifices came out favorable for the Spartans. When this at last happened, they too advanced against the Persians, and the Persians put down their bows and came to meet them. The first fighting was around the wicker shields. When these had been knocked down, there was a fierce fight beside the temple of Demeter, and it went on for a long time, until at last it came to pushing and shoving, for the Persians would grab hold of the Greeks' spears and snap them off. In courage and in strength the Persians were not inferior. But they had no armor, and besides, they were untrained and no match for their opponents in skill. They would dash out one at a time, or in bunches of about ten, some more and some fewer, and throw themselves on the Spartans and die.

63. Mardonius himself was riding a white horse, with the thousand best men of the Persians, picked out from all the rest, around him. Where he was, they pressed their opponents hardest of all. As long as Mardonius was alive, they held their ground, and in defending themselves they brought down many of the Spartans. But when Mardonius was killed, and the men posted around him, who were the strongest part of the whole army, had fallen, then the rest also turned and gave way before the Spartans. What hurt them most was their dress, with no armor: in truth they were light-armed men fighting against hoplites.

64. So Mardonius paid the Spartans the price for the killing of Leonidas, as the oracle given to the Spartans had said he would, and Pausanias son of Cleombrotus son of Anaxandrides won the most glorious victory of any we know of. I have given the names of his earlier ancestors in the list for Leonidas, since as it happens they are the same for both. Mardonius was killed by Arimnestus, a man of standing in Sparta. Later, after the Persian Wars were over, this Arimnestus fought a battle at Stenyclerus with three hundred men against the whole army of the Messenians, who were then at war with the Spartans, and both he and the three hundred were killed.

65. When the Spartans had put the Persians to flight at Plataea, the Persians fled in disorder to their own camp and to the wooden fort they had built in Theban territory. And it amazes me that, although the fighting was beside the sacred grove of Demeter, not a single Persian was found to have gone into the sanctuary or to have died inside it. Most of the dead fell around the temple, on unconsecrated ground. My own guess, if one ought to guess at all about things divine, is that the goddess herself would not let them in, because they had burned her temple, the "palace," as it is called, at Eleusis.

66. That is how far the battle had gone. Now Artabazus son of Pharnaces had been unhappy from the start that Mardonius stayed behind after the king left. Afterward he kept raising objections and getting nothing done, always urging them not to fight a battle. And since he did not like what Mardonius was doing, this is what he did on his own account. He commanded no small force: it numbered as many as forty thousand men. When the fighting began, he knew well how the battle would end, and he led his men carefully. First he gave orders that they should all go wherever he led them, and at the same pace they saw him set. With these orders given, he led his troops out as if he were taking them into battle. But when he was well on his way, he saw the Persians already in flight. Then he no longer led his men in the same order as before. He set off at a run and fled by the quickest road, not to the wooden fort, nor to the walls of Thebes, but toward Phocis, wanting to reach the Hellespont as fast as he could.

67. That was the way they were heading. Meanwhile, the other Greeks on the king's side were deliberately slack in the fight, but the Boeotians fought the Athenians for a long time. The Thebans who had gone over to the Persians were very much in earnest. They fought hard and did not hold back, so that three hundred of them, their first and best, fell there at the hands of the Athenians. When these too were routed, they fled to Thebes, not to the same place as the Persians. And the great mass of the other allies fled without having fought anyone to the end or done anything brave.

68. This is further proof to me that everything on the king's side depended on the Persians: these men ran before they had even come to grips with the enemy, simply because they saw the Persians running. So everyone was in flight except the cavalry, the Boeotian cavalry included. The cavalry did good service to the men fleeing: they kept close to the enemy the whole time and screened their own fugitives off from the Greeks.

69. The victors followed hard after Xerxes' troops, chasing them and cutting them down. While this rout was going on, word reached the other Greeks, who had taken up a position around the temple of Hera and had missed the battle, that a battle had been fought and that Pausanias' men were winning. When they heard this, they set out without forming any ranks. The Corinthians and those near them went along the foot of the mountain and through the low hills, by the road that led straight up to the temple of Demeter. The Megarians and the Phliasians and those near them went by the plain, along the smoothest road. But when the Megarians and Phliasians came near the enemy, the Theban cavalry saw them from a distance, hurrying along in no order at all, and rode out to attack them. The cavalry's commander was Asopodorus son of Timander. They fell on them, killed six hundred, and chased the rest and drove them onto Cithaeron.

70. So these men died without glory. Meanwhile the Persians and the rest of the crowd had taken refuge in the wooden fort and managed to get up onto the towers before the Spartans arrived. Once up, they strengthened the wall as best they could. Then the Spartans came up and attacked it, and a hard fight for the wall began. As long as the Athenians were not there, the defenders held them off and had much the better of the Spartans, because the Spartans did not understand how to fight against walls. But when the Athenians came up to help, the fight for the wall grew fierce and went on for a long time. At last, through courage and endurance, the Athenians climbed the wall and broke a gap in it, and the Greeks poured in through it. The Tegeans were the first inside the wall, and it was they who plundered Mardonius' tent. Besides everything else in it, they took the manger of his horse, which was solid bronze and well worth seeing. The Tegeans dedicated this manger of Mardonius in the temple of Athena Alea, but everything else they took they brought into the common pool of the Greeks. Once the wall had fallen, the enemy no longer formed up in any close body, and not one of them thought of fighting back. They were utterly helpless, as you would expect of men in a panic, with tens of thousands of them penned together in a small space. And the Greeks were able to slaughter them, so that out of an army of 300,000, leaving aside the 40,000 Artabazus took with him in his flight, not even three thousand of the rest survived. Of the Spartan citizens, the Lacedaemonians from Sparta itself, ninety-one were killed in the battle in all; of the Tegeans, sixteen; and of the Athenians, fifty-two.

71. On the king's side, the men who proved best were the Persians among the infantry and the Sacae among the cavalry, and of single men, they say, Mardonius. Among the Greeks, both the Tegeans and the Athenians proved good men, but the Spartans outdid them in courage. I have no other proof of this, since all of them beat the enemy facing them, except this: the Spartans fought against the strongest part of the enemy's force and beat it. In my opinion the man who proved by far the best was Aristodemus, the one who had come back alive from Thermopylae, the only one of the three hundred, and had been living under reproach and disgrace ever since. After him the best were Posidonius, Philocyon and Amompharetus the Spartan. However, when it came to be argued which of them had proved himself best, the Spartans who were there gave this verdict. Aristodemus, they said, had plainly wanted to be killed because of the charge hanging over him, and so he had left his place in the ranks like a man gone berserk and done great deeds. Posidonius, on the other hand, had proved a brave man without wanting to die, and in that he was the better man of the two. But perhaps they said this out of envy. All the men I have named among those who fell in this battle were given special honors, except Aristodemus. Aristodemus, because he wanted to die on account of the charge I mentioned before, was not honored.

72. These were the men who won the most fame of all who fought at Plataea. As for Callicrates, the most handsome man who came to the camp, not only among the Spartans but among all the Greeks of his time, he was not killed in the battle itself. While Pausanias was offering sacrifice, Callicrates was sitting in his place in the ranks when an arrow struck him in the side. While the others were fighting, he was carried out of the ranks, and he died a slow death. He said to Arimnestus, a man of Plataea, that he did not mind dying for Greece. What grieved him was only that he had not used his hands in the fight, and had done no deed worthy of the spirit he had in him to do great things.

73. Of the Athenians, the man who won the most glory is said to have been Sophanes son of Eutychides, of the deme of Decelea. The men of that deme once did a deed that has served them well for all time, as the Athenians themselves tell it. Long ago, the sons of Tyndareus invaded Attica with a great army to bring Helen home, and they were ravaging the demes, not knowing where she had been hidden away. Then, they say, the men of Decelea, or as some say Decelus himself, angered by the arrogance of Theseus, who had carried her off, and afraid for the whole land of the Athenians, told them the whole story and led them to Aphidnae, which Titacus, one of the men born of the soil, betrayed to the sons of Tyndareus. Because of this deed the Deceleans have always had freedom from taxes at Sparta and front seats at the festivals, privileges they still hold to this day. Indeed, in the war that broke out many years after these events between the Athenians and the Peloponnesians, when the Spartans ravaged the rest of Attica, they left Decelea unharmed.

74. That was the deme Sophanes came from, the man who proved himself the best of all the Athenians in this battle. Two different stories are told about him. One is that he carried an iron anchor, fastened by a bronze chain to the belt of his breastplate. Whenever he came up against the enemy, he would throw it down, so that, they say, when the enemy charged out of their ranks they could not move him from his place. And when the men facing him ran, his plan was to pick up the anchor first and then go after them. That is one story. But the other, which disputes the first, goes like this: he carried an anchor as the emblem on his shield, which was always on the move and never still, and not an iron one fastened to his breastplate.

75. Sophanes did another famous deed as well. When the Athenians were besieging Aegina, he challenged Eurybates the Argive, a winner of the pentathlon, to single combat and killed him. As for Sophanes himself, it happened later that while he was general of the Athenians together with Leagrus son of Glaucon, he was killed by the Edonians at Daton, after proving himself a brave man, fighting for the gold mines.

76. When the Greeks had laid the enemy low at Plataea, a woman came over to them of her own free will from the other side. She was the concubine of Pharandates son of Teaspis, a Persian. When she saw that the Persians were destroyed and the Greeks were the victors, she got down from her carriage and came up to the Spartans while they were still at the slaughter. She had decked herself and her attendants with many gold ornaments, and had put on the finest dress she owned. When she saw that Pausanias was directing everything there, she recognized him, for she knew his name and his family well, having heard them often. She took hold of his knees, as a suppliant does, and said: "King of Sparta, save me, your suppliant, from being made a slave as a captive. You have already done me a service by destroying these men, who have no respect for the heroes or for the gods. I am from Cos by birth, the daughter of Hegetorides son of Antagoras. The Persian took me by force on Cos and has kept me prisoner." He answered her: "Woman, take heart, first because you are a suppliant, and also if it turns out that you are telling the truth and are the daughter of Hegetorides of Cos, who is bound to me as a guest-friend more closely than any other man in that part of the world." With these words he put her in the care of the ephors who were present, for the time being, and later he sent her away to Aegina, where she herself wished to go.

77. Right after the woman came, the Mantineans arrived, when it was all over. When they learned that they had come too late for the battle, they were bitterly upset and said they deserved to be punished. Then they heard that the Persians with Artabazus were in flight, and they chased them as far as Thessaly, though the Spartans tried to stop them from pursuing men who were running away. When they got back to their own country, they banished the leaders of their army. After the Mantineans came the Eleans. They too were bitterly upset, like the Mantineans, and went home, and when they got back they too banished their leaders. So much for the Mantineans and the Eleans.

78. At Plataea, among the troops of Aegina, was Lampon son of Pytheas, one of the leading men of Aegina. He hurried to Pausanias with a most unholy proposal, and when he got there he said: "Son of Cleombrotus, you have done a deed of wonderful greatness and glory. Heaven has granted that by rescuing Greece you have won the greatest fame of any Greek we know of. Now finish what remains to be done, so that your fame may be greater still, and so that in the future every foreigner will think twice before starting any outrage against the Greeks. When Leonidas was killed at Thermopylae, Mardonius and Xerxes cut off his head and put it on a stake. Pay him back the same way, and you will have praise first from all the Spartans and then from the rest of the Greeks as well. For if you impale Mardonius' body, you will have taken vengeance for Leonidas, your father's brother."

79. He said this thinking it would please. But Pausanias answered him: "My friend from Aegina, I admire your goodwill and your concern for me, but your judgment has failed you all the same. You raised me up high, me and my country and my deed, and then you threw me down to nothing by advising me to mutilate a dead body and telling me that if I do it, people will think better of me. Such things are more fitting for barbarians than for Greeks, and we blame even them for it. As far as that goes, I have no wish to please the Aeginetans, or anyone else who likes such things, in any such way. It is enough for me to please the Spartans by doing nothing unholy, and saying nothing unholy either. As for Leonidas, whom you tell me to avenge, I say he has been avenged in full already. He has been honored by the countless lives taken here, he and the rest who died at Thermopylae. As for you, never come to me again with a proposal like this, and never give me such advice. And be thankful that you are leaving unpunished."

80. Lampon heard this and went away. Pausanias then had it proclaimed that no one was to lay hands on the plunder, and he ordered the helots to gather everything together. So they spread out through the camp and found tents fitted out with gold and silver, couches plated with gold and plated with silver, golden mixing bowls, and bowls and other drinking cups. They also found sacks loaded on wagons, which turned out to hold cauldrons of gold and of silver. From the bodies lying there they stripped bracelets and collars, and their daggers if they were gold; embroidered clothing they did not even bother to count. The helots stole a great deal and sold it to the Aeginetans, though they also handed over a great deal, as much as they could not hide. And this is where the great wealth of the Aeginetans first came from: they bought the gold from the helots as if it were brass.

81. Then they brought everything together and set aside a tenth for the god at Delphi, and with it they dedicated the golden tripod that stands on the three-headed bronze serpent, close beside the altar. They set aside a tenth for the god at Olympia too, and with it they dedicated a bronze statue of Zeus fifteen feet high; and for the god at the Isthmus, from which a bronze statue of Poseidon ten and a half feet high was made. When they had set these aside, they divided the rest, and each took his rightful share: the Persians' concubines, the gold and the silver and the other goods, and the pack animals too. How much was set aside and given to those who had proved themselves the best men at Plataea, no one reports, though for my part I suppose they were given gifts as well. But Pausanias had ten of everything set aside and given to him: women, horses, talents, camels, and the same of everything else.

82. They also tell this story. When Xerxes fled from Greece, he left his own tent and its furnishings to Mardonius. When Pausanias saw Mardonius' furnishings, worked with gold and silver and hung with tapestries of many colors, he ordered the bakers and the cooks to prepare a dinner just as they used to for Mardonius. They did as they were told. Then, the story goes, when Pausanias saw the couches of gold and silver with their rich coverings, and the tables of gold and silver, and the magnificent spread of the feast, he was amazed at the fine things set before him. As a joke, he ordered his own servants to prepare a Spartan dinner. When both meals were served, the difference between them was enormous. Pausanias laughed and sent for the Greek commanders. When they had gathered, he pointed to the two dinners, one after the other, and said: "Men of Greece, I called you together because I wanted to show you how foolish this leader of the Medes was. He lived like this, and he came to us, who live as poorly as you see here, to take it away from us." That, they say, is what Pausanias said to the Greek commanders.

83. Some time after these events, many of the Plataeans also found chests of gold and silver and other treasures. And later still, something else came to light among the dead here, after the flesh had fallen away from the bones, for the Plataeans gathered all the bones together in one place. A skull was found with no seams in it, all one bone. There was also a jaw, the upper jaw, whose teeth were all joined together in one bone, front teeth and back teeth alike. And the bones of a man seven and a half feet tall were seen as well.

84. Mardonius' body, however, disappeared the day after the battle. Who took it I can't say for certain. I have heard the names of many men from various cities who are said to have buried Mardonius, and I know that many were given gifts by Artontes son of Mardonius for doing it. But who it was that took up Mardonius' body and buried it I cannot find out for certain. Dionysophanes of Ephesus is reported, with some show of reason, to have been the one who buried Mardonius.

85. So Mardonius was buried in some such way. And the Greeks, when they had divided the spoils at Plataea, buried their dead, each people separately. The Spartans made three separate graves. In one they buried the younger Spartans, among them Posidonius, Amompharetus, Philocyon and Callicrates. So in one grave lay the younger men, in the second the rest of the Spartans, and in the third the helots. That was how the Spartans buried their dead. The Tegeans buried theirs all together in a place apart from these, the Athenians buried theirs together, and the Megarians and Phliasians buried those who had been killed by the cavalry. All these graves had bodies in them. But the other graves to be seen at Plataea, as I am told, are all just empty mounds of earth, heaped up by the various peoples for the sake of later generations, because they were ashamed of having missed the fight. Among others, there is one there called the grave of the Aeginetans, which I hear was raised at the Aeginetans' request by Cleades son of Autodicus, a man of Plataea who was their official guest-friend, fully ten years after these events.

86. When the Greeks had buried their dead at Plataea, they decided at once in a common council to march on Thebes and demand that the Thebans hand over the men who had gone over to the Persians, first among them Timagenides and Attaginus, who were leaders among the very foremost. If the Thebans would not hand them over, they resolved not to leave the city until they had taken it. With this decided, they arrived on the eleventh day after the battle and laid siege to the Thebans, demanding the men. When the Thebans refused, the Greeks began to ravage their land and to attack their wall.

87. They did not let up on their ravaging, and on the twentieth day Timagenides spoke to the Thebans: "Thebans, the Greeks have resolved not to give up the siege until either they take Thebes or you hand us over to them. So do not let the land of Boeotia suffer any more for our sake. If what they really want is money, and they are demanding our surrender as an excuse, let us give them money from the public treasury, since we went over to the Persians together with the whole state, not on our own. But if they are really besieging the city to get their hands on us, then we will give ourselves up to stand trial." People thought he had spoken very well and to the point, and the Thebans at once sent a herald to Pausanias offering to hand over the men.

88. They came to an agreement on these terms, but Attaginus slipped out of the city. His sons were handed over to Pausanias, but he cleared them of the charge, saying that the sons had no share in the guilt of going over to the Persians. As for the other men the Thebans handed over, they believed they would get a trial, and they were confident that they could buy their way out of danger. But Pausanias suspected exactly this, and when he got them, he first dismissed the whole allied army, and then took the men to Corinth and put them to death there. That is what happened at Plataea and at Thebes.

89. Meanwhile Artabazus son of Pharnaces, fleeing from Plataea, was by now well along on his way. When he came to the Thessalians, they offered him hospitality and asked about the rest of the army, knowing nothing of what had happened at Plataea. Artabazus knew that if he told them the whole truth about the fighting, he would run the risk of being destroyed, he and the whole army with him, for he believed that everyone would turn on him once they knew what had happened. With this in mind he had told the Phocians nothing, and now he said to the Thessalians: "Thessalians, as you see, I am pressing on by the shortest road to Thrace, and I am in a great hurry, because I have been sent from the army with these men on a certain mission. Mardonius himself and his army will be here soon, marching close behind me. Entertain him and do him good service. You will not regret it in the end." With these words he hurried his army on through Thessaly and Macedonia straight for Thrace, truly in a hurry and taking the shortest road through the country. So he came to Byzantium, having lost great numbers of his army along the way, some cut down by the Thracians, others overcome by hunger and exhaustion. From Byzantium he crossed over by ship. And that is how he made his way back to Asia.
