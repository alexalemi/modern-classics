Chapter 20: The Cloak of Syloson, and Zopyrus Takes Babylon

139. After this, King Darius took Samos, the first of all the cities he captured, Greek or non-Greek, and this is how it came about. When Cambyses son of Cyrus was marching on Egypt, a great many Greeks came to Egypt. Some, as you would expect, joined the campaign to make a profit, and some came just to see the country. Among them was Syloson son of Aeaces, the brother of Polycrates, who was then in exile from Samos. A stroke of good luck came Syloson's way, and it was this. He had put on a flame-red cloak and was walking around the marketplace in Memphis. Darius, who was then one of Cambyses' spearmen and not yet a man of any great importance, saw him and wanted the cloak. He went up to him and offered to buy it. Syloson saw how badly Darius wanted the cloak, and by some divine prompting he said: "I won't sell this for any price. But I'll give it to you for nothing, if it seems it simply has to be yours." Darius accepted and took the cloak from him.

140. Syloson assumed without a doubt that he had simply lost it through his own good nature. But in time Cambyses died, the seven Persians rose up against the Magus, and out of the seven it was Darius who won the throne. Then Syloson heard that the kingdom had gone to the very man who had once asked him for his cloak in Egypt, and to whom he had given it. So he went up to Susa, sat down at the entrance to the king's palace, and announced that he was a benefactor of Darius. The doorkeeper heard this and reported it to the king. Darius was amazed and said to him: "What Greek is my benefactor, that I should owe him gratitude? I have held the throne only a short time, and hardly a single Greek has come up to our court yet. I can almost say I owe nothing to any Greek. Still, bring him in, so that I can find out what he means by this." So the doorkeeper brought Syloson in. When he stood before the king, the interpreters asked him who he was and what he had done to call himself the king's benefactor. Syloson told the whole story of the cloak and how he was the man who had given it. Darius answered: "Most generous of men, you are the one who gave me a gift when I had no power at all. It may have been a small gift, but the kindness counts with me as much as if someone gave me something great today. So in return I will give you gold and silver in abundance, so that you will never be sorry you did a service for Darius son of Hystaspes." Syloson replied: "My king, don't give me gold or silver. Win back my homeland, Samos, and give it to me. Now that my brother Polycrates has been killed by Oroetes, it is in the hands of our slave. Give it to me without bloodshed and without selling anyone into slavery."

141. When Darius heard this, he made ready to send an expedition under the command of Otanes, who had been one of the seven, with orders to carry out everything Syloson had asked. So Otanes went down to the coast and began preparing the expedition.

142. Now Samos was being ruled by Maeandrius son of Maeandrius, who had been left in charge of the government by Polycrates. He wanted to be the most just of men, but he did not manage it. When the news of Polycrates' death reached him, this is what he did. First he set up an altar to Zeus the Liberator and marked out a sacred precinct around it, the one that is still there today in the suburb outside the city. After he had done this, he called an assembly of all the citizens and said: "As you know as well as I do, the scepter of Polycrates and all his power have been entrusted to me, and right now I could be your ruler. But what I blame in my neighbor I will not do myself, as far as I can help it. I never approved of Polycrates being master over men who were just as good as he was, and I don't approve of anyone else who does that sort of thing. Well, Polycrates has met his destiny. I now hand the power over to the people, and I proclaim equality for you all. I do think it right, though, that I should be granted a few privileges: six talents should be taken out of Polycrates' wealth and given to me as a special gift, and on top of that I choose for myself, and for my descendants after me, the priesthood of Zeus the Liberator, since I founded his temple myself, and I am giving you your freedom." Those were the offers he made to the Samians. But one of them stood up and said: "No, you're not fit to rule us anyway. You're low-born, and a nasty piece of work besides. You'd do better to think about how you're going to account for the money you've been handling."

143. The man who said this was a citizen of good standing named Telesarchus. Maeandrius realized that if he gave up the power, someone else would set himself up as tyrant in his place, so he dropped all thought of giving it up. He withdrew into the citadel and sent for the leading men one at a time, pretending he was going to give an account of the money. Instead he seized them and put them in chains. So these men were in chains. But after this Maeandrius fell sick, and his brother, whose name was Lycaretus, expected him to die. To make it easier to take power over Samos himself, he put all the prisoners to death. And all this happened, it seems, because the Samians did not choose to be free.

144. So when the Persians arrived at Samos bringing Syloson home from exile, no one lifted a hand against them. In fact Maeandrius and his party said they were ready to leave the island under a truce. Otanes agreed to these terms and made a treaty, and the highest-ranking Persians had chairs set out for them in front of the citadel and were sitting there.

145. Now the tyrant Maeandrius had a brother who was somewhat out of his mind, named Charilaus. For some offense he had committed, he had been shut up in an underground dungeon. At the time I am describing, he heard what was going on, and when he put his head out of the dungeon and saw the Persians sitting there in peace, he began to shout that he wanted to speak with Maeandrius. Maeandrius heard his voice and ordered him to be released and brought before him. As soon as Charilaus was brought in, he began to abuse and insult his brother, trying to talk him into attacking the Persians. This is what he said: "You lowest of men! You put me in chains and thought me fit for an underground dungeon, me, your own brother, who had done nothing to deserve chains. And now you see the Persians throwing you out of your country and making you homeless, and you don't dare take any revenge, even though they are so easy to beat? Well, if you really are afraid of them, give me your mercenaries, and I'll make them pay for coming here. As for you, I'm willing to let you leave the island."

146. That is what Charilaus said, and Maeandrius took him up on it. In my opinion this was not because he had sunk so far into folly as to think his own forces could beat the king's. It was rather that he begrudged Syloson getting the city from him without any trouble and with no damage done to it. So he wanted to provoke the Persians to anger and leave Samos as weak as possible before he handed it over. He was well aware that once the Persians had suffered harm, they would probably be just as bitter against the Samians as against the men who had done the harm. And he knew he had a safe way off the island whenever he wanted it, because he had had a secret tunnel dug from the citadel down to the sea. So Maeandrius himself sailed away from Samos. Charilaus armed all the mercenaries, threw open the gates, and sent them out against the Persians. The Persians were expecting nothing of the kind; they thought everything had been settled. The mercenaries fell on them and began killing the Persians of the highest rank, the ones who had been carried there in their chairs. While they were busy with this, the rest of the Persian force came to the rescue, and the mercenaries were driven back and forced to retreat into the citadel.

147. Then Otanes, the Persian commander, seeing that the Persians had suffered heavy losses, deliberately forgot the orders Darius had given him when he sent him out: not to kill a single Samian or sell any into slavery, but to hand the island over to Syloson without any harm done to it. He deliberately forgot those orders and told his army to kill everyone they caught, man or boy, without distinction. So while part of the army besieged the citadel, the rest killed everyone who crossed their path, in a sanctuary or out of one, it made no difference.

148. Meanwhile Maeandrius had escaped from Samos and was sailing to Sparta. When he got there, he had the things he had brought away with him carried up to the city, and this is what he did. He would set out his silver and gold cups, and while the servants were polishing them, he would be talking with Cleomenes son of Anaxandrides, who was then king of Sparta, and walk him along to his house. When Cleomenes saw the cups he was struck with wonder at them, and Maeandrius would tell him to take away as many as he liked. Maeandrius said this two or three times. But here Cleomenes proved himself the most honest of men. Not only did he refuse to take what was offered, but he saw that Maeandrius would give presents to other citizens and so win support for himself. So he went to the ephors and told them it would be better for Sparta if this foreigner from Samos left the Peloponnese, in case he persuaded Cleomenes himself or some other Spartan to do something base. The ephors took his advice and banished Maeandrius by public proclamation.

149. As for Samos, the Persians swept it clean of its people and handed it over to Syloson empty of men. Later, though, the commander Otanes even helped to settle people there again. He was moved to do it by a vision he had in a dream and by a disease that struck him, a disease of the genitals.

150. While the fleet had gone against Samos in this way, the Babylonians revolted, and they were extremely well prepared for it. All through the reign of the Magus and the uprising of the seven, through all that time and the confusion that came with it, they had been getting ready to stand a siege, and somehow nobody noticed what they were doing. When they revolted openly, this is what they did. First they set aside their mothers. Then each man also set aside for himself one woman of his own household, whichever one he chose. All the rest of the women they gathered together and strangled. Each man kept the one woman I mentioned to bake his bread, and they strangled the rest so that they would not eat up the food supplies.

151. When Darius heard this, he gathered all his forces and marched against them. He brought his army up to Babylon and began to besiege the city. But the Babylonians did not care in the least about the siege. They would climb up onto the battlements of the wall and jeer at Darius and his army with gestures and with words, and one of them said this: "Why are you sitting here, Persians, instead of going away? You'll take us only when mules have foals." The Babylonian who said this did not imagine that a mule would ever have a foal.

152. So when a year and seven months had gone by, Darius and his whole army with him began to grow frustrated, because they could not conquer the Babylonians. Darius had tried every trick and every possible means against them, and even so he could not take the city. Among his other schemes he had tried the very one by which Cyrus had taken it, but the Babylonians were terribly watchful, and he could not conquer them.

153. Then in the twentieth month a portent came to Zopyrus, the son of the Megabyzus who had been one of the seven men who killed the Magus. To this Zopyrus son of Megabyzus, I say, a portent happened: one of the mules that carried his supplies had a foal. When this was reported to him, he did not believe it, and he went and saw the foal for himself. Then he ordered the men who had seen it not to tell anyone what had happened, and he thought over what to do. He kept in mind the words of the Babylonian who had said at the start that the wall would be taken when mules had foals. With this omen in mind, it seemed to Zopyrus that Babylon could now be taken. For he believed that it was by the will of heaven both that the man had said those words and that his own mule had foaled.

154. Since it seemed to him that Babylon was now fated to fall, he went to Darius and asked him whether he thought conquering Babylon was a matter of very great importance. When Darius answered that he thought it of great importance indeed, Zopyrus considered again how he could be the one to take it, and how the achievement could be his own; for among the Persians good service to the king is honored very highly. He decided that the only way he could bring the city down was to mutilate himself and desert to the Babylonians. So, holding himself cheap, he mutilated his own body past all cure. He cut off his nose and his ears, shaved his hair in a disgraceful way, whipped himself, and in that state went before Darius.

155. Darius was deeply distressed to see the most distinguished man in his service mutilated like this. He leaped up from his seat, cried out, and asked who had done this to him, and for what. Zopyrus answered: "There is no man except you who has power enough to put me in this state. No stranger did this, my king. I did it to myself, because I could not bear that the Assyrians should laugh at the Persians." Darius replied: "You most reckless of men, you have put the fairest name on the foulest deed when you say you have maimed yourself beyond cure because of the people we are besieging. How, you fool, will the enemy surrender to us any faster because you have mutilated yourself? You must have gone out of your mind to ruin yourself like this." Zopyrus said: "If I had told you what I was about to do, you would not have let me do it. So I did it on my own. Now, unless you fail to do your part, we will take Babylon. I'm going straight to the wall as a deserter. I'll tell them that you did this to me. And I think that once I have convinced them, I will be given command of part of their forces. On the tenth day after I get inside the wall, take a thousand men from the troops whose loss you won't mind, and station them by the gate called the gate of Semiramis. After that, on the seventh day after the tenth, station two thousand more, please, by the gate called the gate of the Ninevites. After that seventh day, let twenty days go by, and then bring four thousand others and station them by the gate called the gate of the Chaldeans. Neither the first groups nor this one should have any weapons to defend themselves except daggers; let them have those. Then, right after the twentieth day, order the rest of the army to attack the wall all the way around, and post the Persians, please, by the gates called the gate of Belus and the gate of Cissia. For I think that once I have done great deeds for them, the Babylonians will trust me with everything else, including the keys that draw the bolts of the gates. After that, it will be up to me and the Persians to do what has to be done."

156. With these instructions given, he set off for the gate of the city, turning to look behind him as he went, as if he really were a deserter. The men posted on that part of the wall saw him from the towers and ran down. They opened one leaf of the gate a crack and asked him who he was and what he had come for. He told them he was Zopyrus and that he had come over to them as a deserter. When the gatekeepers heard this, they took him to the Babylonian assembly. Brought before it, he began to lament his misfortunes. He said that the injuries he had in fact done to himself had been done to him by Darius, and that it was because he had advised the king to withdraw his army, since there really seemed to be no way of taking the city. "And now," he went on, "I have come to you, Babylonians, for your very great good, and for the very great harm of Darius and his army and the Persians. He will certainly not get away with treating me like this. And I know every one of his plans."

157. That is what he said. The Babylonians saw the most distinguished man among the Persians with his nose and ears cut off and covered with blood from the whipping, and they were quite sure he was telling the truth and had come to help them. So they were ready to give him whatever he asked for, and what he asked for was the command of a body of troops. When he had got this from them, he did what he had agreed with Darius to do. On the tenth day he led out the Babylonian troops, surrounded the thousand men he had told Darius to station there first, and killed them. The Babylonians saw that his deeds matched his words, and they were overjoyed and ready to obey him in everything. Then, when the agreed number of days had passed, he again picked out a force of Babylonians, led them out, and killed the two thousand of Darius' troops. When they saw this deed too, all the Babylonians had the name of Zopyrus on their lips and were loud in his praise. Then again, when the agreed days had passed, he led them out to the appointed place, surrounded the four thousand, and killed them. After this too, Zopyrus was everything to the Babylonians, and they made him both commander of their army and guardian of their walls.

158. But when Darius attacked the wall all the way around, as they had agreed, Zopyrus revealed his whole trick. While the Babylonians were up on the wall defending it against the attack of Darius' army, Zopyrus opened the gates called the Cissian gate and the gate of Belus and let the Persians inside the wall. Those Babylonians who saw what had happened fled to the temple of Zeus Belus. Those who did not see it stayed each at his own post, until in the end they too found out that they had been betrayed.

159. So Babylon was taken for the second time. When Darius had conquered the Babylonians, he first tore down the wall around their city and pulled down all the gates. When Cyrus took Babylon before him, he had done neither of these things. Next, Darius impaled the leading men, about three thousand of them. But he gave the city back to the rest of the Babylonians to live in. And to make sure the Babylonians would have wives, so that their people would go on having children, Darius did the following. (As I said at the beginning of this account, the Babylonians had strangled their own wives to make their food supplies last.) He ordered the peoples who lived around Babylon to send women there, setting a fixed number for each people, so that in all 50,000 women were brought together. It is from these women that the Babylonians of today are descended.

160. As for Zopyrus, in Darius' judgment no Persian ever surpassed him in good service, either among those who came after or among those who had gone before, except Cyrus alone; for no Persian has ever yet dared to compare himself with Cyrus. And Darius is said to have declared many times that he would rather have Zopyrus unharmed than gain twenty more Babylons on top of the one he had. He also gave him great honors. Every year he gave him the gifts the Persians count most honorable, and he granted him Babylon to rule free of tribute for as long as he lived, and he added many other gifts besides. This Zopyrus had a son, Megabyzus, who was made commander in Egypt against the Athenians and their allies; and this Megabyzus had a son, Zopyrus, who deserted from the Persians and went over to Athens.
