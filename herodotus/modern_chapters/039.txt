Chapter 39: The Great Review at Doriscus, and Xerxes Questions Demaratus

57. When they had all crossed over and set out on their way, a great portent appeared to them. Xerxes took no notice of it, although its meaning was easy to guess: a mare gave birth to a hare. The meaning was easy to guess in this way: Xerxes was about to march an army against Greece in great pride and splendor, but he would come back again to the place he started from, running for his life. There had also been a portent of another kind while he was still at Sardis. A mule gave birth, and the foal was a mule with sex organs of both kinds, male and female, with the male organs above. But Xerxes took no notice of either portent. He went on his way, and the land army with him.

58. Meanwhile the fleet was sailing out of the Hellespont and along the coast, heading in the opposite direction from the land army. The fleet was sailing west, making for the headland of Sarpedon, where it had been ordered in advance to go and wait for the army. The land army, meanwhile, was marching east, toward the sunrise, through the Chersonese. It kept the tomb of Helle the daughter of Athamas on its right and the city of Cardia on its left, and it marched straight through the middle of a town called Agora. From there it curved around the gulf called Melas and crossed the river Melas, which gives the gulf its name. That river's water was not enough for the army this time, and it ran dry. After crossing it, the army went on westward, past Aenus, a city of the Aeolians, and past the lake of Stentoris, until at last it came to Doriscus.

59. Doriscus is a beach and a very large plain in Thrace, and the great river Hebrus flows through it. A royal fortress had been built there, the one that is now called Doriscus, and Darius had kept a garrison of Persians in it ever since the time he marched against the Scythians. So it seemed to Xerxes that this was a convenient place to put his army in order and count it, and that is what he did. When the commanders of the fleet arrived at Doriscus, on Xerxes' orders they brought all their ships to the beach next to Doriscus. On that stretch of coast stand Sale, a city of the Samothracians, and Zone, and at its far end is the well-known headland of Serrheum; in ancient times the region belonged to the Ciconians. They brought their ships in to this beach, hauled them up on land, and let them dry out. During this time Xerxes was counting the army at Doriscus.

60. Now I cannot give exact figures for the number each separate nation supplied, because no one reports it. But the whole land army taken together came to 1,700,000 men. This is how they counted them. They gathered 10,000 men together in one place, packed them in as tightly as they could, and drew a circle around them on the outside. Once they had drawn the circle, they let the 10,000 men go, and built a wall of rough stones around the line of the circle, up to the height of a man's navel. When this was done, they made other men go into the walled space, and then others, until in this way they had counted them all. After they had counted them, they arranged them by nations.

61. Those who served were as follows.

The Persians, with this equipment. On their heads they wore soft felt caps called tiaras. On their bodies they wore colorful tunics with sleeves, covered with iron scales that looked like the scales of a fish, and on their legs they wore trousers. Instead of ordinary shields they carried shields of wickerwork, with their quivers hanging underneath. They had short spears, large bows, arrows of reed, and daggers hanging from their belts beside the right thigh. Their commander was Otanes, the father of Xerxes' wife Amestris. In ancient times the Greeks called these Persians Cephenes, but they called themselves Artaeans, and so did their neighbors. Then Perseus, the son of Danae and Zeus, came to Cepheus the son of Belus and married his daughter Andromeda. A son was born to them, and Perseus named him Perses. He left this son behind there, since Cepheus happened to have no male children, and the people took their name from him.

62. The Medes served in the army with exactly the same equipment, for this equipment is in fact Median, not Persian. Their commander was Tigranes, an Achaemenid. In ancient times everyone called them Arians, but when Medea the Colchian came from Athens to these Arians, they changed their name too. That is what the Medes themselves say about themselves. The Cissians served with equipment like the Persians' in other respects, but instead of felt caps they wore headbands. The commander of the Cissians was Anaphes the son of Otanes. The Hyrcanians were armed like the Persians. Their leader was Megapanus, the same man who later became governor of Babylon.

63. The Assyrians served wearing helmets of bronze on their heads, or else helmets woven in a foreign style that is hard to describe. They had shields and spears, daggers like the Egyptian knives, wooden clubs studded with iron, and linen breastplates. The Greeks call these people Syrians, but the non-Greeks have always called them Assyrians. Among them were the Chaldeans. Their commander was Otaspes the son of Artachaees.

64. The Bactrians served wearing on their heads almost the same headgear as the Medes, with native bows of reed and short spears. The Sacan Scythians wore tall caps on their heads, stiff and upright and coming to a point. They wore trousers and carried native bows and daggers, and also battle-axes of the kind called sagaris. These were called Amyrgian Sacae, though in fact they were Scythians, for the Persians call all Scythians Sacae. The commander of the Bactrians and Sacae was Hystaspes, the son of Darius and of Cyrus' daughter Atossa.

65. The Indians wore clothes made of tree-wool, that is, cotton. They had bows of reed and arrows of reed tipped with iron. That was how the Indians were equipped. They served under Pharnazathres the son of Artabates.

66. The Arians were equipped with Median bows, and otherwise like the Bactrians. The commander of the Arians was Sisamnes the son of Hydarnes. The Parthians, the Chorasmians, the Sogdians, the Gandarians and the Dadicans served with the same equipment as the Bactrians. Their commanders were Artabazus the son of Pharnaces, over the Parthians and Chorasmians; Azanes the son of Artaeus, over the Sogdians; and Artyphius the son of Artabanus, over the Gandarians and Dadicans.

67. The Caspians served wearing coats of skin, with native bows of reed and short swords. That was how they were equipped, and their leader was Ariomardus, the brother of Artyphius. The Sarangians stood out from the rest because they wore dyed clothes. They had boots that came up to the knee, and Median bows and spears. Their commander was Pherendates the son of Megabazus. The Pactyans wore coats of skin and had native bows and daggers. Their commander was Artayntes the son of Ithamitres.

68. The Utians, the Mycians and the Paricanians were equipped like the Pactyans. Their commanders were Arsamenes the son of Darius, over the Utians and Mycians, and Siromitres the son of Oeobazus, over the Paricanians.

69. The Arabians wore loose cloaks belted up, and carried long bows at their right side that bent backward. The Ethiopians wore the skins of leopards and lions tied on them. Their bows were made from a strip of palm wood and were very long, at least six feet. With these they used small arrows of reed tipped not with iron but with a sharpened stone, the same kind of stone they use to carve seals. They also had spears, and on them, for a spearhead, the sharpened horn of a gazelle. They also had studded clubs. When they went into battle they painted half of their bodies with white and the other half with red. The commander of the Arabians and of the Ethiopians who live beyond Egypt was Arsames, the son of Darius and of Artystone the daughter of Cyrus. Artystone was the wife Darius loved most of all his wives, and he had a statue of her made of beaten gold.

70. So Arsames commanded the Ethiopians from beyond Egypt and the Arabians. But the Ethiopians from the east (for there were two bodies of Ethiopians) had been assigned to serve with the Indians. They were no different from the other Ethiopians except in their language and their hair. The eastern Ethiopians have straight hair, while the Ethiopians of Libya have the thickest, most tightly curled hair of any people on earth. These Ethiopians from Asia were armed mostly like the Indians, but on their heads they wore the skin of a horse's forehead, stripped off with the ears and the mane still on it. The mane served as a crest, and they kept the horse's ears standing up straight and stiff. Instead of shields they held up in front of them guards made of the skins of cranes.

71. The Libyans came in leather gear and used javelins hardened in the fire at the point. Their commander was Massages the son of Oarizus.

72. The Paphlagonians served with woven helmets on their heads, small shields, spears of no great size, javelins and daggers. On their feet they wore native boots reaching halfway up the shin. The Ligyans, the Matienians, the Mariandynians and the Syrians served with the same equipment as the Paphlagonians. (These Syrians are the people the Persians call Cappadocians.) The commander of the Paphlagonians and Matienians was Dotus the son of Megasidrus. The commander of the Mariandynians, the Ligyans and the Syrians was Gobryas, the son of Darius and Artystone.

73. The Phrygians had equipment very much like the Paphlagonians', with only small differences. According to the Macedonians, the Phrygians were called Brigians for as long as they lived in Europe as neighbors of the Macedonians. But when they moved to Asia they changed their name along with their country and were called Phrygians. The Armenians were armed exactly like the Phrygians, since they are colonists of the Phrygians. The commander of these two peoples together was Artochmes, who was married to a daughter of Darius.

74. The Lydians had weapons very close to those of the Greeks. In old times the Lydians were called Meonians, and they changed their earlier name and took a new one from Lydus the son of Atys. The Mysians wore native helmets on their heads, carried small shields, and used javelins hardened in the fire at the point. They are colonists of the Lydians, and they are called Olympieni after Mount Olympus. The commander of the Lydians and Mysians was Artaphernes the son of Artaphernes, the man who invaded at Marathon together with Datis.

75. The Thracians served wearing fox skins on their heads and tunics on their bodies, with colorful loose cloaks wrapped over them. On their feet and lower legs they wore boots of deerskin. They also had javelins, small round shields and little daggers. When they crossed over into Asia they came to be called Bithynians, but before that, as they themselves say, they were called Strymonians, because they lived on the river Strymon. They say they were driven out of their homes by the Teucrians and the Mysians. The commander of the Thracians who lived in Asia was Bassaces the son of Artabanus.

76. The next people's name has dropped out of the text as it has come down to us. They had small shields of raw oxhide, and each man carried two hunting spears of Lycian make. On their heads they wore bronze helmets, and on the helmets were the ears and horns of an ox, made of bronze, and crests on top of those. Their lower legs were wrapped in strips of red cloth. These people have an oracle of Ares.

77. The Meonian Cabelians, who are called Lasonians, had the same equipment as the Cilicians, and I will explain what that was when I come to the Cilician contingent in the course of my list. The Milyans had short spears and wore their clothes fastened with brooches. Some of them had Lycian bows, and on their heads they wore leather caps. The commander of all these was Badres the son of Hystanes.

78. The Moschians wore wooden helmets on their heads and carried shields and small spears with long points set on them. The Tibarenians, the Macronians and the Mossynoecians served with equipment like the Moschians'. These were grouped together under the following commanders. The Moschians and Tibarenians were under Ariomardus, the son of Darius and of Parmys, who was the daughter of Smerdis son of Cyrus. The Macronians and Mossynoecians were under Artayctes the son of Cherasmis, who was governor of Sestos on the Hellespont.

79. The Mares wore native woven helmets on their heads and carried small shields of hide and javelins. The Colchians wore wooden helmets on their heads and carried small shields of raw oxhide, short spears and also knives. The commander of the Mares and Colchians was Pharandates the son of Teaspis. The Alarodians and the Saspeirians served armed like the Colchians, and their commander was Masistius the son of Siromitres.

80. The island peoples who came with the army from the Persian Gulf, from the islands where the king settles the people called "the Removed," had clothing and weapons very much like the Medes'. The commander of these islanders was Mardontes the son of Bagaeus, who was one of the generals at Mycale the year after these events and was killed in the battle.

81. These were the nations that served on land in this campaign and had been assigned to the infantry. The men I have named were the commanders of this army. They were the ones who arranged it in divisions and counted it, and who appointed the commanders of thousands and the commanders of ten thousands. The commanders of hundreds and of tens were appointed by the commanders of ten thousands. There were also others who were leaders of companies and nations.

82. The men I have named were the commanders of the army. Over them, and over the whole infantry together, the generals were Mardonius the son of Gobryas; Tritantaechmes the son of that Artabanus who had advised against the march on Greece; Smerdomenes the son of Otanes (these two were sons of Darius' brothers, and so cousins of Xerxes); Masistes the son of Darius and Atossa; Gergis the son of Ariazus; and Megabyzus the son of Zopyrus.

83. These were the generals of the whole infantry together, except for the 10,000. The general of those 10,000 picked Persians was Hydarnes the son of Hydarnes. These Persians were called the "Immortals," because whenever any one of them dropped out of the number, carried off by death or by sickness, another man was chosen in his place. So they were never more and never fewer than 10,000. Of all the nations, the Persians made the most splendid show, and they were also the best fighters. They had the equipment I have described, and besides that they stood out from the rest because they wore gold in great quantities, lavishly. They brought carriages with them, and in them their concubines and a crowd of well-equipped servants. Camels and pack animals carried their food, separate from the rest of the soldiers'.

84. The nations that serve as cavalry are these, though not all of them supplied cavalry, only the ones listed here. The Persians were equipped the same way as their infantry, except that some of them wore headpieces of hammered bronze or iron.

85. There are also certain nomads called Sagartians, Persian by race and language, whose dress is halfway between that of the Persians and that of the Pactyans. These supplied 8,000 horsemen. It is not their custom to carry any weapons of bronze or iron except daggers. Instead they use ropes plaited out of strips of leather, and rely on these when they go to war. This is how these men fight. When they meet the enemy, they throw the ropes, which have nooses at the end. Whatever a man catches with his throw, horse or man, he drags toward himself, and the victims, tangled up in the coils, are killed.

86. That is how these men fight, and they were posted next to the Persians. The Medes had the same equipment as their infantry, and so did the Cissians. The Indians were armed the same way as their infantry, and they both rode horses and drove chariots pulled by horses or wild donkeys. The Bactrians were equipped like their infantry, and so were the Caspians. The Libyans were equipped like their infantry too, and they all drove chariots as well. In the same way, the Caspians and Paricanians were equipped like their infantry, and they all rode camels, which were every bit as fast as horses.

87. These nations alone served as cavalry, and the number of the cavalry came to 80,000, not counting the camels and the chariots. The rest of the cavalry was drawn up in squadrons, but the Arabians were posted behind them, last of all. The horses could not stand the camels, and so the Arabians were placed last to keep the horses from being frightened.

88. The commanders of the cavalry were Harmamithres and Tithaeus, the sons of Datis. The third commander of cavalry with them, Pharnuches, had been left behind sick at Sardis. Just as they were setting out from Sardis, he met with an unlucky accident. As he was riding, a dog ran under his horse's feet. The horse, which had not seen it coming, took fright, reared up and threw Pharnuches off its back. He vomited blood from the fall, and his illness turned into a wasting disease. As for the horse, his servants did at once what he ordered: they led it away to the place where it had thrown its master and cut off its legs at the knees. That is how Pharnuches lost his command.

89. The number of the triremes came to 1,207, and these are the peoples who supplied them. The Phoenicians, together with the Syrians who live in Palestine, supplied 300. They were equipped like this: on their heads they wore leather helmets made very much in the Greek style, and they wore linen breastplates and carried shields without rims, and javelins. These Phoenicians, as they themselves say, lived in ancient times on the Persian Gulf. From there they crossed over and settled on the coast of Syria, and this part of Syria, and all the land as far as Egypt, is called Palestine. The Egyptians supplied 200 ships. They wore woven helmets on their heads and carried hollow shields with broad rims, spears made for fighting at sea, and large axes. Most of them wore breastplates and carried large knives.

90. That was how these men were equipped. The Cyprians supplied 150 ships, and were equipped as follows. Their kings wore headbands wound around their heads, and the rest wore tunics, but in other respects they were like the Greeks. They are made up of various peoples: some come from Salamis and Athens, some from Arcadia, some from Cythnus, some from Phoenicia and some from Ethiopia, as the Cyprians themselves say.

91. The Cilicians supplied 100 ships. They too wore native helmets on their heads, and for shields they carried small round targets of raw oxhide. They wore woolen tunics, and each man had two javelins and a sword made very much like the Egyptian knives. In old times they were called Hypachaeans, and they took their later name from Cilix the son of Agenor, a Phoenician. The Pamphylians supplied 30 ships and were armed in the Greek style. These Pamphylians are descended from the people who were scattered from Troy with Amphilochus and Calchas.

92. The Lycians supplied 50 ships. They wore breastplates and greaves, and carried bows of cornel wood, arrows of reed without feathers, and javelins. They wore goatskins hanging over their shoulders, and on their heads felt caps with a ring of feathers around them. They also had daggers and curved swords. The Lycians were once called Termilae and came originally from Crete. They took their later name from Lycus the son of Pandion, an Athenian.

93. The Dorians of Asia supplied 30 ships. They had Greek weapons and came originally from the Peloponnese. The Carians supplied 70 ships. They were equipped like Greeks in other respects, but they also had curved swords and daggers. What their earlier name was I have already told in the first part of my history.

94. The Ionians supplied 100 ships and were equipped like Greeks. As long as the Ionians lived in the Peloponnese, in the land now called Achaea, before Danaus and Xuthus came to the Peloponnese, they were called, as the Greeks say, the Pelasgians of the Coast. Later they were called Ionians after Ion the son of Xuthus.

95. The islanders supplied 17 ships and were armed like Greeks. They too are a Pelasgian people, but later they came to be called Ionian by the same rule as the Ionians of the twelve cities, who came from Athens. The Aeolians supplied 60 ships. They were equipped like Greeks, and in old times they were called Pelasgians, as the Greeks say. The Hellespontians supplied 100 ships, except for the people of Abydos, whom the king had ordered to stay at home and guard the bridges. The rest of those who joined the expedition from the Black Sea region supplied these 100 ships, and they were equipped like Greeks. They are colonists of the Ionians and the Dorians.

96. On all the ships the fighting men were Persians, Medes or Sacae. Of the ships, the best sailers were supplied by the Phoenicians, and among the Phoenicians, by the men of Sidon. Over all these men, and over the ones of them assigned to the land army too, each people had its own native chiefs. I do not mention them, since the course of my inquiry does not oblige me to. For one thing, the chiefs of each separate nation were not worth mentioning; for another, in each nation there were as many chiefs as there were cities. These men came on the expedition not as commanders but as slaves, like the rest of the soldiers. The generals who held full power and commanded the different nations, the ones who were Persians, I have already named.

97. The commanders of the navy were Ariabignes the son of Darius, Prexaspes the son of Aspathines, Megabazus the son of Megabates, and Achaemenes the son of Darius. Ariabignes, the son of Darius and of Gobryas' daughter, commanded the Ionian and Carian fleet. Achaemenes, Xerxes' full brother on both his father's and his mother's side, commanded the Egyptians. The other two commanded the rest of the fleet. In addition, ships of thirty oars and of fifty oars, light boats, and long transports for carrying horses had been brought together, and their number came to 3,000.

98. Of the men who sailed in the fleet, after the commanders, these were the most notable: from Sidon, Tetramnestus son of Anysus; from Tyre, Matten son of Siromus; from Aradus, Merbalus son of Agbalus; from Cilicia, Syennesis son of Oromedon; from Lycia, Cyberniscus son of Sicas; from Cyprus, Gorgus son of Chersis and Timonax son of Timagoras; and from Caria, Histiaeus son of Tymnes, Pigres son of Hysseldomus, and Damasithymus son of Candaules.

99. I make no mention of the rest of the officers, since I am not obliged to, except for Artemisia. She amazes me more than anyone, a woman who joined the expedition against Greece. Her husband had died, and she held power herself. She had a son who was a young man, so no necessity was laid on her, but she went on the expedition out of her own high spirit and a man's courage. Her name, as I said, was Artemisia. She was the daughter of Lygdamis, and by descent she came from Halicarnassus on her father's side and from Crete on her mother's. She ruled the people of Halicarnassus, Cos, Nisyros and Calydna, and supplied five ships. The ships she supplied had the best reputation in the whole fleet after the Sidonians', and of all the king's allies she gave him the best advice. The states I said she led are, I declare, all Dorian by race: the people of Halicarnassus are Troezenians, and the rest are Epidaurians. So much for the navy.

100. When Xerxes had counted the army and it had been drawn up in divisions, he wanted to drive through it himself and inspect it, and afterward he did. He drove through in a chariot past each nation, asking questions about them, while his scribes wrote down the names, until he had gone from one end to the other of both the cavalry and the infantry. When he had done this, the ships were launched into the sea. Xerxes stepped from his chariot onto a ship of Sidon, sat down under a golden canopy and sailed along past the prows of the ships, asking about all of them just as he had about the land army, and having the answers written down. The captains had taken their ships out about four hundred feet from the beach and anchored them there, all with their prows turned toward the shore in an even line and all their fighting men armed as if for battle. He inspected them by sailing along the inside, between the prows of the ships and the beach.

101. When he had sailed past them all and had come ashore from his ship, he sent for Demaratus the son of Ariston, who was marching with him against Greece. When he had called him, he asked him this:

"Demaratus, it is my pleasure now to ask you something I want to know. You are a Greek, and not only that: as I hear both from you and from the other Greeks who come and talk with me, you are from a city that is neither the smallest nor the weakest in Greece. So tell me this: will the Greeks dare to raise their hands against me? As I see it, even if all the Greeks and all the remaining peoples who live in the west were gathered together, they would not be strong enough in battle to stand up to my attack, if they were my enemies. But I want to hear your opinion as well, and what you have to say about these matters."

That was his question, and Demaratus answered: "My king, shall I tell you the truth, or what will please you?"

Xerxes told him to tell the truth, and said that he would suffer nothing unpleasant for it, any more than he had before.

102. When Demaratus heard this, he said: "My king, since you order me by all means to tell the truth, and to speak as a man who will never later be caught by you in a lie, I say this. In Greece, poverty has always been native to the soil, while courage is something brought in, won by good sense and the strength of law. By means of courage Greece keeps off both poverty and slavery to a master. Now I have praise for all the Greeks who live in those Dorian lands, but what I am about to say does not concern them all, only the Spartans. About the Spartans I say, first, that there is no way they will ever accept your terms, which mean slavery for Greece. And second, I say that they will meet you in battle even if all the other Greeks take your side. And as for their numbers, do not ask how many of them there are, that they can do this. If a thousand of them happen to march out into the field, those thousand will fight you, and so will any number, whether fewer than that or more."

103. When Xerxes heard this he laughed and said: "Demaratus, what a thing to say, that a thousand men will fight this enormous army! Come, tell me this. You say you were once king of these men. Would you be willing, then, to fight ten men on your own, here and now? And yet if your whole state is the way you describe it, you, as their king, ought by your laws to take on twice as many as anyone else. If each of them is a match for ten men of my army, then I expect you to be a match for twenty. That would prove what you say. But if you, who boast so loudly, are men like the Greeks who usually come and talk with me, and no bigger than you yourself are, then watch out that what you have said does not turn out to be empty bragging. Come, let me look at it from every side, and see what is likely. How could a thousand, or ten thousand, or even fifty thousand men stand against so great an army, if they were all equally free and not ruled by one man? Suppose there are five thousand of them: we would still be more than a thousand against each one. If they were ruled by one man, the way we are, they might perhaps, out of fear of him, become braver than their nature, or they might be driven by the whip to fight against greater numbers though they were fewer. But left free, they would do neither. For my part I believe that even if the numbers were equal, the Greeks would hardly dare to fight the Persians alone. No, what you are talking about exists among us, but only in a few men, and rarely. Among my spearmen there are Persians who would gladly fight three Greeks at once. But you have no experience of such things, and so you are talking a great deal of nonsense."

104. To this Demaratus replied: "My king, I was sure from the start that if I told the truth I would not say what pleased you. But since you forced me to speak the plain truth, I told you how things stand with the Spartans. And yet how fond I am of them at present, you know better than anyone. First they took away the rank and the privileges that came to me from my fathers, and then they made me a man without a country, an exile. But your father took me in and gave me a living and a house to live in. Surely no sensible man is likely to push away kindness when it is offered to him; he will accept it and be grateful. Now I do not claim that I can fight ten men, or two. If I had my way, I would not even fight one. But if I had to, or if there were some great cause driving me into the fight, I would most gladly fight one of these men of yours who say they are a match for three Greeks. In the same way, the Spartans are as good as any men in the world when they fight one by one, and when they fight together they are the best of all men. For though they are free, they are not free in everything. They have a master over them, the Law, and they fear it far more than your people fear you. At any rate they do whatever it commands, and it always commands the same thing: never to run from battle, however many men they face, but to stay in their place and either win or die. But if you think I am talking nonsense when I say these things, then from now on I would rather keep silent about the rest. I spoke now only because you made me. Still, my king, may it all turn out as you wish."
